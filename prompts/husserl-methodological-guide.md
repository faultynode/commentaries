# A methodological guide for articles that analyse phenomenological concepts, drawn from Husserl's works

This guide tells a drafter how to analyse a phenomenological concept in the way Husserl analyses one, and how to lay that analysis out as a scholarly article. It tells a reviewer what to check. Every rule is derived from Husserl's own statements of method, or from what he visibly does, in a set of his works, and carries its evidence beside it. The guide concerns method. It does not summarise his doctrines; content appears only where it explains a step.

Use it in this order: read the twelve-rule summary, plan the article with the sequence in section 8, draft with the drafting checklist, and consult the full entries when a rule needs its reason or its limits. A reviewer runs the review checklist against the draft.

## Summary: the twelve rules that matter most

1. **Analyse the thing the concept names.** The article's authority is the phenomenon brought to intuition. A word, a definition or another author's doctrine has no authority of its own (S1, S2).
2. **Suspend, do not deny.** Put out of use every thesis the analysis has not itself established: inherited theories, scientific explanation, the existence of the object analysed. Say which you suspend (S3, S4).
3. **Describe; do not explain or deduce.** State what belongs to the experience and its object as experienced. Causal accounts and deductions may point the way, but each result must be shown directly (S5, D7).
4. **Begin by separating the senses of the word.** List the meanings the term covers, say which one the article is about, and expect more senses to appear later (B1, B2).
5. **Start from one concrete example.** Put a particular case before the reader, then vary it (B3, D2).
6. **Vary freely to find what cannot be removed.** Run the example through imagined variants, hold the variants together, and name what stays the same in all of them. Check that no restriction has been smuggled in (D2, D3, D4).
7. **Match precision to the matter.** Fix generic structures strictly. Do not force exact definitions on what is essentially fluid (D5, D6).
8. **Take the object as the guiding clue and question back.** From the object as it is given, go back to the ways of being conscious of it, describe both sides and keep them apart (I1, I2).
9. **Unfold what is implied.** Bring out the horizons, the merely co-meant sides and the possible further experiences that the object's sense points to (I3).
10. **Treat every term as revisable, and every use as testable.** Fix a term on a clear case, test it at each new use, and prefer a new word to an old one loaded with theory (T1, T2, T3).
11. **Criticise an author on descriptive grounds.** State what was given and what the theory was meant to clarify, show where the theory lost it, and keep what the opponent had right (H2, H3).
12. **Present the analysis as work in progress, and correct yourself in the open.** Go forward and back, return to earlier results, say what is left undone and what you have changed your mind about (O1, O3, O4).

## How to read an entry

Each entry has an **ID**, a **Rule** (what to do), a **Why** (what it does for the analysis), **Evidence** (what the texts say or do), a **Scope**, and sometimes **Apply**. A sentence that goes beyond what the texts show is marked **Inference**. Scope words mean: *stated*, Husserl gives it as a rule of method; *practised*, he visibly does it; *both*. The scope also says in which texts and periods the rule was found.

## 1. The stance of the analysis

### S1. Go back from the word to the thing it means

**Rule.** Treat the concept under analysis as a meaning that must be made good in intuition. Do not argue from the word, from a definition, or from what the concept is usually taken to imply.

**Why.** A concept understood only verbally can be exchanged for a neighbouring one without anyone noticing, and the argument then proves something about a different thing.

**Evidence.** “we can absolutely not rest content with ‘mere words’” and “Meanings inspired only by remote, confused, inauthentic intuitions – if by any intuitions at all – are not enough: we must go back to the ‘things themselves’.” [LI Intro §2, l. 2143] The danger is named just before: “Unnoticed equivocation may permit the subsequent substitution of other concepts beneath our words” [LI Intro §2, l. 2139]. In 1911 he separates this from the analysis of words: the phenomenological analyst “draws no judgments at all from word-concepts, but looks into the phenomena which language evokes through the words in question” *(my translation)* [PSW l. 397]. In 1907: “As little understanding as possible, then, but as pure an intuition as possible” *(my translation)* [IP l. 730].

**Scope.** Stated. 1901 to 1913 and after; the governing rule of everything below.

**Apply.** For every claim about the concept, ask what experience would show it, and put that experience on the page (B3).

### S2. Accept what is given, exactly as far as it is given

**Rule.** Assert what the case before you shows, and stop where it stops. Mark the point at which a claim goes beyond what has been exhibited.

**Why.** The principle gives description its right and also its boundary. Most errors of analysis are overstatements of something correctly seen.

**Evidence.** The “principle of all principles”: “whatever presents itself in “intuition” in primordial form (as it were in its bodily reality), is simply to be accepted as it gives itself out to be, though only within the limits in which it then presents itself” [ID §24, l. 719]. The same limit as a rule for premisses: “Such propositions must not, further, ever be adduced in some other sense than that in which they have been intuitively established.” [LI Intro §7, l. 2211]

**Scope.** Stated. LI (1913 text) and ID.

### S3. Suspend inherited theory about the concept

**Rule.** Do not use any philosophical doctrine about the concept as a premiss, including Husserl's own. Doctrines may be discussed as historical facts and tested (H1, H2); they may not carry the argument.

**Why.** A description that leans on a theory inherits the theory's unexamined content.

**Evidence.** He says of his opening analyses that he has taken the distinctions as they present themselves, “without any admixture of hypothesis or interpretation, and without reading into them anything that might be suggested to us by theories handed down from ancient or modern times”, and formulates a rule: “in respect of the theoretical content of all previous philosophy, we shall abstain from passing any judgment at all” [ID §18, l. 679]. The LI version: freedom from presuppositions means “the strict exclusion of all statements not permitting of a comprehensive *phenomenological* realization.” [LI Intro §7, l. 2203] In 1911: “To the truly unprejudiced it is indifferent whether a finding stems from Kant or Thomas Aquinas, from Darwin or Aristotle” *(my translation)* [PSW l. 704].

**Scope.** Stated. LI, PSW, ID.

**Inference.** The rule bears on an article about Husserl too. A sentence of his is a description to be re-performed and tested on a case. It is not a proof.

### S4. Bracket the existence of what is analysed; keep it as it is meant

**Rule.** Make no use of the claim that the object of the experience exists, or of any scientific account of it. Keep the object in the analysis as it is experienced and meant, and say that its sense has changed in this way.

**Why.** The analysis concerns how the object is given and what its being given involves. Claims about its real existence and causes add nothing to that and bring in premisses the analysis cannot check.

**Evidence.** Bracketing is not denial: “We do not abandon the thesis we have adopted, we make no change in our conviction”; rather “we set it as it were “out of action”, we “disconnect it”, “bracket it”” [ID §31, l. 787]. The time lectures open by requiring, for any phenomenological analysis, “the complete exclusion of every assumption,” stipulation and conviction concerning objective time [TC §1, l. 4391]. The object stays in the description, altered in sense: “One then readily forgets that such subsidiarily described objectivity, which is necessarily introduced into almost all phenomenological description, has undergone a change of sense” [LI Intro §3, l. 2157].

**Scope.** Stated. 1905 onward.

**Apply.** One or two sentences early in the article: what is set aside (for example the physiology of perception, the question whether the remembered event occurred) and what remains as the theme.

### S5. Describe; do not explain

**Rule.** Say what the experience and its object are and what necessarily belongs to them. Do not account for them by causes, origins in fact, or laws of nature.

**Why.** Explanation presupposes the thing explained and uses premisses about real existence. The task is to make the sense of the concept clear.

**Evidence.** “Its aim is not to *explain* knowledge in the psychological or psychophysical sense as *a factual* occurrence in objective nature, but to *shed light* on the *Idea* of knowledge in its constitutive elements and laws.” [LI Intro §7, l. 2205] Phenomenology is characterised as “a descriptive theory of the essence of pure transcendental experiences” [ID §75, l. 1331].

**Scope.** Stated. LI and ID. The later genetic inquiry (I5) asks about origins of sense, which he distinguishes from causal explanation.

## 2. Beginning the analysis

### B1. Begin by laying out the equivocations of the term

**Rule.** Open the analysis proper by listing the different things the term is used for. Number them. Say which sense the article pursues and how the others relate to it.

**Why.** The term reaches the analyst already used in several senses, and disputes about the concept are often disputes between senses.

**Evidence.** Stated: “the first beginning can obviously be no other than to lay bare the coarsest equivocations that first become visible” *(my translation)* [PSW l. 401]; and “By distinguishing among concepts confounded by us, and by suitably modifying our terminology, we then likewise achieve a desired ‘clearness and distinctness’ for our logical propositions.” [LI Intro §2, l. 2143] Practised: Investigation I begins “The terms ‘expression’ and ‘sign’ are often treated as synonyms” [LI I §1, l. 2225] under the chapter title "Essential distinctions". Investigation V begins with a section titled "Varied ambiguity of the term 'consciousness'", says “We must, in either case, distinguish between several thematically cognate, and so readily confounded, notions.” [LI V §1, l. 926], and numbers three concepts. It ends with a numbered list of the senses of 'presentation' that runs to thirteen [LI V §44, ll. 1573–1595]. Section headings in the same manner: "Two senses of 'demonstration'", "An ambiguity in the word 'presentation'", "Separation of varying concepts of abstraction and abstract" [LI headings].

**Scope.** Both. Pervasive in LI; stated as a principle in PSW.

**Apply.** Give each sense one sentence and one example. The list is the result of looking at cases, so each sense should be tied to a different kind of case (S1).

### B2. Do not claim the list is complete

**Rule.** Say that further senses may appear, and add them when they do.

**Why.** New distinctions become visible only as the analysis advances. A list closed at the start blocks them.

**Evidence.** “It need hardly be said that we have not exhausted *all* ambiguities of the term in question.” [LI V §1, l. 934] Progress, seen from outside, largely takes the form of “demonstrations of new ambiguities, only now become visible” in concepts that earlier inquiries had supposedly fixed *(my translation)* [PSW l. 401]. In the list of senses of 'presentation' he notes that the analyses needed to resolve them “have only been partially encountered in our previous expositions.” [LI V §44, l. 1578]

**Scope.** Both. LI, PSW.

### B3. Start from a single concrete example

**Rule.** Introduce the analysis with one particular case, described in the first person and the present tense, plainly and with its circumstances.

**Why.** An essence can be seen only in an instance. The reader needs the same instance in order to check the description.

**Evidence.** Stated: “no essential intuition is possible without the free possibility of directing one’s glance to an individual counterpart and of shaping an illustration” [ID §3, l. 529]; the method “has to place before its own eyes as instances certain pure conscious events, to bring these to complete clearness, and within this zone of clearness to subject them to analysis” [ID §65, l. 1213]. Practised: “Let us start with an example. In front of me, in the dim light, lies this white paper.” [ID §35, l. 843] “We start by taking an example. Keeping this table steadily in view as I go round it” [ID §41, l. 913].

**Scope.** Both. LI (1913 text), ID, PP.

**Apply.** Choose an ordinary case. Keep to the same example through the section so that each new distinction is shown on a case the reader already has.

### B4. Choose examples for their clearness, and replace them when they fail

**Rule.** Prefer cases that stay steady under reflection. When a feature remains obscure, look harder at the case or construct a better one. Imagined cases are as good as real ones, and often better.

**Why.** An unclear instance yields an unclear essence. Some experiences change when attended to.

**Evidence.** “thus we need to scrutinize our illustrative instances more closely or to contrive others that are better suited” [ID §69, l. 1255]. On unstable cases: “Anger reflected upon may dissipate, quickly modifying its content.” [ID §70, l. 1267] On imagined cases: “the freedom of research in the region of the essence necessarily demands that one should operate with the help of fancy” [ID §70, l. 1273], and “We can draw extraordinary profit from what history has to offer us, and in still richer measure from the gifts of art and particularly of poetry.” [ID §70, l. 1275] The qualification follows at once: this enrichment “does not imply that experience as such can be the ground of validity” [ID §70, l. 1275].

**Scope.** Stated. ID. For the most general differences a lower clearness suffices: “It is quite sufficient when grasping essential differences of the most general kind, as those between colour and sound, perception and will, that the exemplifying instances should show a lower grade of clearness.” [ID §69, l. 1261]

## 3. Describing

### D1. Turn from the object to the experience of it, and guard the turn

**Rule.** Make the act and what it means the object of description. Check each sentence for properties of the experienced thing that have been attributed to the experience, or the reverse.

**Why.** The reflective direction runs against habit, and the commonest fault of analysis is sliding back into talk about things.

**Evidence.** “The source of all such difficulties lies in the unnatural direction of intuition and thought which phenomenological analysis requires.” The analyst must “practise ‘reflection’, i.e. make these acts themselves, and their immanent meaning-content, our objects.” From this comes “the well-nigh ineradicable tendency to slip out of a phenomenological thought-stance into one that is straightforwardly objective” [LI Intro §3, l. 2153].

**Scope.** Stated. LI (1913 text); repeated in ID §150: “We do not see what we are actually experiencing” [ID §150, l. 2602].

### D2. Vary the example freely and name what does not vary

**Rule.** Take the example as a model, imagine it altered in every respect you can, and state what must remain if the result is still to be a case of the concept.

**Why.** The procedure separates what belongs to the concept necessarily from what belonged to the first example by accident.

**Evidence.** The question that defines the method: “what holds up amid such free variations of an original -- let us say, of a thing -- as the invariant, the necessary, universal form, the essential form, without which something of that kind, like this thing as an example of its kind, would be altogether inconceivable?” [PP §9a, l. 588] Its three moments: “multiplicity and unitary connection in continuous coincidence, and in addition as a third moment, the identifying of what agrees over against the differences by intuitively singling it out” [PP §9c, l. 618]. The earlier form of the same thought: “the element which makes up the life of phenomenology as of all eidetical science is “fiction”” [ID §70, l. 1277].

**Scope.** Stated. ID (1913) in outline; PP (1925) in full. CM §34 presents eidetic description as a principle pervading the whole method, introduced late in the exposition only to make access easier [CM §34, ll. 2158–2175].

**Apply.** Write two or three variants into the article, including one that breaks the concept. The broken variant shows where the necessity lies.

### D3. Hold the variants together; a few cases compared are not enough

**Rule.** Keep the series of variants in view as an open series that could be continued at will. Do not present a feature shared by a few collected cases as essential.

**Why.** What a handful of instances have in common is a fact about those instances.

**Evidence.** Comparing this red and that red yields “only a universal of just this and that red.” [PP §9d, l. 626] The eidos is won only “if we hold onto the previous fictions” [PP §9c, l. 620]. No exhaustive run is demanded; he rejects “the nonsensical demand actually to produce all possible variants” and requires only the consciousness “I could continue thus.” [PP §9c, l. 612]

**Scope.** Stated. PP.

### D4. Look for hidden restrictions on the variation

**Rule.** Ask what you have silently held fixed while varying: human subjects, this world, a particular sense, a particular language. Either remove the restriction or state it as the scope of the result.

**Why.** A result reached under an unnoticed restriction is narrower than it claims.

**Evidence.** “If we vary freely but secretly stipulate that the variants must be optional tones in the world, tones heard or able to be heard by human beings on earth, then we have indeed something essentially universal or an eidos, but one related to our factually actual world” [PP §9a, l. 594].

**Scope.** Stated. PP.

### D5. Let differences lead to higher genera

**Rule.** When two variants conflict in some respect, name the common genus within which they conflict, and order the results by level of generality.

**Why.** Conflicts between variants reveal the dimensions along which the concept is articulated.

**Evidence.** “every difference in the overlapping with other differences as clashing with it refers to a new universal to be singled out intuitively” [PP §9d, l. 634]. On levels: unambiguous fixing is impossible for the fully concrete flowing experience, but “it is quite otherwise with the essences at a higher specific level” [ID §75, l. 1337], and “Thus we describe and determine with rigorous conceptual precision the generic essence of perception generally or of subordinate species” [ID §75, l. 1339].

**Scope.** Stated. ID, PP.

### D6. Do not demand exactness the matter does not have

**Rule.** Use descriptive concepts with fluid boundaries where the phenomenon is fluid. Do not open with a definition in the manner of the exact sciences, and do not treat vagueness of such concepts as a fault.

**Why.** Exact concepts idealise. Applied to experience they describe something that is not there.

**Evidence.** Of descriptive concepts: “The vagueness of the concepts, the circumstances that they have mobile spheres of application, is no defect attaching to them” [ID §74, l. 1323]. Of those who demand definitions, or who work “with phenomenological concepts won from the rough analysis of a couple of illustrations and taken as firmly fixed”: they “are still so truly beginners that they have not grasped the essential nature of phenomenology” [ID §84, l. 1545]. “Clearness is quite compatible with a certain margin of indeterminacy.” [ID §84, l. 1545]

**Scope.** Stated. ID.

### D7. Use inference only to lead to something that can be seen

**Rule.** An argument, an analogy or a conjecture may guide the analysis. Count nothing as a result until it has been exhibited directly on a case.

**Why.** The results are descriptive. A conclusion that has only been inferred has not yet been tested against the phenomenon.

**Evidence.** “but in the end the conjectures must be redeemed by the real vision of the essential connexions. So long as this is not done we have no result that we can call phenomenological.” [ID §75, l. 1341] On persuasion: “What is grasped and described in the seeing attitude can be understood and tested only in the seeing attitude.” *(my translation)* [PSW l. 536]

**Scope.** Stated. PSW, ID.

**Apply.** Label conjectures as conjectures. Where an argument has carried a step, follow it with the case that shows the same thing.

## 4. Intentional analysis

### I1. Take the object as the guiding clue

**Rule.** Start from the object as it is straightforwardly given, fix its type, and ask back to the ways of being conscious in which an object of that type is given as one and the same.

**Why.** The modes of consciousness are too many and too fluid to survey directly. The identity of the object gives the inquiry its order.

**Evidence.** “The starting point is necessarily the object as it is straightforwardly given at the time, from which reflection goes back to the mode of consciousness at the time and to the potential modes of consciousness enclosed in it as horizon” *(my translation)* [CM §21, l. 1932]. The section is titled "The intentional object as 'transcendental guiding clue'" (*transzendentaler Leitfaden*). The same term in 1913: “the Thing-region may serve as a guiding clue in phenomenological inquiries” [ID §150, l. 2598]. In 1921: “The question is that of the guiding clues of the systematic order. The types of object offer themselves as such” *(my translation)* [PS l. 4497].

**Scope.** Stated. ID, PS, CM.

**Apply.** State the type of object the concept concerns (a perceived thing, a remembered event, a value, a number, another person) before describing the acts.

### I2. Describe both sides and keep them apart

**Rule.** For each structure, say separately what belongs to the experiencing and what belongs to the experienced as such. Flag any term that names both.

**Why.** Terms such as 'meaning', 'content' and 'presentation' name both sides. An analysis that does not divide them is ambiguous throughout.

**Evidence.** Reflection takes three directions, “hyletic, noetic, noematic”, and the question is “How are we to describe systematically the noeses and noemata which belong to the unity of the intuitionally presenting Thing-consciousness.” [ID §150, ll. 2602–2606] He criticises his own First Investigation on this ground: “it has no regard to the distinction and parallelism between the ‘noetic’ and the ‘noematic’” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 977].

**Scope.** Stated. 1913 onward; applied retrospectively to LI.

### I3. Unfold what the experience implies

**Rule.** Do not stop at dividing the experience into parts. Bring out what is co-meant without being given, the further experiences the present one points to, and the conditions under which the object would show itself further or otherwise.

**Why.** The sense of an object exceeds what any one experience presents. Its sense is understood only when these implications are made explicit.

**Evidence.** Intentional analysis differs from ordinary analysis into parts: its distinctive achievement is “the uncovering of the potentialities implied in the actualities of consciousness” *(my translation)* [CM §20, ll. 1920–1922]. It makes clear what is merely co-meant, “like the rear side”, by presentifying the possible perceptions that would make the unseen visible *(my translation)* [CM §20, l. 1926]. The modes of consciousness so disclosed are not arbitrary; they “remain bound to a structural type” *(my translation)* [CM §21, l. 1937].

**Scope.** Stated. CM. The practice is earlier (the table of ID §41).

### I4. Work through the strata in order

**Rule.** Where the phenomenon is layered, describe the founding layer first and say what each further layer presupposes. Revise the account of the lower layer when a higher one shows it in a new light.

**Why.** Features that seem to belong to one structure often have their source in more than one stratum.

**Evidence.** Further determination proceeds by “the splitting up into components or strata”, and terms often turn out to “have their source in more than one essential stratum” [ID §84, l. 1545]. Of the LI: “Ever new phenomenological strata swim into our view and add determination to our conceptions of the earlier ones.” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 965]

**Scope.** Both. LI, ID.

### I5. Static description first, then the question of genesis

**Rule.** First describe the structure as it stands, with the object type as clue. Only then ask how such a structure comes about in the life of consciousness, and say that this is a second inquiry.

**Why.** The genetic question needs a fixed description of what it is the genesis of.

**Evidence.** “It is clear that one will begin from individual basic types” *(my translation)* [PS l. 4497]; “The static clarification of the apperception of the world and of the sense-giving carried out in it has come first” *(my translation)* [PS l. 4490]. He leaves open whether the genetic can be wholly excluded from a static phenomenology [PS l. 4497].

**Scope.** Stated. PS; CM §34 names genetic phenomenology as a further level. Not found as a rule in the parts of LI and ID read.

**Apply.** If the article stays static, say so, and name the genetic questions it leaves.

## 5. Terms and expression

### T1. Fix each term on a clearly given case and hold it there

**Rule.** Introduce a term at the point where the thing it names is before the reader. From then on use it only for that.

**Why.** Results can be kept and used later only if the same words stay tied to the same things.

**Evidence.** Words of common speech “may be ambiguous, and in respect of their shifting sense also vague”; fitted to what is intuitively given they acquire a definite sense [ID §66, l. 1219]. The procedure is “to grasp what is momentarily perceived in faithful conceptual expressions” [ID §65, l. 1213].

**Scope.** Stated. ID.

### T2. Test a term again at every new use

**Rule.** When a term fixed in one context is used in another, check that it means the same there. If it does not, mark the new sense.

**Why.** Habit pulls a word back toward its other meanings.

**Evidence.** “it is a constant necessity, in face of the existing ambiguities of ordinary speech, to be cautious and frequently test whether a word fixed for use in a previous context may be employed in some new context in a sense which is really the same as before” [ID §66, l. 1223].

**Scope.** Stated. ID.

### T3. Avoid terms loaded with theory, or mark the load

**Rule.** Where a traditional term carries doctrines you have suspended, replace it with an unused word, or use it only where the context makes its sense single. Say why.

**Why.** A loaded term reintroduces the suspended theory (S3).

**Evidence.** “I avoid as far as possible the expressions a priori and a posteriori, partly on account of the confusing obscurities and ambiguities which infect their ordinary use” [ID Introduction, l. 485]; he adopts “the terminologically unspent Eidos” [ID Introduction, l. 487]. He restricts his own earlier term for the same reason: “The persistent misunderstandings to which this Act-concept has given rise have compelled me” to be more careful [ID §84, l. 1541].

**Scope.** Both. ID; the LI Foreword regrets keeping an “unsuitable term” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 985].

### T4. Where every available word is ambiguous, fix the sense indirectly

**Rule.** Set equivalent expressions side by side, set against them the expressions to be kept apart, and paraphrase. An image is permitted if it directs the reader to something that can be clearly seen.

**Why.** No single unambiguous word may exist for a newly distinguished structure.

**Evidence.** “we must put together equivalent expressions, and oppose them to expressions to be kept apart from them, and we must employ suitable paraphrases and explanations” [LI V §1, l. 936]. “For the beginning every expression is good, and in particular every suitably chosen image which has the quality of directing our glance to a phenomenological result that can be clearly grasped.” [ID §84, l. 1545] He admits a poor formulation and hands the matter to the reader: “That’s a shocking way of putting it, but I could not see how to avoid it. Let the reader figure the situation for himself in vivid intuition.” [ID §69, l. 1261]

**Scope.** Both. LI, ID.

### T5. Do not let grammar decide a distinction

**Rule.** A difference between two expressions is not yet a difference in the thing, and one expression may cover two things. Decide by going to the clear case.

**Why.** Grammatical form and structure of meaning correspond only roughly.

**Evidence.** “Everyone knows how readily and how unnoticeably an analysis of meaning can be led astray by grammatical analysis.” [LI Intro §4, l. 2165] To decide, “we must go back from *vague* acts of meaning to the correspondingly clear, articulate ones, acts saturated with the fulness of exemplary intuition in which their meaning is fulfilled” [LI Intro §4, l. 2171]. He also warns against the opposite error of dismissing too many distinctions as merely grammatical [LI Intro §4, l. 2173].

**Scope.** Stated. LI.

## 6. Other authors and history

### H1. Let the problem set the agenda

**Rule.** Organise the article by the questions the phenomenon raises. Bring in authors where they bear on a question. Do not organise it as a survey of positions.

**Why.** The article is an analysis of a concept. A report on its treatment by others is a different piece of work.

**Evidence.** “The impulse to research must proceed not from philosophies but from the things and the problems.” *(my translation)* [PSW l. 704] Of the LI: “Even where they proceed critically, they do not lose themselves in discussions of standpoint, but rather leave the last word to the things themselves, and to one’s work upon such things.” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 957] They “do not offer us a mere programme” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 957].

**Scope.** Stated. PSW, LI Foreword.

### H2. Criticise a theory by what it failed to describe

**Rule.** When rejecting an account, first fix what is given and what needed clarifying, then show at which point the account lost sight of it. Do not answer a theory only with a counter-theory.

**Why.** The shared phenomenon is the only ground on which both parties can be tested.

**Evidence.** Against the nominalist theories of abstraction: “no attempt has really been made to pin down what is descriptively given and what demands clarification, and to relate the two to one another” [LI II §14, l. 2795]. He then says “Let us once more run through our own undoubtedly clear, natural train of thought” and sets out the given differences before returning to Mill [LI II §14, l. 2795]. Investigation II is built this way throughout: a chapter of description is followed by chapters on Locke, Berkeley and Hume, each with a section of exposition and a section headed "Criticism" or "Objections" [LI II headings].

**Scope.** Practised. LI, Prolegomena and Investigation II.

### H3. Keep the justified sense of what you reject

**Rule.** Say what the criticised position saw correctly, and give the misused expression its legitimate meaning.

**Why.** A position usually errs by misdescribing something real. Stating what it had right locates the error exactly and guards against the opposite exaggeration.

**Evidence.** A section title: "A look back at the opposed arguments of idealism. Their defects and their justified sense" [LI Prolegomena §43, l. 1711]. In it: “their arguments hit on a point that was undoubtedly correct. But they overlooked the theoretical character of the laws of pure logic” [LI Prolegomena §43, l. 1713]. Of the old talk of universal objects: “But, however much they may have been historically misinterpreted, they must still have a normal interpretation which justifies them.” [LI II §14, l. 2801]

**Scope.** Practised. LI.

### H4. Use history to recover the sense of the problem

**Rule.** Where the concept has a history, inquire back from its present form to the aims with which it was first framed, and move between the two. Do not narrate the history for its own sake.

**Why.** The present concept carries sedimented decisions that are visible only from its beginnings, and the beginnings are intelligible only from what came of them.

**Evidence.** “Certainly we need history too. Not, to be sure, in the manner of historians” *(my translation)* [PSW l. 704]. In 1936 the procedure is named: the understanding of the beginnings and of the present form depend on each other, so “we must go forward and back in ‘zigzag’”, and “relative clarification on one side brings some illumination on the other” *(my translation)* [KR §9l, l. 1076]. He calls this an inquiry back (*Rückfrage*) into the original institution of the goals that bind the generations [KR §15, l. 1288].

**Scope.** Stated. PSW (1911) restrictively; KR (1936) as a positive method. **Inference:** the weight given to history grows between the two texts.

## 7. Order of exposition and the author's stance

### O1. Proceed in zigzag

**Rule.** Follow the systematic order of the subject, but break it to clear up a concept as soon as its obscurity threatens the analysis. Return to earlier results when later ones bear on them, and say that you are doing so.

**Why.** The concepts being clarified must be used in clarifying them, so no strictly linear order is available.

**Evidence.** The investigation can proceed securely only “if it removes conceptual obscurities which threaten the course of investigation *before* the natural sequence of subject-matters can lead up to such concepts. We search, as it were, in zig-zag fashion, a metaphor all the more apt since the close interdependence of our various epistemological concepts leads us back again and again to our original analyses, where the new confirms the old, and the old the new.” [LI Intro §6, l. 2189]

**Scope.** Stated. LI (1901/1913); the same word for historical method in KR (H4).

### O2. Distinguish the order of inquiry from a system

**Rule.** Do not present the analysis as a finished deductive structure. State the limits of its scope, and let the field define itself as the work advances.

**Why.** An order of discovery made to look like a system hides which steps were actually seen.

**Evidence.** “These investigations make no claim to be exhaustive.” and “The paths taken by such an analytic investigation will also naturally differ from those suitable to a final, systematic, logically ordered statement of established truth.” [LI Intro §5, l. 2183] The field “is itself not given to us initially, but becomes delimited in the course of our investigation.” [LI Intro §6, l. 2187]

**Scope.** Stated. LI.

### O3. Raise the reader by levels

**Rule.** Begin with a simplified account that the reader can check, and say that it is provisional. Add the complicating distinctions later and revise the first account in their light.

**Why.** Distinctions that matter at the end look like idle nuances at the start.

**Evidence.** The LI show “a regular ascent from a lower to a higher level” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 965]. Readers at first can reach only preliminary positions; only later “do they see the fundamental meaning of certain distinctions which appeared previously to be insignificant nuances” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 963]. The First Investigation “retains its merely preparatory character” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 975]. CM introduces eidetic description late for the same reason (D2).

**Scope.** Both. LI, CM.

### O4. Correct yourself openly

**Rule.** When the analysis overturns something said earlier in the article or in your earlier work, say what was wrong and why.

**Why.** The reader who followed the earlier step must be told it no longer holds.

**Evidence.** “Was I entitled to mislead the reader once more, through all my omissions, waverings and self-misunderstandings” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 959]. “I no longer approve of the rejection of the pure ego” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 985]. He calls his own treatment of occasional meanings “an act of violence” [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 975]. A footnote of his in a text printed in the same volume as PS reads: “Here I remain in contradiction to the *Ideas*” *(my translation)* [PS l. 4421].

**Scope.** Practised. LI Foreword (1913), PS.

### O5. Put questions of method to the method itself

**Rule.** Where the article makes a claim about how the analysis proceeds, hold that claim to the same standard as the analysis: it must be clear, and its terms must fit what is described.

**Why.** Reflection on method is part of the field it describes.

**Evidence.** “the methodological insights obtained in respect of their establishment come under the very norms which they formulate” [ID §65, l. 1213]. He opens the methodological chapter with the question “But how find the right beginning?” [ID §63, l. 1185] and treats objections to the method as direct questions answered one by one (§§64, 65, 71).

**Scope.** Stated. ID.

## 8. The sequence of an analysis, as an article

This sequence is assembled from the rules above. Husserl nowhere gives it as a list; the order is an **Inference** from LI V, ID §§35–41 and §§63–75, PP §9 and CM §§20–21.

1. **The concept and why it needs clarifying.** State the confusion or dispute that makes the analysis necessary (LI V Introduction: the term 'act' is controversial, so it “should be clarified before all others” [LI V Introduction, l. 912]).
2. **What is suspended.** Theories, explanations and existence claims set aside; what remains as theme (S3, S4, S5).
3. **The senses of the term.** A numbered list; the sense pursued (B1, B2).
4. **The example.** One concrete case (B3, B4).
5. **Description by variation.** What cannot be removed; what conflicts and under which genus; the scope of the result (D2–D6).
6. **Correlational analysis.** Object type as clue; the experienced and the experiencing described separately; horizons and implications; strata (I1–I4).
7. **Confrontation with existing accounts.** Each tested against the description; its justified sense kept (H1–H3). This may come earlier if a received theory blocks access to the phenomenon, as the critique of psychologism precedes the Investigations.
8. **Return.** What the later steps change in the earlier ones (O1, O4).
9. **What remains open.** Unresolved senses, the genetic questions, the limits of the examples used (B2, I5, O2).

## 9. Named moves

- **Separation of senses.** Listing the distinct things a term names before saying anything about "the" concept (B1).
- **Return to the case.** Replacing a verbal claim with the experience that would show it (S1).
- **Bracketing.** Putting a thesis out of use without denying it (S4).
- **Guarding the turn.** Checking that a description of experience has not become a description of things (D1).
- **Free variation.** Altering a model case in imagination to find what must remain (D2).
- **The breaking variant.** A variant that is no longer a case of the concept, used to locate a necessity (D2).
- **Lifting a restriction.** Finding and removing something silently held fixed during variation (D4).
- **Guiding clue.** Taking the type of object as the thread for questioning back to the modes of consciousness (I1).
- **Two-sided description.** Stating separately what belongs to the experiencing and to the experienced (I2).
- **Unfolding.** Making explicit what an experience co-means and points to (I3).
- **Redeeming a conjecture.** Following an inferred step with the case that exhibits it (D7).
- **Justified sense.** Stating what a rejected position had right (H3).
- **Zigzag.** Going back to an earlier result in the light of a later one (O1, H4).
- **Inquiry back.** Asking from a present concept to the aims with which it was first framed (H4).

## 11. Checklists

### Drafting

- [ ] The senses of the key term are listed and the one pursued is named.
- [ ] What is suspended is stated: theories, explanations, existence claims.
- [ ] A concrete example appears before the first general claim about the phenomenon.
- [ ] At least two variants are written out, one of which breaks the concept.
- [ ] The scope of each result is stated, including anything held fixed in the variation.
- [ ] The type of object is fixed, and the experienced and the experiencing are described separately.
- [ ] What the experience implies beyond what it presents is made explicit.
- [ ] Each technical term is introduced on a case and used in one sense; changes of sense are marked.
- [ ] Each criticised account is tested against the description, and its justified sense is stated.
- [ ] Conjectures and inferred steps are labelled, and either redeemed on a case or left as open.
- [ ] Revisions of earlier steps are announced.
- [ ] The article ends with what is unresolved.

### Review

- [ ] Does any step rest on a definition, an etymology or the authority of an author, with no case shown? (S1, S3)
- [ ] Does any sentence go beyond what the example and its variants show, without saying so? (S2)
- [ ] Has a causal, psychological or physiological explanation been offered as part of the analysis? (S5)
- [ ] Is the key term used in more than one of the listed senses? (B1, T2)
- [ ] Are properties of the object ascribed to the experience, or the reverse? (D1, I2)
- [ ] Is a feature called essential on the strength of a few similar cases? (D3)
- [ ] Is an exact definition imposed where the description shows fluid boundaries? (D6)
- [ ] Is an opponent answered only by a counter-theory? (H2)
- [ ] Is the article organised as a survey of positions? (H1)
- [ ] Is a provisional account left standing after the article has gone beyond it? (O3, O4)

## 12. Cautions

**The method and textual scholarship.** These rules describe first-order analysis of phenomena. An article that only reports what Husserl says about a concept does not follow them, however accurate it is (H1, S3). **Inference:** an article that combines the two should keep them visibly apart: what a text says, and what the case shows when the description is re-performed.

**Do not imitate the surface.** The rules concern procedure. The long periods, the nested qualifications and the first-person plural of the translations are features of the German academic prose and of its translators, and no rule here rests on them.

**Do not import the later doctrine into the earlier method, or the reverse.** The reduction as a systematic doctrine is later than the first edition of LI, as he says himself [LI Foreword to 2nd ed., l. 973]; the noetic–noematic division is from 1913 (I2); the genetic method is later still (I5); inquiry back into history is fully stated only in 1936 (H4). An article should say which stage of the method it uses.

## 13. Limits

- The folder holds about fifty files, most of them over a megabyte. This guide rests on the methodological passages listed in the key, found by searching for known statements of method and then read in consecutive ranges. No work was read whole.
- Not consulted at all: *Philosophy of Arithmetic*, *Formal and Transcendental Logic*, *Ideas II* and *III*, *First Philosophy I–II*, *Thing and Space*, the intersubjectivity volumes, the Bernau manuscripts, the lectures on ethics, on meaning and on logic, Hua 20–24 and 27. *First Philosophy II* (theory of the reduction) and *Formal and Transcendental Logic* (the idea of sense-investigation and the critique of evidence) are the first things a later pass should read; the rules on the reduction (S4) and on evidence (S2, D7) are thin without them.
- Rules marked *practised* rest on a few sampled places (LI I §1, LI II §14, LI V §§1 and 44, ID §§35 and 41, section headings). They were not tested across the corpus by count.
- The German texts are cited in my translation and were not machine-checked. The dates given for IP, TC, CM and KR are the usual ones and were not checked in the files; PS is left undated. The time lectures are cited from a file damaged by scanning, for one phrase only.
- The sequence in section 8 is my assembly.

## 14. Constraints on application

Write the article in English. Translate every quotation. Keep the German only for a term of art, or where the argument turns on wording that the English does not carry (a contested rendering, an etymology, a difference between editions); the test is whether a reader with no German would lose anything if the original were deleted. Where the original is kept, it follows the English in parentheses.

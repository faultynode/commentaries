# A methodological guide for articles that analyse phenomenological concepts, drawn from Heidegger's works

This guide tells a drafter how to analyse a phenomenological concept in the way Heidegger analyses one, and how to lay that analysis out as a scholarly article. It tells a reviewer what to check. Every rule is derived from Heidegger's own statements of method, or from what he visibly does, in a set of his works, and carries its evidence beside it. The guide concerns method. It does not summarise his doctrines; content appears only where it explains a step.

Use it in this order: read the twelve-rule summary, plan the article with the sequence in section 8, draft with the drafting checklist, and consult the full entries when a rule needs its reason or its limits. A reviewer runs the review checklist against the draft.

## Summary: the twelve rules that matter most

1. **Treat the concept as something covered up.** What the concept names does not lie open. The analysis has to uncover it against the ways it is ordinarily understood, and say what kind of covering it found (S2, S3).
2. **Work out the question before answering it.** Say what is asked about, which case is examined, and what the analysis is meant to establish (Q1).
3. **Declare what you bring to the analysis.** State what you take the thing as in advance, the respect in which you look at it, and the concepts you reach for. There is no reading without these (S4).
4. **Begin from the everyday, vague understanding.** Take the average way the thing is already understood as the starting material. Do not begin from a theory or a special case (Q2, Q4).
5. **Indicate the concept formally first.** Give an opening characterisation that points the reader in a direction and commits to little. Do not deduce from it (Q5).
6. **Give the received description first, then redescribe the same example.** Show what the received description leaves out and why it passes for unbiased (D1, D2).
7. **Ask how the thing is, not what further facts hold of it.** Lead the description back from the thing to its way of being, and use concepts that fit that way of being (D3, D4).
8. **Show derived forms as derived.** Trace the ordinary or theoretical concept back to the phenomenon it comes from, and give it back its limited right (D5, D9).
9. **Dismantle the inherited concept down to its sources.** Go back through the decisive historical stations to the experiences from which the concept was drawn. The aim is to recover what the tradition made possible (H1, H2, H3).
10. **Interpret an author toward what the text leaves unsaid, and say what guides you.** Go beyond the wording only under a stated guiding idea, and explain why the author could not see what you claim to see (H5, H6, H7).
11. **Use the word's original sense as a pointer, never as a proof.** Separate the senses of the term, show which is primary, and coin a term where the inherited one carries the wrong assumptions (T1, T2, T4).
12. **Present results as provisional and as directions for further work.** Repeat the analysis on a deeper basis when the article reaches one, answer the charge of circularity openly, and do not let a result harden into a thesis (D8, S5, T5, O2).

## How to read an entry

Each entry has an **ID**, a **Rule** (what to do), a **Why** (what it does for the analysis), **Evidence** (what the texts say or do), a **Scope**, and sometimes **Apply**. A sentence that goes beyond what the texts show is marked **Inference**. Scope words mean: *stated*, Heidegger gives it as a rule of method; *practised*, he visibly does it; *both*. The scope also says in which texts and periods the rule was found.

All files cited are English translations. Quotations are copied from the files and were checked against them by script. A citation gives the text's short name, the section, and the line number of the file, for example [BT §7, l. 487]. Inside quotations, the quotation marks, dashes and italics of the file are kept.

**A note on vocabulary.** The translation of *Being and Time* in the folder has its own renderings, and they appear in the quotations: "being-there" for *Dasein*, "destructuring" for *Destruktion*, "pre-having, pre-seeing, pre-ceiving" for *Vorhabe, Vorsicht, Vorgriff*, "on-hand" and "at-hand" for *vorhanden* and *zuhanden*, "ex-sistence" for *Existenz*. The other translations say "Dasein", "destruction" or "dismantling", and "forehaving". Outside quotations this guide uses plain English and gives the German once where it is a term of art.

## 1. The stance of the analysis

### S1. Phenomenology names how the analysis proceeds

**Rule.** Do not adopt a position about the concept in advance, and do not present the article as the application of a school's doctrine. Commit only to a way of proceeding: everything said about the thing must be shown on the thing.

**Why.** A method that brings theses with it has decided the result before looking.

**Evidence.** “The expression “phenomenology” signifies primarily a *concept of method*. It does not characterize what the objects of philosophical research are, what it bears on, but rather its *how*.” [BT §7, l. 435] Phenomenology means “to let be seen, from itself, what shows itself, and in the way it shows itself from itself” [BT §7, l. 483]. The term carries a prohibition: “Keep away from every act of determining that does not draw something identifiable into view!” [BT §7, l. 485] In the lectures: “It is therefore precluded from the start that phenomenology should pronounce any theses about being which have specific content, thus adopting a so-called standpoint.” [BP §5, l. 627] “The principle of research contains no result, no thesis, no dogma” [HCT §8a, l. 974].

**Scope.** Stated. 1923 to 1927, in every text of the period read.

### S2. What is to be shown is at first concealed; win it

**Rule.** Take as the theme of the analysis what does not show itself at first, although it belongs to what does show itself and makes it what it is. Do not take the first appearance of the thing for the thing.

**Why.** If the phenomenon lay open, no analysis would be needed. Straightforward looking delivers the received interpretation.

**Evidence.** The theme is something “that initially and mostly does *not* show itself, one that, in contrast to what does initially and mostly show itself, remains *concealed* while also being something that essentially belongs to what initially and mostly does show itself” [BT §7, l. 487]. “And precisely because phenomena are initially and mostly *not* given is there a need for phenomenology. The counter-concept to “phenomenon” is covered-up-ness.” [BT §7, l. 493] This excludes naive immediacy: the idea of an originary hold on the phenomena stands in “opposition to the naïveté of incidental, “immediate,” and unreflective “beholding.”” [BT §7, l. 499] Earlier: “Hence taking up the subject matter in a straightforward manner guarantees nothing at all.” [HF §15, l. 1076] “One takes a covering up of the subject matter for the subject matter itself.” [HF §15, l. 1074] And: “Because the phenomenon must first be won, scrutinizing the point of departure for access to the phenomenon and clearing the passage through the concealments already demand a high degree of methodological preparation” [HCT §9b, l. 1048].

**Scope.** Stated. HF (1923), HCT (1925), BT (1927).

**Apply.** The article needs three things secured: a point of departure, a way of access, and a passage through the prevailing concealments [BT §7, l. 499]. Name each.

### S3. Say what kind of concealment you are dealing with

**Rule.** Decide whether the phenomenon is undiscovered, was once seen and has been buried, or is disguised by something that passes for it. Decide whether the concealment is accidental or belongs to the thing.

**Why.** Each kind calls for a different route. Disguise is the hardest, because what covers the phenomenon looks like it.

**Evidence.** A phenomenon can be “still entirely *undiscovered*”, or “*buried*”; and “This kind of covering up, i.e., “disguising,” is the most frequent and the most dangerous” [BT §7, l. 495]. “There are incidental cover-ups and there are necessary cover-ups” [BT §7, l. 497]. The same division, in nearly the same words, two years earlier [HCT §9b, ll. 1044–1046].

**Scope.** Stated. HCT, BT.

### S4. State what the interpretation brings with it

**Rule.** Make explicit what the thing is taken as in advance, the respect in which it is looked at, and the concepts already chosen for it. Do not claim to read off "what is there".

**Why.** Every interpretation has these three. Left unstated, they are the interpreter's prejudice at work unnoticed.

**Evidence.** “Interpretation is never a presupposition-less apprehension of something pre-given. If the special concretion of interpretation taking shape as text-interpretation prides itself on citing “what’s there,” we may rest assured that “what’s there” first of all is nothing else than the undiscussed prejudice of the interpreter” [BT §32, l. 2150]. The three are named: “pre-having, pre-seeing, and pre-ceiving” [BT §32, l. 2150]. The demand for observation free of standpoints is called a prejudice [HF §17B, l. 1132]; rightly understood, “freedom from standpoints” is “nothing other than an explicit _appropriation of our position of looking_” [HF §17B, l. 1140]. The quality of the whole analysis depends on it: “The fate of our approach to phenomena and our execution of concrete hermeneutical descriptions of them hangs on the level of the primordiality and genuineness of the forehaving” [HF §16, l. 1112].

**Scope.** Stated. HF, BT.

**Apply.** BT restates all three at a turning point of the book, in one sentence, before going on [BT §63, l. 4594]. Do the same wherever the article changes level.

### S5. Enter the circle in the right way

**Rule.** Admit that the analysis presupposes an understanding of what it analyses. Take the presupposition from the thing, test it as the analysis proceeds, and say so. Do not try to argue from a neutral start.

**Why.** Understanding always starts from something already understood. The only choice is whether that start is taken from the matter or from received opinion.

**Evidence.** “What is decisive is not to get out of the circle, but rather to get into it in the right manner.” The task is “not to let, in each instance, pre-having, pre-seeing, and pre-ceiving be pre-given to it by fancies and popular conceptions, and rather to secure its scientific theme in the course of elaborating these three from the matter itself.” [BT §32, l. 2164] Formal objections of circularity “are always sterile when we are considering concrete paths of investigation.” [BT §2, l. 297] The presupposition is tested by the thing: the interpretation lets what is interpreted come into words “so that it can be decided of its own accord” whether it has the constitution projected for it [BT §63, l. 4618].

**Scope.** Stated. BT, three places.

### S6. Show; do not derive

**Rule.** Establish each structure by exhibiting it. Do not derive one structure from another, and do not build the account as a deduction from a principle.

**Why.** What is sought is the ground of what is already understood, and a ground is laid bare.

**Evidence.** “it is not a matter of deriving anything from a ground, but rather of laying bare the ground, showing it.” [BT §2, l. 299] “There can be no disclosure or deduction of essence from essence, apriori from apriori, one from the other. Rather, each and every one of these must come to demonstrative vision.” [HCT §9b, l. 1048] An existential analysis “does not prove anything at all with “rules of inference.”” [BT §63, l. 4618]

**Scope.** Stated. HCT, BT.

## 2. Posing the question

### Q1. Work out the structure of the question

**Rule.** Before answering, state three things: what the question is about, what is to be examined in order to answer it, and what is to be established.

**Why.** A question whose parts are confused will be answered about the wrong thing or from the wrong case.

**Evidence.** Questioning has “*something pressing* about which it asks”, it “engages *something to be examined*”, and aims at “*something to be ascertained*”. An explicit question is one in which “questioning here becomes transparent to itself, in all three named constitutive elements.” [BT §2, l. 281] The elaboration of the question then requires, among other things, “the preparation for the possibility of properly choosing an exemplary being” and “the elaboration of an intrinsic manner of accessing this being.” [BT §2, l. 295] The Husserl critique in HCT is organised by three such questions about its field [HCT §11, l. 1196].

**Scope.** Both. BT §2; HCT §8 and §11.

### Q2. Take the vague, average understanding as the starting material

**Rule.** Begin from the fact that the concept is already understood in some rough way. Describe that understanding, including its indeterminacy, and note where traditional theories have seeped into it.

**Why.** The explicit concept grows out of this understanding and must in the end explain it.

**Evidence.** “*This average and vague understanding of being is a **factum***.” [BT §2, l. 285] “Yet this very indeterminateness of the understanding of being, in each instance already available, is itself a positive phenomenon that stands in need of clarification.” [BT §2, l. 287] It “can be permeated by traditional theories and opinions” whose role as sources stays hidden [BT §2, l. 289].

**Scope.** Stated. BT.

### Q3. Choose the case to be examined and secure the access to it

**Rule.** Say which case the analysis examines and why that one. Choose a way of approaching it that lets it show itself as it is, and say what ideas you refuse to apply to it in advance.

**Why.** The case nearest to hand is often the hardest to see correctly, and inherited categories are applied to it without notice.

**Evidence.** “From *which* thing that *is* should we read off the meaning of being” [BT §2, l. 293]. “To itself, being-there is ontically “nearest,” ontologically farthest, and yet pre-ontologically not at all foreign.” [BT §5, l. 359] Negatively: no “arbitrary idea of being and actuality, no matter how “self-evident,”” may be applied “by way of dogmatic constructions”; positively, “our manner of access and interpretation must be chosen in such a way that this one being can show itself from itself and as itself.” [BT §5, l. 365]

**Scope.** Stated. BT.

### Q4. Start from average everydayness

**Rule.** Describe the thing first as it is "initially and mostly" met with in ordinary life. Do not start from an exceptional case or from an isolated act of observation.

**Why.** Structures that hold in the undifferentiated everyday case hold throughout. An isolated "experience" is an artificial extract.

**Evidence.** “And the manner should show this being as it *initially and mostly* is — in its average *everydayness*.” What is sought there are “essential structures” that persist through every way of being [BT §5, l. 365]. “What is decisive for the development of a forehaving is seeing Dasein in its _everydayness_.” [HF §18, l. 1152] He warns against taking an isolated act, “an artificial extract, as it were, from life”, for plain experience [HF §18, l. 1172].

**Scope.** Both. HF, BT.

### Q5. Indicate the concept formally before determining it

**Rule.** Open with a short characterisation that fixes the direction of looking and leaves the content to be filled by the analysis. Say that this is its status. Never argue from it as from a premiss.

**Why.** A definition given at the outset is either empty or already a theory. An indication commits the reader to look in a direction and to test what is found.

**Evidence.** “A _formal indication_ is always misunderstood when it is treated as a fixed universal proposition and used to make deductions from and fantasized with in a constructivistic dialectical fashion. Everything depends upon our understanding being guided from out of the indefinite and vague but still intelligible content of the indication onto the right _path of looking_.” [HF §16, l. 1116] Such determinations “are neither nominal definitions” nor “a priori essential laws, nor determinations which genuinely present their object.” [PIA l. 1157] In BT the guiding idea of existence is called “an existentielly non-binding prefiguration of the formal structure” [BT §63, l. 4610]. The term for this is formal indication (*formale Anzeige*).

**Scope.** Stated. PIA (1921–22), HF (1923), HCT (l. 998), BT §63.

**Apply.** One sentence, marked as an indication, then the analysis that fills it. HF gives the model: the indication “_the being-there of Dasein (factical life) is being in a world_” [HF §16, l. 1114], followed by the questions what "world", "in" and "being" each mean [HF §18, l. 1154].

### Q6. Clear away the dominant misdirections first

**Rule.** Before the positive analysis, name the two or three ways of looking at the thing that currently seem obvious and say why they are set aside.

**Why.** These approaches “crowd in” on the reader and will be read into the analysis unless excluded.

**Evidence.** Getting onto the right path “can and must be aided by a precautionary measure which takes the form of rejecting certain positions of looking which are dominant in the situation of research at the particular time” [HF §16, l. 1116]. HF then has a section "Misunderstandings" with two parts, on the subject–object schema and on freedom from standpoints [HF §17]. HCT has "Correcting a few typical misunderstandings of phenomenology which stem from its name" [HCT §9c]. BT has "Contrasting the analysis of being-there with anthropology, psychology, and biology" [BT §10] and "Distinguishing the existential analysis of death from other possible interpretations of the phenomenon" [BT §49].

**Scope.** Both. HF, HCT, BT.

## 3. Describing and interpreting

### D1. Give the received description first, then redescribe the same example

**Rule.** Set out, fairly and at length, the description that currently passes for the unbiased one. Then describe the same example again from the stance the article has prepared. Then say what the first description assumed.

**Why.** The contrast shows the reader that the "neutral" description is a construction, and it keeps the new description answerable to the same case.

**Evidence.** “an inaccurate description can initially be given, and indeed one which is not a fabrication and affectation on our part, but rather the one which everyone would today happily have pass for the most unbiased and straightforward description of what is immediately given” [HF §19, l. 1176]. He then describes a table as a material thing in space with aspects and added value-predicates. Next: “Considered solely in terms of raw subject matter, the “same” example will be held onto and a new description carried out” [HF §20, l. 1188]. The verdict on the first: “This does not mean it is “false,” as if it had no basis in the subject matter.” [HF §20, l. 1196]

**Scope.** Practised, with the procedure stated. HF §§19–20.

### D2. Describe the thing as it is met in dealings

**Rule.** Describe what the thing is for, where it stands, who uses it, what has happened at it. Use the definite, particular case.

**Why.** Things are met first in use and concern. Describing them as objects of mere inspection replaces the first encounter with a derived one.

**Evidence.** “What is there in _the_ room there at home is _the_ table (not “a” table among many other tables in other rooms and houses) at which one sits _in order to_ write, have a meal, sew, play.” [HF §20, l. 1190] “These lines are not just interruptions in the paint, but rather: it was the boys and it still is.” [HF §20, l. 1192] In BT the corresponding section is "The being of beings arising in a circum-world" [BT §15].

**Scope.** Practised. HF, BT.

### D3. Lead the description back from the thing to its way of being

**Rule.** After describing the thing, ask what way of being the description has shown, and project the thing explicitly onto that. Do not answer by pointing to another thing as its cause or origin.

**Why.** Adding facts about a thing never says how it is. That question requires its own kind of showing and its own concepts.

**Evidence.** Investigation turns first to some being, “but then, *in a precise way, it is led away* from that being *and led back to its being*.” [BP §5, l. 631] This leading back is not enough: being “must always be brought to view in a free projection” [BP §5, l. 633]. He calls the two steps reduction and construction, and separates his reduction from Husserl's [BP §5, l. 631]. The first step in understanding being is “not “telling a tale””, that is, not explaining things “by recurring to something else that is” [BT §2, l. 291]. It is one thing to tell stories about how beings are, “another to lay hold of beings in their *being*.” [BT §7, l. 513]

**Scope.** Stated. BT, BP.

### D4. Use concepts that fit the way of being of what is described

**Rule.** Do not describe human existence with concepts made for things, or the reverse. Where the analysis needs a different kind of concept, mark the difference.

**Why.** Concepts carry an understanding of how their objects are. Transferred to another kind of object they misdescribe it from the start.

**Evidence.** “we call the characteristic ways in which being-there *is*: *existentials*. These are to be sharply distinguished from determinations of the ways those beings are that do not take their measure from being-there; these we call: *categories*.” [BT §9, l. 839] Interpretation can draw its concepts from the beings interpreted, “or then again it can force them into concepts to which the beings themselves offer resistance” [BT §32, l. 2148]. The historical diagnosis is the same: the inherited categories were transferred to the subject “with corresponding formalizations and merely negative restrictions” [BT §6, l. 395].

**Scope.** Stated. BT.

### D5. Show derived, deficient and broken forms as modes of the basic phenomenon

**Rule.** Treat absence, breakdown and lack as ways the basic structure shows itself. Present the theoretical or familiar form of the phenomenon as founded on a more basic one, and show the founding.

**Why.** A structure that holds even in its failure is essential. A founded form mistaken for the basic one hides what it rests on.

**Evidence.** “The absence of historiography is no evidence *against* the historicity of being-there; rather, as a deficient mode of the constitution of its being, such absence is evidence *for* it.” [BT §6, l. 387] “Being-alone is, for being-there, also a mode of being-with within world.” [BT §26, l. 1826] On breakdown: “As a deficient mode of taking-care, our helplessness uncovers the things at hand in their merely being on hand.” [BT §16, l. 1239] Headings state the procedure: "Exemplification of being-in by way of a founded mode: knowing the world" [BT §13]; "Assertion as a derived mode of interpretation" [BT §33]; "The primordial phenomenon of truth, and the derivativeness of the traditional concept of truth" [BT §44b].

**Scope.** Practised. Pervasive in BT.

### D6. Keep the phenomenon whole

**Rule.** Present the moments of a structure as aspects of one phenomenon, each a point of view on the whole. Do not assemble the phenomenon from parts.

**Why.** The parts are not there first. Assembling them presupposes the unity that was to be understood.

**Evidence.** “We will not be piecing together the phenomenon of the being-there of Dasein out of these different definitions, but rather our focus at the particular time [_jeweiligen Betonung_] on a _single_ term in this formal indication will always be but one possible point of view on the same unitary phenomenon.” [HF §18, l. 1154] Of the structure of understanding: “This phenomenon is manifestly not one we can break down into “pieces.”” [BT §32, l. 2152]

**Scope.** Stated. HF, BT.

### D7. Find a case that discloses the whole, and seek pre-theoretical confirmation

**Rule.** Look for a particular experience in which the structure shows itself whole and undisguised. Then look for testimony outside philosophy that the same thing has been understood before.

**Why.** The analysis needs a point where the phenomenon itself answers. Independent testimony guards against invention.

**Evidence.** BT turns to "The basic attunement of dread as a pre-eminent disclosedness of being-there" [BT §40], then to "Confirming the existential interpretation of being-there as care in reference to a pre-ontological self-interpretation of being-there" [BT §42], and announces both steps together [BT §39, l. 2758]. Division Two asks for "the attestation of an authentic existentiell possibility" [BT §54]. The principle: “Without an existentiell understanding, every analysis of existentiality will have no basis.” [BT §63, l. 4604]

**Scope.** Practised. BT.

### D8. Call the first analysis provisional and repeat it on a deeper basis

**Rule.** Say at the outset that the first pass only brings the structure into relief. When the article reaches a deeper ground, go through the earlier results again from there.

**Why.** The first description is made before the horizon that explains it is available.

**Evidence.** “The analysis of being-there is, however, not only incomplete, it is at first also *provisional*.” Once the horizon is attained, it “requires repetition on a higher, an authentic ontological basis.” [BT §5, l. 367] The proof must “sustain itself in the repeated interpretation” of the earlier structures [BT §5, l. 369]. Carried out under the heading "The temporality of being-there, and the tasks arising from it of a more primordial repetition of the existential analysis" [BT §66].

**Scope.** Both. BT.

### D9. Give the ordinary concept back its limited right

**Rule.** After showing where the ordinary or traditional concept comes from, say what it is correct about and within what limits.

**Why.** The ordinary concept is derived, and deriving it explains it. The analysis is complete only when it can account for the view it began by setting aside.

**Evidence.** Showing how the ordinary understanding of time springs from temporality, “We thereby restore to the ordinary concept of time its own rightful place” [BT §5, l. 371]. Headings: "Inner-timely-ness and the genesis of the ordinary concept of time" [BT §81]; "The existential interpretation of conscience and its ordinary interpretation" [BT §59]. Of the received description of the table: its results may hold “for a theoretical observing which has a definite direction and focus.” [HF §20, l. 1196]

**Scope.** Both. HF, BT. See L1 for the later form of the move.

## 4. History and other authors

### H1. Dismantle the inherited concept down to the experiences it came from

**Rule.** For the concepts the analysis has to use, ask where they were first formed and from what experience. Follow them back, and state what that origin made it possible to see and what it closed off.

**Why.** Even the most radical new start is “pervaded by traditional concepts” whose fitness cannot be assumed [BP §5, l. 635]. Tradition hands concepts down as self-evident and so blocks the way to their sources.

**Evidence.** Tradition “entrusts to self-evidence what has been transmitted, it dislocates the access to the primordial “wellsprings”” [BT §6, l. 393]. The task is a “*destructuring* of the inherited body of ancient ontology, one returning us to the primordial experiences in which the first, and from then on the guiding determinations of being were obtained.” [BT §6, l. 397] Its intent is positive: “Destructuring does not aim to bury the past in nothingness; it has a positive intention, and its negative function remains tacit and indirect.” [BT §6, l. 399] In the lectures it is one of three components of method, “a critical process in which the traditional concepts, which at first must necessarily be employed, are deconstructed down to the sources from which they were drawn.” [BP §5, l. 635] “And this is not a negation of the tradition or a condemnation of it as worthless; quite the reverse, it signifies precisely a positive appropriation of tradition.” [BP §5, l. 637] In 1923: “For this it is necessary to disclose the history of the covering up of the subject matter.” [HF §15, l. 1076] The German term is *Destruktion*.

**Scope.** Stated. HF, BT, BP.

### H2. Aim the critique at the present

**Rule.** Start the historical part from the way the concept is handled now, and direct the criticism at that handling. Do not criticise the past author for the present confusion.

**Why.** The concealment to be removed is one the present lives in.

**Evidence.** “This destructuring does not relate itself negatively to the past; its critique bears on the “current day,”” that is, on the “currently dominant manner of treating the history of ontology” [BT §6, l. 399]. “The dismantling takes its point of departure from a presentation of today’s situation” [HF §15, l. 1080]. HF devotes a chapter to "Being-Interpreted in Today's Today" before its phenomenological part.

**Scope.** Both. HF, BT.

### H3. Limit the history to decisive stations, and work backwards

**Rule.** Do not survey. Pick the few points at which the concept was decisively formed or passed on, and go from the later to the earlier.

**Why.** The history serves the question. Each later station is understood by what it took over from the one before.

**Evidence.** The destructuring can be carried out “only in regard to the fundamentally decisive stations of this history.” [BT §6, l. 401] The stations named run backwards: Kant's schematism, Descartes' *cogito sum*, Aristotle's treatise on time [BT §6, ll. 403–423]. Against the survey: “One turns history into a story.” [HF §15, l. 1078] Discovering that an author depends on a predecessor gains “nothing” philosophically until the extent of the influence on how the thing is determined has been shown [BT §6, l. 415].

**Scope.** Both. HF, BT.

### H4. Treat a traditional thesis in three steps

**Rule.** State the content of the thesis and its traditional discussion. Clarify the problem that underlies it by going back to the phenomenon. Show that its foundation is inadequate and that a more radical formulation is needed.

**Why.** The thesis is kept as an answer to a real problem, and the criticism lands on its foundation.

**Evidence.** The chapters of BP Part One are built this way. On Kant: "The content of the Kantian thesis", "Phenomenological analysis of the explanation of the concept of being or of existence given by Kant", "Demonstration of the need for a more fundamental formulation of the problem of the thesis and of a more radical foundation of this problem" [BP §§7–9]. On medieval ontology: "The content of the thesis and its traditional discussion", "Phenomenological clarification of the problem underlying the second thesis", "Proof of the inadequate foundation of the traditional treatment of the problem" [BP §§10–12]. The part is titled "Critical Phenomenological Discussion of Some Traditional Theses about Being".

**Scope.** Practised. BP; two chapters checked.

### H5. Criticise from within: ask whether the determinations are drawn from the matter

**Rule.** List the author's determinations of the thing. Ask of each whether it comes from looking at the thing or from somewhere else, such as an inherited idea or a requirement of the author's programme. Then name what was not asked.

**Why.** This holds the author to the author's own principle, so the criticism needs no outside standard.

**Evidence.** The section is titled "Immanent critique of phenomenological research". Husserl's four determinations of consciousness are listed, and then: “Are they determinations which arise from a regard for the subject matter itself? Are they determinations of being which are drawn from the consciousness and from the very entity intended by this term?” [HCT §11, l. 1204] The result is stated as an omission: "Exposition of the neglect of the question of the being of the intentional" [HCT §12]. The critique of Rickert makes the converse point: he takes “an empty verbal concept” of appearance “as a basis for criticizing the concrete labor of a research effort.” [HCT §9c, l. 1056]

**Scope.** Practised. HCT.

### H6. Interpret toward what the text leaves unsaid, under a stated guiding idea

**Rule.** Do not stop at reporting what the author says. Bring out what the text makes visible beyond its formulations. Say what idea guides this step, and let the text decide whether the idea holds.

**Why.** Restating is not interpreting. Going beyond the words without a declared guide is arbitrary.

**Evidence.** “Now, if an interpretation [*Interpretation*] merely gives back what Kant has expressly said, then from the outset it is not a laying-out [*Auslegung*]”; what is decisive is “what it sets before our eyes as still unsaid, in and through what has been said.” [KPM §35, l. 3011] “Certainly, in order to wring from what the words say, what it is they want to say, every interpretation [*Interpretation*] must necessarily use violence. Such violence, however, cannot be roving arbitrariness. The power of an idea which shines forth must drive and guide the laying-out [*Auslegung*].” [KPM §35, l. 3017] BT says the same of its own analysis, which appears as “*doing violence*” to the everyday interpretation, and asks whether there is not some appropriate “*guidance* and *regulation* for these efforts?” [BT §63, l. 4600]

**Scope.** Stated. BT, KPM.

**Apply.** Keep three things apart on the page: what the text says, what you claim it shows beyond that, and the idea that licenses the step. The violence is permitted only with the third stated.

### H7. Explain why the author had to miss it

**Rule.** When the article claims an author neglected something, show what in the author's starting point made the neglect necessary.

**Why.** An omission shown to be necessary is evidence about the tradition. A bare complaint tells the reader nothing.

**Evidence.** “Along the way we will also be able to show *why* this area had to remain closed to Kant” [BT §6, l. 403]. “Two things prevented this insight” [BT §6, l. 409]. The interpretation of Descartes “shows why he came to the opinion that the absolute “certainty” of the *cogito* exempted him from the question” [BT §6, l. 411]. The answer to the guiding question is adequate only when it gives insight into previous thought “as something necessary” [BT §5, l. 381].

**Scope.** Both. BT.

### H8. Retrieve the problem

**Rule.** End the historical part by stating the problem the author was working on in a form that opens what the author left closed.

**Why.** The purpose of the history is to recover a possibility.

**Evidence.** “By the retrieval of a basic problem, we understand the opening-up of its original, long-concealed possibilities, through the working-out of which it is transformed.” [KPM Part Four, opening, l. 3044] The answer sought must be “*old* enough that, from it, we can learn to conceive the possibilities the “Ancients” set up for us.” [BT §5, l. 379] The German term is *Wiederholung*, rendered "repetition" in BT and "retrieval" in KPM.

**Scope.** Stated. BT, KPM.

## 5. Language and terms

### T1. Go back to the original sense of the word, as a pointer

**Rule.** Analyse the term into its components and recover what they meant in the language they come from. Use the result to direct attention to the phenomenon. Do not offer it as proof, and do not narrate the history of the word.

**Why.** Old words often preserve an experience later usage has flattened. A word can prove nothing about the thing.

**Evidence.** §7 clarifies "phenomenology" by taking "phenomenon" and "logos" back to the Greek verbs, while saying: “The history of the word itself, which seems to have originated in the Wolffian school, is here without any significance.” [BT §7, l. 439] The caution: “Recourse to such evidence must guard against uninhibited word-mysticism; still, in the end it is the business of philosophy to preserve the *power of the most elementary words*” [BT §44b, l. 3002]. HCT has the same clarification of the name [HCT §9a].

**Scope.** Both. HCT, BT.

### T2. Separate the senses of the term and show which one founds the others

**Rule.** Lay out the meanings the term has acquired. Do not leave them side by side: show which is primary and how the others depend on it or modify it.

**Why.** A list of senses leaves the confusion in place. Ordering the senses by which founds which explains the confusion.

**Evidence.** For "phenomenon": self-showing, seeming, appearance, mere appearance. “Already in the one meaning of ϕαινόμενον (“seeming”) there lies the primordial meaning (phenomenon: the manifest) as founding the other.” [BT §7A, l. 443] “On this account, phenomena are *never* appearances, but every appearance is dependent upon phenomena.” [BT §7A, l. 447] “The confusing multiplicity of “phenomena”” “can only be disentangled if right from the start we understand the concept of phenomenon” [BT §7A, l. 455]. For *logos*, the apparent scatter of meanings (reason, judgement, concept, ground, proportion) is derived one by one from the basic meaning [BT §7B, ll. 463–477].

**Scope.** Practised. BT §7.

### T3. Move from the formal concept to the concept the analysis needs

**Rule.** First state the concept formally, leaving open what it applies to. Then say what must be added for it to become the concept the analysis works with.

**Why.** The formal concept secures the meaning of the word. The working concept depends on a decision about the subject matter, which should be visible as a decision.

**Evidence.** If it is left undetermined what is addressed as phenomenon, “one has simply obtained the *formal* concept of phenomenon.” [BT §7A, l. 457] Then the question: “What then must we take account of so that the formal concept of phenomenon will be deformalized into the phenomenological one?” [BT §7C, l. 487]

**Scope.** Practised. BT §7.

### T4. Coin a term where the inherited one misleads, and accept the awkwardness

**Rule.** Where the available word carries assumptions the analysis rejects, introduce a term, say that you are doing so, and fix it. Do not smooth the expression at the cost of the distinction.

**Why.** The existing vocabulary and grammar were formed for speaking about things.

**Evidence.** “This one being” “we formulate terminologically as *being-there*.” [BT §2, l. 295] Because "temporal" already has other meanings, a new expression is introduced for the new one [BT §5, l. 377]. On the result: “For this second task, not only are the words mostly lacking, but even more the “grammar.”” [BT §7, l. 513]

**Scope.** Both. BT.

**Inference.** An article should coin sparingly and only at a point where the reader has been shown what the old word would smuggle in.

### T5. Keep results from hardening into free-floating theses

**Rule.** State each result together with the case and the path that yielded it. Do not hand over conclusions that can be repeated without looking.

**Why.** A proposition passed on without its source is understood emptily and then conceals what it once showed.

**Evidence.** “Every phenomenological concept or proposition drawn from its source can degenerate when communicated in an assertion: it gets circulated in a vacuous intelligibility, loses its rootedness, and becomes a free-floating thesis.” [BT §7, l. 497] “The possibility of radical discovery at the same time brings with it the corresponding danger that phenomenology may become hardened in its own results.” [HCT §9b, l. 1046] The answer to the guiding question “is not grasped in the recitation of what it asserts in propositional form” [BT §5, l. 379].

**Scope.** Stated. HCT, BT.

## 6. Order of exposition and the author's stance

### O1. Let the method come out of the work

**Rule.** Do not open with a free-standing methodology. Say only what is needed to orient the reader, and place reflection on method where the analysis requires it.

**Why.** Method is determined by the subject matter, and becomes technique when detached from it.

**Evidence.** “The more genuinely a concept of method unfolds itself,” “the more primordially it is rooted in the struggle with the matters themselves and the more it distances itself from what we call technical manipulation” [BT §7, l. 435]. “Scientific method is never a technique.” [BP §5, l. 631] His own remarks on method have “nothing to do with providing a methodology for phenomenology, an undertaking of the most dubious kind” [HF §16, l. 1104]. HCT takes its principle from the work done: “We are making no deduction from the idea of phenomenology but are *reading the principle from its concretion in the research work*.” [HCT §8a, l. 978] BT places its fullest methodological reflection in §63, after the analyses it reflects on.

**Scope.** Both. HF, HCT, BT, BP.

### O2. Mark limits and do not overestimate results

**Rule.** Say what the analysis does not cover and that a more original starting point may yet be found.

**Why.** The matter is deeply concealed, and each result reopens the question of its own basis.

**Evidence.** “every investigation should refrain from overestimating its results. For this kind of questioning constantly forces itself to face the possibility of the disclosure of a still more primordial, still more universal horizon” [BT §6, l. 427]. The analysis “cannot hope to provide a complete ontology of being-there” and offers only “pieces” [BT §5, l. 367]. A successful demonstration “still does not say anything about the primordiality of the forehaving” [HF §16, l. 1114].

**Scope.** Stated. HF, BT.

### O3. Advance by questions

**Rule.** At each transition, state the questions the previous step has raised, in a series, and let them set the next step. Say which cannot yet be answered.

**Why.** The questions show the reader why the next step is necessary and keep the conclusion from being presumed.

**Evidence.** After the analysis of interpretation: seven questions in a row, then “Before we answer these questions (the foregoing analysis falls way short of equipping us for answering them) we must investigate” [BT §32, ll. 2152–2154]. §63 proceeds through more than twenty questions about its own justification [BT §63, ll. 4600–4618]. The Husserl critique opens with six [HCT §11, l. 1192].

**Scope.** Practised. HCT, BT.

### O4. Raise the strongest objection to your own procedure

**Rule.** Put to yourself the objection that the analysis is circular, arbitrary or one-sided, in its strongest form, and answer it from the nature of the subject matter.

**Why.** The objection will be made. Answered from the matter, it becomes part of the analysis.

**Evidence.** “But is not such an undertaking obviously circular?” [BT §2, l. 297] “The “violent” presentation of possibilities of ex-sistence might be methodologically required, but can it escape being merely arbitrary?” [BT §63, l. 4606] “Isn’t everything already illuminated, even if dimly, by the light of our “presupposed” idea of ex-sistence? Where does this idea get its justification?” [BT §63, l. 4608]

**Scope.** Practised. BT.

### O5. Offer the answer as a directive for further inquiry

**Rule.** Close on what the result now makes it possible to investigate. Do not close on a thesis that claims novelty.

**Why.** A result that only sets a new "standpoint" beside the old ones has missed what the question asked for.

**Evidence.** “Whether the answer is “new” has no bearing and remains an external consideration.” “By its very meaning, the answer provides a directive for concrete ontological research to begin questioning investigatively within the exposed horizon — and this is all the answer provides.” [BT §5, l. 379]

**Scope.** Stated. BT.

## 7. A later form of the procedure

These entries rest on the opening pages of one essay of the 1950s and are labelled one-text. They are included because they show the earlier moves D9 and D3 in a later shape.

### L1. Seek the true by way of the correct

**Rule.** Grant the current definition of the thing as correct. Then ask what the definition itself takes for granted, and follow that.

**Evidence.** After stating the instrumental definition of technology and agreeing that it is correct: the “merely correct is not yet the true”, and the task is to seek “the true by way of the correct” [QCT l. 485]. The next step asks what the instrumental itself is.

**Scope.** One text. Practised and stated.

### L2. The essence of a thing is not another thing of that kind

**Rule.** Do not answer the question what something is by pointing to more instances or features of it.

**Evidence.** “Technology is not equivalent to the essence of technology.” [QCT l. 463]

**Scope.** One text. Compare D3, where the same point is made in the vocabulary of 1927.

### L3. Attend to the way, not to isolated sentences

**Rule.** Write so that each step depends on the one before, and warn the reader against lifting sentences out.

**Evidence.** “Questioning builds a way.” [QCT l. 457] The reader is asked to attend to the way and not to isolated sentences and topics [QCT l. 457].

**Scope.** One text. Compare T5.

## 8. The sequence of an analysis, as an article

This sequence is assembled from the rules above. Heidegger nowhere gives it as a list; the order is an **Inference** from BT §§2–7 and the layout of BT Division One, HF §§15–20, and BP §5 with the chapters of BP Part One.

1. **The question.** What is asked about, what is examined, what is to be established; the vague understanding the question starts from (Q1, Q2).
2. **The present situation.** How the concept is handled today and what that handling takes for granted (H2, S3).
3. **The term.** Its components and original sense as a pointer; its senses ordered by which founds which; the formal concept (T1, T2, T3).
4. **What the analysis brings with it.** The formal indication, and the three presuppositions made explicit (Q5, S4).
5. **Misdirections set aside** (Q6).
6. **The received description of an everyday example, then the redescription** (Q4, D1, D2).
7. **The way of being shown by the redescription**, in concepts that fit it; derived and deficient forms shown as modes; the phenomenon kept whole (D3–D6).
8. **A disclosing case and pre-theoretical confirmation** (D7).
9. **The historical dismantling**, by decisive stations and backwards; each thesis in three steps; why each author had to miss what was missed; the problem retrieved (H1–H8). In BT this is announced early (§6) and carried out in part alongside the analysis, as in the discussion of Descartes placed inside the analysis of world (§§19–21).
10. **Repetition on the deeper basis**, and the ordinary concept given its limited right (D8, D9).
11. **The objection to the procedure, raised and answered** (S5, O4).
12. **What the result directs further inquiry toward, and its limits** (O2, O5).

## 9. Named moves

- **Positioning the question.** Stating what is asked about, what is examined and what is to be established before any answer (Q1).
- **Formal indication.** An opening characterisation that fixes a direction of looking and may not be used as a premiss (Q5).
- **Stating the presuppositions.** Naming what the thing is taken as, the respect of looking, and the concepts chosen (S4).
- **Precautionary rejection.** Setting aside the approaches that currently seem obvious, before the analysis (Q6).
- **Double description.** The received description of an example followed by a redescription of the same example (D1).
- **Leading back.** Turning from the thing described to its way of being (D3).
- **Founded mode.** Showing a familiar or theoretical form as resting on a more basic one (D5).
- **Evidence from deficiency.** Using absence, lack or breakdown to show that a structure holds (D5).
- **Disclosing case.** A particular experience in which the whole structure shows itself (D7).
- **Attestation.** Pre-theoretical testimony that the structure has been understood before (D7).
- **Repetition.** Going through earlier results again from a deeper basis (D8).
- **Restoring the right.** Stating what the ordinary concept is correct about once its derivation is shown (D9).
- **Dismantling.** Tracing an inherited concept back to the experience it was drawn from (H1).
- **Immanent critique.** Asking of an author's determinations whether they are drawn from the matter (H5).
- **Reading toward the unsaid.** Going beyond an author's wording under a declared guiding idea (H6).
- **Necessary omission.** Showing why an author's starting point made a neglect unavoidable (H7).
- **Retrieval.** Restating an author's problem so that its closed possibilities open (H8).
- **Ordering the senses.** Showing which meaning of a term founds the others (T2).
- **The true by way of the correct.** Granting a definition and asking what it presupposes (L1).

## 11. Checklists

### Drafting

- [ ] The question is stated in its three parts.
- [ ] The present handling of the concept is described, and the kind of concealment is named.
- [ ] The term is analysed, its senses are ordered, and no claim rests on etymology alone.
- [ ] A formal indication is given and marked as such.
- [ ] The three presuppositions of the interpretation are stated.
- [ ] The misdirections set aside are named.
- [ ] One everyday example is described twice: the received way, then from dealings.
- [ ] The way of being is stated in concepts that fit it; any coined term is announced.
- [ ] Derived and deficient forms are shown as modes of the basic phenomenon.
- [ ] Each historical station is treated in three steps, with the reason for the omission.
- [ ] Where a reading goes beyond the wording of a text, the guiding idea is stated.
- [ ] The ordinary concept is given its limited right.
- [ ] The objection to the procedure is raised and answered.
- [ ] The article ends on what is opened for inquiry and on its limits.

### Review

- [ ] Does the article claim a neutral or presuppositionless start? (S4, S5)
- [ ] Is the opening characterisation used as a premiss for deductions? (Q5, S6)
- [ ] Does the analysis start from a theory or an exceptional case? (Q4)
- [ ] Is the first appearance of the thing, or the received description, taken as the phenomenon? (S2, D1)
- [ ] Is the thing explained by another thing, or by more facts about it? (D3)
- [ ] Are concepts made for things applied to human existence without comment? (D4)
- [ ] Is the phenomenon assembled from parts? (D6)
- [ ] Is an etymology offered as proof? (T1)
- [ ] Is the history a survey, or a list of influences? (H3)
- [ ] Is an author criticised by an outside standard, or for an omission that is not explained? (H5, H7)
- [ ] Does a reading go beyond the text with no stated guide? (H6)
- [ ] Is the traditional concept simply discarded? (H1, D9)
- [ ] Are results stated as detachable theses? (T5, O5)

## 12. Cautions

**The licence in H6 is narrow.** "Violence" is permitted only under a declared guiding idea, with the text as the test. An article must mark where it leaves the wording of a text. **Inference:** a reviewer should treat an unmarked departure as an error, whatever its merit.

**Etymology and coinage are his idiom and are easily overdone.** He warns against word-mysticism himself (T1). The awkwardness he accepts is tied to a specific task [BT §7, l. 513]. No rule here asks an article to write in that manner.

**His reduction differs from Husserl's.** He takes over the word “in its literal wording though not in its substantive intent” [BP §5, l. 631]. An article using D3 should not describe it as bracketing or as a return to consciousness.

**The rules belong to 1921–1929.** They were found in texts of that period. Section 7 rests on one later essay. The later writings may proceed differently, and this guide does not describe them.

**Do not imitate the surface.** Hyphenated compounds, scare quotes and italics in the quotations are partly the translators'. One translation renders the central terms in its own way (see the note on vocabulary).

## 13. Limits

- The folder holds about eighty files. This guide rests on the methodological passages listed in the key, found by searching for known statements of method and then read in consecutive ranges. No work was read whole.
- Excluded as not by Heidegger alone or not philosophical exposition: the guidebook to *Being and Time* by another author, the two volumes of correspondence, and an edited volume on *Holzwege*. Duplicates and the French and German versions of works available in English were not used.
- Not consulted: GA 17, 18, 19, 21, 22, 25, 26, 27 among the lecture courses of the 1920s; GA 59 and 60, which contain the earliest treatment of formal indication; everything after 1929 except the opening of one essay, including *Introduction to Metaphysics*, *Contributions to Philosophy*, *The Principle of Reason*, *What Is Called Thinking?*, the Nietzsche and Hegel volumes and the *Ponderings*.
- A later pass should read first GA 60 §§11–13 and GA 59 on formal indication (Q5 rests on four short passages), GA 25 and KPM as a whole for the practice of interpretation (H6 rests on two passages), and GA 19 and GA 18 for the practice of reading Greek texts (T1 rests on BT §7 alone).
- Rules marked *practised* rest on sampled places and on section headings. They were not tested across the corpus by count.
- Every text was read in translation. Claims about wording are claims about the translations. Translators were checked in the files for all texts except QCT.
- The sequence in section 8 is my assembly.

## 14. Constraints on application

Write the article in English. Translate every quotation. Keep the German or Greek only for a term of art, or where the argument turns on wording that the English does not carry (a contested rendering, an etymology, a difference between editions); the test is whether a reader with no German or Greek would lose anything if the original were deleted. Where the original is kept, it follows the English in parentheses. Rule T1 is subject to this constraint: an etymology is given only where the argument uses it.

# A style guide for scholarly articles in phenomenology, drawn from Rudolf Bernet's writing

This guide tells a drafter how to build and write a scholarly article in phenomenology, and tells a reviewer what to check. Every rule is derived from a set of Rudolf Bernet's own texts and carries its evidence beside it. The guide concerns style and argumentative method. It does not summarise his positions; content appears only where it explains a move.

Use it in this order: read the twelve-rule summary, draft with the drafting checklist, and consult the full entries when a rule needs its reason or its limits. A reviewer runs the review checklist against the draft.

## Summary: the twelve rules that matter most

1. **Start from what readers already believe, then turn it.** Open with a received view, a standing question or the state of the sources, and make the article's problem out of what that view misses (A1).
2. **State the thesis early as something you will show.** Say it in the first person and in one sentence. Do not write "I argue" (A2, X1).
3. **Declare your texts and your omissions.** Name the texts the article rests on and what it leaves out, and give the reason (A3).
4. **Reconstruct before you criticise.** Present each author in that author's own terms and at full strength. Hold your objection until the reconstruction is done (A10, V3).
5. **Stage a confrontation around one shared question.** Give the parallels first, then the differences, then the root of the differences. Explain each author in words the other could accept (A7, A8, A9).
6. **Name each step.** Mark the logical relation of each sentence to the last with a connective of consequence, contrast or reformulation (P3, M12).
7. **Ask the reader's question yourself and answer it at once.** Raise the natural objection as a direct question, grant what is right in it, and state what does not follow (M4, M5).
8. **Separate the text from your reading of it.** Say when a consequence is yours and the author did not draw it (M10, W5).
9. **Show a tension and leave it standing when the text leaves it standing.** Locate both answers in the text and say which one the author ends with (M9).
10. **Fix every technical term at first use.** Attach it to the author who uses it, give the original once in parentheses, and state your own usage where it departs from the author's (T1, T2, T3, T4).
11. **Quote terms and phrases; quote passages rarely.** Introduce a passage, then say what it shows (W2, W3).
12. **End on the sharpened result and its limit.** Do not summarise the sections. Close with the thesis in its strongest form, what stays open, or what the criticised author still got right (A12, A13).

## How to read an entry

Each entry has an **ID**, a **Rule** (what to do), a **Why** (what it does for the argument), **Evidence** (what the texts do), a **Scope**, and sometimes **Apply**. A sentence that goes beyond what the texts show is marked **Inference**. Scope words mean: *pervasive*, found in almost every text examined; *common*, found in many texts across more than one language or period; *occasional*, found in a few; *one text*, found in one.

Quotations are copied from the files and were checked against them by script. A citation gives the text's short name, the section where the text has sections, and the line number of the file, for example [HHI §1, l. 14]. Where the file is not in English, the English is mine and is marked *(my translation)*; the original follows in parentheses only where the point depends on the wording. Inside quotations, double quotation marks of the source are printed as single ones.

A number is always a count of my own, made by a script or by reading, over a stated set of files. "The 40 article files" means the set marked ● in the key below. These counts measure the files as they stand, with their scanning errors, and are indications of scale.

## 1. Profile

**The corpus.** The folder holds 65 files. The rules rest on 43 article-length works, held in 48 files because several exist in two languages, and on the front matter of three books, all by Bernet alone. They run from 1978 to 2020. They are in German, French, English and Dutch. Most of the English texts are translations from his French or German by more than ten different hands; six English texts name no translator. The subjects are Husserl on time, perception, imagination, truth, logic and idealism; Husserl with Heidegger; Derrida; Levinas; Gadamer; Sartre; Bergson; Schopenhauer; Freud and the drive; and two historical pieces on the Husserl Archives.

**What was set aside.** Six co-authored or edited volumes were excluded entirely, including the chapters by Bernet in them. Two stub files and the curriculum vitae were excluded from evidence; the curriculum vitae was used only for dates. Four files are exact or near-exact second copies and were not counted. Where a text exists in two languages, one copy was counted for each measurement and both may be quoted, with the translator named.

**The stable core.** Across languages, periods and subjects the same things recur. The article is a reading of named texts. It begins from a received view and makes its problem from what that view misses. It reconstructs an author patiently before judging. It sets two authors or two readings against each other around a shared question. It marks every logical step. It distinguishes what the text says from what the reader adds. It ends on a sharpened result and a stated limit.

**What varies.** Texts that expound Husserl contain few questions and little first-person judgement. Texts that confront one author with another, reviews, and the texts on psychoanalysis contain many questions and more open judgement (P5, G1–G5). The German texts use a denser academic notation than the French and English ones (C2). The late English texts are plainer and shorter in the sentence than the early German ones, but the same moves are present in both. Book introductions and the pieces on Derrida allow extended figures of speech that the articles do not (C3).

**Inference.** The moves are his; the surface of the translated English is partly the translators'. Rules about architecture, moves and stance draw on all languages. Rules about sentence length, connectives and punctuation draw only on texts in the language of the file with no translator stated, and each such claim says which.

## 2. Architecture of an article

### A1. Open from a received view, a standing question or the state of the sources

**Rule.** Begin with what the field already holds about the topic, a question that is commonly asked, or the condition of the sources. Then show in the same paragraph what this leaves unexplained.

**Why.** The reader starts on known ground and sees why the article is needed. The problem is given by the field, so it does not look invented.

**Evidence.** HHI opens with a question in quotation marks that it says “is asked frequently” [HHI l. 8], and then reports that new source publications have changed the situation. TIR opens: “It is also well known that this standpoint immediately incited dispute as well as astonishment” [TIR l. 7]. TP15 opens: “Husserl’s characterization of phenomenology as a transcendental philosophy has been criticized and rejected from the very beginning.” [TP15 l. 7] LCH opens: “It seems to be generally accepted that” [LCH l. 7], and four sentences later: “We are then confronted with the paradox whereby” [LCH l. 17]. VBL opens by saying that a text by Husserl has “found astonishingly little attention in the secondary literature” *(my translation)* [VBL l. 32] and gives three reasons for the neglect before setting them aside. BIB opens with the long-standing orientation of Husserl scholarship toward perception [BIB l. 22].

Count: of the 43 article-length works whose openings I read, I classed 16 as opening from a received view, received question or state of scholarship (by reading; one copy per work). Formulas of the type "it is well known", "generally accepted", "as one knows" and their German and French equivalents occur 41 times in 25 of the 40 article files (script).

**Scope.** Common. All four languages, 1978 to 2020, research articles and companion chapters.

**Apply.** Write the received view in one or two sentences, without a list of names. The turn should come before the end of the first paragraph.

### A2. State the thesis early, in the first person, as something to be shown

**Rule.** Within the opening, say in one sentence what the article will show. Use "I" or "we" with a verb of showing or attempting.

**Why.** The reader can test every later step against the announced result. A verb of showing commits the writer to evidence from the texts.

**Evidence.** “In what follows, I wish to show how a text almost contemporaneous with *Ideas I* develops a version of phenomenological idealism that is not only more precise but that is also less problematic.” [TIR l. 29] “In what follows I would like to show that,” [ISO l. 16]. “I will attempt to show this by referring especially to the course from the summer-semester of 1925” [HHI l. 8]. In German: “In what follows I will restrict myself to the consideration of” *(my translation)* [BIB l. 45]. In French, in a critical study: “I would like to devote the main part of my critical study” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 5]. Abstracts do the same in the third person: “The author claims that the relation between philosophical questions and questions belonging to natural life involves both continuity and a radical rupture.” [PR l. 41]

Count: a plan or aim marker of this kind occurs within roughly the first 7,500 characters of 21 of the 40 article files (script; a lower bound, since wording varies).

**Scope.** Common. All languages and periods. Absent from the historical narrative VSM and from the review REV, which opens with a verdict instead (G3).

**Apply.** One sentence. Put the claim in a "that" or "how" clause after the verb. See X1 for the verbs he does not use.

### A3. Declare the textual basis and the omissions, with reasons

**Rule.** Say which texts the article rests on, which it leaves aside, and why. If an omission may surprise, say so and justify it.

**Why.** A reading can be checked only if its base is known. Stated omissions cannot be held against the article as oversights.

**Evidence.** GZ: “The present text aims neither to contribute to research on the historical relations … nor to draw on unpublished Husserlian sources” *(my translation)* [GZ l. 30], followed by the list of texts that alone will serve as basis [GZ l. 32]. ISO: “This objective will allow me to dispense with the necessity of dwelling in detail on” [ISO l. 50]. DRV sets out three limits in a row, beginning: “We shall return to all these questions, although we shall be forced to limit ourselves to approaching them from a very specific and partial perspective.” [DRV l. 64] It then meets the objection to its choice of authors: “These might seem like somewhat arbitrary choices” [DRV l. 86]. BIB: “It may seem strange that I also forgo any consideration of the problem of truth.” *(my translation)* [BIB l. 57] VBL: “Nevertheless, in the present contribution I will restrict myself exclusively to the treatment of the *Prolegomena*. This decision needs an explanation” *(my translation)* [VBL l. 56]. SEC justifies a new reading by a new source: “Rather than summarizing and commenting on these comments, my objective is to reread Poe’s text anew in the light of a text by Heidegger which was not available to” [SEC l. 30].

Count: formulas of self-limitation ("cannot deal with", "this is not the place", "limit myself", and German and French equivalents) occur 44 times in 23 of the 40 article files (script).

**Scope.** Common. All languages and periods.

**Apply.** Two to five sentences after the thesis. Name editions or manuscript groups, not just titles, where more than one version exists (W6).

### A4. Set out the guiding questions as questions when the topic is open

**Rule.** Where the article explores a problem and does not defend a single reading of one text, list its guiding questions as direct questions at the end of the opening.

**Why.** A set of questions gives the reader the article's agenda and lets later sections be read as answers.

**Evidence.** GAD ends its opening with eight questions in a row, beginning: “Is that to say that the subject according to Gadamer is little else than this Dasein” [GAD l. 94]. DRV opens with a statement about Freud and then some fourteen questions, the first being: “Is that to say that our psyche is entirely subordinated to the pursuit of pleasure?” [DRV l. 14] PR begins its first numbered section with five questions [PR l. 73]. The book FDD takes its questions from an epigraph: “These questions, raised by Antoine Thibault, will guide the reflections in this work.” [FDD l. 76]

**Scope.** Occasional. Found in texts on Gadamer, Freud and the drive, and on method, after 2000. Texts that expound Husserl do not do this: questions are 0.4% of the sentences in MAP and 1.0% in BER (P5).

**Apply.** Three to five questions. Each must be taken up again by name or by order later in the article (M3, M8). See C5 on length.

### A5. Order the sections by the matter: exposition first, freer interpretation after

**Rule.** Put the plain exposition of each author or text first and your own more personal interpretation after it. For two authors use three parts: the first author, the second author, the confrontation. For one body of texts use: how the texts came about, what they contain, what is new in them.

**Why.** The reader can judge the interpretation because the material has been laid out independently of it.

**Evidence.** The foreword of LVS states the principle for its chapters: the texts are arranged “so as to give first a relatively technical exposition of the main lines of Husserl’s conception” before “a freer and more personal interpretation” *(my translation)* [LVS foreword, l. 166]. GZ has three untitled parts numbered I, II, III: Husserl, Heidegger, then the comparison, which begins by matching Heidegger's notion to Husserl's with the words “corresponds exactly” *(my translation)* [GZ III, l. 593]. BER has three parts: the history of the manuscripts, their content set against the earlier texts, and what is new; the second begins: “If, following this short sketch of the history of the genesis of the Bernau time-manuscripts, we now turn to their systematic content” [BER II, l. 168]. HHI follows the order of the lecture course it reads, and marks the change of text at the end of its first section (A6).

**Scope.** Common in the texts read whole (GZ, BER, HHI, GAD, MAP); stated as a principle in one text (LVS).

### A6. Head sections with numbers and noun phrases; do not head them "Introduction" or "Conclusion"

**Rule.** Number the sections. Give each either no title or a noun phrase that names its subject matter. Let the opening and the close stand without generic headings.

**Why.** A title that names the matter tells the reader what the section is about. A generic heading tells the reader only where they are.

**Evidence.** HHI: “The intentionality of sensible and categorial acts”, “The being of intentional consciousness” [HHI l. 10, l. 232]. MAP has eight numbered sections, among them “Pure Phantasy and Memory” [MAP l. 260] and “Eidetic Sciences and Sciences of Facts” [MAP l. 636], and no section called conclusion. GAD has three parts with roman numerals and run-in titles such as “The Art Lover and the Football Player.” [GAD I, l. 126] GZ and BER use bare roman numerals.

Count: of the 39 article files that can be searched by line, 4 have a heading "Conclusion" (TIR, SAR, UNC, and ISO, where the scan spells it "Conlusion") and 3 have a heading "Introduction" (PL, PR, UNC) (script, checked by reading).

**Scope.** Pervasive for numbered sections with noun-phrase or no titles. Generic headings are occasional and appear from 1994 on, in journals that may have required them. **Inference:** the generic headings follow venue; nothing in the files settles this.

### A7. Stage a confrontation around one question that both authors ask

**Rule.** When two authors are compared, name at the start the single question under which they are compared, and derive the structure of the comparison from that question.

**Why.** Two bodies of thought can be compared on indefinitely many points. One question gives the comparison a measure and keeps it from becoming a list.

**Evidence.** GZ compares the two authors “from the point of view of the question they share, that of the phenomenological origin of time” *(my translation)* [GZ l. 36], and says that the logic of this question leads both to distinguish levels of time-experience. HHI turns the common question “What has become of intentionality in Heidegger's phenomenology?” into a pair of alternatives: “Is there really continuity between Husserl and Heidegger in the sense of a more profound investigation into the conditions of possibility of intentionality? Or is there not rather a rupture” [HHI l. 16]. It comes back to exactly this pair at the head of its last section (A11). LOH narrows a debate to its real ground: “The heart of the debate between Levinas and Heidegger thus concerns the world less than the relation between being and appearing.” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 109]

**Scope.** Common. German, French and English, 1987 to 2005, in every text read that sets two authors together.

**Apply.** Put the shared question in the opening as a question or as an "either … or". Use its terms in the section titles or first sentences.

### A8. Give the parallels first, then the differences, then the root of the differences

**Rule.** In the comparison itself, state the agreements precisely before the disagreements. Then say where the disagreements come from.

**Why.** Agreement stated first shows that the two authors are talking about the same thing, so the later difference is a real difference. Naming its root turns a list of differences into an explanation.

**Evidence.** GZ III begins with exact correspondences and only then turns: “Naturally there are also quite weighty differences between Husserl's and Heidegger's analyses of time.” *(my translation)* [GZ III, l. 634] After two differences it names their source: “Here lies the real reason for” *(my translation)* [GZ III, l. 667] the two authors' different assessments. HHI's last section sets out what Heidegger credits to Husserl and then what he criticises with “On the one hand” and “On the other hand” [HHI §3, l. 289], and traces the difference to one distinction. LOH lists what cannot make the difference between the two authors, in a sentence built on "neither … nor" [LOH l. 177], before stating what does.

**Scope.** Common in the confrontations read whole (GZ, HHI, LOH); the same order appears inside GAD when it compares the player and the cultured person (M13).

### A9. Explain each author in terms the other could have understood

**Rule.** Do not set two vocabularies side by side. Restate each position in plain terms, or in the other author's terms, until the two can be compared directly.

**Why.** Positions stated in incompatible vocabularies cannot be shown to agree or to disagree.

**Evidence.** After a first run of analogies, GZ judges its own procedure: “with such an external juxtaposition little is gained either philosophically or historically” *(my translation)* [GZ II, l. 362]. It then states the requirement: only an elucidation of Heidegger's concepts “in a language that Husserl too could have understood immediately creates the conditions for a philosophical dialogue” *(my translation)* [GZ II, l. 365]. The same text later describes Husserl's retention with a word of Heidegger's and marks the borrowing with the phrase “to speak with Heidegger” *(my translation)* [GZ III, l. 623]. HHI restates a scholastic formula of Heidegger's and then begins the next sentence: “Expressed in more ‘Heideggerian’ language, this means that” [HHI §3, l. 299].

**Scope.** Stated as a principle in one text (GZ); practised in the confrontations read whole.

**Apply.** For each technical term of author A that the comparison needs, write one sentence that author B's reader would accept as a description of the same thing. Mark any borrowed word as borrowed.

### A10. Hold your criticism until the reconstruction is complete

**Rule.** Give the author's position its full development, including the best case for it, before the first objection of your own. Place the main objection late and keep it to one or a few points.

**Why.** An objection carries weight only against a position the reader has seen at full strength. One well-placed objection reads as a result of the analysis.

**Evidence.** GAD develops Gadamer's account for three parts. The author's own question arrives four pages from the end: “I do not aspire to treat all these questions, nor do I wish to suggest that Gadamer leaves them unanswered. I shall content myself with just one question, to which Gadamer seems not to have paid enough attention.” [GAD III, l. 1116] LOH gives “a brief general presentation” of the book before what it calls a “personal dialogue” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 7]. REV, a severe review, still interrupts itself: “book is, however, not without merits” [REV l. 41], and limits its own critique: “I will limit my (indicative) demonstration of this point and refer only to three important questions raised by Derrida” [REV l. 263]. SES opens its account of a book with: “One must pay tribute to Antoine Vergote's book” *(my translation)* [SES l. 27].

**Scope.** Common. Research articles and critical studies in French, German and English.

**Apply.** In a draft, find the first sentence in which you disagree with the author under study. If it comes before the reader has the author's whole argument, move it.

### A11. Open each section by tying it to what came before, and close it on what remains

**Rule.** Begin a section by saying what has been reached and what has not. End a section with a sentence that names what the next section must supply.

**Why.** The article reads as one argument. The reader always knows which question is being answered.

**Evidence.** Section openings: “What is at stake in this new confrontation with Husserl is the question of” [HHI §2, l. 234]. “We have as of yet not answered the question as to whether there is continuity or discontinuity” [HHI §3, l. 287]. “Hence, we find ourselves confronted with the same hermeneutic circle observed earlier with respect to the work of art.” [GAD II, l. 763] BER III opens by correcting an impression its own second part may have left: “Our exposition … must not give the impression, however, that these texts would be little more than a mere supplement” [BER III, l. 622].

Section endings: “This change in the assessment of the work of Husserl goes together with a change of scenery: Heidegger moves from a reading of the *Logical Investigations* to the *Ideas*.” [HHI §1, l. 218] “This operation, at once paradoxical and mysterious, becomes fully comprehensible only with the help of the Husserlian theory of inner consciousness.” [MAP §3, l. 320] The next section of MAP is titled with that theory. GZ I ends a stage by stating the result and then showing that the question has only moved: “It has been shown how” … “In reality the question of origin has thereby merely been shifted by one level” *(my translation)* [GZ I, l. 173, l. 177].

**Scope.** Pervasive in the eight texts read whole.

**Apply.** Draft the last sentence of each section and the first sentence of the next together. The pair should make sense with everything between them removed.

### A12. Close without a summary

**Rule.** Do not restate the sections. End in one of three ways: (a) the thesis in its sharpest wording; (b) a limit, an open question or a task; (c) what the author you criticised still achieved.

**Why.** A reader who has followed the argument does not need it repeated. The last paragraph is the place for the result and for an honest statement of where it stops.

**Evidence.** (a) MAP's last sentence gives the result in a new and tighter phrase, ending “as ideal or, better, really irreal possibilities” [MAP §8, l. 680]. VBL ends by naming “the real core of Husserl's refutation of psychologism” *(my translation)* [VBL l. 964]. (b) UNC ends on “the still incomplete fulfilment of this task” [UNC l. 1078]. BDF ends: “It seems that just like Schopenhauer’s metaphysics, Bergson’s new philosophy of life cannot give us a satisfactory response to this question.” [BDF l. 1556] PDP ends on what “a phenomenology to come” *(my translation)* [PDP l. 1161] will have to investigate. GZ ends on a concept of Heidegger's for which, it says, “it seems hopeless” to find a Husserlian equivalent *(my translation)* [GZ III, l. 706]. LIM ends by saying where the debate goes beyond the article: “But on this new ground, the debate between Deleuze and Heidegger stretches far beyond the questions concerning” [LIM l. 1027]. (c) HHI, after three sections of Heidegger's criticism of Husserl, ends: “It is without doubt in his phenomenology of time and temporalization that Husserl has made his most original contribution” [HHI §3, l. 343]. LUP ends by saying that Husserl's late work had already prepared the way for the task the article sets [LUP l. 2641].

Count: a phrase of concluding ("in conclusion", "to conclude", "by way of conclusion", "we are able to conclude", and equivalents) occurs in 16 of the 40 article files (script), so the close is often marked in a phrase. It is headed as a section in 4 (A6).

**Scope.** Pervasive. No text among those whose close I read ends with a section-by-section recapitulation.

**Apply.** Write the last paragraph so that it could not have been written before the article was. If it could, it is a summary.

### A13. Return at the close to the opening's terms, and answer the title's question even if the answer is no

**Rule.** Let the last paragraph pick up a phrase, question or image from the opening. If the title or opening asked a question, say plainly what answer the article reached, including a negative one.

**Why.** The return shows that the announced question has been dealt with. A plain negative keeps the article honest.

**Evidence.** DTK speaks in its opening of an “original interweaving between the thing, consciousness, and” [DTK l. 49] language; its final sentence reads: “All of the Logical Investigations are but a unique and tireless effort to think about the original interweaving between the thing, consciousness, and language” [DTK close, l. 652]. LOH begins and ends with the same pair of motifs, fidelity in infidelity and a saying that unsays itself [LOH l. 22, l. 372]. DRV ends on the word of its subtitle [DRV l. 1339]. ISO, whose title asks whether there is an intentionality without subject or object, concludes: “I have to concede that my enquiry has not corroborated the hypothesis of an ‘intentionality without a subject’.” [ISO close, l. 992] It then states the half that held: “But I have succeeded in demonstrating the existence of an ‘intentionality without an object’” [ISO close, l. 995]. ITP says of its own procedure: “Fruitful as it may have been,” [ITP close, l. 1258] it did not yield a wholly new understanding of time, and adds: “Yet we had not counted on such a result either,” [ITP close, l. 1261].

**Scope.** Occasional for the verbal return (DTK and its French original DCI, LOH, DRV, ISO). A reported negative result appears in two texts (ISO with its French original at LVS l. 12205, and ITP).

## 3. Argumentative moves

Each move has a name so that a drafter can plan with it and a reviewer can ask for it. A one-line list of the names follows the entries.

### M1. Numbered distinction

**Rule.** When a term or a claim has more than one sense, separate the senses with numbers or with "first … second", name each in a phrase, and say where the distinction comes from.

**Why.** Later sentences can then refer to one sense by its number or name, and the reader cannot slide between them.

**Evidence.** “Heidegger distinguishes two basic meanings of the term ‘intentum’: (1)” [HHI §1, l. 21]; the first meaning is then divided again into (a), (b), (c) [HHI §1, l. 36], and the origin is given: “This distinction goes back to Husserl” [HHI §1, l. 23]. LCH gives two reasons numbered (1) and (2) [LCH l. 53]. MAP builds its first section on “the first transformation” and “The second transformation” [MAP §1, l. 113, l. 125]. TIR's conclusion rests on a pair: “In line with Husserl, it is useful to make a distinction between idealism in its broad and in its strict senses.” [TIR §4, l. 499]

**Scope.** Pervasive. All languages and periods.

**Apply.** Two or three members. If you reach four, check whether two belong together. HHI's list of four theses [HHI §2, l. 259] is the longest I met.

### M2. Neither–nor–but

**Rule.** Place a position by saying which two named alternatives it rejects and what it holds instead.

**Why.** A position is easier to grasp between two it is not. Naming the alternatives also records whose views they are.

**Evidence.** Husserl understood retention “neither as an act (in the mode of phantasy) of representation (Brentano), nor as a categorial act of a subsequent synthesis (Meinong), but instead as a modality of the perception itself” [BER II, l. 199]. Pure phantasy involves “neither the modification of a present actual perception (as is the case for the perceptual phantasy), nor the reproduction of a past actual perception (as is the case with remembering)” [MAP §3, l. 265]. In German the same frame closes VBL: “Not the disregard of every relation of logic to a subject … but the working out of this many-layered nature” *(my translation)* [VBL l. 964].

**Scope.** Common. German, French and English; early and late.

### M3. Parked question

**Rule.** When a question arises that cannot be answered yet, state it, say that you will come back to it, and come back to it in so many words.

**Why.** The reader sees that the difficulty has been noticed and is not being avoided, and the main line is not interrupted.

**Evidence.** HHI raises a question about Heidegger's procedure and continues: “Before returning to this question, let us consider” [HHI §1, l. 36]. Forty lines later: “Thus we return to our earlier question as to why Heidegger directs so much of his attention” [HHI §1, l. 77]. The same text parks two further points: “We will return later to this Aristotelization of Husserl” [HHI §1, l. 123]. TIR: “But let us return to the question of the status of the actual reality of a *singular* thing. We have already said that” [TIR l. 457]. The German reply TEL does it with an exclamation: “But back to Husserl!” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 293]

Count: formulas of return occur 15 times in 12 of the 40 article files; back-references of the type "we have seen", "we have already said" occur 68 times in 27 (script).

**Scope.** Common.

**Apply.** Every "we will return to this" in a draft must have a matching return. A reviewer should search for each and check.

### M4. Self-objection

**Rule.** At the point where a reader would object, put the objection as a direct question beginning "Is this to say that …?" or "Does this mean …?". Answer it immediately, and split the answer: what is right in it, and what does not follow.

**Why.** The objection is met where it arises. The split answer fixes the exact scope of the claim.

**Evidence.** “Is this to say that one comprehension of intentionality is criticized in the name of another comprehension” [HHI §2, l. 263]. The answer: “In a certain way one must answer yes, since” [HHI §2, l. 263], and then: “However, this is not to say that the critique of Husserl by Heidegger is only valid if one adopts the standpoint of fundamental ontology.” [HHI §2, l. 263] In French the same move is shorter and sharper: “Is this to say” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 112] that for Heidegger what does not appear is not? “Certainly not!” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 115] BER uses it inside an exposition: “Does this mean that only empirically real objects have an essential reference to time” [BER III, l. 753]. TP15 asks and answers in two sentences: “What is a phenomenological phenomenon? A first helpful indication can be taken from the observation that” [TP15 §1, l. 23].

**Scope.** Common. All languages; most frequent in confrontations and critical studies.

**Apply.** One question, one answer in the next sentence. Do not leave the question standing as rhetoric.

### M5. Calibrated concession

**Rule.** Grant the true part of the opposing point in a clause beginning "It is true that", "Even if" or "True,", and state your claim in the main clause that follows.

**Why.** The reader sees that the claim survives the strongest thing that can be said against it.

**Evidence.** “Even if it is obvious that” one notion is not the other, “it is also clear that the two are not without some relation.” [HHI §3, l. 297] “True, such a doubling of intentional consciousness also happens in memory” [MAP §4, l. 345], followed by “However, unlike in memory”. “It is true that in the text of the revision of the Sixth Logical Investigation this consequence is not yet formulated *expressis verbis*. However, it necessarily follows from Husserl's insistence on the *hic et nunc* of phenomenological consciousness.” [TIR l. 483] In an exposition, against its own account: “It must be admitted, however, that this new doctrine concerning the consciousness of the originary process does not obtain throughout all the Bernau texts” [BER II, l. 542].

Count: "it is true that" occurs 4 times in 3 of the 6 English files with no translator stated and 22 times in 15 of the 20 translated English files; French *même si* occurs 17 times in 5 of the 8 French files (script).

**Scope.** Pervasive as a move. The exact English wording is partly the translators'.

### M6. Reversal

**Rule.** After presenting how things look, turn with a marked sentence to how they are. Use the turn once per stretch of argument.

**Why.** The first view is one the reader may hold; reversing it in the open shows what the analysis has gained.

**Evidence.** HHI spends a paragraph on Heidegger's apparent allegiance to Husserl and then: “However, we should not be deceived by all of this. Under the guise of saving Husserl, Heidegger in fact uses Husserl for his own ends” [HHI §1, l. 14]. GZ: Husserl's separation of immanent and transcendent time “results in truth less from a requirement of the phenomenological method than from a metaphysical prejudice” *(my translation)* [GZ I, l. 94]. TIR: “Despite all appearances, this conception” [TIR l. 453] does not imply a return to the Neo-Kantians. GAD warns before a reversal: “We must not be misled here by the metaphysical language” [GAD I, l. 331].

Surface note: the German marker is “in reality” (*In Wirklichkeit*, GZ l. 177), 5 occurrences in 4 of the 6 German files. In the French files *loin de* ("far from") occurs 11 times in 5 of 8, and in the English files with no translator stated "far from" occurs 7 times in 3 of 6 (script).

**Scope.** Common.

### M7. Objection turned

**Rule.** When an objection to an author can be shown to follow from the author's own principles, say so, and treat it from then on as part of the author's position.

**Why.** The reading becomes stronger than either the objection or the defence, and the author is credited with the resources of the text.

**Evidence.** GZ raises what it calls a classic objection to Heidegger, that his account of authentic time depends on arbitrary decisions about the meaning of life, adds that one could also call the account bound to its historical moment, and then turns: “What was first meant as an objection to Heidegger proves to be a genuinely Heideggerian thought” *(my translation)* [GZ II, l. 539]. In the reply TEL the author redirects an objection made to him: the objection “is thus directed less at me than at Husserl himself” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 119].

**Scope.** Two texts, both German (1978, 1987/88).

### M8. Ordered replies

**Rule.** When several objections or questions are in play, number or letter them when you raise them and answer them in that order under the same labels.

**Why.** No objection is lost, and the reader can find the reply to each.

**Evidence.** TP15 announces: “there is a threefold limitation to the very idea of intentional correlation” [TP15 §2, l. 69], and replies under letters, beginning: “(A) Husserl’s phenomenology is not defenseless against the first objection.” [TP15 §2, l. 71] MAP sets two questions: “What is then the relation between essences (or the eidetic sciences built on them) and imagination? And what is the relation between essences and possibilities?” [MAP §7, l. 619] It answers with “To answer the first question,” [MAP §7, l. 623] and “Turning to the second question,” [MAP §8, l. 638]. It does the same with two numbered statements: “We must thus say: 1) The belief or affirmation that what one imagines is really possible belongs to an act that goes beyond the content of an act of imagination.” [MAP §6, l. 532] and then “The first statement is in need of more nuance.” [MAP §6, l. 537] The reply TEL gathers its correspondent's questions into four numbered groups before answering: “I take the liberty of bundling your fireworks of questions, for my easier use, as follows” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 68].

**Scope.** Common. 1978 to 2020; German and English.

### M9. Two answers

**Rule.** Where an author gives two incompatible answers, or where the texts do not decide, say so. Set out both answers with their textual support, say which the author moves toward, and do not supply a unity the text lacks.

**Why.** A real hesitation in a text is a finding. Hiding it weakens the reading; showing it tells the reader where further work lies.

**Evidence.** “One receives the impression that the author of *Truth and Method* hesitates between two possible answers.” [GAD I, l. 376] The two answers follow with “On the one hand” and “On the other hand”, and then the direction: “Gadamer substitutes this second conception of the player for the first.” [GAD I, l. 401] BER reports that two of Husserl's insights were re-examined “and admittedly, it seems without conclusive results” [BER II, l. 338]; it sets out two paths Husserl tried and says of the first: “It seems, however, that what remains undecided in the Bernau Manuscripts are the questions of whether and how” [BER II, l. 587]. It then offers a diagnosis marked as a guess: “Perhaps it is simply the case that there can be no account of the origin of time which does not already presuppose time.” [BER II, l. 609] TEL sets out two readings of one doctrine that depend on where one puts the accent, and then chooses: “The latter interpretation … seems to me the more convincing.” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 152] ITP ends on the tension it has traced, without removing it (A13).

**Scope.** Common. German and English, 1978 to 2010; expositions of Husserl as well as critical readings.

**Apply.** Three parts: the hesitation named; both answers, each tied to a passage; what the text does in the end. If you prefer one answer, say that the preference is yours.

### M10. Unthought consequence

**Rule.** When you draw a consequence the author did not draw, or reconstruct premises the author did not state, say that the step is yours.

**Why.** The reader can tell report from interpretation. The interpretation gains credit because it is not passed off as the text.

**Evidence.** After setting out four theses, HHI says: “This deduction of the ontological presuppositions of the idealism in the *Ideas* cannot be found as such in Heidegger's text, but it is without doubt that each of these four theses about being is profusely questioned and criticized by Heidegger.” [HHI §2, l. 261] GZ: “Husserl himself was unable to draw this consequence of principle from the phenomena he had himself uncovered” *(my translation)* [GZ I, l. 268]. TEL: “Husserl himself did not draw this consequence, and for that reason it could well go unmentioned in my article.” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 165] GAD marks an addition in a parenthesis: “(not explicitly touched upon by Gadamer)” [GAD I, l. 544]. TIR marks the step in the concession quoted under M5.

**Scope.** Pervasive in the texts read whole; German, French-derived and English.

**Apply.** Use a short marker: "this is not found as such in the text", "X does not draw this consequence", "not explicitly stated by X". Put it in the sentence that makes the step.

### M11. Symptom reading

**Rule.** Treat an author's choice of words, change of terms, or silence as evidence, and say what it is evidence of.

**Why.** Much of what a text commits to shows in its vocabulary and omissions. Using them extends the textual base without leaving the text.

**Evidence.** “The use of such vague Latin terms by Heidegger is not merely by chance” [HHI §1, l. 18]; the text goes on to say what the terms allow him to cover up. On an omission: “it is striking that even the mere words” [HHI §1, l. 151] for a Husserlian notion are never mentioned by Heidegger. On a change of terms: “This alteration in terminology is already a first indication that it was no longer self-evident for Husserl” [BER II, l. 307]. On a substitution: “It is significant that in the course of his analysis of the work of art” [GAD I, l. 399]. The same test is applied to another reader: GZ notes that Heidegger does not mention retention or the double intentionality of the flow “with a single word” and calls his account “abridged and therefore also prematurely critical” *(my translation)* [GZ II, l. 313].

**Scope.** Common. Texts read whole; German and English.

**Apply.** State the observation as a fact about the text first (which word, where, how often), then the inference.

### M12. Reformulation

**Rule.** After a dense or technical sentence, say the same thing again more plainly, and mark that you are doing so.

**Why.** The second wording checks the first. If the two do not say the same thing, the writer finds out.

**Evidence.** “Stated otherwise,” [MAP §3, l. 317]. “Put differently,” [MAP §4, l. 344]. “In short, the phenomenological investigation of intentionality leads to the discovery of the transcendence of *Dasein*” [HHI §2, l. 245]. “Put succinctly, this doctrine consists in” [BER II, l. 360]. “Reduced to its simplest expression, Husserl's program consists in investigating what type of consciousness corresponds to which mode of being of an object.” [TIR §4, l. 515] German: “In short:” (*Kurz:*, GZ l. 130).

Count: "that is" as a reformulating phrase occurs 10.4 times per 10,000 words in the six English files with no translator stated; French *c'est-à-dire* 8.2 per 10,000 words in the eight French files; German *d.h.* 17.2 per 10,000 words in the six German files (script; "that is" includes some non-reformulating uses).

**Scope.** Pervasive.

### M13. Open self-correction

**Rule.** When a formulation you have used turns out to be too rough, correct it in the text and keep the first version visible.

**Why.** The reader follows the thought becoming more exact and trusts the final wording more.

**Evidence.** “In fact, rather than speaking of ‘shifts’, it might be better to speak of an exercise in walking a tight-rope.” [HHI §1, l. 147] “It is therefore not completely correct to say that the investigation of the ontological signification of intentionality is only partial in Husserl” [HHI §3, l. 295]. “Rather than speaking, as we have done, of a double *Darstellung*, one must thus reckon with an infinite series” [GAD I, l. 344]. After a long contrast built by the article itself: “The rift between the football player and the cultured person who can appreciate a work of art is thus less profound than it might at first have seemed.” [GAD I, l. 505] The next move restores what remains of the contrast: “All the same, a game of football is not a work of art.” [GAD I, l. 513] At the scale of a phrase: “only see (or, better, quasi-perceive) Lear” [MAP §2, l. 196]; “de-factualized or, better, de-realized” [MAP §7, l. 598]; “Better still,” [TIR §4, l. 507].

**Scope.** Common. English texts of 1990 to 2020 with and without a named translator; one German instance (*oder besser:*, DUA l. 40).

### M14. Consequence chain

**Rule.** Draw consequences one at a time, each in its own sentence with its own marker, and say when a result only moves the question to another level.

**Why.** The reader can stop at the exact step they doubt.

**Evidence.** After the four theses of HHI: “From this follows the relative and presumptive existence of transcendent objects” [HHI §2, l. 259]. “It follows that phenomenological idealism—far from leading to a sort of solipsism—not only implies intersubjectivity but an intersubjective community of embodied subjects.” [TIR l. 493] BER walks through a regress step by step, naming the first, third and fourth impression in turn, and then judges it: “This first regressus ad infinitum is comparatively harmless because” [BER II, l. 403]. GZ closes a stage and shows the displacement in the pair of sentences quoted under A11.

Count: in the six English files with no translator stated, "thus" is the most frequent connective counted (19.5 per 10,000 words, in 6 of 6); in the German files *somit* (16.1) and *also* (13.8); in the French files *donc* (40.6, in 8 of 8) (script).

**Scope.** Pervasive.

### M15. Carried example

**Rule.** Give an abstract distinction one concrete, everyday example and keep using that example until the distinction is fully drawn.

**Why.** One example worked through shows the structure. A new example for each point shows only that examples exist.

**Evidence.** HHI explains categorial synthesis with the sentence “My dog is bigger than yours” [HHI §1, l. 131] and analyses its parts for the rest of the paragraph. MAP uses an actor playing King Lear across four paragraphs to separate perceptual phantasy from illusion (from [MAP §2, l. 162]), a centaur for pure phantasy, and then both together: “Nothing justifies thinking that the world in which centaurs wreak havoc and the one in which Snow White sleeps her mortal slumber would form one and the same imaginary world.” [MAP §5, l. 411] GAD takes the football player from its first section title to its final pages and uses the figure to state its result: “It would be more to the point to say that every hermeneutic subject is at bottom a clumsy player.” [GAD III, l. 1155]

**Scope.** Common in the English texts read whole (1990, 2005, 2020). The German texts read whole use fewer examples.

**Apply.** Choose an example with at least two parts that can vary independently, so that it can carry a distinction.

### M16. Relocation

**Rule.** When a dispute is usually placed in one spot, show that its real ground is elsewhere, and say where.

**Why.** Moving the issue is often the main result of a reading. Stating it as a move makes the result visible.

**Evidence.** “The fundamental error of psychologism was therefore not to have related logical idealities to subjective experiences, but to have reduced them to psychological facts.” *(my translation)* [DCI l. 51] LOH relocates a debate in the sentence quoted under A7 and, later, the danger: “It is therefore not in” one place “that the danger … lies in wait, but rather in” another *(my translation)* [LOH l. 273]. GZ corrects a common account with “not simply, as is often thought” *(my translation)* [GZ II, l. 451]. BER corrects a date: the breakthrough to genetic phenomenology “did not first happen, as is generally assumed,” [BER III, l. 673] in the lectures of 1920–21.

**Scope.** Common. All languages.

### Named moves at a glance

- **Numbered distinction (M1):** separate senses by number and name; say whose distinction it is.
- **Neither–nor–but (M2):** place a position between two named alternatives it rejects.
- **Parked question (M3):** state a question, defer it aloud, return to it aloud.
- **Self-objection (M4):** ask the reader's objection as a question; answer yes in part, no in part.
- **Calibrated concession (M5):** "It is true that … However …".
- **Reversal (M6):** how it looks, then how it is.
- **Objection turned (M7):** show that an objection belongs to the author's own thought.
- **Ordered replies (M8):** label objections or questions and answer in the same order.
- **Two answers (M9):** show a hesitation in the text and leave it standing.
- **Unthought consequence (M10):** mark the step the author did not take as yours.
- **Symptom reading (M11):** use word choice, changes of term and silences as evidence.
- **Reformulation (M12):** say it again more plainly, with a marker.
- **Open self-correction (M13):** replace your own rough wording in view of the reader.
- **Consequence chain (M14):** one consequence per sentence; note when the question has only moved.
- **Carried example (M15):** one concrete example kept through the whole distinction.
- **Relocation (M16):** move the dispute to its real ground.

## 4. Paragraph and sentence

Claims in this section about length, connectives and punctuation rest on files in their own language with no translator stated: six English (MAP, AE, PL, LIM, PR, REV), six German (BIB, GZ, DUA, LUP, VBL, TEL) and eight French (DCI, DIV, PDP, VSM, LST, ODT, LOH, SES). That the six English texts were written in English is my inference from the absence of a translator's name. Translated files are used only where stated.

### P1. One step of the argument per paragraph

**Rule.** Give each paragraph one job: set out a position, raise an objection, draw a consequence, or make a transition. Let its first sentence show its link to the previous paragraph and its last sentence state what has been gained.

**Why.** The reader can follow the argument by first and last sentences and can find the paragraph where a step is taken.

**Evidence.** In HHI §2 five successive paragraphs begin: “The main point in Heidegger's discussion of Husserl's *Ideas* is to show” [HHI §2, l. 247]; “Heidegger patiently dissembles the various ontological meanings” [HHI §2, l. 249]; “Before pursuing the path which leads Husserl to the transcendental being of consciousness, let us pause for a moment and consider the consequences” [HHI §2, l. 255]; “In the movement of the text of *Ideas*, the subjectivization of intentionality acquires a new dimension” [HHI §2, l. 259]; “This deduction of the ontological presuppositions” [HHI §2, l. 261]. Each announces a different job. MAP's paragraphs open with markers of the same kind: “Finally, perceptual and pure phantasies also differ” [MAP §2, l. 240]; “Likewise,” [MAP §3, l. 290]; “Yet, since it is nevertheless the same person” [MAP §5, l. 429].

Length, measured only where the file keeps one paragraph per line: TIR, 25 paragraphs, median 170 words, range 48 to 313; TP15, 50 paragraphs, median 171.5, range 91 to 303; ODT (French, no translator stated), 23 paragraphs, median 191, range 42 to 470 (script). TIR and TP15 are translations; paragraph breaks are assumed to follow the originals, which is my inference.

**Scope.** Pervasive for function. The length figures rest on three texts of 1987 to 2015.

**Apply.** Aim for roughly 130 to 250 words. A paragraph over 300 words usually holds two steps.

### P2. Sentences of middle length, with a few short ones at the turns

**Rule.** Write most sentences at about 20 to 35 words. Use a short sentence where the argument turns or pauses. Keep sentences of 50 words or more rare.

**Why.** Middle-length sentences hold a claim with its qualification. A short sentence makes a turn audible.

**Evidence.** Median sentence length in words (script; sentences split at full stops, question and exclamation marks; texts with at least 100 detected sentences): English with no translator stated, MAP 24, PL 24, LIM 27, PR 23, REV 28; German, GZ 24, TEL 23, BIB 25, DUA 27, LUP 27; French, LOH 28, ODT 28, DIV 27, PDP 30, LST 30, SES 35. The narrative piece VSM has a median of 18. Sentences of 50 words or more: 1% to 9% in the five English texts, 4% to 9% in the German, 5% to 15% in the French. Sentences of 12 words or fewer: 4% to 14% in the five English texts. AE was left out because the scan has lost the letters that begin many sentences.

Short sentences at turns: “Not so Derrida.” [REV l. 318] “Obviously, more explanation is needed here.” [MAP §3, l. 271] “This limitation marks a constraint, but also an opening.” [MAP §8, l. 655] “This is no longer true for Merleau-Ponty’s later texts.” [PL §1, l. 57]

**Scope.** Pervasive across the three languages and from 1978 to 2020. **Inference:** the medians differ little between languages, so the habit is the author's and not one language's.

### P3. Mark the relation of each sentence to the last

**Rule.** Begin or thread most sentences with a word that states their logical relation to what precedes: consequence, contrast, concession, reformulation or addition. Prefer the plain ones.

**Why.** The reader does not have to guess whether a sentence continues, limits or opposes the one before.

**Evidence.** Occurrences per 10,000 words (script). English with no translator stated, 46,125 words: thus 19.5; however 15.0; that is 10.4; not only 6.1; despite 3.9; rather than 3.7; indeed 3.5; yet 3.5; instead of 3.5; therefore 2.8; consequently 2.6; on the other hand 2.4. German, 52,816 words: *jedoch* 22.0; *somit* 16.1; *also* 13.8; *nämlich* 12.1; *zwar* 12.1; *vielmehr* 8.5; *allerdings* 4.9. French, 53,953 words: *donc* 40.6; *cependant* 9.1; *c'est-à-dire* 8.2; *en effet* 5.7; *pourtant* 4.3; *au contraire* 3.9.

**Inference.** In each language the two commonest markers are one of consequence and one of contrast. The prose advances by drawing a consequence and then limiting it.

**Scope.** Pervasive. See C1 for connectives that are frequent only in the translations.

**Apply.** Read a draft paragraph aloud without its connectives. Where the relation between two sentences is unclear, a marker is missing. Where three sentences in a row begin with one, cut the weakest.

### P4. Use paired frames to hold two things together

**Rule.** When a sentence must keep two aspects in view, use a paired frame: "not only … but also", "on the one hand … on the other", "far from … it is", "rather than … one must".

**Why.** The frame tells the reader at the start of the sentence that a second member is coming and how it relates to the first.

**Evidence.** “On the one hand, any excessive ‘subjectivization’ of intentionality should be avoided.” [HHI §2, l. 237] The second member follows: “Still, on the other hand, this intentional relation can only be fully understood on the basis of a new comprehension of the subject.” [HHI §2, l. 245] “This anchoring of the imaginary in the real, far from being the consequence of a simple logical or metaphysical requirement, is clearly established by the inner consciousness of the real-ego.” [MAP §5, l. 463] The review opens with two such pairs in five sentences [REV l. 10–19].

Count: "not only" occurs in all 6 English files with no translator stated (28 times), *nicht nur* in all 6 German files (42), *non seulement* in all 8 French files (25); "on the other hand" in all 6 English files (11) (script).

**Scope.** Pervasive.

**Apply.** Make sure the second member arrives. Do not use the frame when the two members are not of equal weight.

### P5. Use questions to move the text, in proportion to the kind of article

**Rule.** Use a direct question to open a paragraph or a section, to raise an objection (M4), or to set the agenda (A4). Answer it at once unless it is part of a stated agenda. Use few questions in an exposition and more in a confrontation or review.

**Why.** A question shows the reader what the next sentences are for. Too many questions leave the reader holding claims the writer has not made.

**Evidence.** Questions that open a unit and are answered at once: “How then does consciousness go about freely and creatively producing the fictive objects that are given in a pure phantasy or quasi-perception?” [MAP §3, l. 262] German: “What is one to understand by Husserl's” immanent time-consciousness? “In the first place an intentional experience” *(my translation)* [GZ I, l. 109, l. 110]. TIR opens its conclusion with one [TIR §4, l. 499] and puts a possible reproach as another: “Regarding this idealism in a strict sense, in what ways could one be tempted to reproach it?” [TIR §4, l. 509]

Share of sentences that are questions (script): expositions of Husserl, MAP 0.4%, UNC 0.4%, TP15 0.7%, BER 1.0%, LUP 1.4%, PDP 1.8%, GZ 1.9%; confrontations and studies of other authors, LOH 5.0%, LST 7.4%, TIR 7.7%, CHR 7.8%, GAD 8.6%; highest, DIV 12.8%, PR 12.9%, SAR 13.3%, REV 16.1%. Paragraphs that end on a question: none in 20 of the 38 files measured, at most 6 in any (script).

**Scope.** Pervasive, with the variation by kind shown.

**Apply.** In an expository article keep questions to a handful. Do not end a paragraph on a question unless the next paragraph answers it.

### P6. Signpost forward and back

**Rule.** Tell the reader briefly what is coming and remind the reader of what has been established, using "we".

**Why.** Long arguments over difficult texts need fixed points.

**Evidence.** Forward: “It will soon appear, however, that the opposition between objects that really exist, and objects that have no actual existence, is insufficient to fully account for” [MAP l. 51] the acts in question. “By way of conclusion, we will attempt to appreciate the importance of this distinction” [HHI §3, l. 287]. Back: “We have seen that Husserl characterizes” [BER II, l. 426]; “We have already remarked that” [GAD I, l. 350]; “as the already quoted letter from Husserl to Ingarden has it” [BER III, l. 731]. Counts are given under M3.

**Scope.** Pervasive.

**Apply.** One clause, not a paragraph. Do not announce the plan of every section at its head.

### P7. Parentheses, dashes and quotation marks each have set jobs

**Rule.** Use parentheses for the original of a term, for references, and for a brief aside or a mark of your own addition. Use paired dashes for a qualification inserted into a sentence. Use quotation marks around an author's term each time the term is being used as that author's.

**Why.** The reader learns the code and can tell reference, aside and borrowed term apart at a glance.

**Evidence.** Parentheses for the original: “imaginative re-presentation (Vergegenwärtigung)” [MAP l. 50]. For a reference inside the sentence: a quotation from Heidegger is followed by “(p. 56; *GA* 20, p. 75)” [HHI §1, l. 129]. For an aside: “(or the real Julius Cesar)” [MAP §2, l. 188]. For the author's own addition: see M10. Dashes: “– without losing view of everything which separates the intuitive givenness of an imaginary object from the perceptive givenness of a real object” [MAP l. 37].

Quotation marks around terms: in 19 of the 22 English files in which the script found double-quoted spans, more than half of the spans are one to three words long (for example 123 of 158 in HHI, 83 of 88 in BER, 45 of 53 in LIM, 27 of 34 in REV). The terms so marked are the authors' own: “what Husserl calls the ‘correlation’ between ‘intentio’ and ‘intentum’, and what Heidegger calls their ‘belonging together’” [HHI §1, l. 18].

Italics for the operative word and for enumerators such as *First*, *Secondly* are visible only in the two files that preserve them (HHI l. 259; TIR l. 461), both translations; no rule is drawn from them.

**Scope.** Parentheses and term-marking are pervasive. Whether double or single marks are used varies by venue.

### P8. Make authors and texts the subjects of active verbs

**Rule.** Write "Husserl distinguishes", "Heidegger avoids", "the text insists". Narrate what the author does in the text, in the present tense for the argument and the past tense for the history of the work.

**Why.** The reader always knows whose claim a sentence reports. A reading written this way stays close to its texts.

**Evidence.** “Heidegger picks up the thread of his polemical discussion” [HHI §1, l. 202]. “Heidegger also systematically avoids any mention of intentional ‘acts’.” [HHI §1, l. 18] For the history of a work BER uses the past tense with Husserl as subject throughout: “Husserl first used the name ‘Primary Memory,’ but soon after he coined the term ‘Retention.’” [BER II, l. 196] For the content of an argument it uses the present: “Husserl does make a significant advance by now paying more attention to protentional consciousness.” [BER II, l. 482]

**Scope.** Pervasive in the English texts read whole. **Inference:** this is a feature of the exposition and survives translation; the German originals carry more of the content in compound nouns (C2).

## 5. Terms of art

### T1. Introduce a term under its owner's name

**Rule.** At first use, attach a technical term to the author who uses it with a verb of naming: "what Husserl calls", "Heidegger's term for this is".

**Why.** In phenomenology the same word means different things in different authors. Ownership stated at first use prevents the reader from importing the wrong sense.

**Evidence.** “what Husserl calls the ‘correlation’ between ‘intentio’ and ‘intentum’, and what Heidegger calls their ‘belonging together’” [HHI §1, l. 18]. “which is distinguished not only from perceptions in real life, but also from what Husserl calls ‘perceptual phantasies.’” [MAP §2, l. 159] “We know that Gadamer's term for the virtue shared by the historian and the man of action” [GAD II, l. 860] is phronesis. Definitions follow at once and by example: “A perceptual phantasy consists in seeing something unreal (which is to say, having no actual existence), for example seeing King Lear on stage.” [MAP §2, l. 160]

**Scope.** Pervasive.

### T2. Give the original once, in parentheses, after the English

**Rule.** At the first use of a term of art, put the original-language word in parentheses directly after the English term. After that use the English term alone, unless the argument turns on the original (T6).

**Why.** The specialist can identify the concept; the general reader is not asked to read a second language.

**Evidence.** “imaginative re-presentation (Vergegenwärtigung)” [MAP l. 50]; “‘crossed-out’ (durchgestrichen) perception” [MAP §2, l. 183]; “‘thinghood’ (*Dinglichkeit*)” [HHI §1, l. 61]; a term of Husserl's given with its original in the handbook chapter [AE l. 34]. The same order holds in the French original DCI, where the German follows the French term in parentheses [DCI l. 1027].

Translator note: two translations put the original in square brackets instead: “representation [Vergegenwärtigung]” [BER II, l. 220] (Mark Green and the author) and ITP l. 44 (Wilson Brown). The bracket is those translators' convention.

**Scope.** Pervasive in the English and French files, 1990 to 2020.

### T3. State your own usage where it differs from the author's

**Rule.** If you use a word in a sense that is not the author's technical sense, say so at first use and say what the author's sense is.

**Why.** A commentary that uses the author's key words loosely misleads exactly the readers who know the author best.

**Evidence.** “In the present essay, we shall reserve the terms ‘reality’ or ‘actual reality’ (*Wirklichkeit*) to denote actually existing *empirical* objects” [TIR l. 31]. The accompanying note adds: “It is worth mentioning that this sense of ‘real’, meaning that which actually exists, is not synonymous with what Husserl himself calls ‘real’.” [TIR note 4, l. 21] TRA fixes a word of its own in the same way: “By this disappearance we do not mean physical death, but” [TRA l. 17] the abolition of subjective identity.

**Scope.** Occasional (TIR 2004; TRA and its French original LST 2000).

**Apply.** One sentence in the text of the form "we shall reserve the term X for …", and a note if the author's own sense needs stating.

### T4. Treat the same word in two authors as two terms

**Rule.** When two authors use the same word, give each sense in its own sentence, with the author as subject, and then state the relation between the two senses.

**Why.** A shared word hides a difference of doctrine. Stating both senses side by side makes the difference the object of the argument.

**Evidence.** On "world-time": “For Husserl ‘world-time’ is synonymous with the ‘objective time’ of causally determined natural reality. Heidegger by contrast understands ‘world-time’ as the unity of the ecstematic horizons of temporality” *(my translation)* [GZ III, l. 685]. On the two senses of the intended object: “In Husserl, the relation between the ‘object intended’ and ‘how the object is intended’ concerns” [HHI §1, l. 84] the correspondence between a thing and its givenness; “In Heidegger, the same relation between the two significations of the ‘intentum’ becomes the object of *ontological* interrogation.” [HHI §1, l. 90]

**Scope.** Common in the confrontations (GZ, HHI); also in GAD for two German words that English renders by one (T6).

### T5. Record changes in an author's terminology and date them

**Rule.** When an author renames something, give both names, say which is earlier, and say what the change shows.

**Why.** Readers meet both names in the sources. The change itself is often evidence of a change in doctrine (M11).

**Evidence.** “Husserl first used the name ‘Primary Memory,’ but soon after he coined the term ‘Retention.’” [BER II, l. 196] Husserl called a certain givenness “primary impression” in the early texts and “primary presentation” in the later Bernau manuscripts [BER II, l. 280–284]. A note in MAP: “This is why Husserl, in the 1920s, will cease using the term ‘neutralization’” [MAP note 3, l. 294].

**Scope.** Occasional; expositions of Husserl (2010, 2020).

### T6. Hold a term fixed, and keep the original only where English would erase a distinction

**Rule.** Use one English rendering for one term throughout. Keep the original word in the running text only where two original terms would fall together in English and the argument depends on their difference. Flag any published translation you alter.

**Why.** Varying the rendering suggests a change of concept. Where English cannot separate two words, the original is the only way to keep the argument.

**Evidence.** GAD turns on two German words that both become "representation": “by characterizing the game as a representation in the sense of *Darstellung*” [GAD I, l. 263], Gadamer distances himself from representation as *Vorstellung*. The text then uses the German words. Where a phrase of Gadamer's matters it gives the German and the published English: “(rendered in the English translation as: ‘a claim to permanence and the permanence of a claim’)” [GAD note 7, l. 445]. It names a German word for its exactness: “which the German tongue denotes by the precise term, *Wechselspiel*” [GAD I, l. 704].

Altered translations are flagged: “(translation modified)” [GAD note 13, l. 623]; “(translation occasionally altered)” [HHI note 1, l. 361]. Count: such flags occur 19 times in 6 of the 40 article files, English and French (script).

**Scope.** Occasional. Keeping the original in the running text for a whole article occurs in one text (GAD).

### T7. Flag your own coinages

**Rule.** When you introduce a label of your own for a position, mark it as yours with "what one could call" or "one can speak of".

**Why.** The reader should not look for the label in the author under discussion.

**Evidence.** “what one could call ‘a subject without qualities.’” [GAD l. 30] “Consequently, one can speak of an ontologization of the game in Gadamer” [GAD I, l. 215]. The first of these labels is later referred back to as “What we have called a minimal conception of subjectivity” [GAD I, l. 474].

**Scope.** One text (GAD), three instances.

## 6. Working with texts

### W1. Tie every claim about an author to a place in a text

**Rule.** For each claim about what an author holds, give the work and the section or page, in the running text where the reference is short.

**Why.** The reading can be checked sentence by sentence.

**Evidence.** HHI gives the paragraph number of the lecture course for each step of its exposition in one sentence: “Heidegger begins with an ‘elucidation’ (*Aufweis*) of the phenomenon (§ 5a)” [HHI §1, l. 12], and so on through § 5c and § 6. GZ cites Husserl by paragraph in parentheses throughout, for example “(§ 36)” [GZ I, l. 211]. MAP cites the collected edition by volume and section or page: “(Hua XXIII, p. 519)” [MAP §2, l. 224].

**Scope.** Pervasive.

### W2. Quote terms and phrases; quote whole passages rarely

**Rule.** Weave the author's terms and short phrases into your own sentences inside quotation marks. Quote a full sentence or passage only when its wording is itself the evidence.

**Why.** Short quotation keeps the author's vocabulary in view while the argument stays the commentator's. A long quotation hands the argument to the source.

**Evidence.** The count under P7: in 19 of 22 English files most quoted spans are one to three words. A sentence built from an author's phrases: Heidegger says the surrounding world is one where one can “recount (*erzähle*) the very particular as well as trivial story of the chair as it is here now and day after day” [HHI §1, l. 57]. Whole passages are few: I found one block quotation in GAD [GAD I, l. 589–597], none in MAP, none in GZ.

The exception is the historical narrative VSM, whose abstract says that the letters are “abundantly quoted” *(my translation)* [VSM l. 19] and whose text consists largely of dated extracts (G5).

**Scope.** Pervasive in research articles in all languages.

### W3. Lead into a quotation and say afterwards what it shows

**Rule.** Introduce a quoted sentence with a clause that says why it is quoted. After it, state in your own words what the reader should take from it.

**Why.** A quotation left alone proves nothing; the reader does not know which of its features matters.

**Evidence.** Lead-ins: “The following passage is quite revealing:” [HHI §1, l. 164]; “Heidegger summarizes this reflection in a memorable formulation:” [HHI §1, l. 129]; “Gadamer writes:” [GAD I, l. 588]; in French, “As proof I take what Sebbah writes on page 36:” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 147]. Follow-ups: after the passage introduced at HHI l. 164 the next sentence states what Heidegger has gained by it: “Having protected himself from both empiricism and Platonism” [HHI §1, l. 169]. After the block quotation in GAD the next sentence turns it into a question: “Is it then legitimate to think that this tragic disclosure of a totality of meaning” [GAD I, l. 598] can also be realised in other arts?

**Scope.** Pervasive wherever a full sentence is quoted.

### W4. Paraphrase in the author's vocabulary and keep attributing

**Rule.** When you report an author at length, use the author's terms and repeat the attribution every few sentences: "for Husserl", "in Heidegger's view", "according to this account".

**Why.** In a long report the reader can lose track of whose view is being stated. Repeated attribution prevents the commentator from seeming to assert what is only reported.

**Evidence.** One paragraph of HHI attributes six times: “The primary concern of Husserl is”, “This explains why Husserl insists”, “In the eyes of Husserl” [HHI §2, l. 249], and in the next paragraph “For Heidegger, this implies” [HHI §2, l. 255]. MAP: “For Husserl, this intuitive presence of an absent object is the result” [MAP §3, l. 277]; “According to Husserl’s theory of (temporal) inner consciousness” [MAP §4, l. 328].

**Scope.** Pervasive.

**Apply.** In a reported paragraph, check that no sentence could be read as your own assertion unless you mean it as one.

### W5. Keep four voices apart: the text, the author's other texts, other readers, and you

**Rule.** Make clear in each sentence whether it states what the text says, what the author says elsewhere, what another reader says, or what you conclude.

**Why.** Disputes in this field are mostly about readings. A reader can accept your report and reject your conclusion only if the two are separable.

**Evidence.** The text and the commentator: M10. The author elsewhere: “Heidegger himself stresses in some contexts the continuity between intentionality and transcendence, … and in other contexts the discontinuity.” [HHI §3, l. 297] Other readers: “Michel Henry is therefore right to stress” [ISO close, l. 1018]. The commentator's own position, named as such: “The Husserlian conception of the divided subject of intentionality which I have defended” [ISO close, l. 1031]. In the reply TEL the author asks, with some irony, how his exposition of Husserl could have been taken “for the expression of my own opinion” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 259].

**Scope.** Pervasive.

### W6. Use the history of the text: versions, dates, manuscripts, letters, marginal notes

**Rule.** Where the composition of a text bears on its meaning, give the facts: when it was written, in what versions, what the author said of it in letters, what the manuscripts show. Cite unpublished material by its archive signature and date.

**Why.** Many phenomenological texts are lecture courses, revisions and posthumous manuscripts. A claim about "the" doctrine has to say which state of the text it rests on.

**Evidence.** BER I is a history of a group of manuscripts built from dates and from two letters quoted at length [BER I, l. 78–85]. HHI dates Heidegger's lecture courses by year of delivery and says so: “Other references to the *Gesamtausgabe* give the volume number followed by the year in which the lecture was delivered.” [HHI note 1, l. 363] BIB cites a manuscript by signature, leaf and conjectural date: “(B III 12, 172a, wohl Ende 1910)” [BIB l. 28], that is, "probably end of 1910". GZ quotes what Husserl wrote in the margin of his own copy of *Being and Time* [GZ note 10, l. 767]. ETA uses a book in Husserl's library as evidence that he read it [ETA I, l. 25]. HHI dates a position: “This view by Heidegger can be traced back at least to 1919-1921.” [HHI note 15, l. 398]

Uncertainty about such facts is stated: “cannot be reconstructed with certainty. It is certain, however, that Husserl was conscious of the new direction” [BER I, l. 73].

**Scope.** Common. German and English, 1979 to 2010; strongest in expositions of Husserl.

**Apply.** Give only the facts that the argument uses. GZ dismisses the history it does not need in one phrase: it “is quickly told” *(my translation)* [GZ l. 7].

### W7. Cite the translation and the original together

**Rule.** For a text read in translation, give the page of the translation and of the original edition. Say once, in the first note, how references will be given.

**Why.** Readers work from different editions. A stated convention keeps later references short.

**Evidence.** “All references with just page number are to this text (translation occasionally altered), followed by a noting of the original German text” [HHI note 1, l. 360]. “Cited with page reference first to the French, then to the English edition.” [REV note 1, l. 456] “Following the initial reference, all volumes from Husserliana are cited as *Hua* with volume and page references.” [TIR note 2, l. 11]

**Scope.** Common in the English files.

### W8. Put the translation in the text and the original in the note

**Rule.** Quote in the language of the article. Where the original wording should be available, put it in a note or after the translation in parentheses.

**Why.** The argument stays readable in one language and the specialist can still check the wording.

**Evidence.** TIR quotes Husserl in English in the text and gives the German in the note: the text has “The being of the actual transcendent reality is an Idea in the Kantian sense.” [TIR l. 481] and note 30 the German [TIR l. 487]. The French original ODT translates a marginal note of Husserl's and adds the German after it in parentheses [ODT note 34, l. 410–414].

**Scope.** Occasional (2004; 1987). Other files leave German or French untranslated in notes; see C4.

### W9. Test other readers against the text they read

**Rule.** When you assess another interpreter, say exactly what in the primary text the interpreter left out or changed, and what follows from the omission.

**Why.** The criticism is then about the text and can be checked there.

**Evidence.** GZ lists what Heidegger's remarks on Husserl pass over and says why this matters: the abridged account hides “the striking similarities” *(my translation)* [GZ II, l. 315] between the two analyses. REV says that a critic's blindness to the philosophical issues “is not only due to his prejudices but also to the narrow textual basis on which he operates” [REV l. 100], and names the two texts he should have used. HHI notes that neither of its two authors addresses a difficulty: “It is rather surprising that neither Husserl nor Heidegger show much concern with the circularity of this distinction.” [HHI §1, l. 114]

**Scope.** Common. German and English, 1987 to 1995.

## 7. Voice and stance

### V1. "We" for the course of the argument, "I" for aims, limits and judgements

**Rule.** Use "we" when leading the reader through the argument. Use "I" when stating what the article will do, what it leaves out, and what you yourself judge.

**Why.** "We" keeps the reader inside the reasoning. "I" marks the points at which the writer takes responsibility.

**Evidence.** HHI uses "I" in its opening for the aim and for a judgement of the sources (“To the best of my knowledge, this text contains the most extensive and profound discussion Heidegger ever devoted to Husserl.” [HHI l. 8]) and "we" from then on (“We will return later”, “let us pause”). GAD uses "we" for three parts and "I" only where it states its own question [GAD III, l. 1116]. FDD mixes them in the same way: “We will begin the history of a metaphysics of forces” and “it is my view that” [FDD introduction, l. 160, l. 171].

Occurrences per 10,000 words, "I" then "we" (script; the counts include the "I" of descriptive examples such as “when I imagine a centaur” [MAP §2, l. 243]): MAP 16.2 and 27.0; PL 10.0 and 21.3; LIM 17.5 and 22.2; PR 5.4 and 28.5; AE 1.9 and 46.5; REV 26.9 and 6.7. German *ich* then *wir*: BIB 23.1 and 54.7; LUP 21.0 and 20.3; DUA 10.5 and 5.9; VBL 7.6 and 7.6; GZ 0 and 5.2; TEL 93.5 and 6.4. French *je* then *nous*: DCI 11.2 and 44.7; PDP 17.7 and 76.1; LST 27.3 and 58.7; ODT 15.3 and 13.9; LOH 27.2 and 16.3; SES 0 and 66.6.

**Scope.** Pervasive. Both persons appear in almost every text. "I" outweighs "we" only in the review, the two critical studies REV and LOH, the reply TEL and one long German article (DUA).

### V2. Commit plainly where the text is clear; hedge exactly where it is not

**Rule.** Say "there can be no doubt" when the text settles a point. Say "seems", "perhaps" or "presumably" when it does not, and attach the hedge to the precise claim that is uncertain.

**Why.** If every sentence is hedged the reader cannot tell which claims are secure. A hedge used rarely carries information.

**Evidence.** Commitment: “Still, there can be no doubt that Heidegger always views the relation” [HHI §3, l. 297]; “It is beyond doubt that this manner of understanding the difference” [HHI §1, l. 123]. Hedge: “It presumably concerns a much more fundamental problem” [BER II, l. 607]; “Heidegger seems completely spellbound by the doctrine of the *Logical Investigations*” [HHI §1, l. 12]; “However, it is perhaps not as certain as Gadamer seems to think that” [GAD III, l. 1262]. A pair of both in consecutive sentences is quoted under W6.

Count: forms of "seem" occur 5.4 times per 10,000 words in the six English files with no translator stated; "perhaps" once in those six files (script).

**Scope.** Pervasive.

### V3. Credit first and by name; criticise second and to the point

**Rule.** Before criticising a reader or an author, say what they got right, in a sentence with their name as subject. Then state the criticism as a specific omission or step, in measured words.

**Why.** Criticism that begins from agreement is read as addressed to the argument and not to the person.

**Evidence.** “Michel Henry is therefore right to stress” one thing; “But without a doubt he has not been” attentive enough to another [ISO close, l. 1018, l. 1020]. “Evans is more convincing when it comes to the issue of Husserl's pretended ‘metaphysics of presence.’ He is certainly right to criticize” [REV l. 174]. “Gadamer is certainly right to think, along with Heidegger, that” [GAD I, l. 646]. “Lacan is not mistaken in thinking that” [SAR close, l. 837]. “Husserl is of course correct in presuming that” [TP15 close, l. 199], followed by “However, once one widens”. Praise of a critic's work even in a hostile review: “These summaries are not only accurate, they are truly excellent” [REV l. 81].

Count: formulas of the type "is right to", "rightly", *a raison*, *à juste titre*, *zu Recht*, *hat recht* occur 26 times in 15 of the 40 article files; formulas of the type "is wrong", "is mistaken", *à tort*, *zu Unrecht* occur 6 times in 5 (script).

**Scope.** Pervasive in every text that discusses another reader.

### V4. Be even-handed: fault both sides where both are at fault

**Rule.** When you defend one author against another, also say where the defended author falls short, and where the critic has a point.

**Why.** A reading that finds one side always right looks like advocacy and is trusted less.

**Evidence.** In a review that defends Derrida against a critic: “But this is of course not to say that in differing from Husserl Derrida is always wrong. Husserl might have been wrong as well” [REV l. 161], and later, against Derrida: “But it is true that one gets the impression that he is moving too quickly” [REV l. 402]. The same review faults the critic's opponents for using the same strategy as he does [REV l. 31–37]. LOH defends Heidegger against some criticisms and then charges the book under review with having “underestimated the depth of the Heideggerian evil” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 187]. The foreword of LVS assesses five successors of Husserl in turn, each with a merit and a fault [LVS foreword, l. 95–114], and declines school polemic altogether: “The time of polemic between different philosophical schools seems to us definitively past.” *(my translation)* [LVS foreword, l. 78]

**Scope.** Common. French, German and English, 1987 to 2002.

### V5. Keep irony rare, dry and aimed at arguments

**Rule.** Do without sarcasm. If you use irony, use it once, in a subordinate clause, about an argument or a habit of the field.

**Why.** Irony costs the trust of readers who side with its target.

**Evidence.** The sharpest instances I met are in the review: “It is difficult to believe that Evans was fully unaware of the rhetorical character of his critique of Derrida's ‘rhetoric.’” [REV l. 236] The same review opens its account of the author with respect for the person: “That a scholar with the exceptional intellectual gifts and the personal honesty of J. Claude Evans felt compelled to write such a book is understandable only within a larger context.” [REV l. 27] It states the principle behind its impatience: “Discussions, where despite a great sophistication in the argumentation the basic philosophical issues are taken for granted on both sides, strike me as particularly unfruitful.” [REV l. 34] In the research articles irony is a parenthesis: every football spectator believes himself an expert [GAD I, l. 564]; “He will be bored out of his skull” [GAD I, l. 558] is said of a hypothetical spectator, not of a colleague.

**Scope.** Occasional; concentrated in the review and one long article.

### V6. Say what you do not know and what the article cannot do

**Rule.** State the limits of your knowledge and of the article in plain first-person sentences, where they apply.

**Why.** A stated limit protects the claims that are made. It also tells later readers where to continue.

**Evidence.** Knowledge: “To the best of my knowledge” [HHI l. 8]; “As far as I know, Heidegger never commented on” [SEC l. 26]; “Knowing little about this, I am no less surprised” [REV l. 31]. Scope: “Indeed, posed in such a general way, this question cannot be answered in a short essay.” [HHI §3, l. 287] “This is not the place to say where I agree and disagree with the many (ad hoc) arguments Evans develops, or to explain what I would have done in his place.” [REV l. 118] Force of a criticism: “Without a doubt, all of these critical questions would have to be deepened, and I do not claim that M. Henry’s book is without resources to face them.” [CHR close, l. 667] Modesty of aim: “This paper has no other ambition than to provide a short historical and systematic overview” [BER abstract, l. 9]. Counts are under A3.

**Scope.** Pervasive.

### V7. Admit an error or a change of view

**Rule.** If an objection to your earlier work is right, say so in one sentence and go on. If your view has changed, do not hide it.

**Why.** The concession costs little and lets the remaining disagreement be stated cleanly.

**Evidence.** “You are right; I should have stressed this explicitly in my text.” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 142] The same reply then states what agreement has been reached and what has not: “On the substance we are thus essentially agreed about the objection formulated in your first point.” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 168] It ends by naming the disagreement that remains: the author cannot follow all his correspondent's consequences [TEL l. 361]. In the foreword of LVS: “I did not want to hide that the writing of these texts was spread over some ten years during which my attitude toward Husserl's thought was not always the same.” *(my translation)* [LVS foreword, l. 158]

**Scope.** Two texts (1978, 1994), German and French.

### V8. Anticipate how the article will be received

**Rule.** If the project is likely to be misread or to annoy a camp, say so in the opening and say what the project is not.

**Why.** Readers who see their objection named are more willing to read on.

**Evidence.** “One could find such an endeavor surprising.” [SAR l. 36] “One should not, however, mistake the intent of this venture!” [SAR l. 54] ODT: “An approach like mine, which tries to bring out deep analogies between the analysis of time in Husserl and in Heidegger, is therefore perilous in many respects.” *(my translation)* [ODT l. 51] It predicts that it will be suspected of infidelity to Husserl and will irritate some readers of Heidegger even more [ODT l. 60]. CHR guards against a different suspicion, that of “establishing a purely arbitrary relation” [CHR l. 62] between two authors. FDD: “A few words of explanation and justification on this twofold choice are needed.” [FDD introduction, l. 100]

**Scope.** Common. French-derived and English texts, 1987 to 2013; texts that bring together authors usually kept apart.

## 8. Apparatus

This section describes what the notes and references do in these texts. It does not prescribe a format.

### N1. Most notes are bare references; a few carry prose

**What the texts do.** By my reading: HHI has 27 notes, of which 5 contain more than a reference; BER has 10, of which 1; MAP has 7, of which 3; LIM has 9, of which 2 contain prose and 1 a quotation; REV has 3, all bibliographic; LOH has 1, bibliographic. A typical note: “See also *BP*, pp. 56 ff; GA 24 (1927), pp. 78 ff.” [HHI note 4, l. 372]

**Recommendation.** Keep the argument in the text. A reader who skips the notes should lose nothing the argument needs.

**Scope.** Pervasive in the six texts whose notes I counted (1990 to 2020).

### N2. A prose note does one of four jobs

**What the texts do.** (a) It limits a claim made in the text: “This is not to say, of course, that the neutralization of the belief in the existence of the real world,” [MAP note 1, l. 90] involves an act of imagination. (b) It carries a side-argument that would break the line of the text: “Incidently, it is not difficult to realize that the same infinity applies also to protention.” [BER note 6, l. 795] A note in HHI follows a verbal echo between Heidegger and Kant and ends in two questions [HHI note 22, l. 408–415]; a note in MAP distinguishes dreaming from imagining at length and ends with its own summary [MAP note 5, l. 435–454]. (c) It gives original wording (W8). (d) It names another study with a word on its value: a “new and very readable account” *(my translation)* [GZ note 8, l. 749]; a “remarkable account of this problem” *(my translation)* [LUP note 21, l. 2617], with the reason for the praise given in the next sentence.

**Recommendation.** Before writing a prose note, decide which of the four it is. If it is none, cut it or move it into the text.

**Scope.** Common.

### N3. References to the main text move into the running text after a first full note

**What the texts do.** The first note gives the edition and the convention; later references are parentheses in the text. See W7 for the wording. In LOH the single note says that all page references to the book under review will be given in the text [LOH l. 38].

**Scope.** Pervasive where one primary text dominates.

### N4. The author's other studies are cited for what the article leaves out

**What the texts do.** Where the article declines a topic (A3), a note often points to a study of the author's own that treats it. HHI ends on a remark about time and a note that begins “For a further development of this point see” [HHI note 27, l. 423]. GZ does the same twice [GZ notes 3 and 4, l. 724–734]. MAP: “Cf. Bernet (2005).” [MAP note 4, l. 434]

**Recommendation.** A drafter should apply the function, not the habit: when a topic is set aside, give the reader one place to find it treated.

**Scope.** Common, 1987 to 2020.

### N5. Reference systems follow the venue

**What the texts do.** Footnotes (TIR, LOH, GZ's endnotes headed "Anmerkungen"), endnotes (HHI, BER, REV), and author–date references with a list of works (LIM, AE; BIB in German in 1979) all occur. Nothing in the files shows a preference of the author's.

**Scope.** Varies by text.

## 9. Genre variants

The rules above describe the research article. The other kinds of text in the corpus keep the stance and most of the moves but change the architecture.

### G1. Research article

**What the texts do.** Received view, thesis as something to be shown, declared texts and omissions, numbered sections, reconstruction before criticism, close without summary (A1 to A13). Notes are mostly references. "We" predominates.

**Scope.** The large majority of the corpus, all languages, 1978 to 2020.

### G2. Overview, companion or handbook chapter

**Rule.** State a modest aim, give the history and the standard doctrine before the difficulties, keep questions and first-person judgement low, and end on what is unresolved or on wider relevance.

**Evidence.** BER: aim stated as “no other ambition than” [BER abstract, l. 9] an overview; a history of the manuscripts first; open problems reported as open (M9); last words on what “may also be of interest for today’s logicians” [BER III, l. 775]. AE places itself in its volume in the first sentence: “The present volume demonstrates well enough that phenomenology is not only concerned with the visual perception of ‘things’.” [AE l. 12] and clears away a prejudice in the second paragraph: “It is also necessary to do away with the prejudice that a phenomenology of perception necessarily involves some kind of ‘intuitionism’ or ‘metaphysics of presence’.” [AE l. 29] ETA begins from the widest frame: “Even a fleeting glance at the history of philosophy shows that thinking has attempted to approach the riddle of time in two main ways.” [ETA I, l. 6] Questions are 1.0% of sentences in BER; "I" occurs twice in BER and twice in AE (script).

**Scope.** Four texts (BER, AE, LCH, ETA), 2006 to 2012. LCH is nearer to a research article and opens on a paradox (A1).

### G3. Review and critical study

**Rule.** Write in the first person singular. Give your overall judgement or your recommendation in the first paragraph. Present the book before discussing it. Choose a small number of issues and say that you are choosing. End on a question or a pointed doubt, not a verdict.

**Evidence.** REV opens: “let me admit right from the beginning that in my opinion this is a strange book indeed” [REV l. 9]. LOH opens with its plan and a recommendation of the book “which I cannot recommend too highly to the reader” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 8], and then: “My intention is not only to defend Heidegger” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 76]. SES spends its first paragraph on why the topic matters before naming the book [SES l. 4–26]. Selection: three questions in REV (A10). Endings: REV ends on a question: “Should its possible failure then not rather be measured against the possible impossibility of the enormous task it has set for itself?” [REV l. 447] SES ends: “It is not certain that the narcissism of self-perfection suffices to fill this gap.” *(my translation)* [SES l. 541] CHR allows itself one bold comparison and labels it: it will close “by a new peremptory affirmation” [CHR close, l. 670]. "I" outweighs "we" in REV and LOH (V1). Questions reach 16.1% of sentences in REV.

**Scope.** Four texts (REV, LOH, SES, CHR), 1994 to 2002, English and French.

### G4. Published reply to a critic

**Rule.** Thank the critic for the substance of the objections. Group the objections and answer them in order. Concede what is right, say which objections are really addressed to the author you were expounding, restate what you did and did not claim, and end by naming the disagreement that remains.

**Evidence.** TEL does each of these: thanks because the questions “really hit the substantive core of my text” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 39]; grouping (M8); concession and remaining disagreement (V7); redirection (M7); restatement: “I merely said” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 311]. It also declines a grand ending: “If I were sure that this would not be understood as an edifying closing phrase” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 343].

**Scope.** One text (1978, German, in letter form).

### G5. Historical and commemorative pieces

**Rule.** Narrate. Put documents in front of the reader and let them carry the account. A personal first sentence is in place. Do not force a thesis.

**Evidence.** VSM has descriptive subheadings, the first on the founding of the Husserl Archives [VSM l. 61], gives dates and family facts, quotes dated letters at length, and ends by taking up two phrases from the last letter quoted [VSM l. 530–538]. Its median sentence is 18 words (P2). VBW begins: “It was not my own idea to treat Van Breda and De Waelhens together in one article.” *(my translation)* [VBW l. 11]

**Scope.** Two texts (2015), French and Dutch.

### G6. Book introduction

**What the texts do.** The three book openings read preview the chapters in order with "we" in the future tense (a first proof is promised "from our first chapter on" [CEE introduction, l. 142]), map agreement and disagreement in the field before taking a position (the introduction of LVS lists three points on which everyone agrees and then says: “Beyond this third point of agreement, however, opinions begin to diverge” *(my translation)* [LVS introduction, l. 217]), and separate the project from neighbouring ones: “Rather than draw up a mere list of Freud’s borrowings from Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, or Lacan’s from Hegel and Sartre (a task already accomplished long ago by others), the aim here is to construct” [FDD introduction, l. 114] a framework. They are freer in figure than the articles (C3).

**Scope.** Three books (1994, 2004, 2013 in French; FDD read in Sarah Allen's English). Only the opening pages were read.

## 10. What he avoids

Each claim rests on a search over a stated set of files. A search can show that something is absent or rare; why it is absent is inference.

### X1. No "I argue", "I claim", "I contend"

**Evidence.** Search for "I" or "we" followed, with or without "will", "shall", "would", "want to", "wish to", by "argue", "claim", "contend", "maintain" or "submit": 0 hits in the 26 English article files (6 with no translator stated, 20 translated by more than ten translators). The thesis is introduced with "show", "attempt", "would like" (A2).

**Rule.** Announce the thesis with a verb of showing. Do not write "I argue that".

**Scope.** All 26 English files. Since many translators agree, I take this as the author's practice; that is inference.

### X2. No evaluative sentence adverbs in the manner of "arguably"

**Evidence.** "arguably", "interestingly", "crucially": 0 hits in the 26 English article files. For comparison, "importantly" occurs 7 times and "famously" 4 times in the same files (script).

**Rule.** Do not grade your own points with an adverb. If a point matters, say why.

**Scope.** All 26 English files.

### X3. Sections headed "Introduction" and "Conclusion" are rare

**Evidence.** A6: 4 of 39 files have a "Conclusion" heading and 3 of 39 an "Introduction" heading.

**Rule.** Do not add these headings unless the venue requires them.

### X4. Flat verdicts on other readers are rare

**Evidence.** V3: 6 verdicts of the type "is wrong" or "is mistaken" in 5 of 40 files, against 26 of the type "is right to" in 15. Where a verdict of error is given, it follows a statement of what the reader got right (ISO close) or is immediately balanced (REV l. 161).

**Rule.** Do not write that a reader "is wrong" without first saying what that reader saw correctly and without saying exactly which step fails.

### X5. Opinion formulas are rare outside reviews and replies

**Evidence.** "I think", "I believe", "in my opinion", "in my view", "it is my view" and French and German equivalents: 20 hits in 10 of 40 article files; 8 of the 20 are in the review REV, the reply TEL and the critical study LOH (script).

**Rule.** In a research article, state the claim. Reserve "in my opinion" for places where you depart from a view you have just reported fairly.

### X6. Exclamation marks are few

**Evidence.** Sentence-final exclamation marks found by the sentence script in the English files with no translator stated: MAP 0, PL 0, LIM 1, PR 1, REV 2. Across the 20 translated English files the range is 0 to 3. They are commoner in the French files (LST 5, PDP 2; VSM 6, mostly inside quoted letters).

**Rule.** None, or one. See C6.

### Not avoided

Three things a drafter might expect to be excluded are present: the first person singular (V1), direct questions (P5), and plain statements of certainty (V2). Do not remove them in the name of academic neutrality.

## 11. Cautions against imitation

These are features of the texts that belong to a translator, a language, a venue or a single occasion. They have evidence like any rule; the recommendation is not to copy them.

### C1. Connectives that belong to the translators

**Evidence.** "moreover" and "in fact" do not occur in the six English files with no translator stated; in the 20 translated files they occur 51 and 46 times, each in 15 files. "therefore" occurs 2.8 times per 10,000 words in the first group and 9.5 in the second; "nevertheless" 0.9 and 4.6 (script). **Inference:** these render French *en outre*, *en fait*, *donc*, *néanmoins* or German equivalents and reflect the translators' choices.

**Recommendation.** Take the function from P3 (consequence, contrast, reformulation) and choose the plainest English word. Do not copy the frequency of any particular connective from a translated text.

### C2. German academic notation

**Evidence.** In the six German files *bzw.* ("or, respectively") occurs 41.3 times per 10,000 words and *d.h.* ("i.e.") 17.2; both are in all six. An impersonal subject for the article itself ("the present text aims neither"; *Der vorliegende Text will weder*, GZ l. 30) stands where the English and French files use "I" or "we"; GZ has no *ich* at all. Sections are bare roman numerals.

**Recommendation.** Do not reproduce "respectively", "i.e." or "resp." at this density in English, and do not make "the present text" the subject of verbs of intention. Use "I" (V1).

### C3. Extended figures of speech

**Evidence.** The introduction of CEE develops a military allegory over twenty lines, with a “pitched battle” *(my translation)* [CEE introduction, l. 77], officers and army corps, and then a figure of horses. The German article on Derrida opens with a phenomenon that “slips away with quiet mockery” from the nets laid for it *(my translation)* [DUA l. 31]. GZ uses two charged political metaphors for Husserl's separation of two kinds of time [GZ I, l. 101]. These occur in book introductions, in pieces on Derrida, and once in a short article. I did not find figures of this length in MAP, BER, TIR or HHI.

**Recommendation.** Do not imitate. A figure of speech should fit in a clause and carry a distinction (M15). Political metaphors of the kind in GZ should not be used at all.

### C4. Untranslated German and French

**Evidence.** LOH leaves German words unglossed in French sentences (*Anspruch*, *entspricht*, [LOH l. 236–237]). MAP quotes a German sentence without translation in a note [MAP note 6, l. 603]. PL quotes French without translation in its notes [PL l. 803–828]. GAD leaves a German anecdote untranslated, calling it “impossible to translate” [GAD note 6, l. 312]. TIR's notes give Husserl's German without English where the text has already translated it, but several notes give only German [TIR notes 25–29].

**Recommendation.** Follow section 13: translate everything, and keep an original only under the conditions stated there.

### C5. Long runs of questions

**Evidence.** DRV opens with about fourteen questions; GAD's introduction ends with eight and its conclusion begins with nine more [GAD III, l. 1088–1115]; the final paragraph of REV is almost entirely questions and ends on one. These runs occur in introductions, conclusions and reviews of texts on open problems (A4, G3).

**Recommendation.** Use the function, an explicit agenda, and cap it at three to five questions. Do not end a research article on a run of rhetorical questions.

### C6. Exclamations and second-person address

**Evidence.** “Certainly not!” *(my translation)* [LOH l. 115] in a critical study; “But back to Husserl!” *(my translation)* [TEL l. 293] in a letter; “One should not, however, mistake the intent of this venture!” [SAR l. 54]. The reply TEL addresses its reader directly throughout.

**Recommendation.** These belong to the review, the letter and the lecture. Leave them out of an article.

### C7. Literary epigraphs

**Evidence.** Epigraphs from novels stand at the head of BDF (DeLillo), PR (Coetzee) and FDD (Martin du Gard); GAD and SCH have philosophical ones. Where the epigraph is used, it is taken up in the text: “Just like the image of the athlete evoked by DeLillo,” [BDF l. 39]; FDD l. 76 (A4); SCH answers its second epigraph in its first paragraph. All five texts date from 2005 or later.

**Recommendation.** Optional. Use an epigraph only if the first page puts it to work.

### C8. Conventions of abstracts and self-reference

**Evidence.** Abstracts refer to "the author" in the third person (PR l. 41; LST l. 29; the French abstract of DIV writes "l'A."). BER refers to its author as editor in the third person [BER I, l. 121]. Self-citation is frequent (N4).

**Recommendation.** These follow venue and the author's position as editor of the texts discussed. Do not copy them as style.

## 12. Worked contrasts

Each contrast sets a passage from the corpus beside a weaker version of the same move. **Every weaker version is constructed by me for this guide. None is by Bernet.**

**1. Opening (A1, A2).**
Corpus: the article begins with a question in quotation marks, calls it one that “is asked frequently” [HHI l. 8], says that until recently readers were hard-pressed to justify Heidegger's praise of Husserl from the published work, reports that newly published lectures have changed this, and then: “I will attempt to show this by referring especially to the course from the summer-semester of 1925” [HHI l. 8].
Constructed (mine): "The relation between Husserl and Heidegger is one of the most important topics in phenomenology. This paper examines intentionality in both thinkers and argues that there are similarities and differences."
What is lost: the constructed version names a topic. The corpus version names a question people ask, the reason it could not be answered before, the text that now allows an answer, and the writer's commitment.

**2. Thesis (A2, X1).**
Corpus: “In what follows, I wish to show how a text almost contemporaneous with *Ideas I* develops a version of phenomenological idealism that is not only more precise but that is also less problematic.” [TIR l. 29]
Constructed (mine): "I will argue that Husserl's idealism is, arguably, more defensible than is usually thought."
What is lost: the text on which the claim rests, the two respects in which the claim holds, and the verb that promises a demonstration from that text.

**3. Confrontation (A7 to A9).**
Corpus: after listing analogies between two authors, the article stops and says that “with such an external juxtaposition little is gained either philosophically or historically” *(my translation)* [GZ II, l. 362], because the second author's basic ideas, terminology and structure have “so far remained completely in the dark” *(my translation)* [GZ II, l. 365]. It then explains them in terms the first author could have followed, and only afterwards compares.
Constructed (mine): "Husserl speaks of retention, protention and primal impression. Heidegger speaks of ecstases, horizon and temporalizing. Thus both thinkers have a threefold structure of time, although Heidegger's is ontological."
What is lost: the constructed version matches labels. It does not show that the labels name comparable things, so its "thus" has nothing under it.

**4. Self-objection (M4).**
Corpus: “Is this to say that one comprehension of intentionality is criticized in the name of another comprehension” [HHI §2, l. 263]? “In a certain way one must answer yes, since” [HHI §2, l. 263] the main reproach is indeed that Husserl did not follow the analysis to its foundation. “However, this is not to say that the critique of Husserl by Heidegger is only valid if one adopts the standpoint of fundamental ontology.” [HHI §2, l. 263]
Constructed (mine): "Some might object that Heidegger merely presupposes his own position. But this objection is misguided."
What is lost: the corpus version concedes the true part of the objection and states exactly what does not follow. The constructed version dismisses.

**5. Marking one's own reconstruction (M10, W5).**
Corpus: “This deduction of the ontological presuppositions of the idealism in the *Ideas* cannot be found as such in Heidegger's text, but it is without doubt that each of these four theses about being is profusely questioned and criticized by Heidegger.” [HHI §2, l. 261]
Constructed (mine): "Heidegger shows that Husserl's idealism rests on four ontological theses."
What is lost: the constructed sentence attributes the commentator's ordering to the author. A reader who looks for the four theses in Heidegger will not find them and will distrust the rest.

**6. Two answers (M9).**
Corpus: “One receives the impression that the author of *Truth and Method* hesitates between two possible answers.” [GAD I, l. 376] Both answers are then set out with their support, and the article reports which one the text ends by adopting [GAD I, l. 401].
Constructed (mine): "Gadamer's considered view is that the subject of understanding is a cultured personality, and passages that suggest otherwise should be read in this light."
What is lost: the hesitation is a fact about the text and the article's later argument depends on it. The constructed version removes the fact to obtain a tidy doctrine.

**7. Open self-correction (M13).**
Corpus: “The rift between the football player and the cultured person who can appreciate a work of art is thus less profound than it might at first have seemed.” [GAD I, l. 505] Then: “All the same, a game of football is not a work of art.” [GAD I, l. 513]
Constructed (mine): "The football player and the art lover are both similar and different."
What is lost: the corpus version tells the reader that a contrast drawn earlier has been weakened by the analysis, and then says what survives of it. The constructed version states a balance with no history.

**8. Section hinge (A11).**
Corpus: “This operation, at once paradoxical and mysterious, becomes fully comprehensible only with the help of the Husserlian theory of inner consciousness.” [MAP §3, l. 320] The next section is on that theory.
Constructed (mine): "Having discussed phantasy and memory, we now turn to inner consciousness."
What is lost: the corpus sentence says why the next section is needed. The constructed sentence only says that it comes next.

**9. Credit, then criticism (V3).**
Corpus: “Michel Henry is therefore right to stress” [ISO close, l. 1018] the affective character of the self-manifestation of subjective life. “But without a doubt he has not been” [ISO close, l. 1020] attentive enough to the intentionality by which it comes about.
Constructed (mine): "Henry's account of self-affection fails because it ignores intentionality."
What is lost: the reader of the corpus version learns what to keep from Henry and exactly what to add. The constructed version leaves nothing standing.

**10. Close (A12).**
Corpus: after three sections that follow Heidegger's criticism of Husserl, the last paragraph turns to time: “The affair is less clear-cut concerning the approach to time in Husserl and Heidegger.” [HHI §3, l. 337] It ends: “It is without doubt in his phenomenology of time and temporalization that Husserl has made his most original contribution” [HHI §3, l. 343].
Constructed (mine): "In conclusion, this paper has examined intentionality in section 1, the being of consciousness in section 2, and transcendence in section 3. We have seen that Heidegger criticises Husserl on each point."
What is lost: the corpus close adds a limit to the article's own result and credits the criticised author. The constructed close repeats the table of contents.

## 13. Constraints on application: language

This section does not come from the corpus. It is a standing constraint on any article drafted with this guide, and it overrides any habit described above (see C4).

- Write in English. Translate every quotation from another language.
- Keep an original-language word or phrase only in two cases: it is an established term of art that the article uses as such, or its sense departs from the English in a way the argument turns on. The second case covers a contested rendering, a pun or an etymology, a phrase one author turns against another, and a difference between editions.
- Apply one test: delete the original. If a reader with no French or German loses nothing, leave it out and quote or paraphrase in English.
- Where an original is kept, it follows the English in parentheses.

Rules T2, T6 and W8 are consistent with this: the original appears once after the English term, or stays in the text only where two original terms would fall together in English, as in GAD.

## 14. Checklists

### Drafting checklist

Before writing:
- [ ] I can state the received view or standing question the article starts from (A1).
- [ ] I can state the thesis in one sentence beginning "I wish to show that" or similar (A2).
- [ ] I have listed the texts I rely on, by edition, and what I leave out and why (A3, W6).
- [ ] For a comparison: I have the one question both authors ask (A7).
- [ ] Sections are ordered exposition first, my interpretation after (A5), with numbered noun-phrase titles (A6).

While writing:
- [ ] Each author is reconstructed in full before I object (A10).
- [ ] Each paragraph does one job and shows its link to the last (P1, P3).
- [ ] Each technical term is introduced under its owner's name, with the original once in parentheses (T1, T2); my own usage is stated where it differs (T3).
- [ ] The same word in two authors is given two separate statements (T4).
- [ ] Every claim about an author has a place in a text (W1).
- [ ] Quotations are mostly terms and phrases; each full-sentence quotation has a lead-in and a follow-up (W2, W3).
- [ ] Every step that is mine is marked as mine (M10).
- [ ] The obvious objection is asked as a question and answered in two parts (M4).
- [ ] Hesitations in the text are shown and not smoothed (M9).
- [ ] Each section ends on what the next must supply (A11).

At the end:
- [ ] The close contains no section-by-section summary (A12).
- [ ] The close states the result in its sharpest form and at least one limit, open question or credit to the author criticised (A12).
- [ ] If the title or opening asked a question, the close answers it, even with "no" (A13).
- [ ] All non-English is translated; any original kept passes the deletion test (section 13).

### Review checklist

For each item, mark pass or fail and cite the paragraph.

Architecture
- [ ] First paragraph: a received view, question or state of sources is present and is turned within the paragraph (A1).
- [ ] A one-sentence thesis with a verb of showing appears in the opening (A2). Search for "argue", "claim", "contend" with "I" or "we": there should be none (X1).
- [ ] Texts and omissions are declared with reasons (A3).
- [ ] No heading "Introduction" or "Conclusion" unless required (A6, X3).
- [ ] In a comparison: one shared question is named; parallels precede differences; the root of the differences is stated; neither author is explained only in the other's untranslated vocabulary (A7, A8, A9).
- [ ] The first sentence of disagreement with the main author comes after the reconstruction (A10).
- [ ] Last sentence of each section and first sentence of the next read as a pair (A11).
- [ ] The final paragraph could not have been written before the article (A12).

Moves
- [ ] Every "we will return to" has a return (M3).
- [ ] Every direct question outside a stated agenda is answered in the next sentences (M4, P5).

## 15. Limits

**What was not read.** The corpus is about 12 MB. Eight texts were read whole (HHI, GZ, GAD, MAP, BER, REV, LOH, TEL). For 35 other article-length texts I read the opening pages and the closing pages, and for TIR the last sections as well. I did not read the middle of those 35 texts, and I did not check their internal section transitions beyond what a search for headings showed. Of the three books I read only the foreword or the first pages of the introduction, and the last page of LVS; no chapter of *La vie du sujet*, *Conscience et existence* or *Force, Drive, Desire* was read. The six co-authored or edited volumes were not opened beyond their title pages.

**Files that could not be used well.** `bernet-endlichkeit-und-unendlichkeit-in-husserls-phaenomenologie-der-wahrnehmung.md` and the French translation `bernet-phenomenologie-et-psychologie-cognitive-le-concept-husserlien-de-noeme.md` are too damaged by scanning to quote or count. `bernet-die-ungegenwartige-gegenwart-…` (the German original of ITP), `bernet-heimwee-en-nostalgie.md` and the Dutch `…lust-en-onlust…` are partly scrambled into tables; I read their openings only. The Dutch translation `bernet-bergson-over-het-driftmatig-karakter-van-bewustzijn-en-leven.md` duplicates BDF and was not opened beyond its first lines. The single-line English file on Bergson was read at its opening and close only and could not be measured by sentence. Several other files have lost line-end hyphens, ligatures or italics, which affects the counts slightly.

**Weak claims.** (1) The six English texts "with no translator stated" are treated as written in English; the files do not say so, and the long article GAD names its translator only in its last note, so a missing name is weak evidence. (2) Paragraph length rests on three files. (3) The count of opening types (A1) is a classification by reading. (4) Rules labelled one text or two texts (A9 as a stated principle, A13 for the negative result, M7, T7, V7, G4) should be applied with that in mind. (5) Counts by fixed word lists miss variant wordings and are lower bounds for presence, not proofs of absence, except where the result is zero over all 26 English files.

**Where translation clouds the picture.** All claims in section 4 about English are drawn from at most six late texts (1994 to 2020). The early and middle periods are represented in English only by translations, so nothing is claimed about his English surface style before 1994. The French and German surface claims rest on originals from 1978 to 2015. Nothing is claimed about Dutch surface style: five of the six Dutch files are translations or damaged, and the sixth was language-corrected by a colleague.

**What a later pass should read next.** First, the middles of three long originals, to test the rules on moves against whole arguments: DUA and LUP in German and LST in French. Second, one chapter each of LVS and CEE, to see whether the book chapters keep the architecture of the articles. Third, the texts on psychoanalysis read whole (UNC, DRV, SCH), since the present rules on questions and on genre (A4, P5) suggest that this group behaves differently and only their edges were read. Fourth, a comparison of DCI with DTK and of LST with TRA sentence by sentence, to measure what the translators change.

# A comparative guide to phenomenological method: Husserl, Heidegger, Sartre, Henry, Marion, Derrida

It is commonly said that phenomenology is a method and not a doctrine, and that its practitioners, however far apart their results, share the maxim "to the things themselves". The authors themselves are less confident. Heidegger grants that the maxim “is abundantly self-evident” and asks why a commonplace should give a kind of research its name [BT §7, l. 437]. Henry lists four principles on which phenomenology claims to rest and judges that all four “remain in fact fundamentally indeterminate” *(my translation)* [QP, l. 643]. A reader who wants to work in the manner of one of these authors, or to set two of them against each other, is therefore not helped by the maxim. What has to be known is what each author does in its name.

In what follows I wish to show two things. The first is that the methods of the six authors can be compared under one question which all of them ask: what stands between us and the phenomenon, and what operation removes it? The second is that their procedures differ because their answers to the first half of that question differ. Each later author keeps the name of Husserl's operation, the reduction, and changes where it leads, and each change follows from a new account of what conceals the phenomenon.

The guide rests only on the primary texts held in the sources repository, and within them on the passages listed in the key below. Six authors are treated because the repository holds works of theirs in which the method is stated: Husserl, Heidegger, Sartre, Henry, Marion and Derrida. Brentano's *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* is used for one purpose, to mark the point from which Husserl departs. Three omissions need a word. Bernet, Bégout, Moran, Žižek and the yearbook volumes are commentators and are left out by the terms of the task. Descartes, Kant and Eckhart are in the repository but are not phenomenologists. Merleau-Ponty, Levinas and Scheler are not in the repository at all, so a reader should not take the six for the whole tradition. It may seem strange to count Derrida among phenomenologists. I include him because the texts held here define his procedure by its relation to Husserl's, and because he says himself that his thought “can no more break with a transcendental phenomenology than be reduced to it” [OG, l. 1200]. Whether that is enough is taken up again at the close.

The guide is comparative. It does not replace a guide to any one author's method, and it does not summarise doctrines; a doctrine appears only where it explains a step of method.

## 1. The shared question and the common ground

Before the differences can be measured, it must be shown that the six authors are speaking of the same thing. Three commitments are common to them, and each can be read off the texts.

The starting point is Brentano. His standpoint is stated in two clauses: “My psychological standpoint is empirical; experience alone is my teacher.” [PES Foreword, l. 273] The source of that experience is the inner perception of one's own mental phenomena, and Brentano at once limits it: “One of the characteristics of inner perception is that it can never become inner observation.” [PES I.2, l. 537] The method is thus description of what is experienced, drawn from a source that cannot be turned into an object of scrutiny at will. Husserl keeps the description and changes its field. Against a psychological phenomenology he sets one that is to be established “not as a science of facts, but as a science of essential Being” [ID Introduction, l. 471]. Everything that follows is a dispute about what the description describes and how the describer gets to it.

The first commitment is that a claim must be made good by showing and not by construction. Husserl's version is the best known: one “can absolutely not rest content with ‘mere words’” [LI Introduction §2, l. 2143]. Heidegger turns the maxim against “all free-floating” constructions and against “taking over concepts only apparently demonstrated” [BT §7, l. 437]. Sartre separates “the description of the image” from “inductions concerning its nature” and says that in passing from the one to the others one goes from the certain to the probable *(my translation)* [IM, l. 495–498]. Henry states “the principle of every radical critique, of every phenomenological critique” as “the demand to make seen the reality one speaks of” *(my translation)* [PV, l. 167]. He states it in order to contest it, as section 5 will show, but he states it as the principle he inherits.

The second commitment is that the phenomenon is not a surface with something else behind it. For Heidegger “phenomena are *never* appearances” in the sense of signs of what does not show itself [BT §7, l. 447]. Sartre opens *L'être et le néant* by crediting modern thought with “reducing the existent to the series of appearances that manifest it” *(my translation)* [EN Introduction, l. 56]. Marion and Henry both begin from the formula "so much appearing, so much being", which Marion calls a thesis “common to Husserl and Heidegger” *(my translation)* [RD, l. 1597] and Henry counts as the first of the four principles [QP, l. 629].

The third commitment is that method is not a technique which could be learned apart from the matter. “Scientific method is never a technique. As soon as it becomes one it has fallen away from its own proper nature.” [BP §5, l. 631] Husserl says that the methodological insights of phenomenology “come under the very norms which they formulate” [ID §65, l. 1213]. Henry sets aside “as non-original the definition of phenomenology as method”, because the method “refers back to the object of phenomenology and is possible only when supported by it” *(my translation)* [PNI, l. 910]. Derrida writes that he has “no ambition to illustrate a new method” [OG Preface, l. 802].

Is this to say that the six have no method to compare? In a certain way one must answer yes: none of them offers a procedure that could be applied without deciding first what a phenomenon is. However, this is not to say that their procedures are arbitrary. Each author binds himself to an operation, names it, and says what it is for. The operations can be compared precisely because each answers the same question about access. To them we now turn, one author at a time and each in his own terms.

## 2. Husserl: suspension and the insight into essences

What stands between us and the phenomenon, for Husserl, is twofold. The first obstacle is verbal understanding and inherited theory. The second is the natural standpoint, in which the world is simply taken as being there.

Against the first he sets a rule about evidence, which he calls the principle of all principles: “every primordial dator Intuition is a source of authority (Rechtsquelle) for knowledge”, and whatever presents itself in intuition “is simply to be accepted as it gives itself out to be, though only within the limits in which it then presents itself” [ID §24, l. 719]. The rule has two halves, and the second matters as much as the first. Intuition is the authority, and its authority reaches exactly as far as the giving does.

Against the second he sets the suspension (ἐποχή): “We put out of action the general thesis which belongs to the essence of the natural standpoint” [ID §32, l. 801]. Husserl is careful to say what the operation is not. “I do not then deny this “world”, as though I were a sophist, I do not doubt that it is there as though I were a sceptic” [ID §32, l. 803]. Put differently, nothing is removed from experience; a thesis is put out of use, and what was naively lived through becomes a theme.

The description that follows is of essences, and its instrument is imagination as much as perception. Husserl puts the point as a paradox that he then endorses: “the element which makes up the life of phenomenology as of all eidetical science is “fiction”” [ID §70, l. 1277]. He also fixes the kind of rigour to be expected. Phenomenology is a descriptive science of essences that belongs to a class “wholly other than that to which the mathematical sciences belong” [ID §75, l. 1345]. Exact deduction is therefore not its model.

Finally, the order of the work is not the order of a system. “We search, as it were, in zig-zag fashion”, because the concepts depend on one another and later analyses lead back to earlier ones, “where the new confirms the old, and the old the new” [LI Introduction §6, l. 2189].

In short: the obstacle lies in an attitude of the inquirer, the operation is a change of attitude followed by description under the control of intuition, and the authority is the giving intuition within its limits. It is this last point that every later author takes up.

## 3. Heidegger: interpretation of what is at first concealed

Heidegger keeps the maxim and gives it a formal sense: phenomenology means “to let be seen, from itself, what shows itself, and in the way it shows itself from itself” [BT §7, l. 483]. The decisive step is the next one. What must be explicitly let be seen is, he says, “a theme that initially and mostly does *not* show itself”, something concealed which nevertheless belongs to what does show itself and constitutes its “meaning and ground” [BT §7, l. 487]. This is “not this or that being” but “the *being* of these beings” [BT §7, l. 489]. Hence the thesis: “*Ontology is only possible as phenomenology*.” [BT §7, l. 491]

The obstacle is accordingly not an attitude that one could put out of action. Heidegger distinguishes its kinds: “There are various ways in which phenomena are covered up.” [BT §7, l. 495] A phenomenon may be still “*undiscovered*”, or “*buried*” after having once been uncovered, and the commonest and most dangerous case is “disguising”, where what was once seen remains visible only as seeming and is passed on inside a system as needing no further justification [BT §7, l. 495]. Concealment thus has a history, and the concepts with which one would describe are part of it.

Three consequences for procedure follow. First, description becomes interpretation: “the method of phenomenological description proceeds as *interpretation*” [BT §7, l. 505]. Second, the tradition has to be dismantled, in a task that returns to “the primordial experiences in which the first, and from then on the guiding determinations of being were obtained” [BT §6, l. 397]. Heidegger insists that this is not a rejection of the past; its critique “bears on the “current day,”” [BT §6, l. 399]. Third, the reduction changes direction. In the lectures he says that he adopts Husserl's term “in its literal wording though not in its substantive intent”: for him it means “leading phenomenological vision back from the apprehension of a being, whatever may be the character of that apprehension, to the understanding of the being of this being” [BP §5, l. 631]. Since being is not found lying before us, “it must always be brought to view in a free projection”, which he calls construction [BP §5, l. 633]. The method thus has three components that belong together, “reduction, construction, destruction” [BP §5, l. 637].

The objection that such a method presupposes what it seeks is raised by Heidegger himself and declined in its formal version: arguments about circularity “are always sterile when we are considering concrete paths of investigation” [BT §2, l. 297].

His criticism of Husserl follows from the same analysis. He first reconstructs Husserl's phenomenology in a definition he accepts as its self-understanding: “*Phenomenology is the analytic description of intentionality in its apriori*.” [HCT §8, l. 990] He then says where the operation fails: “the reduction is in principle inappropriate for determining the being of consciousness positively” [HCT §12, l. 1250]. The objection is addressed to what the suspension leaves out of view and not to the demand for showing, which Heidegger keeps.

## 4. Sartre: reflection purified, and conduct as the guiding thread

Sartre's texts give two answers to the question of where description starts, and they should be shown as two.

The first is that of *L'imaginaire*. To describe the image as image one must reflect, and “a reflective consciousness delivers absolutely certain data” *(my translation)* [IM, l. 467]. The rule that follows is short: “We shall leave theories aside” *(my translation)* [IM, l. 502], and the method is simply to produce images in oneself, reflect on them and describe them [IM, l. 511]. Here the obstacle is the psychologist's habit of building explanatory hypotheses, and the remedy is Husserl's: description before theory, on the authority of reflection.

The second answer is that of *L'être et le néant*. It begins by restricting the first. There is “no kind of primacy of reflection”; on the contrary, “there is a pre-reflective cogito which is the condition of the Cartesian cogito” *(my translation)* [EN Introduction, l. 258]. The certainty that the first answer credited to reflection now belongs to a consciousness that is not yet reflection. Reflection itself is divided. The pure kind is the original form and also the one “never given first”, which “must be won by a sort of catharsis” *(my translation)* [EN, l. 3640]; the ordinary kind is called impure or accomplice. The obstacle has moved: it is no longer only the theorist's hypotheses but a tendency of reflection itself.

The same book declines Husserl's reduction as a beginning. To question experience about its conditions of possibility, as Kant does, or to carry out a phenomenological reduction, as Husserl does, “is deliberately to begin with the abstract” *(my translation)* [EN I.1 §1, l. 614]. The concrete is the human being in the world, and “it suffices to open one's eyes and to question in all naivety this totality” *(my translation)* [EN I.1 §1, l. 616]. The practical rule is to take human conducts as the thread, on condition that they are regarded “as objectively graspable realities and not as subjective affections” disclosed only to reflection *(my translation)* [EN I.1 §1, l. 620]. The first conduct chosen is the question itself.

A third element is added at the end of the book under the name of existential psychoanalysis. Its starting point is experience, its support is the pre-ontological comprehension each person has of the human person, and “the essential work is a hermeneutic, that is, a deciphering, a fixing and a conceptualisation” *(my translation)* [EN, l. 11914]. “Its method is comparative”, since each conduct both symbolises the fundamental choice and masks it *(my translation)* [EN, l. 11918]. What is sought is not “a datum buried in the darkness of the unconscious” but a free and conscious determination *(my translation)* [EN, l. 11992].

Which answer does Sartre end with? The texts read here move from reflection as an unquestioned source (1940) to reflection as something to be purified, with conduct as the point of entry (1943). That reading of the direction is mine; Sartre does not retract the earlier rule. One early remark shows that the question of access troubled him from the start. In 1937 he observes that the suspension “appears in Husserl’s phenomenology like a miracle”, since nothing in the natural attitude motivates it, and proposes that on his own account of consciousness it “is no longer an intellectual method, a skilled procedure” [TE Conclusion, l. 398]. What Husserl treats as a free act of the inquirer Sartre here treats as something that befalls consciousness.

## 5. Henry: the reduction of appearing itself

With Henry the shared question receives an answer that turns against the first commitment of section 1. We must not be misled by his vocabulary, which remains that of reduction and givenness. What conceals the phenomenon, on his account, is the kind of appearing that all the earlier methods rely on.

His argument starts from the object of phenomenology. The other sciences study particular phenomena; phenomenology asks about “what allows a phenomenon to be a phenomenon, that is, about pure phenomenality as such” *(my translation)* [PV, l. 485]. Method comes second. As method, phenomenology is “a making-seen, a bringing into evidence” *(my translation)* [PNI, l. 910], and a making-seen presupposes an appearing in which seeing is possible. That appearing is a particular one: the horizon of visibility in which things are set at a distance. Henry's charge is that this particular appearing has been taken for appearing in general. He calls it a “catastrophic confusion of the appearing of the world with the universal essence of appearing” *(my translation)* [QP, l. 707].

From here his reading of his predecessors follows. Husserl's principle of all principles claims universality and yet names by "intuition" one mode of appearing, that of intentional consciousness directed at an object set before it. Since life, as Henry understands it, never appears in that way, the principle excludes it; his verdict is that the principle “is a murder” *(my translation)* [QP, l. 720]. Heidegger's criticisms of Husserl are judged ineffective for the same reason: what he opposes to intuition only states its condition, and the search is handed over to “a hermeneutic” charged with never finding what is in question *(my translation)* [QP, l. 722]. Henry is right to observe that both predecessors define access by a showing at a distance; the texts quoted in sections 2 and 3 bear him out. Whether his own alternative can still be called a method is the difficulty he has to meet.

He meets it by radicalising the reduction. He credits Marion's principle with “restoring to the reduction a positive meaning” *(my translation)* [QP, l. 731]: the reduction opens and gives. But the reduction must then be carried through to its end. “The radical reduction reduces appearing itself” *(my translation)* [QP, l. 748]; it sets aside the lit expanse called world in order to find that without which no horizon would become visible, the self-affection of life. The authority changes accordingly. Life's original revelation “never gives itself under the gaze of evidence” *(my translation)* [QP, l. 744]. In the early book the consequence for the discipline is drawn in one sentence: “*Phenomenology is rather a critique of all revelation*”, of its forms and its conditions *(my translation)* [EM §7, l. 315].

Does this mean that Henry gives up showing? The answer has two parts. He gives up the claim that what is ultimate can be placed before a gaze. He does not give up the demand that it be experienced and not inferred: the appeal throughout is to an undergoing that needs no distance. How a written analysis can be controlled by such an undergoing is not said in the pages read here, and I leave the question open.

## 6. Marion: reduction as the measure of givenness

Marion's contribution to method is a rule and a schema, and both are comparative from the outset. He is the only one of the six who presents his own procedure as the third term of a series.

The rule is stated at the end of *Réduction et donation*. Appearing suffices for being only in so far as what appears gives itself, and it gives itself perfectly only in so far as it is reduced. Hence “givenness unfolds according to the direct measure of the reduction: the more the reduction is radicalised, the more givenness unfolds”, and the older formula is doubled by a more essential one, "so much reduction, so much givenness" (*autant de réduction, autant de donation*) *(my translation)* [RD, l. 1597]. A small fact about the wording is worth recording. Henry cites the rule as "all the more reduction, all the more givenness" (*d'autant plus de réduction, d'autant plus de donation*) [QP, l. 641]. Marion's formula states an equivalence; Henry's states an increase. Marion's own gloss, quoted above, supports the comparative reading, so the difference is one of emphasis, but it is the comparative form that Henry needs for his radical reduction.

The schema is a set of four questions to be put to any reduction. One must say *(a)* to whom or what things are led back, *(b)* what is thereby given, *(c)* in what mode or horizon, and *(d)* what that mode of givenness excludes [RD, l. 1599]. Marion applies it three times. The first reduction, the transcendental one, leads back to the constituting I, gives constituted objects within the horizon of objectness, and excludes whatever cannot be led back to objectness, namely the differences between ways of being [RD, l. 1601]. The second, existential or ontological, leads back to Dasein, gives or claims to give the ways of being and the "phenomenon of being" within the horizon of time, and excludes what does not have to be [RD, l. 1603]. The third leads back to the one who is addressed by a call, before any determination as I or Dasein; it gives the gift itself, within no horizon but that of the call, and according to Marion it excludes nothing [RD, l. 1605].

For Marion, then, what stands between us and the phenomenon is a condition placed on givenness in advance. He finds such a condition already in Husserl's breakthrough, which extends presence without limit and at the same time restores objectivity as its measure; the starting point of Heidegger, as he puts it, is that “the objective of phenomenology does not coincide with objectivity” *(my translation)* [RD, l. 139]. His own step repeats that criticism one level further on, against the horizon of being.

Marion states the limit of his result himself. It “remains to be understood precisely and conceptually” how the pure fact of the call can allow the strictest reduction *(my translation)* [RD, l. 1607]. The book, on its own account, establishes that a third reduction is required and does not yet work it out.

## 7. Derrida: reading from the inside

Derrida's procedure is the hardest to place under the shared question, because he contests the terms in which the question is put. It is not completely correct, however, to say that he has left it. He asks what stands between us and the phenomenon, and answers that the phenomenon understood as presence is itself the product of what was supposed to stand in the way.

The answer is prepared in his reading of Husserl. The constitution of the other and of time, he writes, refers phenomenology to a zone in which its principle of principles is put into question, and he glosses that principle in a parenthesis: “its metaphysical principle: the original self-evidence and presence of the thing itself in person” [WD "Genesis and Structure", l. 1439]. The criticism has a definite form. It is drawn from Husserl's own descriptions: in originary temporalisation “as Husserl actually describes them, non-presentation or depresentation is as “originary” as presentation” [OG, l. 1200]. Derrida is thus not opposing a counter-theory to phenomenology. He holds that its descriptions say more than its principle allows, and that phenomenology “nonetheless remains dominated by a metaphysics of presence” [WD "Violence and Metaphysics", l. 1286].

Two consequences follow for procedure. First, the reduction is neither performed nor rejected: “We must then *situate*, as a simple *moment of the discourse*, the phenomenological reduction” [OG, l. 1200]. What the passage through it leads to is no origin and no essence. The trace, he says, “exceeds the question *What is?*” [OG, l. 1280]. Second, the work becomes a reading of texts carried out within them. “The movements of deconstruction do not destroy structures from the outside”; they operate “necessarily from the inside”, borrowing their resources from the structure they inhabit [OG, l. 942]. The reader aims at a relation the writer does not perceive between what he commands and what he does not command of his language, “a signifying structure that critical reading should *produce*” [OG, l. 1938].

On the question of method itself Derrida gives two answers and leaves both standing. On the one hand he has “no ambition to illustrate a new method” [OG Preface, l. 802]. On the other hand a section is entitled "Question of Method" and speaks of “methodological considerations” [OG, l. 1944]. The resolution, so far as there is one, is that a method requires a justified beginning and none is available: “We must begin *wherever we are*”, since it was “impossible to justify a point of departure absolutely” [OG, l. 1966]. What takes the place of a founding evidence is modest: “This avowal of empiricism can sustain itself only by the strength of the question.” [OG, l. 1966]

## 8. The differences, point by point

The six positions can now be set side by side. To do so I borrow Marion's four questions and add a first one of my own, about the obstacle. The extension of his schema to Sartre, Henry and Derrida is not found in his text; he applies it only to Husserl, Heidegger and himself. The entries for those three, and for Marion's own first two rows, are his where a citation to RD stands and mine elsewhere.

Three differences deserve to be stated in sentences, since a table suggests more symmetry than there is.

The first concerns the word "reduction". It occurs in five of the six authors and names five operations. For Husserl it is a suspension of a thesis by the inquirer. For Heidegger it is a leading back of the gaze from a being to its being, and it is incomplete without projection and historical dismantling. For Sartre in 1943 it is a way of beginning with the abstract, and he does not begin with it. For Henry it is carried into appearing itself and suspends the visible. For Marion it is a rule of proportion and happens three times. Derrida situates it as a moment. A comparison that matches the word across authors compares nothing.

The second concerns evidence. Husserl's rule makes the giving intuition the source of right. Heidegger keeps the showing and denies that it is first given: it has to be won from concealment. Sartre moves certainty from reflection to what precedes reflection. Henry keeps certainty and removes sight. Marion keeps givenness and removes the conditions under which intuition gives. Derrida removes the claim that anything is given "in person". Each step can be described as a restriction of Husserl's second half-sentence, "only within the limits in which it then presents itself": every successor argues that the limits lie elsewhere than Husserl drew them. This formulation is mine.

The third concerns history and other authors. Husserl and the Sartre of *L'imaginaire* set theories aside in order to describe. Heidegger makes the dismantling of the tradition a component of the method. Henry, Marion and Derrida, in the texts read here, proceed almost wholly by reading their predecessors: Henry by testing four principles, Marion by reconstructing two reductions before proposing a third, Derrida by producing what a text does not command. The later the author, the more the method is exercised on texts. One could find this a loss of contact with the things themselves. The authors' reply would be that the predecessor's concepts are among the things that conceal, which is already Heidegger's point about disguise.

## 9. The root of the differences

Where do these differences come from? Naturally one could list influences and periods, but with such a list little is gained. The texts themselves point to a single root: the place assigned to what conceals.

For Husserl the obstacle lies in an attitude of the inquirer. An attitude can be changed by a free act, and the suspension is that act; once it is performed, the field lies open to description. For Heidegger the obstacle lies in the way phenomena come to be covered and in the history of the concepts one inherits. No single act removes that, and so description becomes interpretation and takes the dismantling of the tradition into itself. For Sartre the obstacle lies partly in theory and partly in consciousness's own way of taking itself, which is why reflection has to be purified and why conduct, which can be grasped objectively, is the safer entry. For Henry the obstacle lies in phenomenality itself, in the appearing by which all the earlier methods proceed; a method that is a making-seen cannot remove it, and so the reduction is turned on appearing. For Marion the obstacle lies in the horizon within which givenness is admitted, and the remedy is to reduce again until no horizon is presupposed. For Derrida the obstacle lies in the measure by which access was defined from the start, the presence of the thing itself; there is then no access to secure, and what remains is a reading that shows the measure at work.

Stated otherwise, the obstacle moves from the inquirer's attitude, to the inquirer's history and way of being, to appearing as such, and finally to the idea of an unobstructed presence. With each move the remedy becomes less like an act one can perform and more like a labour without a fixed end. This ordering of the six is mine; no one of them proposes it, though Marion's series of three reductions and Henry's review of four principles are partial versions of it made from inside.

One objection must be met. Does the ordering not suggest that each later method refutes the earlier? It does not, and for two reasons found in the texts. Each successor uses the predecessor's operation in order to go beyond it: Heidegger adopts the reduction "in its literal wording", Henry radicalises it, Marion repeats it, Derrida requires that its necessity be felt before it is erased. And each successor's criticism has the same form. It says what the predecessor's operation cannot bring to appearance, and it says so on the predecessor's own ground, the demand to show. That form is Husserl's.

## 10. Using the comparison

A drafter who analyses a concept in the manner of one of these authors, or who compares two of them, can take five questions from the foregoing. They are Marion's four with the question of the obstacle placed first.

1. What, according to this author, conceals the phenomenon under analysis: an attitude, a tradition, a kind of reflection, a kind of appearing, a prior condition, a value?
2. What operation is set against it, under what name, and in which text is it stated?
3. To what does the operation lead back, and what is thereby given?
4. What authorises a claim once the operation is performed?
5. What does the operation leave out, and which later author says so?

Three cautions follow from section 8.

- State each author's operation in plain words before using its name. "Reduction", "phenomenon", "givenness", "evidence" and "description" change sense from author to author, and the comparison depends on the change.
- Do not criticise one author's results by another's standard of evidence without saying that the standard has changed. Henry's objection to Husserl holds only if appearing has two modes; Heidegger's holds only if being is at first concealed. Say which premise carries the objection.
- Keep apart what an author says his method is and what he visibly does. This guide rests almost wholly on statements of method. Whether Husserl's analyses, or Sartre's, keep to their stated rules is a separate inquiry, and it needs the analyses themselves.

## 11. What the comparison settles, and what it does not

The received view was that the six share a maxim. The texts allow a more exact statement. They share a demand, that what is claimed be brought to show itself, and a form of criticism, that an operation is to be judged by what it cannot bring to show itself. They do not share an operation, an authority, or even a concept of the phenomenon, and the reason is that they place the obstacle to the phenomenon at different depths. A method in this tradition is an answer to a prior diagnosis of concealment. To compare two methods is therefore to compare two diagnoses.

Two things stay open. The first is the question deferred at the outset, whether Derrida belongs in the series. On the evidence used here he fulfils the form of criticism exactly, since he draws his objection from Husserl's own descriptions; but he gives up the demand in its original sense, since nothing is finally to be brought to presence. The same doubt, in a different way, attaches to Henry, for whom what is ultimate is never seen. The comparison shows where each departs. It does not decide whether the departure is a continuation of phenomenology by other means or its end, and I do not think the passages read here can decide it.

The second is a limit of the evidence. The holding for Marion is one book of 1989 and a study of Descartes; the later works in which the third reduction is worked out are not in the repository. Derrida's book on Husserl's theory of signs is not there either, nor is Henry's volume on material phenomenology, and for Sartre the later writings on method were set aside as lying outside the phenomenological period. No work was read whole: the passages were found by searching for statements of method and then read in their surroundings. For Husserl and Heidegger only English translations were used, although the German is held. The French passages are given in my translation and were checked against the files by reading.

It remains true, after six sections of criticism addressed to him, that every one of the later authors states his method by restating Husserl's. The principle of all principles is quoted, contested or rewritten in each of them, and none of them replaces its second clause: that what gives itself is to be accepted only within the limits in which it gives itself. They disagree about the limits.

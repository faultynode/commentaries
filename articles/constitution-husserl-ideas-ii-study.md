# What Is Constituted on What?

## Constitution in Husserl's *Ideas II*

It is generally accepted that the second book of Husserl's *Ideas* constitutes reality in layers. Material nature comes first. The animate body is built on it, the soul on the body, and the person, with the social and cultural world, on the soul. The book's three sections follow this order, and Husserl's own programme in the first book announced it: of all the regions of reality, he wrote in 1913, "as the lowest level, material reality finally lies at the basis of all other realities" (Hua III/1, 355).[^1] Heidegger, who read the manuscript in 1925, drew the critical consequence. In *Ideas II* the "fundamental layer" remains "the actuality of nature"; the psychic is built on it, and the spiritual on the psychic, so that the person is taken as "a many-layered thing of the world" (GA 20, 172–73). Later readers have kept the description and dropped the reproach. Filip Mattens calls the stratification shown by Husserl's concept of the phantom "the paragon of constitutive analysis".[^2] Yet the same book says, in its last paragraph, that if we "strike out all spirits from the world, there is no more nature", and that nature is "a field of thoroughgoing relativities" borne by an absolute, spirit (Hua IV, 297). It says that the attitude in which nature comes first is not on a par with the attitude in which persons are given, but "subordinates itself" to it and wins its independence by "a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal ego" (184). And in its last chapter it places a lower layer inside the person, as the "dark underground" on which the spirit's own life depends (276), an underground that one of the chapter's sources described as the soul of naturalistic psychology and that Husserl, correcting the typescript after 1924, refused to read so (280, apparatus). If the layers run from nature to spirit, these sentences cannot be placed. If they do not, it has to be said what the layers are.

In what follows I wish to show that constitution in *Ideas II* keeps a layered form from the first draft of 1912 to Husserl's last notes of 1928, but that the bearer of its layers changes, and that it changes in two steps. In the texts of 1912 and 1913 the founding order is an order among constituted realities: the soul is founded on the body and the body on the material thing, in an ascent set out *idealiter* for an ego thought of as isolated, and the person does not appear in that series. In January 1917 Husserl stratifies the person's own life. Its lower layer, sensibility, is the ego's "possession", in part the sediment of its own activity, and the site where nature is constituted; but he still calls this layer "nature". Only in his notes of 1924–28 does he reject the reading of the person's underground as the soul of naturalistic psychology, and make the absolute of the last paragraph intersubjective and historical. The relativity of nature to spirit, which that paragraph states, is older than either step: the manuscript of 1913 already makes nature a unity constituted among persons. But in 1913 it stands beside the layered ascent as the other side of what Husserl himself calls a circle, and the two cease to be rival orders only as the lower layers come to belong to the person as its own. The question of the title, what is constituted on what, has therefore more than one answer in *Ideas II*, and the answers belong to dated states of the text.

The study rests on a closed body of texts read in the files of one working archive, in German except where noted. Its centre is *Ideas II* in the Husserliana edition of 1952 (Hua IV), with the editor's account of the manuscripts and the textual apparatus at the end of the volume, which together allow most pages of the book to be assigned to a manuscript and many of those to a year. Beside it stand the unused continuation of the first draft of 1912, printed as the first supplement to *Ideas III* (Hua V); §§ 149–153 of *Ideas I* (Hua III/1), where the programme of the second book is announced; one supplement of 1918 from the first volume on intersubjectivity (Hua XIII); and, for the opposing reading, § 13 of Heidegger's lecture course of the summer of 1925 (GA 20) and the note on Husserl in § 10 of *Being and Time*. The English translation of *Ideas II* is held, but in a copy so damaged by scanning that I translate throughout and give the English page only where the passage could be found in it.

Five omissions need to be named, because each bears on a claim made below. I have not read Husserl's lecture courses entitled "Nature and Spirit" of 1919 and 1927, which would show what order of constitution he taught in the years between the second arrangement of *Ideas II* and its last revision. I have not read Paul Ricœur's two-part study "Analyses et problèmes dans *Ideen II* de Husserl", Maurice Merleau-Ponty's essay "The Philosopher and His Shadow", or Robert Sokolowski's book *The Formation of Husserl's Concept of Constitution*. As far as I know, the first two are the nearest published readings of the tension this study traces, and the third is the standard account of the concept's development; but what I know of them is from memory and from bibliographies, not from the texts. I therefore make no claim of novelty against them, and where they would have tested a step I say so. Finally, the study is confined to *Ideas II* and its immediate surroundings. It does not follow constitution into the *Cartesian Meditations* or the late manuscripts on intersubjectivity, except where one dated text is needed to test a prediction.

A word on method. Most of what follows is historical and textual: it dates passages and reads them in the order in which they were written, which is not the order in which they are printed. At two points, in parts 2 and 4, the study re-performs a description on an example of Husserl's own, the blue spectacles of 1913 and the habit that sinks into sensibility in 1917, in order to see what the text's claim amounts to when one carries it out. Those stretches are marked as descriptive. Nothing in them is offered as evidence of what Husserl held; they test what his sentences say. Part 6 then gives Heidegger's reading its full strength and answers it.

## 1. The book and its strata of composition

### 1.1 The text that one cites

The book cited as *Ideas II* was never finished by its author, and the edition says so plainly. Husserl wrote a first draft of the second book in November and December of 1912, directly after the manuscript of the first; the editor, Marly Biemel, dates it from the university invitations on the backs of three of its sheets, for the 21st and 28th of November and the 4th of December 1912, and from Husserl's own dating (Hua IV, 398).[^3] That draft, a pencil manuscript of eighty-four sheets, treated the constitution of intuitive nature and, "in immediate connexion with it", that of body and soul, and then passed to the sciences of these objects (XVI). Its second half, on the relation of phenomenology to the sciences, was kept almost unchanged and became *Ideas III*; the part on constitution was worked over again and again until about 1928. The editor describes the shift in a sentence that sets the scale of what follows: it was "no longer a matter of outlining 'phenomenological sketches'", for "the proper task of phenomenology now becomes to clarify the constitution of every reality" (XV).

The working-over has layers that the apparatus distinguishes and dates. The main manuscript of the third section, on the constitution of the spiritual world, was written in 1913, on folio sheets in longhand, and was meant as a fair copy for the press; Husserl says that the fair copy "did not then get beyond an initial piece", and about half of it is lost (399). In 1915 he produced a new elaboration of the first two sections, compiled "from the lectures of 1913 and 1915" (398). Edith Stein made a first arrangement of the whole around 1916 and a second, more than twice as long, around 1918; the second already contained substantially the printed text except for the supplements, and the second half of the second section was assembled "from the most various manuscripts", among them sheets from the war years whose dates reach to January 1917 (XVII, 399). Ludwig Landgrebe typed the whole in 1924/25. Husserl then annotated Landgrebe's typescript until about 1928, and the apparatus distinguishes "three layers of notes" (400). The edition worked his corrections and additions into the text, his outlooks and criticisms into footnotes, loose sheets into supplements, and his marginal expressions of dissatisfaction into the apparatus (XIX). The chapter titles of the third section and the titles of the books are the editor's (XX).

What follows from this for a reader? Not that the book is unreliable, but that a sentence of *Ideas II* has no date until it is given one. The edition makes that possible. At the end of the apparatus a table matches each stretch of printed text, by page and line, to the sheets of Husserl's shorthand manuscripts where these survive.[^3] Where a stretch rests on the manuscript of 1912 or 1915, on the main manuscript of 1913, or on a dated sheet from the convolute "Nature and Spirit" of 1916–17, its date can be stated. Where the table has a dash, no original was found, and the stretch can be dated only by the arrangement that contains it. Reading *Ideas II* for its concept of constitution therefore means reading it twice: once in the order of the book, and once in the order of the sheets.

### 1.2 Where the layers lie

Three facts from the table bear on the question of this study. The first concerns the stepwise ascent of the first section. The paragraph in § 18 that says the real world "is originally constituted in levels", with the manifold of sense-things as the lower layer, the body as the centre of orientation, and causality as the realization that completes the thing (65), rests on a group of sheets that were inserted into the manuscript of 1915 from other contexts and still carry their old signatures. The apparatus says only that these groups "come from other contexts" and were put in later, "probably on the occasion of Stein's second arrangement" (398); their own date is not given. In this form, then, the stepwise ascent is older than Stein's second arrangement, but it cannot be dated more closely, and it was not written for the place it now occupies. We shall see in part 2 that the main manuscript of 1913 sets out the same ascent, so that its age is not in doubt.

The second fact concerns the third section. Its opening, from § 48 to the middle of § 52 (Hua IV, 172–213), rests on the main manuscript of 1913, sheet for sheet, with few gaps. This is where Husserl introduces the personalistic attitude, says that the naturalistic attitude "subordinates itself" to it, and finds himself in a "vicious circle" between the nature presupposed at the start and the nature constituted among persons at the end (184, 210). The same manuscript, in the sheets that the edition prints as supplements I and X, contains a summary of the first two sections written from the standpoint of the third, and a page on "the levels of the constitution of objective actuality" against which Husserl wrote, in Landgrebe's typescript, "Very important!" (417).[^4] Stein had marked the first of these as "dispensable because of the first section" (415). The order of the printed book, in which the stepwise sections stand first and the personal world comes after, is therefore Stein's order and not the order of composition: much of what reads as the destination of the ascent was written before the ascent was elaborated.

The third fact concerns the end of the book. The later pages of the third section, including §§ 61 to 64, are among those for which no manuscript was found: the table has a dash from page 269 to page 302. Two of their sources can be dated indirectly. The doctrine of § 61 on the "underground" of the spirit has a parallel, which the edition prints as the first part of supplement XII, and the apparatus dates that manuscript to "the end of January 1917", written in shorthand "probably in one sitting" (418); a note to the title of § 61, probably the editor's, refers the reader there (276, n.). A second source is older. At pp. 279–80 § 61 reuses a manuscript that the edition prints as supplement XIV and that the apparatus calls "certainly written before 1917" (423); there the person's underground of "passive nature ('my nature')" is "the psychic in the sense of physiological, natural psychology" (388). Husserl's correction of the corresponding sentence of § 61, made in Landgrebe's typescript, will matter in part 4. And the last lines of § 64 rest on a single sheet from a convolute that the apparatus dates to 1912 (399). For the rest of § 64 the date is that of Stein's second arrangement, about 1918, minus whatever Husserl changed afterwards, and the apparatus records what he changed (414). We shall see in part 5 that those changes have a pattern.

### 1.3 The second section and the body

The second section, on the constitution of animal nature, is the part of the book in which the layers are thickest, and its sheets show why. The apparatus says that "above all the second half of the second section, beginning from § 32, is a compilation from the most various manuscripts" (399), and the table bears this out. The paragraph that distinguishes the naturalistic from the personalistic attitude for the first time, § 34, rests from line 22 of p. 139 to p. 143 on two sheets of the pencil manuscript of 1912. The paragraphs on the body as bearer of localized sensations, §§ 36 and 37, with the analysis of the touching and touched hand that later readers have made the most famous pages of the book, rest on sheets of 1912 and on three sheets marked 1e, 1m and 1p, which belong to a group that "carries the early date 1908" (398). Further on, single sheets from the convolute "Nature and Spirit" of November and December 1916, and from a bundle dated "before 1917", have been worked in (399). A quarter of the sources of this section is lost.

Two consequences follow for this study. The first is that the distinction of attitudes, which the received view treats as the turn of the third section, is already in the draft of 1912, in the paragraph Stein placed in the middle of the second; we shall read it in part 3. The second is that the body, which part 2 will find at the lowest level of the ascent, was analysed as a body of sensings as early as 1908, before any of the layered expositions were written. Neither fact settles what the layers mean. Both show that the order in which the book presents its topics is not a record of the order in which Husserl came to them.

### 1.4 Two orders, and a question about them

The history of the text does not by itself answer the question of this study, and it is worth saying why before going further. It would be convenient if the layered ascent were early and the priority of spirit late, or the reverse, so that one could call one of them Husserl's considered view. Neither is the case. The ascent and the priority are both present in the manuscripts of 1912 and 1913; the editor even reports Husserl's sentence of 1912 that nature, body and soul "constitute themselves in mutual relatedness, at one with one another", and reads it as excluding "every psychologistic theory of layers" (XVII).[^5] What the dates show is something else. They show that the layered form of constitution was never simply the order of being, since the texts that set it out say from the start what kind of order it is; and they show that the lower layer came to be described, in a manuscript of January 1917, as a stratum of the constituting person, and was freed from the vocabulary of naturalistic psychology only in the notes of 1924–28. Parts 2 to 5 follow these findings in the order of the sheets.

One may still ask whether the arrangement matters, since Husserl himself read and annotated Stein's text and Landgrebe's copy of it. It matters in two ways. It matters for the reader of 1925, who met the ascent first and the personal world second, as the lecture course of that summer shows (part 6). And it matters for the dating of doctrine: a reader who takes the printed order for the order of thought will see the priority of spirit in § 64 as the conclusion of an ascent that began with matter, and will either praise it as a reversal or dismiss it as an afterthought. Neither description fits the texts. The next part asks what the layers are in the texts of 1912 and 1913, and why Husserl could call their order "the constitutive order" while setting it out *idealiter*.

## 2. The strata of constituted reality, 1912–1915

### 2.1 Four senses of "constitution"

The word that gives *Ideas II* its subtitle is used in the book in more than one sense, and the senses must be separated before the layers can be. I distinguish four. Only the first is defined by Husserl; the numbering and the names of the others are mine. (1) In the most general sense, which *Ideas I* gives in 1913, an object "constitutes itself" in consciousness when a type of object, taken as a guiding clue, prescribes the system of experiences in which an object of that type can be given as one and the same: "every objective region constitutes itself in consciousness" (Hua III/1, 344).[^6] Constitution in this sense is a correlation, not a production. (2) Within that correlation, constitution is also a *realizing* apprehension. A unity already given is apprehended anew as the appearance of a further unity, and so acquires, in a phrase of 1913, "the dignity of mere appearances" of a true thing (306). The sense-thing becomes the appearance of the material thing in this way, and the material thing the bearer of a soul. (3) Constitution is spoken of, thirdly, as an order among regions, in which one region is founded on another: the body on the material thing, the soul on the body, the person on the soul. This is the sense in which *Ideas I* calls material reality the lowest level. (4) From the third section of *Ideas II* onward, finally, the word is used for what the person does with itself: the person constitutes itself through motivation, habit and the sediment of its own acts.

The question of this study can now be put more exactly. It concerns the relation of the second and third senses to the fourth. The received view takes the third sense as the order of the book and reads the second as the means by which it is carried out: realizing apprehensions build the founded regions one on another from below. I wish to show that the texts of 1912 and 1913 already treat the third sense as derived from the second, and the second as an idealization; and that from 1917 the fourth sense displaces the third, so that the founding order is relocated, in two steps, within the person. The list is not closed. A fifth sense, intersubjective constitution, cuts across all four, and we shall meet it at each stage without treating it on its own.

### 2.2 The programme of 1913

*Ideas I* announces the second book in its last chapter, and the announcement already contains the ambiguity that the second book inherits. Husserl takes the region "material thing" as his example of a guiding clue, and he describes its constitution in "stages and layers". Each stage and each layer within a stage, he says, constitutes "a unity of its own" which is "a necessary intermediate link for the full constitution of the thing" (Hua III/1, 352). At the stage of simple perception, related to a single stream of consciousness, there are sensuous schemata and "visual things" of higher and lower order; at the top of this stage stands the substantial-causal thing, "already a reality in the specific sense, but still constitutively bound to an experiencing subject". Then follows a higher stage: "the intersubjectively identical thing, a constitutive unity of higher order", related to an open plurality of subjects in mutual understanding (353). So far the stages are stages of realization, the second sense. Each is the correlate of a wider system of experience, and the lower is taken up into the higher as its appearance.

The next paragraph moves to the third sense, and the move is quiet. The experiencing subject, Husserl continues, is itself constituted as a reality, as human being or animal, and its communities, "although essentially founded in psychic realities, which for their part are founded in physical ones", prove to be new objects of higher order; the same holds for values, practical objects and cultural formations such as "state, law, custom, church" (354). Their constitution "leads back" to that of spatial things and psychic subjects, since they are founded in such realities. And then the sentence that the received view rightly cites: "as the lowest level, material reality finally lies at the basis of all other realities", and to the phenomenology of material nature "an excellent position" therefore belongs (355). The founding order of regions is stated as a consequence of the order of realization.

The same paragraph goes on, and its continuation limits what it has just said. The founded unities, "looked at without prejudice and traced back phenomenologically to their sources", are founded and new; what constitutes itself with them "can never, as the intuition of essences teaches, be reduced to mere sums of other realities" (355). Every type of such reality therefore brings with it "its own constitutive phenomenology". Two claims thus stand in one paragraph of 1913: that matter lies at the basis of everything else, and that nothing founded on it is composed of it. The first is a claim about the order in which regions are founded. The second denies that this order is an order of composition. The programme of the second book, in short, promises a layered ascent and forbids in advance the reading of the layers as parts.

### 2.3 The draft of 1912: mutual constitution

The first draft of the second book was written a few months before *Ideas I* appeared, and its last surviving pages on constitution say something that the programme does not. Husserl has described how the sensations that adumbrate spatial things acquire, step by step, a relation to the body: optical sensations to the eye, which is itself constituted by touch as a movable member of the body. He concludes that "in all perceiving, in all experiencing, the body with its sense organs is involved", and that every experienced thing has a relation to the bodily "already with respect to the most fundamental layers of experience" (Hua V, 124). The body, interwoven with the psychic subject, is "the *subjective object*" to which all other objects stand opposite as surrounding objects. And then the sentence that the editor of *Ideas II* singled out: if we restrict ourselves to the mere data of theoretical experience and exclude all values and practical objects, "it is an important result of our consideration that 'nature' and the body, and in their interweaving with the latter again the soul, constitute themselves in *mutual relatedness*, at one with one another" (124).

The restriction to theoretical experience matters, and it should not be passed over. Husserl does not claim that the person, or the world of values, constitutes itself at one with nature; he claims it of nature, body and soul as objects of theoretical experience. Within that limit, however, the claim is strong. It says that there is no order of constitution among these three. Nature is not first constituted and the body then added, for the most fundamental layers of experience already involve the body; the body is not constituted before the soul, for it is constituted as interwoven with it. The editor draws the conclusion for the whole book: the unity of the constitution of thing, body and soul, their rightly understood "simultaneity", excludes "every psychologistic theory of layers", and it is "stressed still more strongly in the first draft than in the later versions" (XVII).[^7] What remains to be seen is how the layers of the later versions can stand beside it. Part of the answer is in a text of the following year.

### 2.4 The ascent of 1913 as an idealization

The main manuscript of 1913 contains a step-by-step description of the constitution of "the merely material thing, the body, the animate thing or living being, then the personal subject". The edition prints it as the first supplement, because it "at once summarizes and introduces the first two sections" (415). It is the clearest statement in *Ideas II* of the layered ascent, and it states what kind of order the ascent is.

Husserl begins: "If we pursue the constitution of the realities nature and spirit, which are related to one another, then we can distinguish *idealiter* the following layers. Following the constitutive order, we have to begin with the constitution of the mere sphere of things, that of material nature" (305; *Ideas II*, 315). The two sentences pull in different directions. The second calls the ascent "the constitutive order", and so seems to confirm that matter comes first in constitution. The first says that the layers are distinguished *idealiter*, ideally, and the next sentences say what the ideality consists in. At the lowest level stand the sense-things as correlates "of the ego thought of as isolated and not yet realized", that is, of an ego which has not yet been apprehended as a human being with a body and a soul. At this level, "for which no dependence on the body is yet taken into account", a material thing is in causal relation only to material things, and "this proposition remains valid further on, after further realizations have been drawn in, which for us, incidentally, count not as ideal necessities, but only as ideal possibilities" (305). Two pages later the result is stated in the vocabulary of founding: there constitute themselves together "the physical thing as nature, the body as aesthesiological unity, the soul as founded, therefore non-independent reality on the underground of the body" (310).

These sentences must be read exactly, since they are the strongest support in *Ideas II* for the received view. The phrase "only as ideal possibilities" qualifies the further realizations, not the order. What it says is that the higher layers are possible additions to a lower layer that is thinkable without them: a material world without bodies, a body without a soul. That is one-sided founding, and the soul is called "founded, therefore non-independent" in so many words. The received view is right about this series, and I correct here a reading I first gave it. What the ideality concerns is the lowest stage: it is the world of an ego "thought of as isolated", for which even the dependence of things on its own body is not yet counted. And the same page that founds the soul on the body adds: "In this series we do not come upon the personal ego" (310). The personal ego is introduced only with the next paragraph, "with this we turn into the constitution of the personal ego", as the subject that directs its sense organs, moves its eyes and touches the surfaces of things (310). The founding series of 1913 runs from thing to body to soul. It does not run to the person.

Is this to say that the ascent is a mere fiction, a didactic device without bearing on what constitution is? It is not. Each layer is a real layer of sense: the sense-thing is a unity in its own right, and the higher apprehension that makes it the appearance of a material thing really adds something, the "unsensuous" that the higher level of apprehension "inserts" into the sensuous (307). But the lowest stage is reached by an abstraction that the concrete texts undo: the draft of 1912 says that the body is involved "already with respect to the most fundamental layers of experience", and § 18 builds the lowest layer with the body as centre of orientation. Read together, the texts give a one-sided founding among constituted realities, stated for an abstract starting point that excludes the body and does not reach the person. That the abstraction of 1913 and the concrete mutual constitution of 1912 describe the same experience from two sides is my reconstruction; neither text refers to the other.

### 2.5 The blue spectacles

Husserl gives the step from the sense-thing to the true thing an example, and it is worth carrying out, because it shows what kind of layer a realizing apprehension adds. What follows is a descriptive stretch: I re-perform the example to see what Husserl's sentences claim, not to establish what he held.

The body brings a new kind of dependence into the things that were first constituted without regard to it, and these things thereby receive "a new layer of apprehension" and with it "the dignity of mere appearances" of true things. The example: the sense-thing changes its sensuous quality, "its colour for example, if I put on blue spectacles"; its "true" quality, the quality of the true thing which only presents itself here, does not change (306; *Ideas II*, 321). The sensuous quality is "not deception", but the appearance of the true quality that is required under the given circumstances.

Let us carry this out. I look at a white sheet of paper and then put on blue spectacles. The sheet now looks blue. Two descriptions of this are possible, and the example is chosen to separate them. On the first, the sheet has changed: before it was white, now it is blue. This is how the change would be given to an ego that had no apprehension of its own body as the organ of its seeing, an ego "thought of as isolated and not yet realized". On the second, the sheet has not changed; I see it differently, because of the spectacles in front of my eyes. This second description is what the "new layer of apprehension" consists in. It takes the colour I see as the appearance of a colour the sheet has, relative to circumstances that include my body and what is placed before it. Nothing in the visual field distinguishes the two descriptions. What distinguishes them is the system of further experiences to which each points: on the second, taking the spectacles off will bring the white back.

Now vary the example. Suppose I wear the spectacles for years and forget them. Husserl foresees this case: normality and anomaly "are interchangeable", since the characters of the usual and the unusual can exchange their roles; "the unusual can become the usual" (306). The blue world becomes the normal one, and the white sheet seen without spectacles becomes the anomaly. What persists through this exchange is not a privileged set of circumstances but "an identical", which appears now so and now so according to the circumstances (306). Suppose next that the spectacles are replaced by an illness of the eye. Husserl names this case too: health and illness of the body bring the whole manifold of appearances of a sense-thing into dependence on the body, which itself appears as a sense-thing. Finally, try the variant that breaks the example. Suppose that there is no body whose states could be the circumstances of the seeing: no eyes to cover, no organs to fall ill. Then the change of colour can only be a change of the thing, and the distinction between sensuous and true quality cannot be drawn. The new layer of apprehension has nothing to attach to.

What does the variation show? It shows, first, that the layer which makes a sense-thing into the appearance of a true thing is a layer of the *body's* involvement, not a layer of matter added to a layer of sense. The true colour, Husserl says a page later, is "no sensuous colour" but an "unsensuous unity", an index for a relativism of dependences of a higher level (307). It shows, secondly, that the more determinate apprehension is not built on the less determinate one as a storey on a storey. It reinterprets it: the colour I saw is still there, but it now counts as appearance. Husserl's word for this, in a text we shall meet in part 5, is revaluation. And it shows, thirdly, why the ascent can be called both "the constitutive order" and an idealization. It is an order, because the true thing cannot be apprehended without the sense-thing it reinterprets. And its starting point is ideal, because the ego who has the sense-thing without the true thing, who sees the paper turn blue, is an abstraction: the concrete ego who puts on spectacles already has a body. The variation does not show that the order is reversible; the true thing still presupposes the sense-thing it reinterprets.

### 2.6 The ascent written out: § 18 and its sheets

The first section of the printed book carries out this ascent at length, and the paragraph most often cited for it states it in the terms of 1913. "Originally the real world constitutes itself in levels, in such a way that the manifold of sense-things (of full schemata) builds itself up as the lower layer in the unity of the spatial form" (65; *Ideas II*, 70). The sense-things constitute themselves "in the subjective manner of 'orientation'", with a distinguished sense-thing, the body, as constant bearer of the centre of orientation. Realization then completes itself when the sense-things become states of real things, and the system of real properties constitutes itself under the title "causality" (65).

The received view reads this paragraph as stating that nature is the lowest layer. It says something narrower, and the next paragraph says it plainly. What gives all things "the most original psychophysical conditionality" is the constitution of the lower layer, and the sense-things are what they are only "as unities 'in' a manifold of perceptions and kinaesthetic constellations of subjectivity" (66). The lowest layer of the real world is therefore not physical nature. It is the layer of sense-things, and that layer is constituted in correlation with the body's movements; the body is in it from the start, as the sentence of 1912 required. Physical nature in the sense of the natural sciences is a late product of the ascent, the result of intersubjective realization and of theory. The paragraph that seems to put matter at the bottom puts there the sense-thing in its relation to the moving body.

A word on the sheets, since the date of this paragraph was left open in part 1. The stretch rests on sheets inserted into the manuscript of 1915 from other contexts (398). Whatever their date, the doctrine they carry is the doctrine of the supplement of 1913: a stepwise realization, starting from the sense-thing of a subject considered alone, with the body as centre of orientation already at the lowest level. Nothing in them asserts the founding of the person on matter. The first section of the printed book opens, indeed, by restricting its topic in a way that rules such an assertion out there: it treats "nature" as the correlate of the theoretical attitude of natural science, and the title of its first paragraph announces the "exclusion of the predicates of significance", which the following paragraphs take to include predicates of value and use (§§ 1–2, 11). Husserl's marginal note in Landgrebe's typescript calls the first chapter a set of "badly compiled and badly elaborated manuscripts" and says that it "must be completely reworked" (apparatus, note to p. 1). He did not say that its doctrine was wrong.

### 2.7 What the layers of 1912–1915 are

The texts of 1912 and 1913 thus agree on three points, and the first section of the printed book does not contradict them. First, the layers of constitution are layers of realizing apprehension, the second sense of the word: each takes what the previous one gave as the appearance of something more determinate. Secondly, among the realities so constituted there is a one-sided founding: the soul is "founded, therefore non-independent", on the body, and the body is a material thing among material things (310). Thirdly, this founding series is set out from an abstract starting point, an ego "thought of as isolated" for which even its body does not yet count, and it stops before the person. Concretely, in 1912, nature, body and soul constitute themselves "in mutual relatedness, at one with one another", and the body is involved in the most fundamental layers of experience.

The founding order of regions, the third sense, is therefore real in these texts, but it is narrower than the received view makes it. It holds between thing, body and soul, as realities constituted for an abstractly isolated ego; it is not said of the person. *Ideas I* extends it to communities and cultural formations in § 152, and limits it in the same breath by denying that any founded reality is a sum. Matter is "the lowest level" of realities, not the lowest layer of the experience in which realities are constituted.

A tension remains, and it should be left standing. Husserl does call the ascent "the constitutive order", and he does not say, in 1913, whether its abstract starting point limits its scope or merely simplifies its exposition. A reader who takes the first will find a founding of the soul on the body and no founding of the person, and will have to explain why *Ideas I* extends the founding to communities of persons. A reader who takes the second will find a founding of the person on matter, as Heidegger did, and will have to explain why the series of 1913 stops before the person. The texts of 1912 and 1913 do not settle this. What settles the place of the ascent is what happens when the abstraction is lifted, and the ego is no longer thought of as alone but as a person among persons. That is the subject of the main manuscript of 1913, and of the next part.

## 3. The reversal of the attitudes, 1913

### 3.1 The "plus" of 1912

Before the manuscript of 1913 there is a page of the draft of 1912 that already asks the question of this part, and Stein placed it in the second section, as § 34, under the title "Necessity of distinguishing between the naturalistic and the personalistic attitude". Husserl has been describing the psychic subject as the unity constituted through its psychophysical and "idiopsychic" dependences: on the body, and on its own past. Then he stops. "But the following is striking: the apprehension in which the human being is given to us in the human body, in which he is given as a personality that lives, acts, suffers, and is conscious to us as a real personality behaving now so and now so under the circumstances of its personal life, seems to contain a *plus* which does not present itself as a mere complex of constitutive moments of apprehension of the type we have described" (140).

The example that follows shows what the plus is. I find myself in an environment of things, and some of these things affect me through my body: "my hand has been struck by the stick, therefore I feel touch, pressure, slight pain". But I also find myself determined by things in quite another way, "without such psychophysical dependences appearing to play a role". This thing here draws my glance to itself; I choose the cloth for the sake of its beautiful colour and soft texture; the noise of the street irritates me, and I decide to close the window. "In my theoretical, emotional and practical behaviour … I feel myself conditioned by things; but that evidently does not mean psychophysically conditioned" (141). The same holds of others, and still more of my dependence on persons, on communities and institutions, "state, custom, law, church". The human being as a real personality is "a being that maintains itself in its commerce with the things of its thingly and the persons of its personal surrounding world, and in that maintains its individuality" (141).

Three features of this page anticipate the third section. The plus of the person is not a further layer of apprehension of "the type we have described"; it is a different kind of dependence, a dependence on the sense of things and not on their causal action. The person is defined not by what it is made of but by what it maintains: its individuality, through its commerce with things and persons. And the word "conditioned" is split into two senses, psychophysical conditioning and being conditioned by things as they are meant, which the third section will call causality and motivation. The page of 1912 does not itself name two attitudes, and the title of § 34 belongs to the later arrangements, from which the division into paragraphs comes (XX); but the page already knows that the ascent through realizing apprehensions does not reach the person.

It knew it a year earlier still. In "Philosophy as Rigorous Science", published in the first volume of *Logos* (1910/11), Husserl had said of the "empirical unities" human being, soul, personality and character that they are "of a kind different in principle from the thing-like objects of nature, which are by their essence givennesses through adumbrating appearances". And he had used the vocabulary of founding to say so: "Only the founding substrate 'human body', but not the human being himself, is a unity of thing-like appearance, and still less personality, character and so on" (Hua XXV, 38).[^11] The body is called founding here, and the human being is denied the mode of givenness of the founding layer in the same sentence. The founding relation and the difference in kind are present together from the start, which is the configuration that *Ideas I* § 152 states and that the received view divides.

### 3.2 The attitude in which we always are

The main manuscript of 1913 begins the constitution of the spiritual world by changing the attitude in which the first two sections were written, and it says so at once. The naturalistic attitude, in which the preceding analyses moved, has "the 'objective' physical total nature" before it, with bodies, sensibilities and psychic life scattered in it "as founding" and founded. Human beings and animals are then zoological objects, and Husserl adds a remark that is easy to read past: one could call them "physio-psychic", and this "inversion of the usual expression 'psychophysical' indicates quite aptly the order of founding" (183; *Ideas II*, 193). The order of founding is named here for the first time in the third section, and it is named as the order that belongs to one attitude. In that attitude the physical comes first and the psychic is founded on it, and the word for this is to be read from left to right: physio-psychic.

The other attitude is introduced by description, not by definition. It is "the *personalistic attitude*, in which we always are when we live with one another, speak to one another, shake hands in greeting, are related to one another in love and aversion, in disposition and deed, in speech and reply", and in which we are also when we regard the things around us "as our surroundings and not, as in natural science, as 'objective' nature" (183). Husserl insists on two features. The attitude is "a thoroughly natural one and not an artificial one", which would have to be won and kept by special means; and even the zoologist lives in it as a person, though as a researcher he sees only nature. "He has habitual blinkers" (183). The sentence that closes the description gives the personalistic attitude its formula: "To live as a person is to posit oneself as a person, to find oneself, and to bring oneself, into conscious relations to a 'surrounding world'" (183).

What this description does to the order of the first two sections is said on the next page, and the sentence is the hinge of the book. "On closer consideration it will even turn out that we have here not two attitudes of equal rank and equal order, or two apperceptions of fully equal right that interpenetrate one another, but that the naturalistic attitude subordinates itself to the personalistic, and gains a certain independence through an abstraction, or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal ego, thereby at the same time wrongly absolutizing its world, nature" (184). Three claims are folded into the sentence, and they should be unfolded one at a time. First, the two attitudes are not coordinate. Secondly, the naturalistic one is subordinate, and its independence is gained, not original. Thirdly, the way it gains independence is an abstraction, which Husserl at once corrects into a self-forgetfulness of the person; and what it gains, in absolutizing nature, it gains "wrongly".

### 3.3 Two subordinations

Read against part 2, the sentence seems to reverse everything. The ascent of the first sections began with nature and reached the person last; now the attitude in which nature comes first is subordinate to the attitude in which persons are given. But the next paragraph forbids a simple reversal, and it is there that the text of 1913 is most careful. "Naturalistically considered", Husserl writes, "all consciousness, all experiencing in general, is bodily founded", and so is "the total stock of what intentionally constitutes the world with all its properties in persons". The person's apprehensions of things, its valuings and its social acts are, in this consideration, "natural facts", and they demand a natural-scientific treatment that leads in the end into the domain of physical nature. "From the standpoint of nature everything personal is something subordinate" (185).

There are therefore two subordinations in two consecutive paragraphs, and they do not cancel each other. I number them. (1) Within the naturalistic attitude, the personal is subordinate to nature: it is founded in bodies, and bodies in physical things. This is the physio-psychic order of founding, and Husserl does not deny that it holds within its attitude. (2) As between the attitudes, the naturalistic is subordinate to the personalistic: it is one of the things a person can do, an apprehension that persons perform on their world when they forget themselves. The founding order of regions, the third sense of constitution in part 2, is subordinate in the first way. Its validity is intact, but its scope is limited to an attitude, and that attitude is not the one in which the world is first given.

One may ask whether this is not a mere duplication: whether each attitude simply declares itself first, so that the question of what is constituted on what receives two answers that do not meet. Husserl raises the question himself, and his answer is not symmetrical. The personalistic attitude is "natural" and the naturalistic one is reached by "abstraction"; the first is the attitude "in which we always are", the second the attitude a person adopts. The asymmetry does not rest on a preference. It rests on the description: the naturalist is a person who, as researcher, has acquired habitual blinkers, but the person is not a naturalist who has forgotten nature. Nothing in the naturalistic attitude can account for the fact that it is adopted, while the personalistic attitude contains the possibility of adopting the other. This is how I read the asymmetry; Husserl states it, in this place, only as a result still to be clarified ("let us attempt to gain clarity here", 184).

### 3.4 The vicious circle

The clarification comes in § 53, which also rests on the manuscript of 1913, and it begins by naming a difficulty. "It is now time for us to reflect. We feel here an uneasy difficulty, a certain tension between the nature that stood at the beginning and the nature that has now grown up for us in the context of community" (208). The first sentences give the result in advance: "This naturalistically considered world is, after all, not the world. Rather: the world is pregiven as the everyday world, and within it there grows up for the human being the theoretical interest and the sciences related to the world", natural science among them, under the ideal of truths in themselves (208; *Ideas II*, 219). The difficulty is then stated in its sharpest form. "We fall here, it seems, into a vicious circle. For if at the beginning we posited nature pure and simple, as every natural scientist and everyone naturalistically minded does, and took human beings as realities that have a *plus* over their physical corporeality, then the persons were subordinate natural objects, components of nature. But if we pursued the essence of personality, then nature presented itself as something constituting itself in the intersubjective association of persons, and thus presupposing it" (210).

The circle is the one this study began from, stated by Husserl in 1913. He does not resolve it by choosing one of its sides. He resolves it by asking what a change of attitude is: "Change of attitude means nothing other than thematic transition from one direction of apprehension to another, to which correlatively different objectivities correspond" (210). The question becomes whether the two directions of apprehension are of "fundamentally different phenomenological type", so that the objects of each belong to different "regions"; and Husserl recalls cases in which fundamentally different worlds are nevertheless related by relations of sense, as the world of the reduced consciousness is related to the world of transcendent unities constituted in it (210–11). He then restates the naturalistic attitude in a sentence that should be set beside § 152 of *Ideas I*: "What is experienced here at the lowest level is material (physical) nature, and founded in it is the experience of bodily and psychic being" (211). "At the lowest level" now qualifies what is experienced in an attitude, not what lies at the basis of all reality.

The circle is not vicious, on this account, because its two sides are not two claims about one order. The first side states the order of founding within the naturalistic attitude. The second states the place of that attitude within the personal world, where nature is a constituted unity: the identical correlate of the harmonious experience of all the members of a community, "real or still possibly entering into it" (209–10). The nature that stood at the beginning is the same nature, but apprehended in an attitude that does not know where it stands.

### 3.5 The levels of constitution in 1913

The supplement that Husserl marked "Very important!" shows how the manuscript of 1913 holds the two sides together, and it shows that the ascent of part 2 survives the reversal. It begins with the ascent. "In a marvellous way the levels of the constitution of actuality build themselves on one another: the single ego, finding only itself absolutely, constitutes in sequences of levels of appearances its *outer* world, a world of appearance transcendent to it but, of course, relative to it" (324; *Ideas II*, 336). Through the "comprehension" of the bodies appearing to it, this ego grasps other egos as units that manifest themselves absolutely, finds itself in an association of persons and comes to an understanding with them; and "at one with this" there constitute themselves, on the one side, objective physical nature as an identical unity, and on the other the social personalities of higher level, related to the common surrounding world (324).

So far the levels are those of part 2, with one addition that changes their sense: objective nature constitutes itself together with the community of persons and not before it. Then comes the step that § 49 announced. "With this, however, there is interwoven yet another, differently constituted world." Persons who find other persons before them can also carry out "those changes of apprehension which we designate as the naturalistic attitude". They can take the persons grasped in the appearing bodies as one with those bodies and apprehend these unities naturalistically. What is then given are no longer the units that manifest themselves absolutely, which we call persons, but "in a certain way apperceptive transformations of them", which have themselves taken on the character of units of appearance (325). The naturalistic world is a transformation of the personal one, carried out by persons.

The supplement then gives the difference between the two worlds a name that does not depend on any order of founding. "The realm of nature is the realm of the 'phenomenal', that is, here: of the real units constituting themselves in or by means of 'presentation'"; "the realm of spirit is the realm of the realities given through absolute manifestation (self-manifestation and manifestation through comprehension)", the realities that have behind them only the pure ego as their irreal, absolute bearer (325). The two realms differ in their mode of givenness. Nature is given in presentation (*Darstellung*), as one thing adumbrated in many appearances; spirit is given in manifestation (*Bekundung*), as a life that shows itself as what it is, in itself or through another's comprehension. Husserl still speaks of "founded unity" in the case of the human being, who is given in the personalistic attitude as the unity of body and the expression of spirit, since the spirit is comprehended "on the basis of the perception of the body" (325). But this founding is the founding of expression on what expresses it, not of a higher layer of being on a lower.

### 3.6 The solipsistic world inside the personal one

One more passage of 1913 shows where the lowest layer stands at this date, and it is needed for the comparison of part 4. § 52, on "subjective manifolds of appearance and objective things", rests on the main manuscript (201–02). It considers the appearances of the things of intersubjectively constituted nature. Each subject has its own appearances, and no two subjects can have the same appearances at the same moment of objective time; "all the sensuous schemata, adumbrations, sensuous stuffs of which we spoke in connexion with the constitution of things" belong here, and "they are *specifically* 'subjective'" (201).

Husserl then draws a distinction that settles the place of these layers in 1913. One might go on to say that the same holds of the apprehending characters and of the whole stream of consciousness. "However, the essential difference is not to be overlooked. In the stream of subjective lived experiences the subject manifests itself as real personality; the lived experiences are at the same time its states. By contrast, in the sensuous schemata, in the subjective appearances of things, it is not the subject that manifests itself, but its surrounding things" (202). The appearances are not states of the things either; but that they are not states of the subject is clear from the fact that they are transcendent to its real states, its lived experiences. And the passage ends with a sentence that joins the ascent of part 2 to the personal world: the thingly surrounding world of the person, as it is intuited, "coincides with the world as it appeared to the solipsistic subject: things which, appearing in manifolds of adumbrations, group themselves in space around a central here" (202).

Two results follow, and the second is the more important for what comes next. First, the world of the stepwise ascent is not a different world from the personal one. It is the person's own surrounding world, described as it would appear if the person were alone. This confirms, in Husserl's words of 1913, the reading of part 2: the ascent is the personal world under an abstraction of isolation. Secondly, § 52 divides what the lowest layer of the ascent contained, and it divides it in a definite way. The schemata and adumbrations, taken as appearances of things, stand on the side of the constituted: in them the things manifest themselves. The lived experiences in which they appear stand on the side of the subject, and in them the subject manifests itself "as real personality". What § 52 does not do is divide the subject's own stream into levels. In 1913 the person manifests itself in its lived experiences as one real personality; it is not yet described as a higher, free life resting on a lower, passive one.

### 3.7 Two abstractions

What has the manuscript of 1913 done to the layers of part 2? It has not removed them. The single ego still constitutes its outer world "in sequences of levels of appearances", and the supplement on these levels follows the ascent from sense-thing to objective nature. What it has done is to place the ascent and to place the founding order, and to place them differently.

Two abstractions are at work in the texts of 1913, and the study needs to keep them apart; the distinction is mine, not Husserl's. (1) The first is the abstraction of isolation. The ego is "thought of as isolated" (305), "finding only itself" (324), and the levels of apprehension are described as they would be for such an ego. This abstraction produces the stepwise ascent, with its one-sided founding of the soul on the body, a series that stops before the person (310); it is lifted step by step within the ascent itself, when comprehension brings in the others and objective nature is constituted "at one with" the community. (2) The second is the abstraction of self-forgetfulness. The person, already among persons, apprehends itself and others as natural objects and so absolutizes nature (184). This abstraction extends the founding to the person: it produces the physio-psychic order in which "everything personal is something subordinate" (185), and it is not lifted within its attitude; it can only be seen through from the other attitude, as the "apperceptive transformation" of a personal world. The received view, I suggest, fuses the two. It reads the founding of the soul on the body, stated for an ego isolated by abstraction, as a founding of the person, which belongs to an abstraction of the second kind. Husserl kept them apart in 1913, though he did not name the difference.

The tension of part 2 thus becomes sharper rather than disappearing. The ascent is "the constitutive order" (305), and it is written for an ego that is isolated by abstraction; the founding order is the order of an attitude that subordinates itself to the personal one by self-forgetfulness. Neither of the two is the order in which the person itself is constituted. In 1913 the person appears at the top of the ascent and as the bearer of the attitudes, but not as something with layers of its own. It lives in its surrounding world under the law of motivation, and nature is constituted in its community. Where, then, is the lowest layer once nature is relative to persons? The texts of 1913 leave it with the single ego and its sense-things, below the person. The texts of 1917 stratify the person itself. That is the subject of the next part.

## 4. The underground of spirit, 1917

### 4.1 Where the lowest layer goes

By the end of the manuscript of 1913 the order of constitution has been answered twice. Within the naturalistic attitude, nature founds the body, the body the soul and the soul the personal; within the personal world, nature is a unity constituted in the community of persons, and the naturalistic attitude is one of the apprehensions persons perform. One thing has not been answered. The stepwise ascent begins with an ego "thought of as isolated" and its sense-things, and the person appears only at its top. If nature is relative to persons, what becomes of the layers below the person: the sensations, the sense-things, the kinaesthetic constellations of the body? Do they belong to the constituted world that persons find before them, or to the persons themselves?

The printed book answers in § 61, "The spiritual ego and its underground", and its answer is the subject of this part. Its date is not simple. The table records no manuscript for these pages. A note to the title of § 61, probably the editor's, refers the reader to the first part of supplement XII, whose manuscript the apparatus dates to "the end of January 1917", written in shorthand, "probably in one sitting", as part of a group of manuscripts "from the war years up to 1917" which Stein already had and partly used, and which Landgrebe copied under the title "Supplements to the third section of *Ideas II*" (276, n.; 418).[^8] Landgrebe's copy, the apparatus adds, received from Husserl "numerous, often very extensive" additions, so that the printed supplement also carries later layers. But § 61 has an older source as well: at pp. 279–80 it reuses supplement XIV, "certainly written before 1917" (423). And the notion of an underground is older still. The page of 1912 that § 34 prints already considers that the psychic and the personal ego "are the same in their underground", one state of consciousness under two different apperceptions (141–42). The underground, then, is not the discovery of 1917. What can be dated to January 1917, by the supplement, is a particular description of it: as a stratified, partly sedimented life of the person in which nature is constituted. I treat § 61 as Stein's working of older and newer manuscripts into the book around 1918, and I date each claim below by the source that carries it.

### 4.2 Two levels of subjectivity

§ 61 begins from the understanding of other persons and of their development. To understand how a human being became what he is, I must describe "from level to level" the surrounding world in which he grew up and how its things and people motivated him. In doing so I come upon "a factual stock that is unintelligible in itself": this child has an original delight in tones, that one not; one tends to anger, the other to patience. Natural causality enters too: a man becomes a cripple after a fall, and "certain groups of motivation drop away" from then on. Husserl adds at once that the real-causal analysis of these consequences "is of no interest here"; medical knowledge can serve to insert the right psychic effects into the understanding of a subject's motivations, and "the physical serves there as an indication for what is to be inserted" (276). Nature comes into the understanding of a person as an indication of what is to be understood, not as a layer on which the person stands.

The next sentence introduces the layers, and they are now layers of the person. "If we take the personal ego in its nexus of development, we find two levels, which may perhaps separate (for example, the lower level as 'pure' animality), a double 'subjectivity'" (276; *Ideas II*, 289). The higher is "the specifically spiritual one, the layer of the *intellectus agens*, of the free ego as ego of the free acts", among them all properly rational acts, positively or negatively rational. To it also belongs the unfree ego, unfree in the sense in which this holds of an actual ego: "I let myself be dragged down by sensibility". And this specifically spiritual ego "finds itself dependent on a dark underground of dispositions of character, original and hidden dispositions, and on the other hand dependent on nature" (276).

Husserl names the distinction he has arrived at: "We come here again to the old distinction, so early forced upon us, between reason and sensibility" (276). Sensibility too has its rules, rules of agreement and disagreement, so that it is "a layer of hidden reason", "at first in any case as far as the constitution of nature reaches". And then the sentence on which this part turns: "In the sensuous sphere, in the sphere of the most broadly conceived underground, we have associations, perseverations, determining tendencies and so on. This 'makes' constitution of nature, but it also goes further, after nature is there for the spirits" (277). Through all the life of the spirit there runs the "blind" efficacy of associations, drives, feelings, tendencies emerging in the dark, which determine the further course of consciousness by "blind" rules.

What has happened to the layers? In the series of 1913 the lowest stage was the sense-thing of an isolated ego, and above it stood the material thing, the body and the soul; the person stood outside the series. Now the person itself has two levels, and the lower, sensibility with its associations and tendencies, "makes" the constitution of nature. The page that follows says so. The world of things in which the spirits live is "an objective world constituted from the subjective surrounding worlds", and as an intersubjective rule of possible appearances it "points back to a lower level of all spiritual existence". "Every spirit has a 'nature-side'. That is precisely the underground of subjectivity, its having of sensations, its having of reproductions of sensations, its associations, its formation of apperceptions, and indeed of the lowest ones, those constituting unities of experience" (279). And finally: "The lower level is the site of the constitution of a world of appearances, or of objects of appearance, the world of the mechanical, of dead lawfulness: all mere things found" (279). A caution on date: the stretch of § 61 reused from the pre-1917 supplement XIV begins at line 32 of this page, and the file does not show whether these sentences fall before or after that line. Their wording does not occur in supplement XIV, which says of the underground only that it is "my nature" and that position-takings too "have a nature-side" (388); but the edition says that the reuse was "not quite verbatim". I treat them as of Stein's arrangement, in agreement with the supplement of January 1917.

The contrast with 1913 must be stated exactly, and I correct here a formulation I first gave it. It is not that a layer has changed sides. In § 52 the noematic schemata were on the side of the things, but the lived experiences, the having of sensations among them, were already the subject's (202). What is new in § 61 is that the subject's own life is divided into levels, and that the lower level, "its having of sensations, its associations, its formation of apperceptions", is described as the "underground of subjectivity", its "nature-side", and the site where the world of appearances is constituted (279). I read this as a transposition, and the word is mine: the stratification that the series of 1913 drew among constituted realities is drawn, in § 61, within the constituting life. There are two levels, a lower and a higher, and the lower "makes" constitution while the higher acts freely. The lowest of them is no longer material nature. Nature is what the lowest layer constitutes. The "nature-side" of the spirit is not a piece of physical nature attached to the spirit; it is the side of the spirit's own life on which the world of nature is constituted, and which, apprehended naturalistically, appears as the spirit's dependence on nature.

### 4.3 Sediment and teleology: the supplement of January 1917

The supplement to which § 61 refers carries the transposition further, and in a direction that the received view cannot accommodate. Its first section is titled "Distinction of primal sensibility and *intellectus agens*". It begins with the spiritual sphere as the sphere of "ego-subjectivities", of cogitations radiating from the ego as activities, which constitute "personal unities" in contrast with real, natural, spatial unities. This sphere has, notwithstanding its own coherence, "its underground in the lowly 'psychic'", and it has besides "its immanent teleology in the lawful transformations of the higher into the lower, of the spiritually active into passivities, into a secondary sensibility, which creates pre-givennesses for future actions of the ego and at the same time prescribed paths of reproduction properly so called, of reconversion into activities" (332).

The direction of the layers has changed. In the ascent of 1912 and 1913 the higher was built on the lower; here the higher turns into the lower. What the ego does actively does not vanish when the act is over. It sinks, and becomes a "secondary sensibility" that is there for the ego as something pregiven, as a tendency, an expectation or a familiar path, and that can be reconverted into activity. The supplement draws the consequence for all personal intentionality: "All personal intentionality points to activity and has its origin in activities. For it is either originally arising, and then active, intentionality, or it is *sediment* of activities", significant as such and pointing back in its sense to the active or constitutive connexions, "and this in many levels built on one another" (333). The levels are still built on one another. But they are now built by the person, in the course of its life, and the lower among them can be the deposit of the higher.

The supplement does not make all sensibility a sediment, and the limit must be stated with the same care as the claim. "In sensibility we distinguish *primal sensibility*, which contains nothing of sediments of reason, and secondary sensibility, which has grown out of the production of reason. Accordingly we also distinguish reason into *original reason*, *intellectus agens*, and reason sunk down into sensibility" (334). Primal sensibility, the supplement says a page later, is the sensuous data, colour-data in their visual field, the sensuous feelings founded in them, the drives; it "does not arise from immanent grounds, from psychic tendencies, it is simply there, it occurs". "The ego always has a possession. Primal sensibility is its primal possession" (334–35). There is, then, a layer that is not a sediment of the person's activity and that the person presupposes "as affection, as stimulus". The founding from below has not disappeared. What has changed is what lies at the bottom: not material nature, but the sensuous data and drives of a subject, which are the material on which the constitution of nature works.

The supplement does not, however, drop the vocabulary of nature, and its limits must be stated too. It says that the factual laws of sensuous impressions "are to be had only as psychophysical laws", and that their order is obtained by "reinterpretation" after nature has been constituted (337). It says that "the whole working of nature, the 'mechanism of nature', is presupposed" for the motivations of the ego (338; the file prints *Naturtriebe*, which I read as *Naturgetriebe*). And it says that the empirical ego "is the ego of body-soul nature"; but it adds at once: "it is not itself a body-soul unity, but lives in it", it is "the ego that is related back to the lived experiences of the sensuous sphere as its possession" (339). The sensuous sphere is called nature in two senses on these pages: as what natural science would describe, and as the passive possession of the ego. In January 1917 Husserl does not choose between them. The choice comes later, and part 4.6 shows where.

### 4.4 The evening drink

Husserl's example for the secondary sensibility is a modest one, and it can again be re-performed. To the lawfulness of the underground there correspond, he writes, "habitual ways of behaving of the subject, acquired peculiarities (for example, the habit of drinking one's glass in the evening)" (277). What follows is a descriptive stretch, under the same reserve as the spectacles in part 2.

Suppose that one evening, after work, I decide to have a glass of wine. I weigh it, perhaps: I am tired, there is wine open, it will do me good. The drinking is an act of the free ego in the sense of § 60, an act motivated, in the sense of § 56, by reasons I could state. A year later I find myself reaching for the glass every evening at the same hour. I no longer decide. The evening, the hour, the end of work present the glass to me as what comes next, and if I do not drink I notice the absence. This is what the supplement calls a transformation of the higher into the lower. The decision has sunk into a tendency. The tendency belongs to my sensibility in the broad sense of § 61: it acts "blindly", without my taking a position, and it determines the course of my evening as associations and perseverations do. Yet it is not primal sensibility. It has a history, and it points back to the decision it came from; I can still recall the reasons, take them up again, and decide anew, which is the "reconversion into activity" of which the supplement speaks.

Vary the example. Suppose the habit was never decided: I drink in the evening because my parents did, and I took it over without ever weighing it. The tendency still points back to activities, but to the activities of others, received in the community; the sediment is not mine alone. This is where the supplement's remark that the regulation of sensibility "is an intersubjective one" would have to be followed up (336). Suppose, next, that what draws me to the glass is thirst after a hot day. The pull is then not a sediment of any activity at all. It is a drive, part of primal sensibility, simply there. This is the breaking variant: it shows that the lower layer of the person is not wholly made of sunk activity, and that the transposition does not abolish a lowest given. Suppose, finally, that an illness changes what wine tastes like to me. Then nature enters, as in the case of the fall in § 61, and a "group of motivations drops away"; but it enters as the index of a change in my sensibility, which I must insert into the understanding of my evenings, and not as the floor on which my evenings rest.

The example shows what the transposition amounts to. In the ascent of 1913 a higher layer reinterpreted the lower: the true colour made the seen colour into appearance. In the person of 1917 a higher layer can also produce a lower: the decision becomes the habit. The relation between levels has become temporal and two-directional, and only one of its directions, the upward one, can be described as founding.

### 4.5 The ego from life

Two further texts show what kind of subject the person of 1917 is. The supplement of January 1917 has a third section, "Development of the ego: action of the ego and affection of the ego". And § 58 of the printed book treats "the constitution of the personal ego before reflection". Its page 252 rests, from line 27, on a sheet from the convolute "Nature and Spirit" of 1917 (table; 399); but the sentences quoted below stand at lines 12 to 23, as the apparatus shows by listing Husserl's additions to them: "associative and active", "as active" and "associative" were added in Landgrebe's typescript, after 1924 (413). They are therefore Stein's text with words of the last layer, and I cite them as such.

The supplement asks whether what goes out from the ego as affection, the motivating pull that draws the ego before it yields, is "no longer nature", and it answers with a hedge: "No, affection probably belongs after all to the sphere of nature and is the means of the connexion of ego and nature" (338). Every action and affection of the ego stands under the law of association, takes its place in time and acts afterwards as an affecting force. "The ego lives constantly in the medium of its 'history'; all its earlier vitalities have sunk down and act on, in tendencies, in ideas that occur to it, in transformations or assimilations of earlier vitalities." The example is a cigar: a sensuous drive to smoke stirs, "I reach for the cigar and light it, while my attention, my activities of the ego, even my conscious being-affected, are quite elsewhere". The conclusion is put in a figure that the supplement keeps short: "even every free act has its comet's tail of nature; but it itself has not become out of nature … but has become ego; ego and nature are contrasts, and every act has its nature-side too, namely its natural underground" (338).

"Nature" in these sentences is not only physical nature. It is also the passive sphere of the subject: association, affection, the sinking of acts into tendencies. That is the transposition of this part in Husserl's own words, and it explains why § 61 can say both that the spirit depends on nature and that its underground "makes" the constitution of nature. The word "nature" is being carried across from the constituted world into the constituting life, as the name of its passive side; but in 1917 it still carries its first sense with it, as the hedge "probably after all" shows.

§ 58 draws the consequence for the person's knowledge of itself. "Reflecting, I always find myself already as personal ego. But originally it constitutes itself in the genesis that pervades the flow of lived experience" (252). The question is whether the personal ego constitutes itself "on the basis of reflections of the ego", of pure self-perception and self-experience, and the answer moves away from reflection. "Originally I am not really a unity of associative and active experience", where the words "associative and active" are Husserl's addition after 1924. "I am the subject of my life, and living, the subject develops; it does not primarily experience itself, but constitutes objects of nature, things of value, tools and so on." "The ego is originally not from experience, … but from life (it is what it is, not for the ego, but is itself the ego)." The ego can be more than the ego as apperceptive unity; it can have hidden capacities not yet objectified. "Someone does not 'know' himself, does not 'know' what he is; he learns to know himself" (252).

These sentences will matter in part 6, where Heidegger objects that the person of *Ideas II* is reached only by reflection. For the present part they show the fourth sense of constitution at work. The person constitutes itself, but not as a thing constitutes itself in the apprehensions of a subject: it constitutes itself by living, by acting on things, and by the sinking of its acts into a history it does not survey. Its layers are produced in this way, and so is its knowledge of itself, which comes late and is never complete.

### 4.6 The hardest case: dependence on nature

The strongest objection to this reading comes from § 61 itself, and from § 62, which follows it. § 61 says plainly that the spiritual ego is "dependent on a dark underground" and, "on the other hand, dependent on nature" (276). And § 62, which opens the chapter that the editor titled "The ontological priority of the spiritual world over the naturalistic", turns at once to this dependence. When we apprehend the underground of the spirit as its "nature-side", "we come to a point where the two attitudes that we separated, the naturalistic and the personalistic, … and correlatively the two kinds of reality, nature and spirit, enter into relation with one another" (281). The flow of the stream of experience, Husserl writes, stands under a law "that has its index in the reality soul, in its dependence on the natural body". "The spirit in its spiritual acts is dependent on the soul, … and so the spiritual ego depends on the soul and the soul on the body: thus it is conditioned by nature" (281).

Is this not the founding order of the first sections, restored inside the person? If the spirit depends on the soul and the soul on the body, the person stands on nature after all, and the transposition is only a change of vocabulary. The objection is right on one point. Husserl does not withdraw the dependence, and he does not say that the person is free of nature. The transposition does not dissolve the conditionality that the naturalistic attitude describes; § 62 calls it "a different cut through absolute consciousness" (281), and the cut is real.

It does not follow, however, that the founding order of regions has returned. The same paragraph of § 62 limits the dependence in the sentence that concludes it: the spirit "is conditioned by nature; but it does not therefore stand in a relation of causality to nature. It has an underground that is conditionally dependent; it has, as spirit, a soul, a complex of natural dispositions, which as such are conditioned by physical nature and dependent on it" (281). The dependence passes through the underground. It is the underground, the soul as a complex of dispositions, that is conditioned by physical nature, and the spirit depends on its own underground, not on nature directly. Conditionality, moreover, is not causality. The fall that makes a man a cripple is, for the understanding of his life, the indication of a change in what can motivate him (276); it is not a cause that produces his acts.

The apparatus shows how late this second answer was secured, and the place is the page of § 61 that rests on the older supplement XIV. In the text as Stein and Landgrebe transmitted it, the underground was said to be "the psychic in the sense of natural psychology". Husserl changed the sentence in Landgrebe's typescript and wrote beside it: "In the sense of natural psychology? I consider this view of the soul wrong" (414).[^12] In the same years he inserted the word "founding" into the sentence that says the soul belongs to the person as its underground (280, with apparatus, 414). Both changes go in one direction. The underground remains the person's founding layer, but it is no longer to be read as the soul that physiological psychology studies; it is the person's own passive life. In 1913 the founding of the soul on the body was stated for an isolated ego. In January 1917 the person's life was divided into a passive and an active level, with nature still named on both sides of the division. Only after 1924 did Husserl reject, in the text of *Ideas II* itself, the naturalistic reading of the person's lower level. The transposition has two steps, and the second belongs to the last layer of the book.

Two answers therefore stand in the text, and neither is withdrawn. On the one hand, the spirit is dependent on nature through its soul and body, and so conditioned by it (276, 281). On the other hand, the soul on which it depends is its own underground, the site where nature is constituted (277, 279), and the lowest given in that underground is not material nature but primal sensibility, simply there (334). The second answer does not refute the first; it places it. What the first describes as the spirit's dependence on nature, the second describes as the spirit's dependence on its own passive life, which, apprehended naturalistically, appears as the soul conditioned by the body. Husserl does not say which description has the last word. He moves, in the chapter that follows, toward the second, under a title, the ontological priority of the spiritual world, which the editor supplied but which Husserl's own last paragraph bears out. That paragraph is the subject of part 5.

### 4.7 The date of the transposition

Why January 1917? The question has an answer that comes from outside this study's texts, and I give it with the reserve due to secondary sources. The introduction to Husserl's phenomenology by Bernet, Kern and Marbach dates the first outline of a properly genetic phenomenology to "the period of 1917 to 1921", and characterizes it as an a priori comprehension of the motivational complexes of transcendental consciousness, not as causal explanation.[^9] The supplement of January 1917 fits this date: its levels are levels of development, its lower layers are partly sediments of the higher, and its teleology is the movement of activity into passivity and back. Dieter Lohmar, in an article I know only from its abstract, traces Husserl's motives for the genetic turn to the analyses he wrote for *Ideas I* and *Ideas II* between 1910 and 1916, where the understanding of others and their part in the constitution of objectivity is interwoven with research into how individual experience shapes our access to the world.[^10] If that is right, the transposition of the layers traced in this part is one of the places where the genetic turn shows in *Ideas II* itself.

I draw from this only what the texts of this study support, and with the limits that parts 4.1 to 4.6 have found. The underground is older than 1917; what January 1917 adds is its stratification as a history, in which the founding of the higher on the lower is joined by the sinking of the higher into the lower. The bottom of that history is primal sensibility, which is not material nature, though the supplement still calls the whole passive sphere "nature" and hedges when it does. The naturalistic reading of the person's underground is rejected in the text of *Ideas II* only after 1924. The transposition is therefore a movement in two steps, and its date is the date of each step: January 1917 for the genetic stratification of the person, 1924–28 for its release from naturalistic psychology. What remains to be seen is what the book's last paragraph makes of nature, once nature's constitution has been placed in the person's underground.

## 5. Nature relative, spirit absolute: § 64 and its last layer

### 5.1 The thought experiment

The last paragraph of *Ideas II* draws its conclusion from "the considerations so far", and the conclusion is a limit. "There results the limit of possible *naturalization*: the spirit can be grasped as dependent on nature and itself be naturalized, but only up to a certain degree." An unambiguous determination of spirit by merely natural dependences, "a reduction to something like physical nature", is "unthinkable". "Subjects cannot be absorbed in being nature, since then what gives nature its sense would be missing. Nature is a field of thoroughgoing relativities, and can be so because these are always relative to an absolute which thereby bears all relativities: the spirit" (297; *Ideas II*, 311).

The next sentences grant everything that part 4 found in § 61 and § 62, and then deny that it touches the point. The spirit is determined by its surrounding world, and it has a natural regulation insofar as it shows dependences of many kinds in relation to the nature that constitutes itself in relation to the personal world. "But that does not prevent it from being *absolute*, *irrelative*" (297). Husserl then gives the thought experiment that Heidegger would quote. "Namely, if we strike out all spirits from the world, there is no more nature. But if we strike out nature, the 'true', objective-intersubjective existence, there still remains something: the spirit as individual spirit." What is lost is "the possibility of sociality, the possibility of a comprehension that presupposes a certain intersubjectivity of the body". The individual spirit is then no longer a person "in the narrower, social sense". Yet "with all the enormous impoverishment of 'personal' life" there remains "an ego with its life of consciousness", and in it the ego keeps its individuality, "its way of judging, of valuing, of letting itself be motivated in its position-takings" (297).

The thought experiment is asymmetrical, and the asymmetry is the point. Strike out the spirits and nature goes with them, because nature is the correlate of their harmonious experience. Strike out nature and the spirits remain, impoverished, because they are not the correlate of anything; they are what nature is a correlate for. The order of dependence is the reverse of the order of the ascent, and the word "absolute" says so. But what remains when nature is struck out deserves attention. It is not the pure ego of *Ideas I*, a pole of acts. It is "an ego with its life of consciousness" which has "its individuality, its way of judging, of valuing, of letting itself be motivated". The residuum has a style. That is the first sign that § 64 is written from the person of part 4 rather than from the isolated ego of the ascent.

### 5.2 The argument from individuation

The thought experiment is followed by an argument, and the argument concerns individuation. I reconstruct it in three steps; the order is mine, and each step is Husserl's.

First, things have no individuality of their own. A thing has its individual essence "as what is here and now", but this "what" is itself a universal: every thing is an exemplar of a generality, "already the thing at the level of mere harmoniously thought 'sensuous' experience", and every thing can be thought as repeated any number of times (298). What distinguishes two qualitatively identical things is their real-causal connexion, "which presupposes the here and now", and with this "we are necessarily referred back to an individual subjectivity, whether a single or an intersubjective one, in relation to which alone the determinacy of the positing of place and time constitutes itself. No thing has its individuality in itself" (299).

Secondly, the spirit has its individuality in itself. "The spirit, however, is experiencing, position-taking, motivated. Every spirit has its own way of motivation; unlike the thing, it has its motivation in itself. It is not an individuality only through being at a determinate place in the world" (299–300). Absolute individuation belongs already to the pure ego of each cogitation; "but the ego is not an empty pole, but the bearer of its habituality, and in that lies that it has its individual history" (300). Lived experiences carry their individuation in themselves; a lived experience can be thought repeated, but it is then thought "as the *same* individual", as unique (300).

Thirdly, all other individuation derives from this one. "The only originally individual thing is consciousness concretely with its ego. Everything else individual is something appearing and has the principle of its individuation in actual and possible appearing, which for its part refers back to an individual consciousness" (301). "The surrounding world constitutes an ego, or an intersubjectivity, for itself", and whatever in it is determined by the ego has only "the *secondary individuation*" of what stands opposite (301). Hence the conclusion of the paragraph: spirits are not "unities of appearances" but "unities of absolute nexuses of consciousness, more precisely ego-unities"; their absolute being "precedes the relative being of appearances"; and "all individuation of the latter depends on the absolute individuation of the former, all natural existence on the existence of absolute spirits" (302; *Ideas II*, 315). The paragraph ends on the formula that Heidegger would also quote: "Nature is the X and in principle nothing other than X, which determines itself through general determinations. The spirit, however, is no X, but what is given in the experience of spirit itself" (302).

### 5.3 What Husserl added

The main text of § 64 rests on no surviving manuscript, and its date, as part 1 showed, is at best that of Stein's second arrangement, about 1918, with its last lines from a sheet of 1912. What can be dated is what Husserl added to it afterwards, in Landgrebe's typescript between 1924 and 1928. The apparatus lists these additions word by word (414), and they have a pattern. I set them out as facts about the text before drawing any inference from them.

(1) In the thought experiment, the struck-out nature is "the 'true', objective-*intersubjective* existence": the word "intersubjective" is an addition (297). (2) In the first step of the argument, the subjectivity in relation to which things are individuated is "an individual subjectivity, *whether a single or an intersubjective one*": the clause is an addition (299). (3) In the second step, the sentence "But the ego is not an empty pole, but the bearer of its habituality, and in that lies that it has its individual history" is an addition in its entirety (300). (4) In the third step, the only originally individual thing is "consciousness *concretely with its ego*": the last words are an addition (301). (5) In the same step, "the surrounding world constitutes an ego, *or an intersubjectivity*, for itself": again an addition (301). Two further additions concern the thing: that the real nature in whose connexion a thing is what it is "is constituted in itself as throughout homogeneous", and a sentence on the thing at the level of sensuous experience (298). And Husserl struck out a paragraph that asked whether there could be only one thing, whether every part of a thing is itself a thing, and whether "the Kantian antinomies" belong here (414).

What do the additions show? Read as a symptom of the direction in which Husserl was moving, they show two things. First, three of them extend the absolute from the individual spirit to intersubjectivity. In the text of about 1918 the absolute that bears nature is "the spirit as individual spirit"; after 1924 it is the individual spirit "or an intersubjectivity". Secondly, two of them make the absolute concrete and historical. The ego that individuates is not an empty pole but "the bearer of its habituality", with "its individual history", and the originally individual is consciousness "concretely with its ego". Both directions are those of part 4. The person of January 1917 has layers that are its own history, "the medium of its 'history'" in the supplement's words: sediments of activity, habits, a secondary sensibility; and its sensibility, the supplement said, is regulated intersubjectively. The additions bring § 64 into line with that person, and they belong to the same layer of revision as the marginal remark at p. 280 that rejects the naturalistic reading of the soul (part 4.6).

This is an inference, and its limits should be stated. Husserl does not say why he made the additions, and the apparatus does not date them more closely than 1924–28. The sentence on habituality could have been suggested by other work of those years as much as by the supplement of 1917. What can be said is that the additions are all of one kind, that none of them moves in the direction of the ascent, and that together they make the absolute of § 64 into the absolute of a person with a history and a community.

### 5.4 The minster

The edition attaches one more supplement "to the third section, in particular § 64", and its history fits the dates of this study closely. Husserl noted on its first page: "Copy of a *reflection* on yearbook printing paper (1917), supplement to *Ideas II*"; the apparatus judges that the copy, "hardly a verbatim one", seems to come from the first half of the twenties (423). The doctrine is therefore of 1917, the wording possibly of the years in which Husserl annotated Landgrebe's typescript, and single words in it, the word "life-world" among them, cannot be dated to 1917 on its authority.

The supplement sets the "personal subjectivity" as a theoretical theme against the subject "considered as a reality united with physical nature", the subject "that now senses red because such and such ether vibrations strike its normally functioning retina" (372). Its example is a building. I stand before "our minster" and see it in its bodily presence; another stands beside me, looking at it, and I understand his seeing at once as an immediate having of the same object; with every step I take its aspect changes, "but I see immediately it itself" (373). In the natural-scientific attitude, by contrast, I am a body among bodies, and my perceptual appearances acquire the sense of end-members of a physical-causal and psychophysically conditional process: vibrations of the ether go out from the physical body "minster", propagate through space, and act as stimuli on my nerves. "Is that the real truth, and the immediate seeing of living subjects, by contrast, a mere semblance?" (373).

The answer has three steps, and each repeats a finding of this study in the language of 1917 or of its copy. First, the interpretation would be "wrong", since "all psychophysical knowledge, all knowledge of reality, rests on immediate experience". "In other words: the natural attitude", meaning here the naturalistic, "is a particular attitude within life; only I do not make the ego, as intentional subject of life and of this naturally interested life, the theme. That remains in self-forgetfulness" (373). Secondly, simple perception "contains in its sense nothing of an apprehension of a causal relation" between my soul, my sensations and the thing; I learn only "in series of experience of my own" that certain organs are organs of perception, and finally that such and such psychophysical causalities obtain (374). This is the order of the forty-seventh supplement to Hua XIII, in which the psychophysical layer is "a new apperceptive layer". Thirdly, the theoretical attitude toward nature is "a particular ego-life and habituality of ego-life, in which however this ego-life itself remains anonymous"; the human and natural sciences study "correlative 'worlds' that do not disturb one another"; and in the world of living persons "the fundamental relation … is not that of causality, but that of motivation" (374–75).

The naturalistic attitude, in this text, is itself a habituality: a sediment of an ego-life which does not see itself. That is the phrase in which the two arrangements of this study meet. In 1913 the naturalistic attitude was an abstraction performed by persons who forget themselves. In this text the forgetting has become a habit, and a habit, by the doctrine of the supplement of January 1917, is the higher sunk into the lower. The founding of the person on nature, which part 3 found to be the order of one attitude, is here the order of a habitual way of living. Since the wording may be that of the copy of the early 1920s, I date this formulation no more closely than 1917 to about 1925.

### 5.5 What the relativity gains from the transposition

The relativity of nature to spirit is not new in § 64. The manuscript of 1913 already says that nature presented itself "as something constituting itself in the intersubjective association of persons, and thus presupposing it" (210), and its supplement says that "the single personality is a unity of absolute manifestation", as is every social unity of higher level (323). The last lines of § 64 themselves rest on a sheet of 1912. § 64 does not need the transposition in order to state the relativity. If the relativity is of 1913, what does the transposition add to it?

It adds a resolution of the circle. In 1913 the relativity stands beside the ascent as the other side of a circle. Nature is constituted in the community of persons; but the persons are introduced at the top of an ascent that begins with sense-things and bodies, and the ascent is called "the constitutive order". Husserl explains the circle by the difference of attitudes, but the explanation leaves one question open: in which attitude is the ascent itself written? It cannot be the naturalistic attitude, since the ascent begins from the sense-things of an ego and not from physical nature. Nor is it simply the personalistic attitude, since the ego of the ascent is "thought of as isolated and not yet realized", that is, not yet a person. The ascent hangs between the attitudes. This is not found as such in the text; it is my diagnosis of why the circle of § 53 is explained but not dissolved.

The transposition of 1917 gives the ascent a place. Once the sensations, associations and lowest apperceptions are the person's own underground, "the site of the constitution of a world of appearances" (279), the stepwise ascent describes the life of that underground. It is no longer the history of an abstract ego that becomes a person at the end. It is a stratum of the person, the stratum that "makes" the constitution of nature. And then the relativity of § 64 and the ascent of the first section are no longer two orders. The ascent describes how nature is constituted in the lower stratum of persons; § 64 states that what is so constituted is relative to those in whom it is constituted. Read on the first arrangement, § 64 stands against the order of the book as the other side of a circle. Read on the second, it states what the book's order meant once its layers had been placed in the person. The second reading is mine; Husserl does not say that the ascent describes the person's underground, and the text of § 61 that would come closest to saying it carries, at p. 280, his own late correction.

### 5.6 A prediction and its result

Before reading one text of these years I wrote down what the reading of this study predicts it will say, so that the reading could be tested on a case it was not built from. The text is the forty-seventh supplement to the first volume on intersubjectivity, dated 1918 in its heading: "The constitution of nature that exists in itself intersubjectively, on the basis of the constitution of the psychophysical at the solipsistic and the higher level of apperception. The wrong introjection of the appearances into the body" (Hua XIII, 385). The prediction, made from the heading alone, had three parts. (i) The "solipsistic level" would be a level of apprehension in the constituting subject, not a level of being. (ii) The "wrong introjection" would be the error of taking the constituting appearances as states of the body, that is, of reading the constituting subject naturalistically, which § 49 called self-forgetfulness. (iii) The text would not make physical nature the founding layer of the person.

The text bears out the first two, bears on the third only indirectly, and adds something that was not predicted. (i) It begins from a "constitutive lower layer of the apperception of the world, including the body as a thing", which "genetically possibly (and necessarily?)" comes first (Hua XIII, 385). The levels are levels of apperception, and they are described as genetic. (ii) The introjection is the error the prediction named. Once things and bodies with subjects are distinguished, each subject has its system of adumbrations and appearances "as its own", and this, related to the subject's body, "suggests the wrong introjection, a putting of all psychic acts and states, but also of all sensations and appearances, into the body" (389). And Husserl adds: "The introjection of sensations and appearances into a subject, or their apprehension as merely subjective, stems from intersubjectivity" (389). At the level of a thing-constitution thought of as solipsistic, it is "questionable, indeed more than questionable", whether the appearances can be called subjective at all: "the appearances, and in them the sensations, are not my states" as a joy is, in which I live (388–89). This needs to be set beside part 4. It does not place sensations on the side of things; it says that they are not states of the ego. The supplement of January 1917 had said the same in its own terms: the sensuous sphere is the ego's "possession", not its acts (335, 339), and a footnote to the third chapter of the third section calls appearances "subjective" as "had", "not as its life, but as field, as medium, as what is had" (a footnote whose text the file prints at p. 288, with its reference in §§ 61–62; its date is not given). The underground of the person is its field and possession, not its states. (iii) The text does not speak of the person, and the sentence "the body cannot be constituted first" (386) concerns the order of body and outer things, not the founding of the person. What it does show bears on the third prediction only indirectly: the real, psychophysical dependence of sensations on the physical body is "a new apperceptive layer, a new level in the constitution of the world" (390), which only experience can establish, and which presupposes that the experience of body and things is already there. The physio-psychic order is a higher apprehension, not the lowest.

What was not predicted is the direction of the relation between levels. When a higher level of apprehension is reached, the lower is not simply kept beneath it. "If, however, the having [of colours] is revalued in a higher level, according to its new sense", then the object "appears as coloured only if we live ourselves into the lower layer", but it does not count as coloured in itself when taken in its full sense (391). The higher level revalues the lower, as the true colour revalued the seen colour in the example of the spectacles. In 1918, then, the levels of constitution are genetic, they belong to the apprehending subject, and they run in both directions: the lower makes the higher possible, and the higher revalues the lower. That is the structure this study found in the supplement of January 1917, and the test confirms the dating. It does not confirm more. The supplement of 1918 is about the constitution of nature and the body; it says nothing about the person's secondary sensibility, and it does not decide whether the person's lower layer is in part a sediment of the higher.

## 6. Heidegger's reading at full strength

### 6.1 The reading

Heidegger's lecture course of the summer of 1925 is the earliest critical reading of *Ideas II*, and it remains the strongest statement of the view this study contests. It deserves to be reconstructed at full strength, for three reasons. Heidegger read the manuscript, which Husserl handed to him in the winter of 1924/25 (168), and which was then, I infer from the dates, Landgrebe's typescript or the arrangement it copied; he read it with attention, quoting the last paragraph at length; and his objection is not to a detail but to the order of the whole. One small fact points to a version other than the printed one: Heidegger cites the last paragraphs of the third section as "§ 12" to "§ 15" (171), where the edition has §§ 61 to 64. Which arrangement numbered them so, I cannot say.

The reading begins with a concession that the received view usually forgets. Heidegger reports the personalistic attitude in Husserl's own terms: it is "very natural, but not naturalistic", we slip "constantly and quite effortlessly" from one attitude into the other, and "the attitudes are not even coordinate, but the naturalistic is subordinated to the personalistic". "Here the priority of the personalistic attitude, and the understanding of it, is clearly stated theoretically" (GA 20, 169). He also reports, from the third section, that "each such ego has at the same time its nature-side as the underground of subjectivity", and he quotes the thought experiment and the formula of the X from the last paragraph (169–70). Then he turns: "This is the same consideration as concerns pure consciousness as the residuum of the annihilation of the world. Husserl only comes back here to his primal division of being under another title. Ontologically everything remains as of old" (171).

The criticism proper has three points, which Heidegger numbers (171–72). First, the inquiry "remains within the question of the constitution of reality and objectivity". Its context is prescribed by *Ideas I*, and "this question is posed in the order in which the matters of the real themselves stand. The *fundamental layer* remains the *actuality of nature*, built on it is the psychic, and on this the spiritual". Although "the genuine naturalness of the personalistic attitude is emphasized thematically, the executing consideration still gives priority to the investigation of nature. *The being of the person is not primarily experienced as such*" (172). Secondly, the mode of access to the person is "nothing other than the already determined immanent reflection (*inspectio sui*) on the lived experiences", from which all the theses of absolute givenness derive; the person is reached by reflection on acts, and the being of acts remains undetermined (171–72). Thirdly, and Heidegger calls this "the most important" insight, the prior determination of the unity of lived experience as spirit and person "keeps the traditional definition of man, *homo animal rationale*, as guiding thread" (172). Even if the being of acts were determined, the question would remain of the being of the full concrete human being. "Can its being be *assembled*, so to speak, from the being of the material substratum, the body, the soul and the spirit? Is the being of the person the product of the kinds of being of these layers of being?" Or does it show that by this way of prior division and subsequent combination "the person is taken as a *many-layered thing of the world*" (173)? Husserl's analysis, he concludes, "does not take man as a reality of nature, but still as a reality of the world, which constitutes itself as transcendence in absolute consciousness" (173).

Two years later, in § 10 of *Being and Time*, Heidegger kept the criticism, with a note that names *Ideas II* as the place where Husserl's investigations of personality were "largely advanced" (47 n.). In the text of the paragraph he credits Husserl with demanding "for the unity of the person an essentially different constitution than for the things of nature" (SZ 48), though the note attached to that sentence refers to the *Logos* essay of 1910/11, not to *Ideas II*. But the interpretations of personality in Husserl and in Scheler "agree in the negative: they no longer pose the question of 'being a person' itself" (47), and the being of the human being "cannot be computed summatively from the kinds of being of body, soul, spirit, which have moreover yet to be determined" (48).

The reading has textual support at every step, and that support should be stated before any reply. The order of the printed book is the order Heidegger describes. *Ideas I* § 152 does say that material reality lies at the basis of all others. The supplement of 1913 does call the ascent "the constitutive order". § 49 does name the "order of founding" from the physical up. § 61 does speak of the "lower level as 'pure' animality" and of the *intellectus agens*. § 54 is titled "The ego in the *inspectio sui*". And § 64 does conclude, as the residuum of the annihilation of the world concluded in *Ideas I*, that something remains when nature is struck out.

### 6.2 Replies, in Heidegger's order

I take the three points in Heidegger's order, and then the comparison with *Ideas I* on which his verdict rests. On each, something must be granted and something denied.

(1) *The order of the matters.* Heidegger is right that the printed book proceeds from nature to spirit, and right that this order is the order of constitution as *Ideas I* announced it. He is also right, and more than he could know, that the order of execution gives the investigation of nature a priority that the doctrine of § 49 denies it: that is what Husserl's own marginal notes say when they call the first chapter one that "must be completely reworked" and say of a later stretch that "from here on everything seems to need improvement" (apparatus, notes to pp. 1 and 73). He is right, too, that the book founds the soul on the body: the series of 1913 calls the soul a "founded, therefore non-independent reality on the underground of the body" (310). But two of Heidegger's inferences do not follow. It does not follow that the person is founded on the actuality of nature. The founding series of 1913 stops before the person ("in this series we do not come upon the personal ego", 310), and the founding of the person on nature is placed by Husserl within the naturalistic attitude (183, 185, 211), a placement Heidegger reports himself. The lowest layer of § 18, moreover, is the sense-thing in correlation with the moving body (66). It must be added, in fairness to Heidegger, that the text in which the ascent is called an idealization, the first supplement, was copied neither by Stein nor by Landgrebe (415), and he cannot have read it. Nor does it follow that the order of the book is the order of Husserl's thought. Heidegger believed that the second part of the *Ideas* was "carried out in one go with the first" (171). The edition shows otherwise: the section on the spiritual world rests on a manuscript of 1913, the first two sections on an elaboration of 1915 and on inserted sheets, and the order in which he read them was Stein's. The priority of the personal attitude was not a destination reached at the end of the ascent; it was written before the ascent was elaborated.

(2) *Access by reflection.* Heidegger is right that the person of *Ideas II* is reached by reflection on acts, and that § 54 calls this reflection *inspectio sui*. But § 58, four paragraphs later, asks whether the personal ego constitutes itself on the basis of reflections at all, and answers that "originally it constitutes itself in the genesis that pervades the flow of lived experience", that the ego is "originally not from experience … but from life", and that "someone does not 'know' himself … he learns to know himself" (252). These sentences were in the text Heidegger read; only a few words in them were added later (part 4.5). Husserl does not raise the ontological question of the being of acts in the terms Heidegger demands, and this study, which is historical, cannot decide whether he should have. What can be said is that the person of § 61 is not exhausted by what reflection finds. It has a "dark underground", "hidden dispositions", a "layer of hidden reason", and "ultimately a background that lies before all behaviour, rather is presupposed by all behaviour"; it is "in a way a root-soil in dark depths" (276–79). Its lower layers are sediments of its own past activity, which reflection meets as tendencies and not as acts. And after 1924 the ego of § 64 is not an empty pole but "the bearer of its habituality", with "its individual history" (300). A person with an underground and a history is not given in the inspection of a present cogitation. Heidegger saw the underground (169), but he read it as one more layer in the stack. The texts of 1917 make it the place where the stack is produced.

(3) *The guiding thread of the animal rationale.* Here the objection must be turned, for what Heidegger says is truer of the texts of 1917 than of the ascent he had chiefly in view. § 61 says, in so many words, that the personal ego in its development shows "two levels", the lower possibly separable "as 'pure' animality" and the higher the "layer of the *intellectus agens*", and it adds: "We come here again to the old distinction, so early forced upon us, between reason and sensibility" (276). The supplement of January 1917 opens with the "distinction of primal sensibility and *intellectus agens*" (332). The transposition of the layers into the person is, literally, the reinstatement of the old definition of the human being as a living being that has reason. Heidegger's third point therefore belongs to Husserl's own account, and Husserl knew it.

What does not follow is the conclusion Heidegger drew from it, that the person is "assembled" from layers and is the "product" of their kinds of being. In the texts of 1917 the layers do not combine; they turn into one another. Reason "sinks down" into sensibility and becomes a secondary sensibility, which in turn prescribes the paths of its "reconversion into activity" (332, 334). Primal sensibility is the ego's "primal possession", the material on which the constitution of nature works (334–35). The *animal* of this *animal rationale* is, in these texts, not only a thing present in the world, to which reason is added; it is also the passive life in which the world of things is constituted, and part of it is reason's own deposit. Heidegger is right that Husserl did not keep the two senses apart in 1917; the supplement still calls the passive sphere "nature" with a hedge, and in the text Heidegger read, § 61 still identified the underground with the soul of natural psychology. The correction came in the margin, after his reading (part 4.6). Heidegger's question, whether the being of the whole person can be computed from the being of its layers, is the right question to put to the ascent of 1913. It does not fit the person of 1917, whose layers are not summands but stages of one history.

(4) *The residuum.* One more step in Heidegger's reading needs an answer, because it is the step by which he joins *Ideas II* to *Ideas I*. The thought experiment of § 64, he says, is "the same consideration as concerns pure consciousness as the residuum of the annihilation of the world" (171). The parallel is real. § 49 of *Ideas I* had said that the being of consciousness "would indeed be necessarily modified by an annihilation of the world of things, but not touched in its own existence" (Hua III/1, 104); § 64 says that if nature is struck out, the spirit remains, impoverished. Both arguments establish an asymmetry of dependence by an imagined removal.

What remains, however, is not the same in the two texts, and the difference is the one this study has traced. In *Ideas I* the residuum is consciousness, "every stream of lived experience", taken in its essence. In § 64 the residuum is "the spirit as individual spirit", an ego that keeps "its individuality, its way of judging, of valuing, of letting itself be motivated", and that after 1924 is "the bearer of its habituality" with "its individual history" (297, 300). It is not a region of being opposed to the world of things; it is a person reduced to its own style, with the world of nature, its own constitutive achievement, struck away. Heidegger is right that the "primal division of being" between consciousness and reality survives in § 64 under another title. He is not right that "ontologically everything remains as of old" (171), if what has changed is what is meant by the term that survives. The absolute of *Ideas I* was a field; the absolute of § 64, in its last state, is someone.

### 6.3 What the reading saw

It would be unjust to leave the reply there. Heidegger himself said, in the same lecture, that his characterization was "in a certain way already somewhat antiquated" (168). Husserl had written to him, on 7 February 1925, that "since the Freiburg beginnings" he had made "such essential progress precisely in the questions of nature and spirit that I would have to carry out an entirely new elaboration with in part entirely changed content" (168). Heidegger added that Husserl, knowing his Freiburg and Marburg lectures and in conversation, "takes my objections substantially into account", and that "my critique today no longer strikes with full sharpness" (168). And he said, of a man whose work he was criticizing: "It hardly needs to be confessed that I still take myself today to be a learner in relation to Husserl" (168). The reading of 1925 was therefore a reading of a text its author no longer held to be adequate, made by a reader who knew it.

What Heidegger saw remains, and it is the limit of this study's thesis. The transposition, in both its steps, relocates the layers but does not abolish them. Husserl still describes the person as a higher, free, rational layer and a lower, sensuous, passive one, and he still asks how the spirit depends on its soul and the soul on its body (281). The being of the whole that these layers make up, the question of *Being and Time*, is not posed in *Ideas II* in Heidegger's sense. The texts of 1917 show that the layers are not parts; they do not show what the whole is of which they are not parts.

### 6.4 The two answers

What is constituted on what? The title's question has more than one answer in *Ideas II*, and the answers belong to dated states of the text. In the manuscripts of 1912 and 1913, realities are constituted in an ascent of realizing apprehensions: the sense-thing of an ego thought of as alone becomes the appearance of a material thing, the material thing receives a body, and the soul is founded on the body as a non-independent reality. The person is not in this series. Concretely, in 1912, nature, body and soul constitute themselves "at one with one another"; and the founding of the person on matter is the order of one attitude, which persons adopt when they forget themselves. In the manuscript of January 1917 and the paragraphs Stein built from it and from older sheets, the person itself is stratified: nature is constituted in its underground, its sensibility, which is the ego's possession, in part the sediment of its own activity, resting at the bottom on a primal sensibility that is simply there. Husserl still calls this sphere "nature", with a hedge. In the notes of 1924–28 he rejects the reading of the underground as the soul of natural psychology, and the last paragraph of the book, with the words he added to it, states the relativity of nature to spirit in the form this answer requires: the absolute is a person with a habituality, a history and a community.

The first answer is the one the printed book seems to give, and it is the one Heidegger criticized; he was right about its founding of the soul and wrong to extend it to the person. The later answer is not the reversal of the first but its relocation, made in two steps, the second of them in the margin of the typescript Heidegger read: the ascent survives as the description of a stratum of persons. That reading of the ascent is mine, and Husserl does not state it. Whether the relocation was Husserl's settled view cannot be decided from *Ideas II*. Its last paragraph rests on no surviving manuscript, and the two lecture courses on nature and spirit of 1919 and 1927, which would show what he then taught, were not available to this study; nor were the readings of Ricœur and Merleau-Ponty, which may already have said part of what is said here. I have shown only that the layers of *Ideas II* have a history, and that its constitution changes direction where its layers change their bearer, more slowly and less cleanly than a first reading of the sheets suggests. Heidegger's question, what the being of the person is if not the sum of its layers, is the one *Ideas II* leaves open. That he could ask it of this book, and that Husserl had already begun to make the sum impossible, is a credit to both.

## 6. What the primary texts changed

- Plan, candidate thesis T1 ("the priority of spirit is older than the stratified exposition"): embarrassed by Beilage I, which sets out the ascent as "der konstitutiven Ordnung folgend" in the same manuscript of 1913. T1 was rejected; the fact about the book's order was kept.
- Plan, thesis T3 ("§ 64's relativity follows from the second arrangement, not from the first"): narrowed by § 53 (p. 210) and Beilage X (p. 323), which already make nature constituted in the association of persons in 1913. The thesis now places the relativity in 1913 and the resolution of the circle in 1917 (plan, revision note).
- Reading log: the Dilthey sentence of SZ § 10 n. was first taken to be absent from the printed text; it is the opening of § 48 in different wording ("erschaute zwar … methodisch sicheren" against SZ "faßte zwar … methodisch richtigen").
- Heidegger's knowledge of § 61 was not expected: GA 20, 169 reports "seine Naturseite als Untergrund der Subjektivität". Part 6 was changed to say that Heidegger saw the underground and read it as one more layer.
- Hua XIII Beilage XLVII added the "Umwertung" of a lower level by a higher one, which the prediction had not foreseen (item 12).
- The dating of § 58's sentences: the 1917 sheet begins at p. 252, l. 27, and the file does not show where the quoted sentences fall; the study dates them only by the arrangement.

- The fresh-context review (item 13) changed the thesis. The underground of the person is older than 1917 (§ 34, 1912, pp. 141–42; supplement XIV, "certainly before 1917", reused in § 61 at pp. 279–80, where the underground is "the psychic in the sense of physiological, natural psychology"). In the text Stein and Landgrebe transmitted, § 61 at p. 280 still said so; Husserl's marginal remark "Diese Auffassung von Seele halte ich für verkehrt" and the insertion of "fundierender" are of 1924–28 (414). The transposition is now dated in two steps (1917; 1924–28), not one.

## 12. The prediction

Text: Hua XIII, Beilage XLVII (1918), read after the plan was fixed. Predicted: (i) the "solipsistic level" a level of apperception, not of being; (ii) the "wrong introjection" the error of taking constituting appearances as states of the body; (iii) physical nature not the founding layer of the person. Result: (i) and (ii) borne out (Hua XIII, 385, 389); (iii) not tested, since the text does not speak of the person (the draft wrongly counted "Der Leib kann nicht zuerst konstituiert sein", 386, which concerns body and outer things, as confirmation; corrected after the review). Not predicted: a higher level "revalues" (*umgewertet*) the lower (391). The text also says that appearances and sensations "sind nicht meine Zustände" (389), which the study now reads with supplement XII ("Habe") as placing them in the ego's field and possession, not its states. Effect on the thesis: the genetic, two-directional layering of apperception is confirmed for 1918; the person's secondary sensibility is not tested (5.6).

## 13. The review

A subagent in a fresh context, given only the study and the primary files, (1) argued the opposing reading, (2) checked ten quotations, (3) named the weakest claim.

(1) Its case: Beilage I founds the soul on the body (310) and its lowest stage excludes even the body (305), so the "ideal possibilities" are one-sided founding, not ideality of the order; § 49 e calls physical nature "die alle sonstige Natur begründende" (181); § 62 keeps the chain spirit–soul–body; supplement XII still presupposes the "Naturmechanismus" and calls the empirical ego "das Ich der leibseelischen Natur"; the underground is already in 1912 (141–42) and in supplement XIV, "certainly before 1917", which § 61 reuses, as the soul of naturalistic psychology; the de-naturalizing is a 1924–28 correction (280); § 52 versus § 61 is an equivocation between noematic schemata and hyletic having; Hua XIII 1918 denies that sensations are the subject's states; § 64's relativity does not need 1917 (1912 sheet; § 63's struck footnote); Heidegger could not have read Beilage I, which neither Stein nor Landgrebe copied.

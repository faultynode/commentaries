# What Is It to Be Constituted?

## Reality and Its Circumstances in Husserl's *Ideas II*

It is generally accepted that the second book of Husserl's *Ideas* shows how reality is constituted in layers. The book has three sections, on the constitution of material nature, of animal nature and of the spiritual world, and they are read as three storeys: the material thing below, the animate body and the soul built on it, the person and its world on top. Husserl's own programme supports the reading. At the end of the first book he wrote that "as the lowest level, material reality finally lies at the basis of all other realities" (Hua III/1, 355). Heidegger, who lectured on the unpublished manuscript in the summer of 1925, made a reproach of it: in *Ideas II* the "fundamental layer" remains "the actuality of nature", the psychic is built on it and the spiritual on the psychic, so that the person is taken as "a many-layered thing of the world" (GA 20, 172–73). Later readers have kept the description without the reproach; Filip Mattens finds in Husserl's concept of the phantom, a thing minus its causal properties, "the paragon of constitutive analysis", because it shows a level that can be set against both a lower and a higher one.[^1] On this view "to constitute" means to found, and the question to put to the book is what is constituted on what. Yet where the book describes constitution most generally, it names no base. "In all constitution and at all levels", Husserl writes of the spatial thing in the middle of the first section, "we necessarily have 'circumstances' related to one another and what 'belongs' to the circumstances; everywhere we find the 'if–then' or 'because–therefore'" (Hua IV, 57/62–63).[^2] A supplement says the same of reality as such: "It is the essence of reality in general to be a unity of dependences" (391/400–401). And the last paragraph of the book does not say that spirit rests on nature. It says that nature is "a field of thoroughgoing relativities" (297/308–311). The received view has no use for the first two sentences and must treat the third as a reversal.

In what follows I wish to show that these sentences state one concept, and that it is the concept of constitution on which *Ideas II* is built. To be constituted as a reality, in this book, is to be the unity of a rule that binds a manifold of states to circumstances. Husserl works this form out on the material thing and then carries it to the body, to the soul and to the person, and at each step one of its three terms changes its sense: the manifold in which the unity shows itself, the circumstances on which the states depend, and the kind of rule, which is causality for the thing, what Husserl calls conditionality for body and soul, and motivation for the person. The layers of the received view are derived from this form, and they are derived in three different ways that the word "layer" runs together. Motivation, finally, does not come in only at the top. It stands at both ends of the series, beneath causality in the "if–then" of bodily movement and appearance, and above it in the "because" of personal life; and the closing doctrine of the relativity of nature and the absoluteness of spirit says where the form of reality applies without remainder and where it stops.

The study rests on the printed text of *Ideas II* in the Husserliana edition (Hua IV, 1952), read in German, with its supplements. Three other texts are used: §§ 149–152 of *Ideas I* (Hua III/1), where the programme of the second book is announced; § 13 of Heidegger's course of 1925 (GA 20), for the opposing reading; and the first section of Mattens's article. *Ideas II* was put together by Husserl's assistants from manuscripts written between 1912 and 1928, and the history of that composition has consequences for the dating of its doctrines. I leave it aside here. The concept I am after runs through the whole of the printed text, and I shall mention the date of a sentence only where it bears on what the sentence can be made to carry.

Several omissions should be named. I have not read Paul Ricœur's study "Analyses et problèmes dans *Ideen II* de Husserl", Maurice Merleau-Ponty's essay "The Philosopher and His Shadow", or Robert Sokolowski's book *The Formation of Husserl's Concept of Constitution*. As far as I know these are the nearest published treatments of the question, and they may already contain part of what is said below; what I know of them is from memory and from bibliographies, so that I make no claim of novelty against them. I also leave aside two kinds of constitution that *Ideas II* itself keeps at its margins. The constitution of immanent time is named in the book and not carried out there (24/26–27). The constitution of an objective world among several subjects is treated only so far as the argument of §§ 47 and 64 requires. It may seem strange that a study of constitution forgoes both. The reason is that neither is constitution of a reality in the sense the book's three titles intend, and that sense is hard enough to fix.

A word on the manner of proceeding. The first five parts are exposition, and they follow Husserl's own order of analysis: the senses of the word are separated first, the material thing serves as guiding clue, and the higher regions are described by what changes in them. At two points I carry out a description again on one of Husserl's examples and vary it, a steel spring in part 2 and a servant greeting his master in part 4. These stretches are marked. They are meant to show what a sentence of the text amounts to when one does what it says, and nothing in them is offered as evidence of what Husserl held. Part 6 gives the layered reading its full strength, in Heidegger's version, and answers it. The analysis throughout is static in Husserl's sense: it describes structures as they stand and only names the questions of genesis that arise.

## 1. The senses of the word

### 1.1 A guiding clue and a lowest level

The programme of *Ideas II* is set out in the last pages of *Ideas I*, and it already contains the two claims that the received view joins into one. The first is a claim about method. "Every region of objects constitutes itself in consciousness" (Hua III/1, 344): an object of a given kind has its prescribed ways of being perceived, presented and shown to be what it is, and phenomenology has to describe them. Since the regions are many, a start must be made somewhere, and Husserl makes it with the thing. "We take, for instance, the region material thing as guiding clue" (344). The title of the next paragraph calls the region thing a "transcendental guiding clue" (*Leitfaden*), and § 152 draws the consequence: what was said "by way of example" of the material thing "must be carried over to all regions of objects, in problem and in method" (354). The thing is here the case on which one learns what a problem of constitution is.

The second claim concerns an order among the realities themselves. Communities, Husserl says, are "founded" in psychic realities, which are in turn founded in physical ones; all objects of value and of practice and all formations of culture lead back to spatial things and psychic subjects; and "as the lowest level, material reality finally lies at the basis of all other realities, and so the phenomenology of material nature certainly has a distinguished position" (355). The sentence gives the thing a second kind of priority. It is first because the others rest on it.

The two priorities are not the same, and the programme itself keeps them apart in the next sentence. The founded unities, Husserl goes on, are "founded and of a new kind"; what is new in them "can never be reduced to mere sums of other realities" (355), and each type of reality brings its own constitutive phenomenology with it. If each has its own, then the phenomenology of the thing cannot be the phenomenology of the higher regions with something added, and the "carrying over" of § 152 must be a transfer of the form of the question. What the programme leaves open is how the two priorities are related: whether the thing guides the inquiry because it is the bottom layer, or whether it guides for a reason of its own.

One further feature of these pages should be recorded, because the layered reading relies on it. Within the constitution of the thing Husserl does speak of levels and strata, and he gives the word a precise sense. "Every level and every stratum in the level is characterized by the fact that it constitutes a unity of its own, which is in its turn a necessary intermediate link for the full constitution of the thing" (352). The sensuous schemata, the "visual things" of lower and higher order, the causal thing for one subject, the thing identical for many subjects: each is a unity, and each serves the next. A stratum in this sense is a unity that mediates. Whether the body, the soul and the person are strata in the same sense is a question the programme does not ask.

### 1.2 Constitution before theory

*Ideas II* opens with a chapter on the idea of nature, and before any thing is analysed it says three things about constitution that the later sections presuppose.

The first is that constitution is not the work of theory. When a subject takes up a theoretical attitude towards an object, grasps it and determines it in judgments, the object "is already constituted in consciousness before these theoretical acts" (4/4–7). The acts that constitute it are not all the experiences related to it. Feelings, for instance, are intentional experiences as well and "constitute new strata of the object" (4/4–7), strata of value and of use, but so long as the subject is not turned towards these they do not belong to the object as theoretically meant. Husserl sums up: "objects are thus already constituted pre-theoretically" (6/6–9). The remark fixes a first distinction. An object can be constituted without being a theme, and a subject can live in the constituting without attending to what it yields. We shall meet this distinction again where the personal ego is said to be constituted before reflection.

The second concerns two ways in which a unity can come about. Some objects arise from spontaneous acts: a state of affairs is constituted in judging, a collection in collecting, a value in valuing, and each can then be made the substrate of new acts. Others are unities without being products. A perceived thing is a synthetic unity of many partial apprehensions, of sides, of the visual with the tactual, and no act of connecting has produced it. Husserl calls the first synthesis categorial and the second aesthetic, in the old sense of the word that refers to sensibility, and marks the difference by one feature: "the categorial synthesis is, as synthesis, a spontaneous act, whereas the sensuous synthesis is not" (19/20–21). A note adds that the aesthetic synthesis is also at work, "as aesthetic-causal", in the higher strata of the thing, and that "it alone is what Kant has in view with his concept of synthesis" (20/20–23). The remark is worth keeping in mind. The synthesis that will make a thing real, the causal one, belongs for Husserl on the side of sensibility. It is undergone and lived through; no judgment performs it.

The third concerns the direction in which constitutive analysis runs. Every object constituted by spontaneous acts refers back to objects that were given beforehand, and these to others, until one reaches objects that contain no such reference: the objects of the senses, "the primal objects, to which all possible objects refer back according to their phenomenological constitution" (17/18–19). Even these have a structure. A violin tone heard as sounding in the next room is a real spatial occurrence; with its materiality set aside it is a spatial phantom of sound; with its spatiality set aside it is a datum of sensation that swells and fades as I move. The datum is "something pregiven that lies before the constitution of the object as object" (23/24–27), and Husserl adds that such data "constitute themselves as unities in original time-consciousness" (24/26–27). At this point the chapter stops going back. The lowest level of the book is therefore not material nature. It is sensation, and beneath sensation a constitution of time that the book names and does not treat.

These three remarks have a common consequence for the reading of the first section's title. "Nature", as the correlate of natural science, is in Husserl's words "a sphere of mere things", and it is reached by "a kind of *epoché*": we bracket every predicate of value and use and let ourselves "be determined only by the doxic, objectifying consciousness and not by the valuing one" (27/28–31). He is plain about what this leaves out. "In ordinary life we have nothing to do with nature-objects at all. What we call things are paintings, statues, gardens, houses, tables, clothes, tools" (27/28–31). The constitution of material nature with which the book begins is thus the constitution of an abstract stratum of the things we live among, a stratum singled out by its predicates, which "in their own sense indicate nothing of the subject and its acts" (15 n./20). The first section does not describe what comes first in experience. It describes what remains when a certain attitude is held to.

### 1.3 Constitution and attitude

One more preliminary is needed, because it governs the relation of the book's sections to one another. What is constituted for a subject depends on how the subject is turned towards it, and Husserl's word for this is attitude (*Einstellung*). The first chapter introduces the theoretical attitude and distinguishes the passage into it from reflection; the third section will say in general that a change of attitude "means nothing other than a thematic passage from one direction of apprehension into another, to which there correspond, correlatively, different objectivities" (210/218–221). An attitude in this sense does not create its objects. The valuable thing and the mere thing are both constituted before I turn to either. It decides which of the constituted strata is taken as the object, and so which predicates count.

Two consequences follow for what comes later. The titles of the three sections name correlates of attitudes: nature in the sense of the first two sections is what there is for a subject that holds to the theoretical interest in mere things, and the spiritual world is what there is for a subject that lives among persons and goods. The sequence of the sections is therefore not, without more, a sequence of things built on one another; whether it is that as well has to be found out from what the analyses say. And since an attitude can be held without being noticed, a subject can take the correlate of its attitude for all there is. Husserl has a word for this as well, which we shall meet twice: the subject of the attitude is then "forgotten".

### 1.4 Seven uses and the one that carries the concept of reality

Husserl does not define "constitution" in *Ideas II*, and the word serves him for operations that differ a good deal. Before any claim is made about "the" concept, they should be laid side by side. I count seven, and I do not think the list complete.

(1) Presentation (*Darstellung*). A spatial shape with its sensuous filling is given in a manifold of sides and aspects that "adumbrate" it; the shape is one, the aspects many. This is how what Husserl calls the schema of a thing is constituted. (2) Realization (*Realisierung*). A real property, the elasticity of a spring or the colour a thing has in the dark, is given in a series of states that depend on circumstances; Husserl says that the property "manifests" itself in them. This is how a thing becomes more than a schema, and the next part is devoted to it. (3) Spontaneous constitution. States of affairs, collections, values, goods and works are constituted in acts built on other acts, and they add to an object new strata of sense. (4) Institution (*Stiftung*). A conviction once formed remains mine; "every 'opinion' is an institution that remains the subject's possession" until motives arise against it (113/120–121). Husserl treats this under the title of a "constitution of unities within the immanent sphere", and stresses that it concerns pure consciousness "before the constitution of the real psychic subject" (117/124–125). (5) Comprehension (*Komprehension*). A person is given with a body, a meaning with a written page, in a unity of expression and what is expressed. (6) Intersubjective constitution. An objective nature, the same for everyone, is constituted in the mutual understanding of several subjects, for each of whom things appear differently. (7) Self-constitution. The personal ego "constitutes itself in the genesis that pervades the flow of lived experience" (251/260–261), before any reflection finds it.

Two observations follow at once. The first is that the uses are not seven species of one genus. In (1), (2) and (6) a unity is given through a manifold of appearances; in (3) and (5) a sense is laid on, or lives in, something already given; in (4) and (7) the constituting subject acquires something itself. The general formula that an object is "formed and given its particular structure" by acts of consciousness, which a recent dictionary of Husserl's terms takes over from the neo-Kantian use of the word, covers (3) well and the others poorly.[^3] The second observation is that Husserl uses the word even where nothing appears. The pure ego, he writes, "is constituted as a unity with reference to this unity of the stream; that is to say, it can find itself as identical in its course" (112/116). The ego so described has no sides and no states that depend on circumstances, and "constituted" here says only that an identity is there to be found. We shall have to ask in part 5 what this use has to do with the others.

Which of the seven carries the argument of the book? Its three titles speak of the constitution of material nature, of animal nature and of the spiritual world, and the first two sections are throughout about realities: the thing, the body, the soul, the human being as a natural object. What Husserl means by a reality he says with all the clarity one could wish, and he says it in terms of use (2). A thing is real in so far as its properties manifest themselves in states that depend on circumstances; "reality" is the title for this relation and for nothing else. Use (1) is its presupposition, since the states of a thing are schemata, and use (6) its completion, since only the thing identical for all is objectively real. The other four uses enter the book at the points where the form of reality meets something it cannot simply take in: values and tools in (3), the ego in (4) and (7), the other person in (5). I shall therefore take realization as the thread, and ask of each region what becomes of it there. What realization is must first be learned where Husserl learned it, on the thing.

## 2. The thing and its circumstances

### 2.1 What a thing has over a phantom

Husserl comes to the concept of reality by a question that looks narrower: what does a material thing have that a mere filled shape lacks? His method, stated at the head of § 15, is to refuse the inherited answers and to follow the perception of a thing in the directions it itself indicates, running through in fact or in phantasy the series of further perceptions in which the same thing would show more of what it is. Carried out on a thing at rest and taken by itself, the method yields a surprise. If we hold to the thing "apart from the nexus of things", we have "no means at all of distinguishing the essence of the thing from the essence of an empty phantom" (36/38–41).

The example is the stereoscope. Two flat pictures fuse and I see a body in space, about which meaningful questions can be asked and truly answered: "this is a red, rough pyramid" (36/38–41). But whether it is heavy or light, elastic, magnetic, are questions for which the perception gives no foothold. The properties of this group are not left undetermined, as the colour of the unseen back of a thing is left undetermined; they are not represented in the apprehension at all. "In this way", Husserl adds, "we also see a rainbow, the blue sky, the sun" (37/40–41). He calls what is seen in such cases a phantom, and the spatial shape with its sensuous filling, which the phantom shares with the thing, the schema.

The point of the comparison lies in what it shows about the thing. A thing that rests and does not change shows us no more than its schema. It is apprehended as material, and yet in what is properly given "nothing would change if the whole stratum of materiality were struck out of the apperception" (37/40–41). The same holds when the thing moves or alters: what comes to actual perception is a continuous run of schemata, and a phantom can move, deform and change colour as well. Materiality, then, is not one more content among the sensuous contents. However long I look at the thing alone, I shall not see it.

### 2.2 The relation to circumstances

The answer is given in one step. "Up to now we have taken the thing in isolation. But the thing is what it is in relation to 'circumstances'" (41/44–45). Husserl's first case is illumination. The same unchanged thing looks different in daylight, in coloured light, at dusk, and it does so in a determinate way: to each change in the source of light there belongs a change in the schema. Here, he says, lies what the phantom lacks.

> Reality in the proper sense, which is here called materiality, does not lie in the mere sensuous schema, in what could belong to the perceived if there were for it nothing like a relation to "circumstances" … rather, it lies precisely in this relation and in the manner of apprehension that corresponds to it. (41/44–45)

Two things are said in this sentence, and both matter for everything that follows. The first is that reality is a relation. It is not found in the thing taken alone, and no closer inspection of the schema could find it there. The second is that the relation belongs to a "manner of apprehension". The series of schemata is experienced differently when the schemata are taken as running off in dependence on something else: they are then "experienced as manifestations (*Bekundungen*) of one and the same" (41/44–45). What is one and the same through the changing looks of the thing under changing light is its objective colour, the colour it has, as Husserl puts it, whether it hangs in sunshine or "in the dark of the wardrobe" (42/44–47). No single look is this colour, and the sum of the looks is not it either. The colour is the unity that holds through them according to the circumstances.

### 2.3 The steel spring

Husserl's second example allows the structure to be seen more exactly, and I shall go through it once in my own person and vary it. The stretch that follows is a description carried out on his example. It tests what his sentences say.

"A steel spring, when struck, performs certain oscillations" (42/44–47). I hold the strip of steel at one end and flick the other. It swings, quickly and narrowly, and comes to rest. I flick it harder: it swings more widely and for longer. I bend it slowly and let go: it returns. In each case I see a course of changing shapes and positions, a series of schemata, and in each case the series answers to what I did. What I call the elasticity of the spring is not any one of these series. It is that the spring answers in this way, each time, to that. Husserl's formula for this is "under like circumstances, like consequences" (42/44–47), and he at once guards the formula against a misunderstanding: the general rule "is not conscious as such; it expresses a form that belongs to the apperception of the real property" (42/44–47). I do not see oscillations and then infer a law. I see the spring as elastic, and seeing it so already contains the articulation into what happens to it and how it responds.

Now the variations. First, I leave the spring alone. Nothing happens. Husserl counts this case in: absence of change is the limiting case of change and falls under the same rule. But it has a consequence. The spring at rest shows me its shape and its grey sheen and nothing of its elasticity; the property is meant, and it is not given. It would be given only if I brought about the circumstances. A real property is therefore, from the start, one that can be absent from what is seen while the thing is fully in view.

Second, I transfer the spring into the stereoscope. I now see a strip of the same shape and colour, and it may even be seen to swing if the pictures are made to change. But there is nothing I could do to it, and nothing that happens in its surroundings could be that to which its swinging answers. The question whether it is elastic has lost its sense. This variant breaks the concept: with the circumstances removed, the same series of schemata no longer manifests anything.

Third, I hold the real spring in a flame until it glows, and let it cool. Flicked again, it bends and stays bent. Husserl uses this very case: the spring "is annealed, it loses its elasticity" (47/50–51). The property has changed. But it has changed under circumstances, and under the same circumstances another spring of the same steel would change in the same way. Hence a new unity appears, of which elasticity and its loss are in turn the states. Husserl draws the conclusion in general form: "the constitution of real properties can also take place at higher levels", such that "in unities of attestation higher unities in their turn attest themselves" (46/48–51).

Fourth, I try to imagine a spring that goes slack from one moment to the next with nothing whatever having happened to it or around it. Husserl asks this question himself, for elasticity, for colour that fades and for water that dries up, and he does not answer that it is inconceivable. That nothing changes without a cause is, he says, "presumably no a priori necessity" (49/52–53). What he holds is that the apprehension of a thing as real prescribes the search for circumstances, and that natural science has made of this prescription a strict idea. I note the hesitation because it shows where the necessity lies. It does not lie in the things, as a law that they could not break. It lies in the sense of "real property": a change that answered to nothing would manifest no property.

The scope of this description should be stated. It was carried out on a solid body, by one perceiver, with a body that functions as usual, and it took the circumstances from the surroundings of the thing. Husserl makes the first restriction himself and justifies it: solid bodies are the things given first, on which the others are understood (53/56–59). The second and third are the ones he lifts in § 18.

### 2.4 Unity, state, circumstance

We can now name the terms. There is, first, a manifold of what Husserl calls states: the momentary looks and positions of the spring, each of which, taken by itself, is a schema. In the apprehension of the thing the schema "is perceived as 'attestation' (originary manifestation) of a real property" and thereby as a state of the thing at that moment (43/46–47). I shall render *Beurkundung* by "attestation" and *Bekundung* by "manifestation"; Husserl uses the first for the case in which the manifesting series actually runs off and the property is thus given in the original, and the second more widely (122–23/128–131). There are, secondly, the circumstances, themselves real, to which the states belong. And there is the unity that holds through the states in their dependence on the circumstances: the property, and through the properties the thing. Where the dependences actually come to givenness, Husserl does not hesitate to say that causality is perceived: the causalities are then "not merely supposed but 'seen', 'perceived'" (43/46–47).

The thing itself is nothing behind this. A note says that "substance signifies here nothing further than the material thing as such", considered as the identical of real properties that realize themselves "in regulated manifolds of states in regulated dependence on pertinent circumstances" (44 n./46–49). And at the end of the chapter Husserl says what the whole analysis was for. He had "on purpose" let "the general of thinghood, 'reality'", stand out more strongly than what belongs to materiality in particular; and "that general something, which would surely best be designated as reality, is called substance" (54/58–59). The material thing has been analysed for the sake of a concept wider than materiality.

I shall call the structure so described the form of circumstances: a unity, a manifold of states in which it shows itself, and circumstances on which the states depend according to a rule. The label is mine. Husserl's own name for it, which we shall meet in § 31, is the "formal-general concept of reality", and his shortest statement of it is that realities "are what they are only in relation to other actual and possible realities" (126/132–135). It should be noticed how much this already contains. A thing is real through its relation to other things; these are real through theirs; no member of the nexus has its reality apart from the nexus. The last paragraph of the book will call nature a field of thoroughgoing relativities. So far as things among things are concerned, the phrase only restates § 15.

### 2.5 A first sense of "level"

The schema, which is a state in relation to the real property, is itself a unity in relation to something else. A shape is given in many aspects as I move my eyes and head, a colour in many shadings; and Husserl is careful to keep this relation apart from the one just described. The attestation of a shape through adumbrations, for all it has formally in common with any constitution of unities in manifolds, "is something in principle other than the manifestation of real properties through states", and "at the level of the schema there is no talk of substantial reality and causality" (131/138–139). Presentation and realization are two operations.

They are nevertheless joined in a definite way, and the way they are joined gives the word "level" its first precise sense in the book. What is a unity in one relation serves as manifold in the next. Aspects are unities of sensation data and manifolds for the schema; schemata are unities of aspects and states of the real property; properties of the first order are states of properties of a higher order. Husserl describes the constitutive make-up of the thing as a "remarkable stratification" which is "only a kind of continuation of another, analogous stratification in which the sensuous schema is already constituted" (53/56–59), and elsewhere he describes the regress: going back step by step from the unities to the manifolds that constitute them, we come at last "to the data of the lowest level, the sensation data in immanent time" (130/136–139). The summary in § 18 says the same in the forward direction. The real world "constitutes itself in levels", with the manifold of sense-things as lower stratum, and "realization is completed in such a way that the sense-things become states of real things" (65/68–71).

A level in this sense is a unity that functions as the manifesting manifold of a higher unity. It is what *Ideas I* called a "necessary intermediate link", and it is what Mattens has in view when he observes that the phantom can be contrasted with a lower and with a higher level. He is right to make it exemplary: within the constitution of the thing, analysis does proceed by taking away an apprehension and seeing what unity is left. But two features of such levels should be marked before the word is applied elsewhere. The lower level is not another reality on which the higher one rests. The schema is not a second object beneath the spring; it is the spring as it looks now. And the lower level is not independent of the higher in the way a foundation is independent of a house: in the perception of a thing the schema is from the start taken as a state, and it is isolated as a mere schema only by abstraction or in the stereoscope.

### 2.6 The body among the circumstances

The third chapter of the first section opens by confessing a restriction. The thing described so far is the thing for a single subject thought of in isolation, and thought of in such a way that this subject "remains, as it were, self-forgotten, and forgotten also by the one who analyses" (55/58–61). The forgetting has to be undone, because the qualities of things as they stand before me in intuition prove "dependent on my own make-up, that of the experiencing subject, related to my body and my 'normal sensibility'" (56/60–63). The thing has circumstances that were left out, and they lie on my side.

Husserl distinguishes two kinds of sensation that take part in every perception of a spatial thing. Some are apprehended as features of the thing, as its colour or its roughness. Others, the sensations of my own movement, receive no such apprehension and yet are indispensable, because the first kind run off in dependence on them: if I turn my eyes so, the image shifts so. It is in describing this dependence that he writes the sentence quoted at the beginning.

> In all constitution and at all levels we necessarily have "circumstances" related to one another and what "belongs" to the circumstances; everywhere we find the "if–then" or "because–therefore". (57/62–63)

The sentence is said of the constitution of spatial things, at all its levels, and it reaches further down than § 15 did. Even the phantom has its circumstances. A coloured shape seen in the stereoscope is, as Husserl had already noted, "an 'appearance' that refers back to kinaesthetic 'circumstances' to which it belongs" (22/22–25). The form of circumstances therefore holds for presentation too. But the "if–then" at this lowest level is of a particular kind, and Husserl names it without emphasis. The sensations that present the thing are "apperceptively related to motivating series, to systems of kinaesthetic sensations" (57/62–63); the sense-things are constantly intended as "motivating for the pertinent aspects as motivated" (65/68–71). That my moving and the changing look of the thing belong together is, in his vocabulary, a relation of motivation. It is not a causal relation between two things. The word had been used in passing before, for the way in which one partial apprehension of a thing points on to others (19/20–21; 40/42–45). It will return in the third section as the name of the fundamental law of spiritual life. Here it names the rule under which there are appearances of things at all.

A second kind of dependence on my side is of another sort again. If I take santonin, things look yellow, as through yellow spectacles; if an organ is injured or diseased, the things perceived by means of it appear altered (73/76–79). Such dependences Husserl calls psychophysical and prefers to describe by the word "conditionality" (*Konditionalität*), on the ground that a cause in the proper sense is something real, whereas "the subjective is, over against reality, an irreality" (64/68–69). They are regular, and they are bound up with causality at one distinguished place: "my body" is the thing at which "a system of subjective conditionality is interwoven with this system of causality" (64/68–69). But they do not enter into what the thing is. The world remains the one that shows itself to normal perception, the altered looks are accounted for by the altered organ, and the conclusion is drawn sharply: "to the constitution of the thing anomalies can contribute nothing"; what they contribute "is only the rule of my subjectivity" (74/78–79).

### 2.7 The form preserved in the thing of physics

The first section does not end with the thing of intuition, and its last step shows once more that what Husserl is following is a form. The dependence of the looks of things on the state of my body leaves a problem that a single subject cannot always resolve. If one of my organs gives testimony that conflicts with the rest, I can usually discount it as abnormal; but Husserl notes that the case may remain wholly undecided "so long as we take the experiencing subject as solipsistic" (73/76–79). The thing of intuition is relative to a normal sensibility, and what counts as normal is settled among several subjects who understand one another. With them comes the demand for a determination of the thing that holds for everyone, whatever his senses: the thing as correlate of any rational subject, determined in logical and mathematical form, of which the intuited qualities are then appearances.

It would be natural to suppose that this highest level of the constitution of the thing replaces the structure found at the lower one. Husserl says the opposite. The higher constitution ascribes to the thing a stock of persisting mathematical properties, "but in such a way that the general structure of the thing, the form of reality-causality, is preserved" (132/138–141). The states too are mathematized, and the causality of the thing of physics indicates the manifold of sensuous causalities. The physicist's thing is again a unity of properties that show themselves in states under circumstances according to laws; only the manifold has changed, which now consists of the things of intuition as they are given to the several subjects. The step from the seen thing to the thing of physics is one more level of manifestation, and what is kept across it is the three-term form and nothing else. This is the place where the text itself uses the word "form" for what the thing hands on, and it is used there to introduce the contrast with the soul, to which, as we shall see, the mathematical version of the form does not apply at all.

By the end of the first section, then, Husserl has three kinds of "if–then" in hand, although he has announced only one concept of reality. There is the motivation that ties appearances to my movements; the causality that ties the states of a thing to other things; and the conditionality that ties what I sense to the state of my body. Only the second constitutes real properties of things. The first lies beneath it, as the rule under which there are schemata to be realized. The third lies beside it, and constitutes, as we shall see, properties of something other than the thing. The received view counts one basis, material nature. The text has so far given us a thing that is real only in a causal nexus, a nexus that is given only in appearances motivated by a moving body, and a body at which causality turns into something else. What that something else constitutes is the question of the second section.


## 3. Body and soul: the form carried over

### 3.1 A reality "in a similar sense"

The first section had announced the second in the language of layers: animal realities are "founded realities, which presuppose in themselves, as a lower level, material realities", bodies that have, besides their material determinations, "new systems of properties, the psychic ones" (33/34–37). The second section has to say what this founding consists in. It does not begin with the body. After an introduction and a chapter on the pure ego it turns to the soul, and it introduces the soul by a comparison. The soul is "in a similar sense as the material body-thing a substantial-real unity, in contrast to the pure ego", which is no such unity (120/126–129). Everything that follows in the chapter is an attempt to say how far "similar" reaches.

Husserl first says what the soul is not. It is not the stream of lived experiences that belongs to this body, and it is nothing that occurs in this stream as a real part of it. It is "a unity that is essentially related to the stream and yet in a certain sense transcendent to it" (121/128–129). The subject of this unity has its lived experiences and lives in them, but they "are not its properties"; they are "its mere modes of comportment, its mere 'psychic states'" (121/128–129). What stands to them as the property stands to the states of a thing is something else: a person's character, abilities, dispositions, the sharpness of sight, the gift for mathematics, the kindness. Each of these is related to groups of actual and possible lived experiences as a material property is related to the schematic appearances in which it shows itself. Husserl states the result in the vocabulary of § 15: "psychic properties are thus 'unities of manifestation'" (121–22/128–131).

The analogy is then pressed in three respects. As a thing is not experienced as material by merely being looked at, so a trait of character is not given in one experience; one has to follow the course of experiences in which it is not only manifested but attested. As properties of a thing can be states of higher properties, so there are levels in the psychic sphere, built on one another "not in the manner of a mere 'summation' but in that of a 'constitution'" (123/130–131): the changing power of sight under changing conditions manifests the one changing property of visual acuity. And as the thing is nothing behind its properties, so "the soul itself, like the thing itself, is nothing further than the unity of its properties" (123/130–131). The phrase about summation deserves a remark. Husserl here opposes constitution to addition: a higher unity is constituted in lower ones when it shows itself in them, and this is the sense of "level" we met in the thing.

What supports the analogy is named expressly as "the main point". In the material sphere the modes of comportment refer back to real circumstances, and "only in the interplay of modes of comportment and circumstances of the comportment" does the real property manifest itself; "exactly so with the soul" (124/130–133). The soul comports itself under circumstances and in a regulated way, and this, Husserl insists, is not a fact established from outside. It lies in the apprehension through which there is such a thing as a soul for us. The contrast with the pure ego confirms the point from the other side. One also says of the pure ego that it "comports itself" so and so in its acts and that it has states. But this is a "totally different" concept of state from the one that holds in the sphere of reality, where all states are, in accordance with the constituting apprehension, related to circumstances; "causality and substantiality are not external annexes, but refer back to fundamental kinds of apperception" (124/130–133).

So the form of circumstances is carried over whole: a unity, the soul or one of its properties; a manifold of states, the lived experiences; circumstances under which the soul comports itself. § 31 draws the conclusion that the two analyses share an "ontological form" and gives it its name, the formal-general concept of reality (125–26/132–135). The following paragraphs take the form apart term by term. I shall follow them in that order, since what changes in each term is what the layered reading has to overlook.

### 3.2 The manifold: states that are not appearances

"The thing constitutes itself as a unity of schemata, more precisely, as a unity of causal necessity in the nexus of dependences" which presents itself in manifolds of schemata. "The soul, on the other hand, does not schematize itself" (127/134–135).

The difference concerns what serves as state. For the thing a state is a schema, and a schema is already a unity given through adumbration: it has sides, it is seen from here, it is transcendent to the experiences in which it appears. A psychic state is not a unity of this kind. Apart from the higher apprehension that makes them states of a soul, psychic states are "nothing other than the immanently perceivable lived experiences of the immanent flow of lived experience", the very flow "in which all 'transcendent' being ultimately manifests itself" (131/138–139). A joy does not show itself now from this side and now from that. It is lived through, and nothing stands between it and the one who lives it.

Two consequences follow, of which Husserl draws the first. Since the manifold of the soul is not spatial, the soul has no shape and no parts; it cannot be divided as a thing can, and a soul cannot remain unchanged as a thing can, because its states are phases of a flow (133–34/140–143). The second consequence is mine, though the text gives all its premisses. The lived experiences are the manifold in which things are constituted: my seeing of the spring belongs to them. If the same lived experiences are now also the states of a soul, then one and the same manifold stands in two relations of constitution at once. As seeings they present the spring; as my states they manifest my sight, my attention, my impatience. The third section will say this in so many words: in the stream of subjective experiences "the subject manifests itself as real personality", whereas in the sensuous schemata "it is not the subject that manifests itself but the things of its surrounding world" (202/212–213). The soul is therefore not constituted out of a material of its own, lying above the material of the thing. It is a second unity read off the same experiences, under another rule.

### 3.3 The circumstances: the body, the past, the others

With respect to the circumstances Husserl again distinguishes, and his distinction is the centre of the chapter. The soul depends on circumstances in three ways, which he calls a kind of stratification of its dependences (135/142–143).

The first is its dependence on the body. All sensations, and through them and their reproductions the whole life of consciousness, depend on the body and on the physical nature in which the body stands. Husserl says precisely what this means for constitution: it means "that the apprehension which constitutes the psychic real assigns to it real properties which find their 'circumstances' in the body and its bodily causalities" (135/142–143). The second way is new. Consciousness shows itself to be "dependent, so to speak, on itself" (136/142–143). Within one and the same soul the present stock of lived experience depends on the earlier ones; nothing that has been lived vanishes without trace; "every lived experience leaves dispositions behind and creates something new with regard to psychic reality" (133/140–141). Association, habit and memory are the familiar titles, but the motivated change of a conviction or of a way of feeling belongs here as well. Husserl calls this side of the soul idiopsychic. The third way is the dependence of one soul on others, which he names and sets aside.

It is the second dependence that strains the form. § 33 holds on to what is common: under the formal idea "unity of lasting properties in relation to pertinent circumstances" both thing and soul can be ranged (136/142–143). But circumstances, it now appears, can be outer or inner, and this difference divides the realities.

> Material things are conditioned exclusively from outside and are not conditioned by their own past; they are *realities without history*. (137/142–145)

A material thing could, as an ideal possibility, return in a cycle to exactly the outer circumstances in which it once was, and it would then have to be in exactly the same total state. A soul cannot. Two identical cycles of outer circumstances would act on the same soul, and yet its states could not be the same the second time, because the earlier state helps to determine the later: "psychic realities have a history" (137/142–145).

The sentence is easily read as a remark on a peculiarity of the soul, and it is more than that. In the form of circumstances as the thing exhibited it, the unity and its circumstances were two: the spring here, the blow there. Now the unity is among its own circumstances. What the soul has become is part of that to which its present comportment answers. The rule "under like circumstances, like consequences" still holds in form, but its condition can never be met twice, since the circumstances include everything the soul has undergone. A reality with a history is a unity of manifestation for which experiment, in the sense in which I flicked the spring a second time, is excluded in principle.

### 3.4 The rule: conditionality

The third term changes with the second. If one takes the idea of substance, as Kant did, from the thing of mathematical physics, then, Husserl says, "there is no soul-substance": the soul has no "in itself" as nature has, neither a mathematical nature like the thing of physics nor a nature like the thing of intuition, since it is no schematized unity. And "if we call causality that functional or lawful relation of dependence which is the correlate of the constitution of persisting properties of a persisting real of the type nature, then with the soul one cannot speak of causality at all. Not every lawfully regulated functionality in the sphere of facts is causality" (132/138–141). The soul is a being "conditionally" related to bodily circumstances; and neither soul nor body thereby acquires a property of nature in the sense of physics.

The word that was introduced in § 18 for the dependence of appearances on the state of the body thus becomes the name of the rule under which the soul is real. Husserl is willing to speak of causality here in a widened sense. If we extend the concepts and call every existent that is related to conditioning circumstances of existence a substance, and every property constituted as so conditioned a causal one, then the soul has "a quasi-nature and a quasi-causality" (138/144–145). But the prefix is meant. We have, by the end of § 33, one formal concept of reality and two realizations of it that differ in every term: schemata there and lived experiences here; outer circumstances there, and here circumstances that lie partly in another reality and partly in the unity's own past; causality there and conditionality here.

### 3.5 The body, where the causal turns

The body is treated in the third chapter of the section, after the soul, and it is treated as the place where the two rules meet. Husserl begins from the hand. When my hand lies on the table I feel the table, firm, cold and smooth; and if I attend to the hand I find in it sensations of touch, of smoothness and cold. One and the same sensation is involved: "the same sensation of pressure, with the hand lying on the table", is apprehended once as perception of the table's surface and yields, with another direction of attention, sensations of pressure in the finger (145/152–153). Husserl coins a word for sensations so localized, which I shall render as "sensings" (*Empfindnisse*), and observes that merely material things have nothing of the kind.

The sensings are not properties of the body as a physical thing. They arise when and where the body is touched, pressed or warmed, so that the body proves to have fields of sensation whose state changes with what happens to it. The apprehension of this, too, has the form we know. To belong to the body means here a relation of "the phenomenological 'if–then': I sense when the hand is touched, pushed" (155/160–163), and the result is stated in the terms of § 33: "the sensitivity of the body thus constitutes itself throughout as a 'conditional' or psychophysical property" (155/160–163). The body so apprehended is "constituted as an objectivity of its own, which falls under the formal-general concept of reality", as something that keeps its identical properties over against changing outer circumstances; only that "the relations of dependence in which it stands to outer nature are other than those of material things among themselves" (156/162–163).

The body is thus constituted more than once, and under more than one of the uses listed in part 1. As a shape presented in aspects it is, in Husserl's dry phrase, "a remarkably incompletely constituted thing" (159/164–167), since I cannot walk round it and some of it I never see. As a material thing among things it stands in the causal nexus: it can be pushed, cut, warmed, lit. As the bearer of sensings it is a reality of a different kind, real under the rule of conditionality. The summary of § 42 binds the last two together in one image. The body is the "turning point" (*Umschlagspunkt*) "at which the causal relations are converted into conditional ones between the outer world and the bodily-psychic subject" (161/166–167). Heat reaches the hand by a causal path; that I then feel warmth is no further step on that path.

### 3.6 A second sense of "level": founding

We are now in a position to say what it means in *Ideas II* that the soul is founded on the body, and to see that it does not mean what "level" meant in the constitution of the thing.

The schema was a lower level in the sense that the real property shows itself in it. The body is not a lower level of the soul in this sense. My character does not show itself in the states of my body as elasticity shows itself in the swinging of the spring; it shows itself in my lived experiences. The body is lower in another respect: some of the circumstances under which the soul comports itself lie in it. That is the whole content of the founding. Husserl says so at the end of § 33, in a passage that begins by granting the naturalistic picture and ends by taking away its independent basis. The human being is in nature only because the body is a material thing in spatial nature; and then: "Psychic reality is constituted as reality only through the psychophysical dependences. It has its unity in itself, but here it comes into consideration as unity in a nexus" (139/144–147).

The third section, looking back, makes the definition explicit and adds a test. Suppose, Husserl says, that body and spirit merely ran parallel, so that certain bodily occurrences regularly went with certain psychic ones and one could infer the one from the other. "A parallelistic connection of this kind creates no reality of a higher level." There would be two realities, each with its own states; no new real property would arise, and there would be no causality joining them, "for that would presuppose that the two realities took on, with respect to their real states, the function of circumstances for one another" (246/256–257). The test follows: on the supposition made, "striking out the one reality would change nothing for the other" (246/256–257). Only because this is not so, because illness of the body has consequences for the soul and the will has consequences in the body, is the human being a real unity (246–47/256–259); "it is precisely through such causality that a founded unity of its own kind is made possible" (246/256–257). A founded reality, then, is a reality whose states depend on another reality as on circumstances, and it is of two levels in so far as there is a "dependence of the occurrences of the upper level on those of the lower" (245/254–257).

Three things distinguish founding in this sense from the levels of part 2. It is a relation between two realities, each of which is a unity of manifestation in its own manifold. It is in principle reciprocal, since each is to serve as circumstance "for the other", although the body has a privilege we shall come to. And it is the same relation that constitutes reality in the first place. The soul is not first real and then, in addition, founded; it is real in the world to the extent that it stands in these dependences. The programme of *Ideas I* spoke of psychic realities "founded" in physical ones as of a relation between finished items. The analysis has turned the relation into the condition under which the upper item is a reality at all. It has also changed what the upper item is. § 33 ends by saying that what is to be set over against material nature as a second kind of reality "is not the 'soul' but the concrete unity of body and soul", the human or animal subject (139/144–147). The soul taken by itself is real only as one side of this unity.

### 3.7 A test on an unread text

When the plan of this study was fixed I had not read the second part of supplement XII, whose first three paragraphs are entitled "The reality of the soul and of the human being", "Psychophysical causality and the causal nexus of things" and "Possibility of inserting the soul into nature". If the reading given above is right, three things were to be expected of it: that it would determine the reality of the soul by dependence on circumstances and not as a layer laid on the body; that it would separate the dependence of soul on body from the causality of things; and that it would let the soul belong to nature only so far as that dependence reaches. I report what the text says, including what I did not foresee.[^4]

It begins exactly where § 15 does. To clarify the reality of the soul one must ask where the concept of reality has its origin, and the answer is the thing (340/350–351): its reality "shows itself in dependence on circumstances; the thing is relative to other things" (341/350–353). Body and soul are then described by mutual dependence. The physical body is not "a concrete reality for itself", it is a human body only because changes in the soul go with it; "and likewise the soul is not a reality for itself" (341/350–353). The second paragraph opens with a retrospect in the first person: "I have tried to distinguish between psychophysical conditionality and physical causality" (343/352–355). The distinction is upheld and sharpened. A thing has real properties only in relation to the causal nexus of physical nature, and "through psychophysical causality no constitutive feature of any persisting spatial thing is constituted" (344/354–357). The third paragraph repeats §§ 32–33 nearly word for word and adds that the soul, even when psychophysical properties are ascribed to it as something like a nature, "in principle does not dissolve into nature" (345/356–357). On all three points the text says what was expected.

Two things I had not foreseen. The first speaks for the layered reading. The body is "privileged" in the unity of the human being, because the universal form of the world is space-time, to be in space is to be a physical body, and "everything real is corporeal, in any case corporeal, though not necessarily that alone" (342/352–353); psychic reality is what it is as reality in the world only "through the bodily reality that founds it" (343/352–355). Husserl thus uses the word "founding" for precisely the relation of dependence, and he grants the body a priority that is not reciprocal: there are bodies without souls and, in the world, no souls without bodies. The second goes the other way and further than I expected. "The soul has in itself an essence of its own, which precedes all the causalities in which it is interwoven", in contrast to the physical body, "which is causal through and through and has no prior essence of its own" (346/356–357). We shall need this sentence when we come to § 64.

### 3.8 Realization from outside

One feature of the second section remains, and it shows from yet another side that the human being as a two-layered reality is a result and not a starting point. So long as I consider only what I can experience of myself, Husserl says, I do not find my psychic life in my body. I find the sensings localized in it, and I find it as the organ of my movements; but it would never occur to me "seriously to put into my body", to introject, my ego, my acts and the appearances I have of things (167/174–175). The unity that the naturalistic attitude calls a human being, a material thing with psychic strata laid in it, is not given in self-experience at all.

It is given in the experience of others. I see another body, understand it as the body of a subject, and take the psychic life I thus co-perceive together with the body as one objective item; "only with empathy" and with this steady direction of experience "does the closed unity 'human being' constitute itself, and I then transfer it to myself" (167/174–175). The apprehension that results is described in the vocabulary of part 2: it is an apperception that "realizes the whole psychic life and being into a kind of unity of appearance", an identical something of manifold appearances and of states localized in them (169/176–177). The soul as a reality in nature is a realization, and one that I perform on myself by taking up the standpoint of someone else.

Two formulations of the chapter on the soul stand side by side at the end of this section, and I do not think the text reconciles them. The soul "is nothing further than the unity of its properties"; that is the thing-model, and it makes the soul exactly as much as its regulated comportment under circumstances. And the soul "has its unity in itself"; that is said in the same breath as its constitution "only through" psychophysical dependences, and the supplement turns it into an essence that precedes all causality. On the first formulation the soul is a reality and nothing else. On the second there is something that enters into the form of reality and is not produced by it. The second section does not say what this is. It has shown that the form of circumstances can be carried from the thing to the body and the soul, and what the carrying costs: a manifold that is no longer appearance, circumstances that include the unity's own past, a rule that is no longer causality. Whether anything of the form is left when we come to the person, and what the unity "in itself" might be, the third section has to say.

A footnote in the supplement notes that parts of its § 3 were used in § 32 of the main text (344 n.). I use it as a parallel statement of the doctrine of §§ 30–33 and draw no conclusion from its date.


## 4. The person and what motivates

### 4.1 An attitude that forgets itself

The third section opens with a change of attitude, and it describes the order of the first two sections from the new standpoint. In the naturalistic attitude, Husserl says, "what is experienced at the bottom is material (physical) nature, and founded in it is the experience of bodily and psychic being" (211/220–221); he even proposes to turn the usual word round and say "physio-psychic", since the reversed expression "indicates quite fittingly the order of the founding" (183/190–193). The layered reading can cite both sentences. Each of them, however, describes how things stand in one attitude, and the same pages say what rank that attitude has. The naturalistic and the personalistic attitude are not on a par: the first "subordinates itself" to the second and gains a certain independence only "through an abstraction, or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal ego" (183–84/190–193).

The word had occurred before. The thing of the first section was the thing for a subject that remained "self-forgotten", and the forgetting was undone by bringing in the body. Now a second forgetting is named. What the natural scientist leaves out of account is himself as a person, who looks, measures, values the knowledge he gains and speaks of it with others, and for whom there are things and bodies at all. The founding order is the order of what such a person finds when he attends to nature alone.

Husserl sees that this makes the argument of the book look circular, and says so. At the beginning nature was posited outright, as the natural scientist posits it, and persons were "subordinate objects of nature"; once the essence of personality was pursued, nature appeared as "something that constitutes itself in the intersubjective association of persons and thus presupposes it". "We seem to fall here into a vicious circle" (210/218–221). One half of the circle is the received view; the other is its reversal. The distinctions of parts 2 and 3 allow us to say, before following Husserl further, why the two halves do not close into a circle of the vicious kind. That the soul is founded on the body means that some of the circumstances of its states lie in the body. That nature is constituted among persons means that there are things only as unities of appearances, and appearances only for subjects who move, perceive and understand one another. The first is a dependence between two realities, under the rule of conditionality. The second concerns the manifold in which any reality is given. A can depend on B in the first way while B depends on A in the second, since the two relations are different. This way of dissolving the circle is not stated in the text. But the text supplies its elements, and the third section adds the one still missing: the rule under which persons are what they are.

### 4.2 The surrounding world as circumstance

"As person I am what I am (and every other person is what he is) as *subject of a surrounding world*" (185/192–195). With this sentence the form of circumstances is taken up a third time, and its second term is named first. A person has circumstances, and they are called the surrounding world (*Umwelt*). The definition that follows separates it at once from nature. The surrounding world is the world perceived, remembered, believed and valued by the person; it is "not a world 'in itself' but a world 'for me'" (186/194–195). Physical reality belongs to it only so far as the person knows of it. Someone who knows nothing of the discoveries of physics does not have the world of physics for surroundings.

Two consequences are drawn. The first concerns the content of this world. In its core it is a world of sensuously appearing things, but on this core the person's valuing and practical acts have built: the thing that burns is fuel, the fuel is a useful good, the good a commodity. "All these are founded objects, which have constituted themselves for the ego through founded acts" (188/196–197). Here the spontaneous constitution of part 1 enters, and with it a third sense of "level", to which we shall return. The second consequence concerns the rule. The natural scientist says that stimuli go out from things and excite the nerves. For the person the word has another sense.

> In place of the causal relation between things and human beings as natural realities there enters the *relation of motivation* between persons and things, and these things are not the things of nature existing in themselves … but things experienced, thought or otherwise posited as such, intentional objectivities of personal consciousness. (189/196–199)

A later paragraph gives the criterion by which the two relations can be told apart. Between me and a thing there may be a real relation: light travels from it to my eye. There is also the intentional relation: I see it, it attracts me, I reach for it. "The real relation falls away if the thing does not exist; the intentional relation remains" (215/224–227). What attracts me, attracts me as it is given to me, and it would do so if I were deceived. Husserl calls the relation "motivational causality" and insists that it is "no real causality" (216/226–227).

The circumstances of the person are therefore of a different kind from the circumstances of the thing, of the body and of the soul. Those were realities. These are objects as meant. And the unity that stands in relation to them is a subject for whom they are there. The form is the same, a unity that comports itself under circumstances; each of its terms has changed once more.

### 4.3 Motivation, wide and narrow

"Motivation is the lawfulness of spiritual life" (220/230–231). The sentence stands at the head of § 56, and the paragraph that it opens is the most carefully divided of the book. It is also the place where the concept reaches down into the first section.

Husserl begins with motivation in the narrowest sense, in which I am the one motivated: I draw the conclusion because I have judged the premisses, I decide because I value. This is the motivation of position-takings by position-takings, and it stands under norms of reason. He then widens. The whole realm of association and habit belongs to motivation too, though no position is taken there: one thought recalls another, a present situation recalls a similar one and I expect the like. What makes these cases of motivation is that one can ask of them: how do I come to this, what has brought me to it? "That one can ask in this way characterizes all motivation whatsoever" (222/232–233). The motives may lie deep and may never have been noticed; they are still of the kind that could be brought to light by asking.

The third extension is the one that concerns us. The experience of things is itself motivated through and through. "The apprehensions of things and of thingly nexuses are 'webs of motivation'" (224–25/234–237): each partial intention points on to others, the given side to the sides not given, the present look to the looks that would follow. And then the relation described in § 18 returns under its new title. In the unity of the consciousness of a thing we have "'conditionalities', a 'because' and 'so'" to unfold in different directions: "if I turn my eyes so, if I set the optical series of experience going so, then I must see this and that" (226/236–237). The "if–then" that tied appearances to my movements was, as we saw, called motivation in passing in the first section. It is here counted expressly among the kinds of motivation, and the next sub-paragraph draws a conclusion of the widest reach.

> If we consider the structure of the consciousness that constitutes things, we see that all nature, with space, time, causality and so on, is completely dissolved into a web of immanent motivations. (226/236–237)

Three pages later we read that "the 'because–so' of motivation has a wholly different sense from causation in the sense of nature" (229/238–241). The two statements seem to pull against each other. If causality is something wholly different from motivation, how can nature, causality included, be dissolved into motivations? One receives the impression that Husserl gives two answers to the question of how the two rules are related.

On the one hand, they are two rules for two kinds of unity, and they exclude each other. Causal dependence holds between realities as realities; it holds whether or not anyone knows of it; its ideal is unambiguous determination by law. Motivation holds between what a subject is conscious of and what it then believes, feels or does; "what I do not 'know'", what does not stand before me as perceived, remembered or thought, "does not determine me spiritually" (231/240–243). The physiological processes in my nerves condition my sensations and do not motivate me. On the other hand, causality as something experienced is itself a unity that shows itself in motivated series. That the spring is elastic is seen in the answering of its swings to my flicks; and that I see this depends on appearances following my movements and one another in the motivated order that makes them appearances of one thing. The form of circumstances that constitutes the thing is embedded in a form of circumstances of another kind.

The text lets both answers stand, and I think it is right to do so, since they answer different questions: what rule holds among things, and under what rule there are things for someone. But the second answer has a consequence that Husserl formulates only in § 61, where sensibility with its associations and tendencies is called "a stratum of hidden reason, at least so far as the constitution of nature reaches", on the ground that all the complicated relations of the "if–then", "all causalities", can become clues for explicit theoretical unfolding (276/286–289). Motivation, which was to be the law of spirit as opposed to nature, turns out to lie beneath nature as well. The series of rules runs from the motivation of appearances through causality and conditionality to the motivation of persons. Its first and its last term bear the same name, and the two terms that concern nature stand between them.

### 4.4 The servant and his master

Among Husserl's examples of the "because" of motivation is this one: "The servant meets his master, and because he recognizes him as his master he greets him with deference" (230/240–241). I shall again go through the example and vary it, as a description of my own that tests what the text says.

I walk down a corridor; someone comes towards me; I see that it is the head of the house in which I serve; I step aside and greet him. If I am asked why I greeted, I say: because it was he. The "because" names what I saw and whom I took him to be. It does not name the light reflected from his face or the process in my retina, of which I know nothing and which I could not cite as my reason.

First variation: it was his brother, whom I took for him. I greeted in the same way and for the same reason; the reason was that I recognized my master, and I was mistaken. The motivating circumstance was the man as I took him to be. This is Husserl's criterion at work. The real relation to my master has fallen away, since he was not there, and the motivation is untouched. He makes the point with a stronger case: "I am afraid of the ghost" although I know that what I see is nothing real (232/242–243).

Second variation: it was my master, and I was reading a letter as he passed. Light and sound reached me as before. I did not greet. Nothing that belongs to the causal circumstances is missing, and everything that belongs to the motivating ones is. What does not stand before me does not move me.

Third variation: as he steps out of the shadow into the light of the window, my pupils contract. This too answers regularly to a circumstance, and it is something that happens in me. But the question "how did you come to that?" finds no purchase. I did not come to it; it does not manifest my deference or anything else about me as a person, and I learn of it, if at all, as I learn of the elasticity of a spring. The variant breaks the concept. A comportment that answers to circumstances of which the subject is in no way conscious is not motivated, however lawful it is.

Fourth variation: I have served in this house for twenty years and greet without thinking, as I would not have done in my first week. The greeting is still motivated, in Husserl's wide sense, and the question how I came to it now has an answer that leads into my past: I have always done so. Here the circumstance to which the act answers includes what I have become. We met this structure in the soul under the name of the idiopsychic dependence, the dependence of consciousness "on itself". In the person it is no longer one kind of dependence beside another. Every motive finds a subject already formed by its earlier motives.

The description was carried out for a person among persons, in a world of roles and customs, and its result holds within that scope. In motivation the circumstance is something as it is meant, it is there for the one it moves, and it moves him as the one he has come to be. What shows itself in such comportment is the person's own manner: in this case a certain deference, which the servant has and which another man in his place might lack.

### 4.5 Expression, and a third sense of "level"

How is another person given? The answer of the second section was empathy, and its result was the human being as a two-sided natural object. The third section describes the same experience in the personal attitude and reaches a different unity. When I listen to someone speak or watch him work, I do not find a body and, joined to it, a soul. I find a human being, and his body is "as body, through and through a body full of soul" (240/250–251). Husserl compares this with reading. I see the printed page, but I do not attend to the marks; I "live in the sense". The sense is not a second thing attached to the first: the spiritual "is nothing second, nothing appended, but precisely animating" (239/248–251). He calls a unity of this kind comprehensive, a unity of expression and what is expressed, and he places persons, books, tools, buildings, states and churches under the one title.

The relation of this unity to the founded reality of part 3 is fixed in two sentences. The belonging of the expressed spirit to the appearing body has "anything but the sense of the founded unity of nature 'human being'", the animal being that is an object of zoology; "rather, it precedes the constitution of every such unity" (244–45/254–257). And: "the unity of expression is a presupposition for the constitution of the founded reality", since through expression the other person "is there at all" for the one who experiences, and must be there in order to enter as one level into a real unity of two (245/254–257). The human being of the naturalistic attitude, the soul founded on a body, is thus a later constitution than the person who is understood in the body. First there is someone, and then this someone can be taken together with the body as a psychophysical object.

And yet Husserl speaks of founding here as well. The apprehension of the physical body is the "founding substrate" for the comprehending apprehension of sense (240/250–251); in a supplement he writes that "one can speak here too of a founded unity", in so far as the spirit is comprehended on the basis of the perception of the body (325/336–337). This is a third relation, different from the two we have met, and it is time to set the three side by side.

(i) A level of manifestation is a unity that serves as the manifold in which a higher unity shows itself. The schema is in this sense a lower level of the real thing, and the property of first order a lower level of the property of higher order. Lower and higher are here the same object under less and more apprehension. (ii) A founding by circumstances is the dependence of the states of one reality on another reality. The body founds the soul in this sense. Lower and higher are here two realities, each real in its own manifold, and the dependence is what makes the higher one a reality in the world. (iii) A founding of sense is the relation in which an apprehension presupposes another whose object it values, uses or understands. The mere thing founds the tool in this sense, the perceived body the person who expresses himself in it, the page the text. Lower and higher are here one object, to which a stratum of sense has accrued, and in living experience the lower is not attended to: the apprehension of the human being, Husserl says, "goes through" the appearance of the body and does not stop at it (240/250–251).

The ladder of the received view, thing, body, soul, spirit, takes one step of each kind and reads all three as steps of the same kind. From the schema to the material thing it climbs by (i). From the body to the soul it climbs by (ii). From the human animal to the person it would have to climb by (iii); and here the text says that the step leads in the other direction, since the unity of expression comes before the founded unity. I do not mean that Husserl never speaks as the received view does. He does, and part 6 will collect the places. But where he says what "founded" means in each case, the meanings are these three.

### 4.6 The form a third time

We can now put to the personal ego the question that was put to the thing and the soul: how is it given as what it is? Husserl's answer in § 57 uses, deliberately, the terms of the earlier analyses. In reflecting on my acts, he says, "I experience how I 'comport' myself under different subjective circumstances", that is, with respect to what is pregiven to me in my surrounding world; and as I enter into the interweaving of my motivations I experience how I am motivated by them and am wont to be motivated, what manner I have as the subject of these motivating circumstances, "what kind of personal subject I am" (248–49/258–261). What shows itself in this experience he calls personal properties or properties of character. A note to the passage draws the parallel to its end: observing others and myself under like circumstances, "I come to know myself as a personal 'reality'" (249 n./258–261).

Everything is there. A unity, the person; properties, the traits of character; a manifold of states, the acts and affections; circumstances; and a rule, expressed in the words "am wont". The parallel with the thing is stated outright. Once the apperception has developed, I am "pregiven to myself just as the thing is pregiven to me once the apperception of things has developed" (250/260–261), and I can set out to know myself better as I can set out to observe a thing. The person is constituted as a reality, under the form of circumstances, with the surrounding world in the place of real circumstances and motivation in the place of causality. Heidegger's first criticism, that the inquiry into the person remains an inquiry into the constitution of a reality, describes § 57 exactly.

### 4.7 "Not from experience but from life"

The next paragraph bears the title "Constitution of the personal ego before reflection", and it takes back the completeness of what has just been said. In reflection I always find myself as a personal ego. "Originally, however, it constitutes itself in the genesis that pervades the flow of lived experience" (251/260–261). The question is then asked whether the unity found in reflection is the original one, and the answer is no.

> The ego is originally not from experience, in the sense of associative apperception in which unities of manifolds of a nexus constitute themselves, but from life (it is what it is not for the ego, but itself the ego). (252/264–265)

The sentence denies of the ego precisely what defined a reality. A reality is a unity of a manifold under circumstances, and it is such a unity for someone who apprehends the manifold so. The ego that lives is not for itself in this way: "it does not primarily experience itself, but constitutes natural objects, things of value, tools" (252/264–265). It develops in doing so; what it does and undergoes has after-effects; it acquires abilities and habits. All this happens before, and whether or not, it becomes an object for itself. Husserl marks the difference from the thing in one line: "In the thing there occurs no apperception 'thing'; that occurs only in subjects" (253/264–265). A subject is the one being in which the apprehension of itself as a unity under circumstances occurs, and for that very reason it is not exhausted by what this apprehension grasps. Someone "does not know what he is; he comes to know himself" (252/264–265). It should not be concealed that the comparison with the thing accompanies even these sentences. The ego can have hidden dispositions, Husserl says in the same place, "just as a thing has properties that have not yet been drawn into the apperception of the thing" (252/264–265). The thing remains the term of comparison at the very point where its form is said not to suffice.

There are, then, two answers in §§ 57 and 58 to the question whether the person is a constituted reality. According to the first the person is a reality like the thing and the soul, a unity manifested in comportment under circumstances, given to itself in a developed self-apperception. According to the second this reality is the person as an object for itself and for others, and the person is in the first place the life in which that object, like every other, comes about. Husserl does not withdraw the first answer; the note to § 57 only confesses that "the analysis of personality is here very incomplete" (250 n./260–261). The use of the word "constitutes itself" for what happens in the genesis of the ego is the seventh of the uses listed in part 1, and we can now say what sets it apart. In every other use something is constituted for a subject. Here the subject is said to constitute itself, and what is meant is that it becomes what it is by living.

### 4.8 The hardest case: the underground

The thesis that the form of reality reaches its limit in the person has to face the last paragraphs of the section, in which the person seems to be given a lower layer after all. The spiritual ego, Husserl writes in § 61, "finds itself dependent on a dark underground of dispositions of character, of original and hidden dispositions, and on the other hand dependent on nature" (276/286–289). There are "two levels" in the person, a lower one that might also occur alone, as "pure animality", and the higher, specifically spiritual one of free acts. And § 62 states the chain in the order of the received view: the spirit in its acts depends on the soul, and the soul on the body, "hence it is conditioned by nature" (281/290–291). Is this not founding by circumstances, carried one storey higher?

In one respect it is, and the concession should be made without reserve. Husserl's example is a man crippled by a fall: the injury "has consequences for his spiritual life", and "certain groups of motivation fall away from now on" (276/286–289). What a person can be motivated by depends on what is pregiven to him, and what is pregiven depends on his body. A blind man has no motives that come from colours.

But the same sentence of § 62 continues: "yet it does not on that account stand in a relation of causality to nature. It has an underground that is conditionally dependent" (281/290–291). The dependence is placed in what the person has, and what the person has is distinguished from what the person does. The distinction was prepared at the beginning of the chapter, where everything that is pregiven to the ego, from the sensations up to the constituted things, is called its "possession" (*Habe*); whatever constitutes itself in acts of the ego becomes, "as something constituted, a 'possession' of the ego and something pregiven for new acts" (214/224–225). Conditionality reaches this possession. It does not reach the answer the ego gives to what it has, which is motivated by it and is not its effect. The cripple's motives are fewer. What he makes of those he has is not settled by his injury.

A second feature of the underground points the same way. § 62 explains its own vocabulary: the reality "body" expresses a regulation, the reality "soul" expresses "a second regulation" related to the first, and spirit is the index of a regulation of its own, for which "another cut, so to speak, is made through absolute consciousness" (281/290–291). The realities are here described as what they were in the analysis of the spring: titles for rules. And the lower level of the person, which § 61 calls its "natural side", is described in the same paragraph as "the site of the constitution of a world of appearances" (279/290–291), that is, of nature. The underground of the person is at once what depends on nature and what nature is constituted in. This is the circle of § 53 again, now inside the person, and it shows that the lower level of § 61 is no fourth storey of the building. It is the place where the first of the rules, the motivation of appearances, has its seat. What the text does not settle is whether this lower life is itself to be understood as soul in the sense of the second section, as a reality under conditionality, or as part of the life from which the ego is. The paragraphs speak both ways, and I leave the matter there.

The third section thus leaves the question of the title half answered. The person can be constituted as a reality and is so constituted, under a rule that is not nature's. The person is also that for which there are realities. What it means to call it, as the last paragraph of the book does, absolute, and nature relative, must now be asked in the light of the form.


## 5. Relative and absolute

### 5.1 What cannot become an index

The doctrine with which *Ideas II* ends is usually quoted from its last paragraph. It is first reached, however, at the end of the second section, before the personal attitude has been mentioned, and it is reached there as a result of the analysis of nature. § 47 looks back over the levels of the constitution of nature and draws up an account of dependences. The things I experience, with their intuitive properties, turn out to "exist only relatively": they are "dependent on one another in what they are, and co-dependent on the bodies of those who experience them and on their souls" (171/178–179). The objective nature of science is determined in such a way that it is independent of any single subject; but it is so by being that which shows itself in all the subjective worlds of appearance, and Husserl calls it, in this function, an "index" of the regulated connection of those worlds. The natural-scientific object is "the x of the 'mathematical' qualities" (169–70/176–179); the bodies of human beings are "identical x as indices of lawful regulations" of appearances (171/178–179); the souls are bound to these.

The paragraph then says what the whole account presupposes. It presupposes "what can never be transformed into an 'index': the absolute subject", with its experiences and its acts of reason, for which the whole of nature, physical and animal, constitutes itself; nature is "a unity of appearances posited by subjects and to be posited by them, and indeed to be posited in acts of reason" (171/178–179). And it adds a clause that guards against the nearest misunderstanding: "these absolutely presupposed subjects are not the subjects as nature, the human beings", which are themselves objectivities for many subjects (171/178–179). The analysis of nature thus "points beyond itself" to "the field of subjectivity that is no longer nature" (172/178–179).

Three relativities have been named here, and each corresponds to one of the dependences we have followed. Things are relative to one another: that is causality, the rule under which a thing has real properties. Things as they appear are relative to bodies and souls: that is conditionality. And the worlds of appearance of the several subjects are relative to the one nature that is their index, as this nature is relative to them: that is intersubjective constitution. "Relative" means in each case what it meant in § 15. Something is what it is in relation to something else, as to its circumstances. The absolute is introduced as what this whole web of relations is for. It is not introduced as a further item in the web, and above all not as its top storey.

### 5.2 A field of relativities

§ 64 begins by drawing a limit. Spirit can be grasped as dependent on nature and can itself be naturalized, "but only to a certain degree"; subjects cannot be wholly absorbed into nature, "since then what gives nature its sense would be missing" (297/308–311). The sentence about the field of relativities follows, and I quote it now with what surrounds it.

> Nature is a field of thoroughgoing relativities, and it can be this because these are, after all, always relative to an absolute, which thus bears all relativities: spirit. (297/308–311)

Read with §§ 15, 31 and 47 in mind, the sentence makes no new claim about nature. That realities "are what they are only in relation to other realities" was the formal concept of reality. What the sentence adds is a condition: a field in which everything is what it is through something else is possible only if the relations have a bearer that is not again one of their terms. A page later Husserl repeats the definition of the thing in the old words, as if to mark the connection: "The thing as real is dependent on real circumstances; it is what it is in the nexus of real nature" (298/310–313).

He then presses the definition further than the first section had done. If a thing is the identical subject of properties, and only its states change with the circumstances, is it "in itself something fixed", with an essence of its own that prescribes in advance how it can behave? Or is the thing "so to speak always on the march", "only something relatively identical", with an open essence that "can take on new properties according to the constitutive circumstances of givenness" (299/312–313)? The phrase "constitutive circumstances of givenness" extends the form of circumstances to its widest. A thing depends on other things, and what counts as its properties depends, in addition, on the circumstances under which it comes to be given, on how far experience has gone. Husserl does not decide the question he asks. But the direction is plain. The relativity of nature is the relativity that defined reality in the first place, now said of nature as a whole.

### 5.3 Two strikings-out

The argument for the absoluteness of spirit takes the form of a thought experiment. "If we strike out all spirits from the world, then there is no nature any more. But if we strike out nature, the 'true', objective-intersubjective existence, something still remains: spirit as individual spirit" (297/308–311). The procedure is the one that § 56 used to decide whether two realities form a founded unity or merely run parallel: there, if "striking out the one reality" changed nothing for the other, they were not circumstances for each other. The test is now applied to nature and spirit, and it comes out asymmetrically. Without spirits there is no nature, because nature is a unity of appearances. Without nature there is still spirit.

What remains should be described as cautiously as Husserl describes it. With nature struck out, "the possibility of sociality" is lost, since understanding one another requires bodies that are there for more than one; what remains is no longer a person "in the narrower, social sense". It is "an ego with its conscious life", which even so has "its individuality, its way of judging, of valuing, of letting itself be motivated in its position-takings" (297/308–311). Husserl does not claim that a spirit could be all that a person is without a world, and he does not say that it would have nothing pregiven to it. He claims that its being someone does not depend on there being an objective nature. Nor does he say that spirit has no circumstances. The paragraph opens with the opposite: "Spirit is determined by its surrounding world, and it also has a natural regulation", in so far as it shows dependences on the nature that is constituted in relation to the personal world. "But that does not prevent it from being *absolute*, *irrelative*" (297/308–311). Spirit stands under the form of circumstances, as § 57 showed. What is denied is that it has what it is from them.

### 5.4 Individuality in itself

The reason given for the asymmetry is an argument about individuation, and here a connection with the second section appears. A thing, Husserl says, has its individual essence as what is here and now, but what it is, is something general: each thing is an instance, and "each can be thought as repeated as often as one likes" (298/310–313). Several things exactly alike in properties and states are conceivable, at once or in succession; one thing can be thought to return periodically to the identical state. "What distinguishes two like things is the real-causal nexus, which presupposes the here and now", and with the here and now we are referred back to a subjectivity in relation to which alone place and time are determined. The conclusion is one of the hardest sentences of the book: "No thing has its individuality in itself" (299/312–313).

Of spirit the contrary is said in the same form of words. A spirit is one who lives, takes positions and is motivated; each "has its own manner of motivation; unlike the thing, it has its motivation in itself", and it is not an individual only by being at a certain place in the world (299/312–313). The main text gives two grounds. The pure ego of each act already has absolute individuation. And the spirit cannot be repeated: "the same spirit cannot be twice, nor can it return to the same total state and show its individuation merely in standing with the same content in different nexuses" (301/314–315). Between these two grounds stands a sentence that Husserl, according to the editor's apparatus, wrote into the typescript in the 1920s: "But the ego is not an empty pole; it is the bearer of its habituality, and this implies that it has its individual history" (300/312–315).[^5]

Whoever has read § 33 recognizes two elements of this argument. There the material things were called realities without history, because they are conditioned only from outside and could return to the same total state; and the psychic realities were said to have a history, because they are conditioned also by their own past and can never return. § 64 uses the same two marks, repeatability and the return to the same total state, to set the thing against spirit; and the supplement said of the physical body that it "is causal through and through and has no prior essence of its own". The difference that § 33 presented as one between outer and inner circumstances, within one formal concept of reality, thus recurs in § 64 among the grounds for calling nature relative and spirit absolute.

It is one ground among others, and in a first draft I had taken it for more. Three things in the text forbid saying that § 64 follows from § 33. First, § 33 ascribes inner circumstances and a history to the soul, and § 64 places the soul, so far as it is a reality of nature, on the side of the general: "everything by which the soul is determined as a natural reality is exemplary and general" (302/314–315). Having a history does not by itself make anything absolute. Secondly, the circumstances of the person are not introduced as inner ones. A note to § 34 says that, to reach the personal subject, "the concept of 'outer circumstances' must be extended to the objects that motivate the subject in its comportment" (139 n./144–147); and § 64 allows the surrounding world only a "secondary individuation", derived from the ego (301/314–315). Thirdly, and most important, the ground that § 64 itself puts first is another. "The pure ego of the respective *cogitatio* already has absolute individuation"; the lived experiences "carry their individuation in themselves"; "the only originally individual is consciousness, concretely with its ego", and everything else that is individual is something that appears (300–01/312–315). This is the individuation of consciousness, which the book brings with it from *Ideas I*, and it owes nothing to the form of circumstances. The sentence that adds habituality and history is, as noted, a later insertion.

What can be maintained is therefore less than a derivation. The closing doctrine has two roots in the book. One is the absoluteness of consciousness. The other is the analysis of reality: since to be real is to be what one is in relation to circumstances, nature is relative through and through, and the form in which this is said had already met, in the soul, a unity that is in part its own circumstance. § 64 joins the two, and the later insertion about habituality shows Husserl shifting weight from the first root towards the second. He does not connect §§ 33 and 64 in so many words. The connection lies in the recurrence of the terms: history, return to the same total state, having something "in itself".

### 5.5 Not unities of appearances

The closing sentences of the paragraph state the result as a difference in the manner of being a unity. "Spirits are precisely not *unities of appearances*, but unities of absolute nexuses of consciousness, more exactly, ego-unities" (301/314–315). Appearances are correlates of such nexuses; their "absolute being precedes the relative being of the appearances", and "all existence of nature depends on the existence of absolute spirits" (302/314–315). The last lines find a formula: "Nature is the X and in principle nothing other than X, which determines itself through general determinations. Spirit, however, is no X, but that which is itself given in the experience of spirit" (302/314–315).

The X is familiar from § 47, where the object of natural science was the x of mathematical qualities and the body an identical x as index of regulations. In the vocabulary of this study, X is the first term of the form of circumstances taken purely: a unity that is nothing but the bearer of what it is determined to be under rules. To say that nature is nothing other than X is to say that in nature the form of reality applies without remainder. To say that spirit is no X is to say that here the unity is not merely the correlate of a rule.

Does spirit then not show itself in a manifold at all? The paragraph itself says that it does: "in the course of the spirit's consciousness its unity, its individuality, manifests itself in every case" (297/308–311). The word is the one that was used for the spring. A supplement to the third section tries to say what is different, and its attempt shows how far Husserl could go with the concepts he had. The states of the ego, it says, are "'absolute' states, namely not constituted in relation to something absolute (to lived experiences) as their intentional unities" (323/334–335). A colour is a unity that stands over against the experiences in which it appears. A decision, a grief, a conviction of mine is not in this way an object beyond the living of it. Hence the summary opposition: all personal unities belong together "from the radical point of view of the manner of their constitution", and so do all things; "the latter are constituted relatively, the former absolutely" (324/334–337). And in a formula that parallels the end of § 64: the realm of nature is the realm of real unities that constitute themselves "in or by means of 'presentation'", while "the realm of spirit is the realm of the realities given through absolute manifestation", which have behind them "only the pure ego as the irreal, absolute bearer of all manifestation of reality" (325/336–337).

Two things should be observed about this attempt. The first is that it keeps the word "reality" for persons and keeps the word "manifestation". Absolute manifestation is manifestation without presentation: a unity shows itself in a manifold that is not a series of its appearances. That is the structure the second section found in the soul, whose states are immanent experiences and not schemata. What § 64 and the supplement call absolute constitution is the constitution of the soul as §§ 30–32 described it, with the realizing apprehension taken away that ties the soul's states to the body as to their circumstances. § 64 says as much in its last lines: the soul is "naturalized spirit", and its individuality "does not lie in what is nature here" (302/314–315). The second thing is that Husserl did not think the attempt had succeeded. Where the supplement extends absolute manifestation from the single person to social unities, a note is attached to the sentence: "That is too quick." These are unities of a new kind, giving themselves in the unities of attestation that we call egos; "'appearance' it is not, but surely analogous to attestation. This must be considered more closely" (324 n./334–337). The hesitation of §§ 57 and 58 returns here in a note. Is the person a reality that manifests itself, though absolutely, or is it that which was said to be "not from experience but from life"? The supplement says the first and marks its own formulation as provisional.

### 5.6 The two ends of the form

We can now return to the list of part 1 and say how its seven uses stand to one another. Presentation, realization and intersubjective constitution are forms of what the supplement calls relative constitution: a unity is given in a manifold of appearances, and it is what it is through its relation to circumstances. They make up the constitution of nature, and the levels of manifestation belong to them. Spontaneous constitution and comprehension are foundings of sense: something already given is taken as valuable, as useful, as the body of someone. They make up the surrounding world of persons. Institution and self-constitution, the fourth and the seventh use, have in common that the subject acquires something in and through its own living: a conviction that remains, a manner of being motivated, a history. These are the uses that § 64 draws on when it calls the ego the bearer of its habituality. They are what "absolute" names positively.

At both ends of the series the realizing sense of the word gives out, and the book says so in each case. At the lower end stand the data of sensation. They are "no longer constituted by any activity of the ego"; they are what is pregiven to all its activity, its first "possession" (214/224–225), and their own constitution as unities in time lies outside the book. At the upper end stands the ego, of which Husserl uses the word in a sense that excludes circumstances: it is "constituted as a unity with reference to the unity of the stream", which means that it can find itself as the same. Between these ends lies everything that is real, and everything real is relative. The pure ego is called in the supplement the "irreal" bearer of all manifestation; in § 18 the subjective had been called "an irreality" over against reality. The form of circumstances is a form of reality, and reality in this book has a region where it holds and two borders.

The reading has a limit that should be stated. I have connected the absoluteness of spirit with the kind of circumstance to which it answers, and § 64 does not use the word "circumstance" of spirit; it speaks of the surrounding world, of motivation and of history. That these are the terms of § 57, where the word is used, seems to me to justify the reading. What the reading does not explain is why Husserl, having the form ready, describes the absolute in § 64 almost wholly by negations: not a unity of appearances, not X, not individual through its place. The opposing reading has an explanation for this, and it is time to hear it.

## 6. The layered reading at full strength

### 6.1 Heidegger's three points

The strongest statement of the layered reading is also the earliest. In his Marburg course of the summer of 1925 Heidegger reported on the manuscript of *Ideas II*, which Husserl had communicated to him, and his report is neither hasty nor ungenerous. He knows that the book contains a personal attitude beside the naturalistic one and that the two are not of equal rank: "the naturalistic is subordinated to the personalistic" (GA 20, 169). He knows the underground of § 61, which he renders as the "natural side" that every ego has "as the underground of subjectivity" (169). He quotes the decisive sentences of § 64 one after another: that subjects cannot be absorbed into nature, that without spirits there is no nature, that spirits are not unities of appearances, that nature is the X and spirit is not (170). Whatever his criticism is, it does not rest on having overlooked the passages on which this study has leaned.

His judgment is that they change nothing. The reasoning of § 64 is "the same consideration" as the one that, in *Ideas I*, left pure consciousness as the residue of the annihilation of the world; Husserl "only comes back to his original division of being under another title. Ontologically everything remains as it was" (171). Three reasons are given, and Heidegger numbers them.

First, the inquiry into the person "remains within the question of the constitution of reality and objectivity" (171). What is asked is "how a being manifests itself as real in its reality" (172), and the question is put "in the order in which the things of the real themselves stand". Then comes the sentence that has fixed the received view.

> The *fundamental layer* remains the *actuality of nature*; on it the psychic is built, and on this the spiritual. (172)

The naturalness of the personal attitude is stressed, Heidegger grants, but the execution gives the investigation of nature the precedence. "*The being of the person is not experienced primarily as such*" (172).

Second, the access to the person is nothing other than the immanent reflection on lived experiences, the *inspectio sui*, from which the theses about absolute givenness derive. It is said that the nexus of experiences has its individuation in itself; "but the mode of being of the acts remains undetermined" (172).

Third, and for Heidegger most important, the whole division follows a traditional definition as its guiding clue (*Leitfaden*): the human being as *animal rationale*, a living thing that "has reason with it" (172, 174). From this comes the question that he puts to the layers: can the being of the concrete human being be "built together" out of the being of the material underground, of the body, of the soul and of spirit? "Is the being of the person the product of the modes of being of these layers of being?" Or does it show itself that in this way of first dividing and then putting together the person is taken, for all the tendency towards the personal, as "a many-layered thing of the world", whose being no determination of the reality of its directing-itself, however far it is carried, will ever reach (173)? The personal attitude does not take the human being as a reality of nature, "but still as a *reality of the world* which constitutes itself as transcendence in absolute consciousness" (173).

### 6.2 What speaks for him

The reading can call on a good deal of the text, and before answering it I shall set out what it can call on, including what the earlier parts have had to concede.

There is the programme, with its "lowest level". There is the order of the book. There is § 35, which announces the constitution of the human being as that of "a material body on which new strata of being, the bodily-psychic ones, are built" (143/148–149). There are the two sentences on the order of founding from the opening of the third section, and the chain of § 62, in which the spirit depends on the soul and the soul on the body. There is the first supplement, which sums up the ascent of the first two sections by saying that the soul constitutes itself "as a founded and therefore non-self-sufficient reality on the underground of the body" (310/322–323). There is the privilege of the body that we found in supplement XII: everything real in the world is corporeal, and the soul is a worldly reality only through the body that founds it. And there is Husserl's own vocabulary in § 61, the "two levels" of the person, of which the lower could occur alone as "pure animality": the *animal rationale* could hardly be described more plainly.

The list is longer than the passages already met. The first section introduces animal nature as "a complex consisting of a lower stratum of material nature" and an upper stratum inseparable from it (28–29/30–33), and animal realities as founded realities with material ones as their lower level (33/34–37). The third section opens with a retrospect in which Husserl says of the fundamental kinds of apprehension that they "were built on one another: physical experience as the basic one; resting on it and including it, the experience of the body", to which the experience of the soul belongs "as a constitutive stratum" (174/182); for natural science the soul is "a mere stratum of real occurrences in bodies" (175/182–185). The first supplement begins its ascent with the words: "Following the constitutive order, we have to begin with the constitution … of material nature" (305/314–315). And in § 56 itself the unity of body and spirit "constitutes itself as a higher unity of two real unities" and is perceptually present "as a formation of two levels" (245/254–257).

More important than these places is a consideration that turns the thesis of this study into an argument for Heidegger. I have maintained that the concept of constitution in *Ideas II* is a form, taken from the thing and carried over to body, soul and person. But a form taken from the thing is the thing's form. If the soul is real "in a similar sense" as the material thing, if psychic properties are unities of manifestation as elasticity is, if I am pregiven to myself "just as the thing is pregiven", then the person is understood from the thing, however carefully the terms are altered on the way. Heidegger says exactly this in his first point, and he says it in Husserl's own word: the question is how something "manifests itself as real in its reality". On this showing the form of circumstances does not refute the reproach of the many-layered thing. It is the more precise description of what is reproached. And it would explain the fact noted at the end of the last part. Where the form no longer applies, Husserl has only negations left, because the form was his only positive concept of being.

### 6.3 Replies, in his order

To the first point. It should be granted that the constitution of the person in § 57 is the constitution of a reality, and that Husserl's concept of reality has its origin in the thing; the supplement says so in those words. It should also be granted that between body and soul there is a founding, with a privilege of the body. What does not follow is the sentence about the fundamental layer. That sentence takes "built on" in one sense throughout, and we have found three. The material thing is not the lower level of the soul as the schema is the lower level of the thing: the soul shows itself in lived experiences and not in bodily states. The body founds the soul in the sense that circumstances of the soul's states lie in it; this founding is the condition of the soul's being a reality in nature, it holds in the naturalistic attitude, and the text calls that attitude an abstraction that forgets the person. And the person is not built on the psychophysical unity: the unity of expression in which a person is there "precedes the constitution of every such unity", even though Husserl is willing to call this unity too, in its own sense, a founded one (325/336–337). Husserl does call the ascent from material nature "the constitutive order", and he does say that the kinds of apprehension are built on one another. But the first is said, in the supplement, of strata distinguished "ideally" for an ego thought of in isolation, and the second of "the whole system of naturalistic experiences" (174/182). The building is a building of apprehensions within one attitude. At the place where the exposition reaches the person, Husserl says that the unity in which a person is there comes before the founded one. And the one passage that describes without restriction to an attitude how "the levels of the constitution of reality are built on one another" begins the building with the single ego, which constitutes its outer world and then, together with other egos, objective nature and the social world (324/334–337).

There is a further point about the bottom of the building. The lowest level that *Ideas II* reaches by its own regress is sensation, and beneath the causal nexus of nature it finds the motivated order of appearances and movements. If one insists on asking what, in this book, everything else rests on, the answer the text gives is not material nature. Nature is in the middle.

To the second point. The access to the person in § 57 is reflective, and Heidegger is right that the being of the acts receives no determination of its own there. But § 58 exists in order to say that the person given in reflection is not the original one. The ego is "not from experience", it "does not primarily experience itself", someone "comes to know himself". These sentences deny that the *inspectio sui* gives the person as it is, and in denying it they say something, however little, about how it is: it is by living, and it is in advance of what it knows of itself. Heidegger does not mention the paragraph. I cannot tell whether it stood, in this form, in the manuscript he read; the printed sentences contain words that the apparatus assigns to Husserl's later revision.

To the third point. Here least can be said in reply, and I do not think the text allows more. § 61 does divide the person into a lower level, sensibility, and a higher, the *intellectus agens*, and it does consider the lower as something that might exist by itself. One can point out that Husserl describes this lower level as the site of the constitution of nature and as "hidden reason", which is not what the tradition meant by the animal in us. But the scheme of the division is the traditional one, and Heidegger identified it correctly.

### 6.4 The objection turned

The question remains whether the form of circumstances, being the thing's form, makes the person a thing. On this the text contains a resource that Heidegger's report passes over, and it is the very objection he raises.

His question was whether the being of the person can be put together out of layers. Husserl asks the same question about the unity of body and spirit in § 56, and what he says there cuts both ways. He does describe the human being as a higher unity of two real unities, a formation of two levels. But he denies, three times over, that such a unity is a putting together. The spiritual is "nothing second, nothing appended"; body and sense are not two things joined, of which each could also be by itself. A merely parallel connection of two realities "creates no reality of a higher level": a sum of layers is not a unity. And where there is a founded unity, it is founded by the fact that each side is a circumstance for the other, so that the unity lies in a rule and not in a stacking. The programme had already said that what is new in a founded unity "can never be reduced to mere sums of other realities". What was meant as an objection to Husserl proves to be a thought of Husserl's own. He keeps the levels and rejects the sum, and he rejects it for the reason Heidegger gives.

The same holds, with a difference, for the form itself. The form of circumstances was read off the thing. But what Husserl kept of it when he carried it over was, at each step, less of the thing. Of the three terms the thing supplied the pattern of all and the content of none: no schemata for the soul, no outer circumstances for the person, no causality for either. And the feature in which the form was first strained, that a unity can be among its own circumstances, is the feature by which § 64 distinguishes spirit from every thing. Husserl did not think the person from the thing in the sense that he ascribed to it the thing's manner of being. He used the thing to learn what it is to be a unity under circumstances, and then described the person by what in it will not fit.

That is also the measure of what Heidegger saw rightly, and I do not want to diminish it. A description by what will not fit is a description by negation. Husserl has a concept of reality that he can state with complete clarity, and he has, for that which is not reality in this sense, the words "absolute", "irreal", "from life" and a note saying "too quick". Heidegger's sentence that the being of the person "is not experienced primarily as such" is unjust to § 58 and just to the book as a whole: the person comes into it at the point where a form developed elsewhere gives out. One may add that Husserl was the first to say so. Heidegger himself told his students that Husserl, on communicating the manuscripts, had written to him that he would now have to carry the whole out anew, "with in part completely changed content", and that the characterization given in the lecture was therefore "in a certain way already somewhat antiquated" (168).

### 6.5 What it is to be constituted

The title asked what it is, in *Ideas II*, to be constituted, and the answer can now be given without the metaphor of storeys. To be constituted as a reality is to be the unity of a rule that binds a manifold of states to circumstances. This holds of the thing, of the body and of the soul, and it holds of the person in so far as the person is given to itself and to others as someone of such and such a character. What differs from region to region is the manifold, the kind of circumstance and the kind of rule; and the "levels" of which the book speaks are three different relations that this form makes possible, the level of manifestation, the founding of one reality by another as its circumstance, and the founding of sense. The question of the received view, what is constituted on what, has therefore no single answer, because "on" has no single meaning. The question the text itself puts is: in relation to which circumstances, and under which rule?

Put in this way, the question has a result that the received view could only register as a reversal. The rules form a series, and the series does not begin with nature. It begins with the motivation that ties appearances to a body that moves; causality and conditionality, the two rules of nature, come second and third; the motivation of persons comes last and takes up the first again. Nature is a field of relativities because the form of reality applies to it without remainder, and spirit is called absolute on two grounds, of which the analysis of reality supplies one: a unity that has a history is among its own circumstances, and what is wholly determined from outside has nothing in itself. So far, and no further, § 64 draws a consequence of § 15. Its other ground, the individuation of consciousness, it brings from the first book.

The result has its limits, and I have tried to mark them as they arose. I have followed the concept through the printed text and have not asked when its several formulations were written; whether the connection I find between § 33 and § 64 was one Husserl saw, or one that Edith Stein's and Ludwig Landgrebe's arrangement of his manuscripts produced, cannot be decided on the basis used here. I have left the constitution of time and the constitution of a common world where the book leaves them. I have not read Ricœur, Merleau-Ponty or Sokolowski on these matters, and what is said here may in part have been said by them. And the tension that runs through the third section remains. The person is a reality constituted under circumstances, and the person is not a unity of manifolds at all but that which lives. The text holds both, and the concept of constitution that it possesses can state only the first.

Is to be constituted, then, to be founded on something lower? For the realities of nature the answer is: sometimes, and in a sense that has to be specified each time. For the person the answer is no. What Husserl achieved in *Ideas II* is more than a building of strata, though he builds. It is the discovery that reality has a form, the patient demonstration of how far the form reaches, and, in the sentence that the ego is not from experience but from life, the admission that the one who constitutes cannot be wholly given in the form in which everything else is. Heidegger asked what the being of that life is. That he could put the question to this book, and find in it the passages from which to put it, is not the least of the book's merits.

## 12. The prediction

Text: Hua IV, supplement XII, part II, §§ 1–3 (340–46), read after the plan was fixed. Predicted: (i) the reality of the soul determined by dependence on circumstances, not as a layer on the body; (ii) psychophysical dependence separated from the causality of things; (iii) the soul in nature only so far as that dependence reaches. Result: (ii) and (iii) borne out in so many words (343–46); (i) borne out in substance ("Seine Realität weist sich aus in der Abhängigkeit von Umständen"; body and soul each "nicht Realität für sich", 341), with the qualification that Husserl calls this dependence "founding" ("die sie fundierende Leibesrealität", 343). Not predicted: the privilege of the body as the corporeal ("Alles Reale ist körperlich", 342), which favours the layered reading; and the soul's "Eigenwesen, das allen Kausalitäten … vorhergeht" (346). Effect: the definition of founding by circumstances stands; the reciprocity claimed for it in 3.6 was limited.

## 13. The review

A subagent in a fresh context, given the study and the primary files, (1) argued the layered reading, (2) checked ten quotations, (3) named the weakest claim.

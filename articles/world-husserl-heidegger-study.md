# Is the World Something Subjective?

## The Concept of World in Husserl and Heidegger

It is commonly held that Husserl and Heidegger mean opposite things by the word "world". For Husserl, on this view, the world is the totality of real things. It stands over against consciousness as its correlate, and consciousness would survive its annihilation. For Heidegger the world is that in which a human existence lives, a whole of significance that belongs to the being of this existence, so that a subject without a world is a fiction. The view has texts on its side, and the sharpest of them are Heidegger's. *Being and Time* says that the relations of significance are "no network of forms that a worldless subject slips over a material" (SZ 366), and the lecture course of 1925 says that what Husserl describes as the natural attitude is "not natural at all" (GA 20, 155). Yet the view leaves several sentences unexplained. In 1913 Husserl writes that the world is not exhausted by what is there for me, that only its "form" is prefigured, and that "reality and world" are "titles for certain valid unities of sense" (Hua III/1, 57, 120). In the *Crisis* he writes that the world does not exist "as a being, as an object" exists (Hua VI, 146). Heidegger, for his part, asks in 1927 whether the world then becomes "something 'subjective'", and he answers that it does (SZ 64, 366). In October of the same year he writes to Husserl that the two of them agree on the main point: what Husserl calls world cannot be clarified by going back to a being of the same kind (Hua IX, 601).[^1]

In what follows I wish to show that the two authors agree on three things and differ on a fourth, and that the fourth is not the one usually named. They agree that the world is not the sum of what is in it, that it is not itself a being among beings, and that it is in some sense "subjective". They differ in what binds the world to the subject. For Husserl the world is at bottom something that holds: a thesis in 1913, a belief in the 1920s, a ground of validity in the 1930s. For Heidegger it is at bottom something for the sake of which: a whole of references in 1927, a whole of possibilities held out by freedom in 1928 and 1929. I wish to show that this difference of modality, between a world that is believed and a world that is projected, explains the differences that the usual contrast of theory and practice only lists. And I wish to show that it appears most clearly where each author considers that the world might not be.

The comparison has a measure because both authors ask one question, and Heidegger gives it the wording I have taken for the title. § 14 of *Being and Time* runs through it in a few lines. Can "world" be a determination of the beings we call innerworldly at all? Is it a character of the being of Dasein? "Does 'world' not thus become something 'subjective'? How then should a 'common' world still be possible, 'in' which, after all, we are?" (SZ 64). Husserl asks the same question from the other side. The *Crisis* takes over from Hume the insight that the world is "a validity that has sprung up in subjectivity" (Hua VI, 99), and it is brought by this insight to the paradox that human beings are subjects for the world and at the same time objects in it. The alternative can be stated in advance. If the world is subjective, is it so as what a subject takes to be, or as what a subject is?

The study rests on a closed body of texts, read in the files of one working archive, and for the most part in German. On Husserl's side these are §§ 27–32, 47–49 and 55 of *Ideas I* (1913); §§ 11, 49 and 50 of *Ideas II*; two passages of the course on First Philosophy of 1923/24; § 6 of the lectures on Phenomenological Psychology of 1925; §§ 7, 28 and 60 of the *Cartesian Meditations*; and §§ 25, 37, 38, 43 and 53 of the *Crisis*, with two of its appendices. On Heidegger's side they are §§ 12, 22 and 24 of the course of the summer of 1925 (GA 20); §§ 14, 18, 21, 40, 43 and 69 of *Being and Time* (1927); § 15 of the course of the summer of 1927 (GA 24); the first enclosure to the letter of 22 October 1927; §§ 11 and 12 of the course of the summer of 1928 (GA 26); the treatise *On the Essence of Ground* (1929); and §§ 34 and 36 of the course of the winter of 1928/29 (GA 27), which the archive holds only in English.

Three omissions need a word. I leave aside Heidegger's concept of world before 1925 and after 1929. The lectures of 1919 to 1923 are held only in part and in translation, the course of 1929/30 on world, finitude and solitude is not held, and the essay on the work of art could not be found in the file that should contain it. The Heidegger of this study is therefore the Heidegger of five years. I leave aside Husserl's manuscripts on the home-world and the alien world in the third volume on intersubjectivity, which I have not read. And I leave aside the constitution of the one world by many subjects, except where the unity of the world depends on it. The asymmetry is plain: twenty-three years of Husserl are set against five of Heidegger. It is justified only by the question, since those five years are the ones in which Heidegger asks whether the world is subjective, and Husserl asks it all his life.

A word on procedure. Parts 1 to 3 are exposition: each reports what a dated text says, in the order of the texts, and marks the few steps that are mine. Part 4 gives the strongest reading opposed to the one proposed here, which is Heidegger's own reading of Husserl, and replies to it. Part 5 is the confrontation. Nowhere do I attempt a description of the world in my own name. The study is historical and comparative; it asks what two authors did with one word, and where a claim of theirs rests on a description, I report the description and do not test it.

## 1. The world as thesis: Husserl, 1913

### 1.1 The world of the natural attitude

Husserl's first published account of the world is a description, and he insists on the word. § 27 of *Ideas I* is written in the first person and asks only what I find when I live "in the natural attitude". The answer begins with extension: "I am conscious of a world, endlessly spread out in space, endlessly becoming and having become in time" (Hua III/1, 56). To be conscious of it means, above all, that I find it there and experience it. Bodily things are "simply there for me", whether or not I attend to them, and so are animals and other human beings. Husserl has a word for this being-there, and he sets it in quotation marks at its first use and often afterwards: the things are "on hand" (*vorhanden*), "in the literal or the figurative sense" (Hua III/1, 56). The word will matter in part 4, because Heidegger makes of the same German word the name of a mode of being. Here it names nothing more than the way in which what I find is found: as there already, before I turn to it.

The second step of the description goes beyond what is found. What is actually perceived is surrounded by what is co-present without being perceived, the unseen parts of the room, the veranda, the garden, and Husserl's attention wanders through them in a sentence that is itself a small inventory of a house. But the world is not exhausted by this either. It reaches "in a fixed order of being into the unlimited", and what is perceived and co-present is "partly pervaded, partly surrounded by a dimly conscious horizon of indeterminate actuality" (Hua III/1, 57). I can send the ray of attention into this horizon and bring some of it to determinacy. For the most part, however, the result is different: an empty mist fills with possibilities and presumptions, "and only the 'form' of the world, precisely as 'world', is prefigured". The sentence that closes the paragraph is short: "The misty horizon, never fully determinable, is necessarily there" (Hua III/1, 57). Two things are said here that a definition of the world as the totality of things could not say. The world is given as a whole only in its form, never in its content. And the indeterminate part of it is no defect of my knowledge; it belongs to the way a world is there at all. The same holds, Husserl adds, for time: the world on hand in each waking now has "its temporal horizon, infinite in two directions" (Hua III/1, 58).

The third step concerns what kind of world this is, and it is the passage most often quoted against Husserl. "This world is not there for me as a mere world of things (*Sachenwelt*), but in the same immediacy as a world of values, a world of goods, a practical world." Without further ado I find things furnished with characters of value as well as with material properties, as beautiful and ugly, pleasant and unpleasant. "Immediately, things stand there as objects of use, the 'table' with its 'books', the 'drinking glass', the 'vase', the 'piano'" (Hua III/1, 58). The same is true of the people around me, who are my friends or enemies, my servants or superiors, strangers or relatives. I shall keep the first of these examples, the table with its books, and ask of each account that follows what it makes of it. In 1913 the answer has two clauses, and they do not sit easily together. The value characters and practical characters are there "in the same immediacy" as the thing; and they "belong constitutively to the objects 'on hand' as such" (Hua III/1, 58). The first clause denies any order of before and after. The second names the table as an object on hand to which the characters belong. Whether this is a layering of use upon thing is the question Heidegger will press, and we return to it in part 4.

§ 28 adds a distinction that fixes the sense of the word "world" from another side. While I am occupied with pure numbers, a world of numbers is there for me too, with its own partly determinate and partly indeterminate horizon. Yet "the arithmetical world is there for me only if and as long as I am in the arithmetical attitude", whereas "the natural world, the world in the ordinary sense of the word, is constantly there for me as long as I live along naturally" (Hua III/1, 59). To live naturally and to have this world are, Husserl says, the same. The two worlds are "without connection", apart from their relation to the I that can turn its regard to either; the natural world is the background of my arithmetical acts, "but it is no horizon into which an arithmetical world fits itself" (Hua III/1, 60). There is, then, a loose use of "world" for any field of objects with a horizon, and a strict use for the one field that I cannot leave by changing my occupation. Husserl gives this one world a second name, which he does not explain. It is the world in which I find myself "and which is at the same time my surrounding world" (*Umwelt*) (Hua III/1, 59), and the title of § 27 already joins the two words: "I and my surrounding world".

### 1.2 The general thesis

Up to this point the world has been described by its content and its form. § 30 says what makes it a world for me, and it does so by naming something that is neither. Husserl first limits his task. A complete description of what is found in the natural attitude would be a science of its own, "extraordinarily important, although hardly seen until now", and it is not the task of this book (Hua III/1, 61). What the book needs is one feature. "I find constantly on hand, as standing over against me, the one spatio-temporal actuality to which I myself belong, as do all other human beings found in it." This actuality I find as existing, "and I also take it, as it gives itself to me, as existing". No doubt or rejection of particular data alters this. "'The' world is as actuality always there; at most it is here or there 'otherwise' than I supposed", and this or that is, so to speak, to be struck out of it under the titles of illusion or hallucination, out of a world which "in the sense of the general thesis is always an existing world" (Hua III/1, 61).

The term is introduced under a title and then defined by what it is not. The general thesis (*Generalthesis*) of the natural attitude "does not, of course, consist in an act of its own, in an articulated judgement about existence". It is something that lasts as long as the attitude lasts, that is, throughout natural waking life. Whatever is perceived or made present, everything of the natural world that I am conscious of "before all thinking", bears in its whole and in each of its parts the character "there", "on hand"; and on this character an explicit judgement of existence can be founded, which then only makes thematic what "already lay somehow in the original experiencing" (Hua III/1, 62). Three features of this definition should be held fast. The thesis is not an act; it is a standing character of all acts of a certain kind. It bears on the whole before the parts, since a doubt about a part leaves it untouched. And although it is no judgement, it is like a judgement in the respect that matters for what follows: one can do with the potential and unexpressed thesis, Husserl says, "exactly what one can do with the expressed thesis of a judgement" (Hua III/1, 62).

What one can do with it is to suspend it. The suspension (ἐποχή) is not a change of the thesis into its negation, nor into conjecture or doubt. "The thesis we have carried out we do not give up, we change nothing in our conviction"; and yet we put it "out of action", we "bracket" it, we "make no use" of it (Hua III/1, 63). § 32 applies the operation to the general thesis itself and so to "this whole natural world, which is constantly 'there for us', 'on hand', and which will always remain there as an 'actuality' for consciousness, even if we choose to bracket it" (Hua III/1, 65). Husserl guards the step against two misreadings in one sentence: "I do not then negate this 'world', as though I were a sophist; I do not doubt its existence, as though I were a sceptic" (Hua III/1, 65). The world stays where it was. What changes is the use I make of my holding it to be.

It is at this point, and before any thought of annihilation, that Husserl's concept of world receives the determination which I take to be the lasting one. The reading that follows is mine, though it uses only his words. If the whole natural world can be bracketed by putting one thesis out of action, then what makes the world a world for me is that thesis. The table with its books is there; the garden I do not see is there; the mist beyond is "necessarily there". Each "there" is the same character, and the character is that of something held to be. The world of § 27 was a horizon with a form. The world of § 30 is that horizon as posited. Husserl says as much at the end of § 47, where the two descriptions meet: every hypothesis of practical life and of empirical science relates to "this changeable but always co-posited horizon, through which the thesis of the world receives its essential sense" (Hua III/1, 102). The horizon gives the thesis its sense, and the thesis gives the horizon its being-there. One further phrase of § 30 deserves notice, because a later Husserl will make a concept of it. The sciences of the natural attitude, he says, aim to solve the tasks that offer themselves "on its ground" (Hua III/1, 61). In 1913 the ground is that of the world as posited, and the word is not yet a term.

### 1.3 The world as correlate and the one world

The chapter that follows the reduction asks what the world is when it is considered from the side of the consciousness that posits it, and the method changes with the question. § 27 described; §§ 47 to 49 vary. Husserl takes the actual course of experience as one case among possible ones and asks what belongs to any course of experience in which a world could be given. This is his eidetic procedure, and its results are claims about possibility and necessity.

The first result is the correlation. The actual course of our experience compels reason to go beyond the things of intuition and to lay under them a "physical truth". But it could be otherwise; our intuitive world could be the last, with no physical world behind it. And one can go further: "in the destruction in thought of the objectivity of things, as correlate of the consciousness that experiences them, no limits hinder us" (Hua III/1, 101). What things are, the things about whose being or not-being we can reasonably dispute, "they are as things of experience". Experience alone prescribes their sense. If, then, we can follow in thought the possible variations of motivated courses of experience, "the correlate of our factual experience, called 'the actual world', results as a special case of manifold possible worlds and surrounding worlds, which for their part are nothing other than correlates of essentially possible variations of the idea 'experiencing consciousness'" (Hua III/1, 101). One sentence draws the consequence for every object: "Never is an object that is in itself one with which consciousness and the conscious I have nothing to do. The thing is a thing of the surrounding world, even the unseen thing, even the really possible thing that is not experienced but can be experienced" (Hua III/1, 101).

It would be a mistake to read this as a doctrine of many worlds, and § 48 excludes the reading. The assumption of something real outside this world is free of formal contradiction. But whatever is real must be experienceable, and not for an I that is merely thought up; it must be so "for some actual I", as a unity that can be exhibited in the connections of its experience. Since what one I can know every I can in principle know, the factually separate worlds of experience can join, through actual experience, "into a single intersubjective world" (Hua III/1, 103). The formal possibility of realities outside the one spatio-temporal world proves to be a material absurdity. Possible worlds are thus variations in thought. There is one world, and its unity is that of a possible agreement among those who experience. Husserl's example is the spirits who may live on the most distant stars; he will use it again eighteen years later (part 2.4).

### 1.4 The annihilation of the world and the world as sense

"On the other hand", § 49 begins, "none of this says that there must be a world at all, or any thing" (Hua III/1, 103–04). The existence of a world is the correlate of experience that runs in certain ordered forms, and nothing in the essence of perception requires that it run so. It is conceivable that experience should dissolve into conflict not only here and there but irremediably, that it should suddenly show itself refractory to the demand that its positings of things be carried through in harmony, and that its connection should lose the fixed order of adumbrations, apprehensions and appearances: "that there is no longer a world" (Hua III/1, 104). Rough unities might still form, passing points of rest for intuition, mere analogues of things. The description is precise about what the annihilation would be. Nothing is removed from anywhere. A style of experience ceases, and with it the correlate that this style alone could have.

From this Husserl draws the two sentences that have defined his concept of world for most of his readers. The being of consciousness "would indeed be necessarily modified by an annihilation of the world of things, but would not be touched in its own existence". Immanent being is therefore absolute in the sense that in principle it needs no thing in order to exist; and "the world of transcendent *res* is entirely dependent on consciousness, and not on a consciousness logically thought up but on an actual one" (Hua III/1, 104). The second sentence carries a test that is seldom quoted with the first. Suppose that everything were present on the side of consciousness that is required for the appearance of a unified world and for its rational knowledge. "Is it then still conceivable, and not rather absurd, that the corresponding transcendent world should not be?" (Hua III/1, 105). The dependence thus runs one way in respect of existence and both ways in respect of sense: consciousness can be without a world, but a harmonious experience of a world cannot be without the world it experiences. The section ends on the phrase that Heidegger will quote against it, the "veritable abyss of sense" between consciousness and reality, and on a formula for the world. The whole spatio-temporal world, to which the human being reckons itself as a subordinate reality, is "according to its sense merely intentional being"; it is a being that consciousness posits in its experiences, that can be intuited and determined only as what is identical in motivated multiplicities of appearance, "and beyond that is a nothing" (Hua III/1, 106).

§ 55 says what kind of thing this "merely intentional being" is, and it is here that the word which governs my reading first appears as a predicate of the world. "In a certain way, and with some caution in the use of words, one can also say: all real unities are 'unities of sense'." Unities of sense presuppose a consciousness that gives sense, which for its part is absolute and does not exist through a giving of sense. If one identifies the universe of realities with the whole of being, one makes it absolute, and that is absurd: "an absolute reality is worth exactly as much as a round square". Then the formula: "Reality and world are here simply titles for certain valid unities of sense", related to connections of pure consciousness that give sense and "exhibit the validity of sense" (Hua III/1, 120). The German has two forms of one word here (*gültige Sinneseinheiten*, *Sinnesgültigkeit*), and the doubling is not an accident of style. A unity of sense that is merely meant is not yet a world; a world is a unity of sense that holds.

Husserl at once meets the objection that this turns the world into subjective semblance. Nothing is taken from "the fully valid being of the world", any more than from the being of the square when one denies that it is round. What is removed is an interpretation, a "philosophical absolutising of the world" that is foreign to the natural view of it. The natural view "lives naively in the performance of the general thesis" and so can never become absurd. Absurdity arises only when one philosophises and fails to notice "that the world itself has its whole being as a certain 'sense'" (Hua III/1, 120–21). A footnote admits that the concept of sense is here widened in an extraordinary way.

Is this to say that Husserl's world of 1913 is, after all, the totality of things that can be thought away? In a certain way one must answer yes. § 49 speaks of the "world of things", § 55 of the "universe of realities", and the consciousness that remains is described without any reference to a world. However, this is not to say that the world is defined as that totality. What can be annihilated in § 49 is a style of harmony in experience, and what the world is said to be in § 55 is a validity. The totality of things is what the valid sense "world" is the sense of; the world as Husserl determines it is that sense as holding. This is why the same book can say that the world is "always there" whatever I doubt, and that it might not be. The first sentence describes the thesis from within. The second considers the experience that sustains it from without.

The account leaves one thing in the dark, and Husserl's later work turns on it. A thesis that is not an act, that precedes every judgement and survives every doubt, is a strange kind of thesis. § 31 says that it can be treated like a judgement, and the suspension depends on that. But a judgement can be given up when its grounds fail, and of the general thesis Husserl has said that I find myself in it "at all times, and without ever being able to change it" (Hua III/1, 58). What kind of holding is this, which I can put out of action and cannot alter? The texts of the next two decades give it other names than thesis.

## 2. The world as ground: Husserl, 1912–1936

The texts of this part do not form a book. They are a draft that was still unpublished when Heidegger cited it in 1927, two lecture courses, a work that first appeared in French, and a last work. I take them in the order of their dates as far as the files give dates, and I ask of each the question on which part 1 ended: what is the holding by which there is a world for me, if it is no act and no judgement? It will be seen that Husserl changes the name of this holding twice and its description once, and that one of his answers of 1913 is first qualified and then, in a late appendix, nearly withdrawn.

### 2.1 The surrounding world of the person

The second book of the *Ideas* was drafted in 1912, directly after the first, and reworked for more than a decade; the editor of the volume reports a new elaboration by Husserl in 1915, two by Edith Stein, of which the second dates from about 1918, and a third by Ludwig Landgrebe into which Husserl made insertions after 1924 (Hua IV, editor's introduction). The printed text therefore has layers, and I cannot assign the paragraphs used here to one of them. What can be said is that Heidegger knew the work in manuscript. A note in *Being and Time* reports its three sections, quotes its first sentence on Dilthey and adds that Husserl has since pursued these problems further in his Freiburg lectures (SZ 47 n.).

The third section of the book opposes two attitudes. In the naturalistic attitude, which is that of the natural scientist, everything is nature: physical things at the base, bodies built on them, souls as a stratum of bodies. In the personalistic attitude I am a person among persons and a member of the social world. Husserl's first claim about the two is one of rank. They are not of equal right. "The naturalistic attitude is subordinate to the personalistic", and it gains a certain independence only "through an abstraction or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal I", whereby it "illegitimately absolutises its world, nature" (Hua IV, 184). The verb is the one that § 55 of the first book used for the philosopher's error. There it was the world that was wrongly made absolute against consciousness; here it is nature that is wrongly made absolute against the world in which persons live.

That world receives the name which the first book left unexplained. "As a person I am what I am (and every other person is what he is) as *subject of a surrounding world*. The concepts I and surrounding world are inseparably related to one another." The definition follows at once: the surrounding world is "the world perceived by the person in his acts, remembered, grasped in thought, surmised or disclosed in this or that respect, the world of which this personal I is conscious, which is there for it, to which it comports itself thus or thus", in experiencing and theorising, or in feeling, valuing, acting and shaping (Hua IV, 185). Two consequences are drawn. Physical actuality is not, as such, anyone's surrounding world; it is so only as far as the person "knows" of it. If he knows nothing of the discoveries of physics, the world does not belong to his surrounding world with the sense that physics gives it. And generally: "the surrounding world is no world 'in itself' but world 'for me'", a world "posited in his intentional experiences with a particular content of sense", and as such "constantly in becoming", producing itself through transformations of sense "with their accompanying positings and cancellations" (Hua IV, 186).

Three things in this passage bear on our question. First, the bond of world and subject is here as tight as it will be in Heidegger: I and surrounding world are "inseparably related", and the person is what he is as subject of a world. Second, what binds them is named in the terms of 1913. The surrounding world is the world as perceived, remembered, thought and surmised, the world as "posited", and its becoming is a history of positings and cancellations. Third, there is a hesitation about the order of what is posited. An early paragraph of the book says without reserve that "in ordinary life we have nothing at all to do with objects of nature. What we call things are paintings, statues, gardens, houses, tables, clothes, tools and so on. All these are value-objects of various kinds, objects of use, practical objects. They are not objects of natural science" (Hua IV, 27). The table with its books is here no natural thing to which something is added; the natural thing is what one reaches by abstaining from the table. Yet § 50 continues, a few lines after the definition just quoted: "To begin with, the world is, in its core, a world appearing to the senses and characterised as 'on hand'", given in simple intuitions of experience; "to this world of experience the I finds itself related in new acts, for example in acts of valuing" (Hua IV, 186). On the first statement the object of use comes first and the mere thing is derived. On the second there is a core on hand and valuing is built upon it. I do not find that the text decides between them, and I leave both standing until part 4.

### 2.2 World-belief and transcendental illusion

The lectures on First Philosophy of 1923/24 return to the thought experiment of § 49 and change its form. The chapter concerned is entitled "World-perception and world-belief" (Hua VIII, 44), and the second word is new. What the first book of the *Ideas* called a thesis is now called a belief (*Weltglaube*), and Husserl says where it comes from. We have lived, as memory tells us, in a harmony of experience that preserved or restored the concordance of all perceptions. This style "necessarily motivates in itself the anticipatory belief that it will continue so. And so we believe, and so we must believe; the belief belongs constantly to the universal structure of streaming world-perception" (Hua VIII, 53–54). The holding that was a standing character of acts in 1913 is here a motivated anticipation, and its motive is the past course of experience itself.

Because it is an anticipation, it can fail. The structure of worldly perception is "contingent, a mere fact, which could also be otherwise" (Hua VIII, 53), and so, while we live in concordant belief, we can see the possibility that this bodily perceived world is a mere illusion. Husserl gives the illusion a name that marks it off from every ordinary one. An empirical illusion is met with on the ground of the world, and it always makes sense to ask what true thing lies behind it. A "transcendental illusion" would be the non-being of the world as a whole, and here the question of what is to be put in its place "would be wholly senseless", no different from asking what real thing exists in place of the water-nymph I have just freely imagined (Hua VIII, 54). The world could not be corrected into another world. It could only cease to hold.

Husserl then guards this result with unusual care, and the passage shows how far he is from asserting what § 49 is often taken to assert. The results must not be given "a perverse sense", as if the world we experience "*perhaps*" were not, or as if one had to be prepared for an end of the world as for a change in the weather. "We say on the contrary: the existence of the world is entirely *beyond doubt*, and this indubitability is contained in the world-perception itself in which we continuously live." Whoever judges otherwise follows verbal arguments and does not look at "the unbreakable world-belief" that lies in experience, "a belief that tolerates beside and within itself not the slightest presumption or real possibility of being otherwise". Nothing speaks for the non-being of the world, and everything for its being. And yet this perfect empirical certainty, as empirical, "leaves open the possibility that the world is not". The outcome is stated as a compatibility: "With our empirically indubitable perceptual certainty of the existence of the world, the proposition that this world is a pure nothing, a mere transcendental illusion, is *compatible*" (Hua VIII, 54–55). A later page says of the experience in which the existence of the world is originally given that it is inadequate through and through and therefore "constantly leaves open the non-being of the world" (Hua VIII, 68).

The position of 1923/24 can be put in two clauses. The belief in the world is unbreakable as a belief; no motive within experience speaks against it, and none could. Its content is nevertheless not necessary; the hypothesis of the world's non-being is free of absurdity. Husserl holds both, and the distinction between an indubitable belief and a necessary truth is what allows him to. In 1913 the same point was made by imagining a chaos. Here it is made by examining a belief, and the change of procedure matters more than it seems. A chaos is something one pictures from outside. A belief is something one has, and of this one Husserl now says that we "must" have it.

### 2.3 The one world of experience

In the summer semester of 1925, the semester of Heidegger's course on the history of the concept of time, Husserl lectured on phenomenological psychology and began with the task he had set aside in § 30 of *Ideas I*: the description of the world as it is found before science. The systematic part opens with the need to go back to "the pre-scientific world of experience". The words we use for what we see, he observes, tables and benches and lecture notebooks, express the things seen in a relation of sense that leads back to our own and others' activities; and he proposes to take what is experienced "exactly as it gives itself" (Hua IX, 57). A first look back and around then yields this: "at all times a world is experienced, and through all change of apprehensions, and before the thinking that may afterwards come into play, there is continuously experienced the one and the same world, about which statements can be made and for which knowledge of truth is to be striven for." All questions about true being presuppose this world of experience. "The true world thus designates a higher product of knowledge", which has its original working material in the flowing universe of what is given in experience; this first actuality is "the primal field" from which the true world is to be worked out by science (Hua IX, 57).

Two things are new, and both prepare the last work. The world found before science is no longer a stage on the way to the reduction, to be described only as far as the reduction needs; it is the presupposition of every question of truth, and a science of its "general structure" is announced, a structure that is "not accidentally factual but a priori necessary" (Hua IX, 57). And the world of science, which in 1913 was the "physical truth" that reason is compelled to lay under the things of intuition, is now a product worked out of the first. What has not changed is the determination of this first world. It is "the one and the same world", and it is one as what is continuously experienced through changing apprehensions, that is, as an identity that holds through them.

### 2.4 The world as phenomenon of validity and as idea

The *Cartesian Meditations*, which grew out of the Paris lectures of February 1929 and appeared in French in 1931 (Hua I, editor's introduction), are the text in which Husserl's way to the reduction is most openly Cartesian, and § 7 states the status of the world accordingly. The being of the world is the most obvious thing there is, so obvious that no one thinks of asserting it; the evidence of world-experience is earlier than all the evidences of life and science, "whose supporting ground it constantly is". Yet it cannot claim to be apodictic. Not only can a single experience be devalued as illusion; the whole surveyable connection of experience can prove to be illusion, "under the title of a *coherent dream*" (Hua I, 57). Husserl does not take this as a proof that the non-being of the world is thinkable. He takes from it only that the evidence of world-experience needs a critique, and he states the consequence in the vocabulary I am following. It is not enough to put the sciences out of validity. "Their universal ground too, that of the world of experience, we must deprive of its naive validity. The being of the world on the basis of the natural evidence of experience may no longer be for us an obvious fact, but itself only a phenomenon of validity (*Geltungsphänomen*)" (Hua I, 58). The paragraph ends on a question that names the stake: what if the world were in the end not the absolutely first ground of judgement, and a ground of being prior in itself were already presupposed with its existence?

The world is here called a ground twice in ten lines, and what the suspension does to it is to turn its validity into a phenomenon. § 28 adds what the world is as a whole. Everything real is given in external experience, and to this kind of evidence there belongs essentially "a many-formed horizon of unfulfilled anticipations that need fulfilment". It always remains open that the belief reaching into the anticipation is not fulfilled. Since an actual object can have sense only as a unity meant in consciousness, which would be given as itself in a complete evidence of experience, it follows "that an actual object of a world, and all the more a world itself, is an infinite idea, related to infinities of experiences to be harmoniously united", a "correlate-idea" to the idea of a complete synthesis of possible experiences (Hua I, 96–97). The horizon of 1913, of which only the form was prefigured, has become an idea in the sense of a task that cannot be completed. The world as a whole is never given; it is what the endless concordance of experience would give.

On one point the *Meditations* say something that the first book of the *Ideas* did not say and that its § 49 seems to exclude. Among the "metaphysical results" of the Fifth Meditation stands this: "My ego, given to me apodictically, the only one that I can posit in absolute apodicticity as existing, can a priori be a world-experiencing ego only by being in community with others like it" (Hua I, 166). The sentence can be read in two ways. Read weakly, it states a condition: if my ego experiences a world, then it does so in community. Read strongly, it says what my ego can a priori be, namely one that experiences a world. The continuation speaks of structures "laid down in me" that imply the co-existence of other monads and with them one single nature, and it adds, of the two worlds that two separate communities of monads might seem to have, that they are "mere surrounding worlds" and "mere aspects of a single objective world common to them" (Hua I, 167). The example is again that of beings on stars invisible to us. In 1913 the one world was the correlate of a possible agreement, and the ego could be thought without any world. In 1931 the one world is prescribed by structures laid down in the ego. Whether such an ego could still be the residuum of an annihilation of the world is not asked in the passages I have read. I record the tension and return to it in part 5.

### 2.5 Pregivenness, ground and horizon

The *Crisis* is the work in which the description of the world found before science, set aside in 1913 and begun in 1925, becomes the way into phenomenology itself. I use five of its sections and two appendices. The file dates several of the appended texts to 1936 and the following winter; I have not checked the editor's account of the composition of the main text and treat it as a work of 1936.

The work states its problem in a sentence that could stand as the heading of this part. The problem that Hume bequeaths, once his sceptical theory is turned back into a question, is this: how is "the naive obviousness of the world-certainty in which we live" to be made intelligible? And what is "objective world", according to sense and validity, once it is seen that "'world' is a validity that has sprung up in subjectivity", and, spoken from the one who philosophises, "in my subjectivity"? (Hua VI, 99). The word of 1913 was thesis and the word of 1923 was belief. The word is now certainty (*Weltgewißheit*), and what the certainty is of is called a validity outright.

§ 37 then says what distinguishes the world from everything in it, and it is the statement the opening quoted. There is "a fundamental difference in the manner of consciousness of the world and consciousness of a thing", although the two form an inseparable unity. Things are given as holding for us in some mode of certainty of being, "but in principle only in such a way that they are conscious as things, as *objects in the world-horizon*. Each is something, 'something out of' the world that is constantly conscious to us as horizon." The horizon, for its part, is conscious only as horizon for existing objects. Then: "the world does not exist as a being, as an object exists, but exists in a *uniqueness* for which the plural is senseless. Every plural, and every singular lifted out of it, presupposes the world-horizon" (Hua VI, 146). The difference between the manner of being of an object in the world and that of the world itself prescribes to each its own manner of consciousness. I note that the file is damaged at the head of this paragraph, where the transition from the preceding page is lost, and that the first word of the sentence just quoted is misprinted; the wording used here is otherwise undisturbed.

Three determinations of the world follow in § 38, and they should be kept apart. The world is, first, horizon in a wider sense than that of 1913: "The pregiven world is the horizon that includes all our goals, all our ends, fleeting or lasting", and "world is the universal field into which all our acts, experiencing, knowing, acting, are directed" (Hua VI, 147). The horizon of § 27 was that of what could be perceived. This one is the horizon of what can be aimed at, and theoretical and practical themes are said to lie in it alike. The world is, second, pregiven (*vorgegeben*). The word is introduced as the title for a change of interest: instead of living straightforwardly into the world, one asks "how, in the change of relative validities, subjective appearances and opinions, the unitary, universal validity world, the world for us, comes about" (Hua VI, 147–49). The world is, third, ground (*Boden*). All sciences so far have been sciences "on the ground of the world"; the new science would be one "of the universal How of the pregivenness of the world, that is, of what makes up its universal being-a-ground for any objectivity whatever" (Hua VI, 149). The phrase that § 30 of the *Ideas* used in passing has become a concept, and it has been joined to the concept that was already there.

An appendix puts the two together in one compound. The general world-certainty, it says, "goes before everything, so to speak, not as an assertion of being and so not as a premise in the proper sense"; it functions "as ground of validity (*Geltungsboden*), on which all sense of being of every kind depends". And the appendix draws the consequence for the word "being": what is, normally means "what is on the ground of the world, what is in the world" (Hua VI, 400).

It remains to ask what has become of § 49. Two texts of the *Crisis* bear on it, and they point the same way without saying the same thing. The first is Husserl's remark on his own earlier path. The "Cartesian way" of the *Ideas* has, he says, the great disadvantage that it leads to the transcendental ego "as if in one leap" and so brings it into view "in an apparent emptiness of content", in which one is at a loss what is supposed to have been gained (Hua VI, 158). This is a criticism of a way and not of a doctrine; the annihilation of the world is not mentioned. The second text goes further. In an appendix which the volume attaches to §§ 16 and following, Husserl asks whether Descartes really showed that the being of the world of everyday experience is doubtful. One may come to doubt whether one is really sitting by the fire in a dressing gown. "But is such a *doubt* about a situation that was at first entirely certain a doubtfulness of the being of the world as such? Is it not the peculiarity of world-certainty that it remains unbroken, in its way even apodictic as world-certainty, in spite of all possible and actual doubts about situations? And that it is nevertheless not one that can be destroyed at will, although the single realities, each in its real situation, admit of doubt?" (Hua VI, 407–08).

Is this to say that Husserl ends by retracting the thinkability of the world's non-being? In a certain way the appendix says more than any earlier text. In 1913 the general thesis could be put out of action "in our perfect freedom". In 1923/24 the world-belief was unbreakable but not apodictic, and the *Meditations* still deny the evidence of the world apodictic rank in so many words. Here the certainty of the world is called apodictic "in its way" and withdrawn from arbitrary destruction. However, the passage is a pair of questions in an appendix, it concerns doubt and not the suspension, and it does not say that a world is necessary. I conclude only that Husserl's last texts hesitate on the point where his first was most confident, and that the hesitation runs in one direction.

One difficulty is stated by Husserl himself and not removed in the pages read here. If the world is a validity that has sprung up in subjectivity, in whose? The universal intersubjectivity into which all objectivity resolves itself can, it seems, be none other than humankind, which is itself a component of the world. "How is a component part of the world, its human subjectivity, to constitute the whole world, namely constitute it as its intentional formation?" (Hua VI, 183). The question is the one Heidegger put to Husserl nine years earlier in other words.

The result of this part can be stated briefly. Through four names, thesis, belief, certainty and ground of validity, one determination persists: the world is what holds for a subject before and beneath everything that holds or fails to hold within it. What changes is the description of this holding. In 1913 it is a posit that freedom can suspend and that experience might cease to sustain. In the last texts it is a ground on which one stands, pregiven before every act, certain in a way that single doubts do not reach. The change is one of emphasis within a constant concept, and it leaves open whether a subject for whom nothing held would still be a subject. Heidegger's concept of world begins from the denial that it would.

## 3. The world as significance and as for-the-sake-of: Heidegger, 1925–1929

Heidegger's concept of world is worked out in lecture courses and in one book, and it changes within the five years considered here. I follow it through three stages: the course of 1925, in which the concept is won against Husserl and Descartes; the book and the course of 1927, in which the world is said to belong to the being of Dasein and the question of its subjectivity is asked and answered; and the texts of 1928 and 1929, in which the first analysis is expressly lowered in rank and the world is determined from freedom. As in part 2, I ask one question of each stage: what binds the world to the one whose world it is?

### 3.1 The world that is encountered

The course of the summer of 1925 contains, in its first half, an extended discussion of Husserl, and it reaches the world by way of a criticism. Husserl's starting point, the natural attitude, is examined for what it takes as given. In it the human being is given as a living thing, "as a zoological object", something that occurs in the world. Heidegger asks whether this is how a human being experiences himself and others, and he answers that such an experience is "not natural at all, but contains a quite definite theoretical stance", one for which every being is grasped in advance as a lawfully regulated course of occurrences in the spatio-temporal apartness of the world (GA 20, 155–56). The criticism is aimed at the description of the I as a member of the world in § 27 and after. It does not yet touch what Husserl says of the world there, and Heidegger extends it to the second book of the *Ideas*, which he reports under its title: the personalistic attitude only brings back the old definition of the human being as rational animal, and "Husserl does not get beyond Dilthey" (GA 20, 173–74).

The positive concept is introduced in § 21 with a formula that the later texts will not repeat. We ask, Heidegger says, about the world as it is there in everyday concern, about the surrounding world, and more exactly about "the worldhood of the surrounding world"; and worldhood (*Weltlichkeit*) is "the specific being of this being 'world'" (GA 20, 230). In 1925, then, the world is still spoken of as a being that has a being of its own. What that being is, is shown first by contrast. § 22 describes the traditional way of determining a thing of the world, with Descartes as the example: one starts from the thing of nature with its material properties, adds sensible qualities, and then, in order to reach the thing as it is first found, adds "certain value predicates: good, bad, ugly, beautiful, suitable, unsuitable", which also attach to all objects of use. The practical thing is then "a thing of nature with the fundamental stratum of materiality, but at the same time laden with value predicates" (GA 20, 247). Heidegger's verdict is that the thing so described "remains naturalised"; the being of the surrounding world has not been reached, let alone the worldhood of this world (GA 20, 248). Applied to our example: on this procedure the table with its books is a body of a certain extension to which usefulness is ascribed, and it is this order of ascription that Heidegger rejects. What he sets against it is significance (*Bedeutsamkeit*), which he calls the mode of presence in view of which every being of the world is discovered, and of which he says that it is the way of encounter and of being of the world itself (GA 20, 287, 291).

The section on the reality of the external world then says what remains of the principle on which Husserl's idealism rests, and it is more than the criticism of the natural attitude would lead one to expect. The question of the being of the world cannot mean an inquiry into how the world manages to be; that would explain being from a being. But from this refusal one arrives at "the familiar proposition that a being always is only *for* a consciousness", the so-called principle of immanence. Heidegger neither accepts nor refutes it. "What the proposition at bottom means, what is seen in it, is not that a being depends in its being on consciousness, and that the transcendent is really at the same time something immanent; the phenomenal finding is: *world is encountered*" (GA 20, 297–98). The finding gives the instruction to interpret the structure of encounter. Here, in 1925, the two authors are nearest. What Husserl calls the being of the world for consciousness and Heidegger calls encounter is one finding under two names, and the dispute concerns what the finding is a finding about: a consciousness that posits, or a being that lets itself be met.

### 3.2 The world as a character of Dasein

*Being and Time* begins its analysis of the world by disentangling the word, and the passage is the place where the question of this study is asked. Neither the description of the beings within the world nor the interpretation of their being reaches the phenomenon "world"; both presuppose it. Can "world", then, be addressed as a determination of those beings at all? Is it a character of the being of Dasein? "And does every Dasein then 'first of all' have its world? Does 'world' not thus become something 'subjective'? How then should a 'common' world still be possible, 'in' which, after all, we are?" (SZ 64). The questions are not answered here. Heidegger instead fixes the term by a numbered distinction of four senses (SZ 64–65). (1) "World" as an ontic concept means the totality of beings that can be on hand within the world. (2) As an ontological term it means the being of those beings, and it can become the title of a region, as in "the 'world' of the mathematician". (3) In a second ontic sense it means not the beings that Dasein is not, but that "in which" a factical Dasein "lives", the public we-world or one's own nearest surrounding world. (4) Finally it designates the ontological and existential concept of worldhood. Heidegger reserves the word for the third sense and writes it in quotation marks when he means the first.

Two things in this list concern the comparison. The first two senses are the ones under which Husserl is commonly placed, and the second is illustrated by an example, the world of the mathematician, that § 28 of *Ideas I* had used for the same purpose and with the same result: a "world" in a loose sense, which is not the world. The third sense, the one Heidegger adopts, is defined by the preposition "in" and by the verb "lives", and it is expressly called ontic; the world in which one lives is something factical, and the ontological concept is its worldhood. One sentence before the list states the thesis that separates Heidegger from every account in part 2: "'World' is ontologically no determination of the beings that Dasein essentially is *not*, but a character of Dasein itself" (SZ 64).

§ 18 says what this character is. Dasein lets beings be encountered as ready for use by having always already referred itself, from a for-the-sake-of-which, to the with-which of an involvement. That in which it understands itself beforehand in this referring is that in view of which it lets beings be encountered. The definition is italicised: "*The wherein of the self-referring understanding, as that in view of which beings are allowed to be encountered in the mode of being of involvement, is the phenomenon of world*" (SZ 86). The whole of the relations of this referring is named significance (SZ 87). The table with its books is, on this account, never first a thing. It is something for writing or for reading, and it is this in a whole of references that ends in a possibility of the one who writes and reads. What binds world and Dasein in 1927 is therefore understanding: the world is that in which Dasein understands itself, and with which it is "originally familiar". Heidegger adds that this familiarity does not require theoretical transparency, and that it "co-constitutes Dasein's understanding of being" (SZ 86).

The description of the natural world as a thing of nature furnished with values is rejected in *Being and Time* in the terms of the lecture course, and more sharply. In the discussion of Descartes, § 21 asks whether the being of what is first encountered can be reached by completing the thing of nature into the full thing of use. It cannot: the addition of value predicates "is not able to give the least new information about the being of goods, but only presupposes again for them the mode of being of pure presence-at-hand. Values are determinations *on hand* in a thing" (SZ 99–100). The word I rendered "on hand" in part 1 is here a term of ontology. Presence-at-hand (*Vorhandenheit*) is, in Heidegger's use, the mode of being of what merely occurs, as opposed to what is ready for use and to Dasein itself. We shall have to ask in part 4 whether the word means in Husserl's § 27 what it means in this sentence.

Two later sections of the book say what the world is when it is taken by itself, and both do so through a negation. § 40 describes anxiety. That in the face of which one is anxious is nothing within the world; the whole of involvements discovered within the world "collapses into itself", and "the world has the character of complete insignificance" (SZ 186). Yet this does not mean that the world is absent. The innerworldly beings are of so little consequence in themselves that, on the ground of this insignificance, "the world in its worldhood is all that still obtrudes itself"; "*that in the face of which one has anxiety is the world as such*" (SZ 186–87). When things cease to matter, then, the world is not lost. It is what remains.

§ 69 gives the temporal ground and returns to the question of § 14. Dasein exists for the sake of a potentiality of its own being, and "that *wherein* existing Dasein understands *itself* is 'there' along with its factical existence"; Dasein "*is*, existing, its world" (SZ 364). The world is neither on hand nor ready to hand: it "temporalises itself in temporality", and "if no Dasein exists, no world is 'there' either" (SZ 365). Since it must already be disclosed for anything to be encountered from out of it, the world is "transcendent", and is "as it were already 'further outside' than any object can ever be" (SZ 366). The question of 1913 and of the tradition, how a subject gets out to an object, is replaced by another: what makes it possible that beings are encountered within a world. Then comes the answer to § 14: "If the 'subject' is conceived ontologically as existing Dasein, whose being is grounded in temporality, then it must be said: world is 'subjective'. But this 'subjective' world, as temporally transcendent, is then 'more objective' than any possible 'object'" (SZ 366).

It should be added that *Being and Time* does not treat the opposite position as a mere error. In the section on reality, idealism is given "a fundamental priority" over realism, provided it does not misunderstand itself psychologically: when it insists that being and reality are only "in consciousness", it expresses the understanding "that being cannot be explained by beings". Its fault is that it leaves the being of consciousness itself unasked (SZ 207–08). And Heidegger states a dependence of his own that sounds like Husserl's: only as long as Dasein is, "is there" being; if Dasein does not exist, there "is" neither independence nor in-itself (SZ 212).

The course of the summer of 1927 states the result with fewer reservations and one new verb. Is the world nature, or the whole of the things that surround us, the context of equipment? Neither: "the world is not nature and not what is on hand at all, just as little as it is the whole of the things surrounding us, the context of equipment, the surrounding world" (GA 24, 235). "The world is not the sum of what is on hand; it is nothing on hand at all. It is a determination of being-in-the-world, a moment in the structure of Dasein's mode of being. The world is something of the kind of Dasein (*etwas Daseinsmäßiges*)." And then: "The world is not on hand; it exists, that is, it has the mode of being of Dasein" (GA 24, 237). Heidegger carries an example through these pages, the shoemaker and the shoe. The shoemaker is not the shoe; but the shoe, as the piece of equipment it is, is intelligible only from the world that belongs to the existence of the shoemaker, a world that is itself said to be existing and that first makes it possible to discover a context of equipment (GA 24, 244). Two points deserve notice. The surrounding world, which in 1925 was the place where worldhood was sought, is here listed among the things the world is not. And the world is said to exist. The second statement will not survive the following year.

### 3.3 Agreement and question: the letter of October 1927

Between the course of 1927 and that of 1928 lies the document in which Heidegger says to Husserl what he takes the two of them to share. It is the first enclosure to his letter of 22 October 1927, written in the course of their work on the drafts of an encyclopaedia article, and its first sentence is this: "There is agreement that the being in the sense of what you call 'world' cannot be clarified in its transcendental constitution by a return to a being of just such a mode of being" (Hua IX, 601). The phrase "what you call 'world'" marks the word as Husserl's and withholds assent to it. What Husserl calls world is, for the Heidegger of *Being and Time*, "world" in the first sense, the totality of beings. But the agreement stated is real, and it is the one that Husserl's § 55 had formulated as the absurdity of an absolute reality: what is in the world cannot be made intelligible from something else that is in the world.

The enclosure then turns the agreement into a question. It does not follow that what makes up the place of the transcendental is no being at all; "rather the *problem* arises: what is the mode of being of the being in which 'world' is constituted? That is the central problem of *Being and Time*". The answer is given in two sentences. The mode of being of human Dasein is totally different from that of every other being, and precisely as such it harbours the possibility of transcendental constitution. And transcendental constitution is "a central possibility of the existence of the factical self", which as existing is never a "worldly real fact", because the human being is never merely on hand (Hua IX, 601–02). Heidegger thus accepts Husserl's verb. The world is constituted, and it is constituted in a being. He contests what Husserl says, or leaves unsaid, about that being. Husserl's own formulation of the same difficulty, nine years later, was quoted at the end of part 2.

### 3.4 The world as for-the-sake-of, as freedom and as nothing

The course of the summer of 1928 and the treatise *On the Essence of Ground*, which its preface dates to the same year and which appeared in 1929, carry the concept beyond *Being and Time* in three respects, and the treatise says of the earlier analysis what it was worth. In a note to its second part Heidegger writes that if one identifies the world with the ontic context of things of use and interprets being-in-the-world as dealing with them, an understanding of transcendence as a basic constitution of Dasein is "hopeless". The analysis of the surrounding world in §§ 14 to 24 of *Being and Time* had the single purpose of leading over to the phenomenon of world, and, "seen as a whole and with a view to the *guiding aim*", it remains "of subordinate significance" (GA 9, 155–56 n.). The remark does not retract the analysis. It tells the reader that the table and the shoe were a way of access, and that what was to be reached lies elsewhere.

Where it lies is said, first, by a history of the word. The treatise follows the concept from the Greek *kosmos* through the New Testament and Augustine to Kant, and it finds throughout that "world" means a how of beings as a whole in its relation to human existence, and in one strand, which runs from Augustine's lovers of the world to Kant's talk of knowing the world and having world, human existence itself in its being with others. From this it concludes that it is "equally mistaken" to claim the expression either "as designation of the totality of natural things (natural concept of world) or as title for the community of human beings (personal concept of world)" (GA 9, 155). The two rejected concepts are, in Husserl's vocabulary, the world of the naturalistic attitude and the world of the personalistic attitude; Heidegger does not name him. What the word aims at is the interpretation of human Dasein in its relation to beings as a whole.

The second step gives this relation its character. "World as a whole 'is' no being, but that from out of which Dasein gives itself to understand to which beings it can comport itself and how." Dasein is such that it exists for the sake of itself; and if the world is that in surpassing to which selfhood first arises, then the world shows itself as that for the sake of which Dasein exists. "The world has the basic character of the for-the-sake-of (*Umwillen*)", in the original sense that it first gives the inner possibility for every factical "for your sake, for his sake, for the sake of that" (GA 9, 157). The for-the-sake-of-which was, in § 18 of *Being and Time*, the last term of the chain of references. It is now the first character of the world, and significance is not mentioned. Dasein is accordingly called world-forming (*weltbildend*): it lets world happen and gives itself with the world an original view which, without being grasped expressly, functions as the model for all manifest beings (GA 9, 158).

The third step names the ground of this character, and the lecture course is plainer than the treatise. A for-the-sake-of is possible only where there is a will. "Only where there is freedom is there a for-the-sake-of, and only there is there *world*. In short: the transcendence of Dasein and freedom are *identical*" (GA 26, 237–38). The world, "primarily characterised by the for-the-sake-of, is the original wholeness of what Dasein as free gives itself to understand", and in projecting it Dasein gives itself its original binding; "the whole of the binding that lies in the for-the-sake-of is the world" (GA 26, 247). Freedom is not meant here as spontaneity. It is the projection of possibilities in which Dasein holds itself and by which it is held, and Heidegger draws from it a consequence for everything actual. "The world, as the whole of the essential inner possibilities of Dasein as transcending, *surpasses* every actual being." Whatever is met with, and precisely when it is met as it is in itself, shows itself only as a restriction, as "one possible realisation of the possible", as what falls short "out of a surplus of possibilities" in which Dasein always already holds itself (GA 26, 248). The table with its books is, on this last account, one realisation of what a being that can read and write holds open for itself, and it is understood as a table from that openness.

On this basis the question of 1927 is asked once more, and the answer has lost its quotation marks. Beings on hand are what they are without Dasein; that they are met with depends on their having the occasion to enter a world. Entry into world (*Welteingang*) "is no process in what is on hand"; it happens when transcendence happens, that is, when historical Dasein exists (GA 26, 251). "World itself there is only in so far as Dasein exists. But is world then not something 'subjective'? Indeed it is. Only one must not now slip in again a common, subjectivist concept of the 'subject'"; being-in-the-world "transforms the concept of subjectivity and of the subjective from the ground up" (GA 26, 252). And the mode of being that the course of 1927 had given the world is withdrawn in the next paragraph: "the world is *nothing*, if 'nothing' means: not a being in the sense of what is on hand", and more than that, not a being at all. "The world: a nothing, no being, and yet something; nothing that is, but being" (GA 26, 252). § 12 determines this nothing from time. The world is nothing in the sense that it is no being, and yet it is something that "there is"; what gives it is temporality temporalising itself, and what temporality yields as the unity of its horizon is the world. "The world is the nothing that originally temporalises itself, what simply arises in and with temporalising; we therefore call it the *nihil originarium*" (GA 26, 272). The treatise has a sentence to the same effect, of which the file prints the last word doubtfully: world "never *is*" (GA 9, 164).

The course of the following winter, which I can read only in translation, gives the world one more name. Taking up Kant's phrase of the "play of life", Heidegger says that world "has the character of play", and that this is "not just a figure of speech". Play means first the playing and second the whole of the rules according to which the playing proceeds; but playing is more original than both, and games exist because we play (GA 27, § 36). I cite the passage for one reason. A game is a whole of rules that binds only those who play it and exists only in being played. The word says in an image what the course of 1928 said in concepts: the world is a binding that Dasein gives itself.

It was said at the outset that Heidegger's concept changes, and the change can now be dated. In 1925 the world is a being whose being is significance. In 1927 it is a character of Dasein, the wherein of its understanding, and it "exists". From 1928 it is no being, the surrounding world is no longer the measure, and its first character is the for-the-sake-of that freedom projects. Through these changes one thing is held fast, and it answers the question put at the head of this part. What binds the world to Dasein is never that Dasein takes something to be. It is that Dasein understands itself from the world, and, in the last texts, that it gives itself the world as what it can be. Heidegger reports in the course of 1928 that when he first put the phenomenon of transcendence to Husserl, two years earlier, Husserl "vehemently opposed it from the outset" (GA 26, 214). Before the two concepts are compared, the reading that grounds this opposition from Heidegger's side must be heard at full strength.

## 4. The opposing reading: the world as nature on hand

The three preceding parts have presented Husserl's world as a validity and Heidegger's as a wherein of self-understanding. There is a reading of Husserl on which the first of these presentations is too generous, and it is the reading Heidegger himself gives in the texts of 1925 and 1927. On it, Husserl's world is from first to last a world of things on hand, and everything said of horizon, sense and validity describes the same world from the side of a subject that looks at it. The working notes on which this study drew in its preparation, and which are named in the register, follow Heidegger here: they say that the world of § 27 "is still *vorhanden*" and that its practical and value characters "are layered on things present". I shall first state the reading as strongly as the texts allow, and then reply to it point by point.

### 4.1 The reading at full strength

The reading has four members, and each can cite Husserl's own words.

The first concerns the attitude in which the world is described. Husserl calls it natural. Heidegger answers that an attitude in which I find myself as "a member" of the one spatio-temporal actuality (Hua III/1, 58), a real unit among real units, is the attitude of someone who already regards everything as an occurrence in nature. The being ascribed to the experiencing subject in it is "that of real occurrences in the world, of living beings that are objectively on hand, built by their being into the 'fundamental stratum' of all reality, into material thinghood" (GA 20, 153). The phrase "fundamental stratum" is not Heidegger's invention. The second book of the *Ideas* describes the kinds of apprehension that constitute nature as "built upon one another: physical experience as the founding one", and resting on it the experience of the body and, as a stratum of the latter, the experience of the soul (Hua IV, 174). If the world is found in such an attitude, then what is found is nature, and the finder is a piece of it.

The second member concerns what the world contains. Husserl's first word for the way things are there is "on hand", and he applies it without distinction to bodies, animals, human beings and the world as a whole. He does say that the world is also a world of values and of goods. But the sentence in which he says it ends by assigning the value characters and practical characters "to the objects 'on hand' as such" (Hua III/1, 58), and the second book states the order outright: the world is "in its core" a world appearing to the senses and characterised as on hand, to which the I then relates itself "in new acts" of valuing (Hua IV, 186). This is the procedure that § 21 of *Being and Time* describes and rejects: a thing of nature is posited first and completed into a thing of use by predicates, and the predicates presuppose for the goods "the mode of being of pure presence-at-hand" (SZ 99–100). The table with its books is, for Husserl, a spatial body that is in addition useful.

The third member concerns the whole. When Husserl speaks of the world as a whole, he speaks of a totality of such things. What is annihilated in § 49 is "the world of things"; what § 55 calls world is "the universe of realities"; and even the *Crisis*, in the section that follows the statement about the uniqueness of the world, describes the world-horizon as the "universal horizon of real, actually existing objects" (Hua VI, 147–48). Horizon and form, on this reading, do not alter the concept. A horizon of objects is the totality of objects taken from the standpoint of one who has not yet reached them all.

The fourth member concerns the subject. Since the world is a totality of what is on hand, it can be thought away, and what remains is a consciousness that needs no thing in order to exist. This is the "worldless subject" of *Being and Time* (SZ 366), and nothing could be further from a being whose basic constitution is being-in-the-world.

The reading can be given one further turn, which is directed at the thesis of this study and is, I think, its strongest form. Let it be granted that Husserl determines the world as a thesis and later as a validity. What is a thesis? It is a positing of something as existing, and Husserl himself says that the general thesis can be treated "exactly" like the thesis of an expressed judgement (Hua III/1, 62). To be on hand and to be posited as existing are then two descriptions of one state of affairs, the first from the side of the object and the second from the side of the subject. A world that is a validity is the world of a subject whose first relation to things is to hold them to be. That is the theoretical subject. So the thesis of part 1 does not answer Heidegger's reading. It restates it.

### 4.2 The replies

I take the four members in order and the further turn last.

(A) *The attitude.* Heidegger is right that Husserl's first description places the I in the world as one of its members, and that the first book of the *Ideas* does not ask what kind of being a member of the world has that can also be the one for whom the world is there. The question returns in the *Crisis* as a paradox and is, on Husserl's own showing, unanswered in 1913. But the reading uses as evidence a passage that describes the naturalistic attitude, and of this attitude the same manuscript says that it is subordinate to another, that it arises "through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal I", and that it "illegitimately absolutises its world, nature" (Hua IV, 184). The order of strata that Heidegger quotes is, for Husserl, the order of an abstraction. Heidegger knows this, since he reports the personalistic attitude in the same lecture course, and his reply is that the person is still described by Husserl as an I of acts with a "natural side" beneath it (GA 20, 170). That reply concerns the being of the person, which is the matter of the letter of 1927 (part 3.3). It does not show that Husserl takes the natural attitude to be the naturalist's.

(B) *The things.* Here the texts give two answers, as part 2 showed, and the reading relies on one of them. The first book says that the world is a world of values and of practice "in the same immediacy" as it is a world of things, and that things stand there "immediately" as objects of use (Hua III/1, 58). The second book says, before it reaches the person at all, that in ordinary life "we have nothing at all to do with objects of nature", that what we call things are houses, tables, clothes and tools, and that the object of natural science is reached by "a kind of ἐποχή" (Hua IV, 27). On these statements the table is first a table. It is true that § 50 of the same book speaks of a core on hand and of acts of valuing that are built on it. However, the two statements are not about the same thing. The first describes what is found; the second describes how the acts in which it is found depend on one another. Husserl holds that a valuing is founded on a presenting, and Heidegger is right to see in this founding of acts a decision about the being of what they are directed at. But it is not completely correct to say that Husserl first posits a thing of nature and then adds predicates. He finds a table, and he analyses the finding into dependent layers. The charge of § 21 holds against the analysis and not against the description.

One must add that the word on which the reading leans is not one word. Husserl's "on hand" is set in quotation marks and glossed as "in the literal or the figurative sense"; it is said of friends and enemies as well as of bodies; and what it names is explained in § 31 as the character "there", which everything bears that I am conscious of in the natural world before all thinking (Hua III/1, 56, 62). It is the mark of the thesis on what is posited. Heidegger's presence-at-hand is one mode of being among three and is defined against readiness-to-hand; a tool in use is precisely not present-at-hand in his sense. Husserl's "on hand" includes the object of use "immediately". The two terms therefore do not coincide, and one cannot read § 27 as asserting of the world what § 21 of *Being and Time* denies of it. What is true in the charge is something else. Husserl has one character, "there", for the stone, the table and the friend. He does not distinguish ways of being there, and to that extent the complaint that the question of being is neglected is just.

(C) *The whole.* That Husserl often speaks of the world as a universe of things or realities is beyond doubt, and the phrase from the *Crisis* shows that he never stops doing so. It does not follow that he defines the world by it. In 1913 the world is what remains "always there" when any thing whatever is struck out of it; only its form is prefigured; and the indeterminate horizon is "necessarily there". None of this can be said of a totality, which is altered by every removal and is not there until its members are. In 1936 the difference is stated as one of manner of being: each thing is something "out of" the world, and the world does not exist as a being exists. The concept of horizon would restate the concept of totality only if a horizon were a collection of things not yet reached. Husserl's horizon is that from out of which each thing is a thing, and he says in so many words that the plural and the singular both presuppose it.

(D) *The subject.* The fourth member is correct for 1913 and has to be qualified for every later text. The lectures of 1923/24 keep the possibility that the world is not, and deny in the same breath that anything speaks for it or that the belief in the world can be shaken. The *Meditations* say that my ego can a priori be only a world-experiencing ego. The *Crisis* criticises the way of the *Ideas* for delivering an ego in "apparent emptiness of content" (Hua VI, 158), and an appendix calls the certainty of the world apodictic in its way. What was first meant as Heidegger's objection to Husserl thus proves, in part, to be a thought of Husserl's own. I say in part, because Husserl's self-criticism is addressed to a way of presentation, and he nowhere says, in the texts read here, that a consciousness without a world is impossible.

(E) *The further turn.* There remains the argument that a world determined as validity is by that very fact the world of a theoretical subject. In a certain way one must grant it. Husserl does model the bond between subject and world on holding something to be. The general thesis is introduced as what a judgement of existence makes explicit; the world-belief is a belief; the *Crisis* speaks of certainty and of "modes of certainty of being". A holding-to-be is what logic studies in the judgement, and Husserl never takes another model. However, it does not follow that the subject of this holding is the theoretical subject in the sense of the first member, the scientist for whom everything is nature. The general thesis is no act and precedes all thinking. The world-certainty of the *Crisis* goes before every positing of being, "but also before every valuing and practical activity", and it is the ground of validity on which their sense depends too (Hua VI, 400). The pregiven world is the horizon of "all our goals, all our ends" (Hua VI, 147). Whatever one does with the table, writing at it or measuring it, one does on a ground that holds, and this holding is neither a use nor a measurement.

### 4.3 A placing that Heidegger does not make

Before the result of these replies is drawn, one further version of the opposing reading should be considered. It is not found in Heidegger's remarks on Husserl; I construct it from his history of the concept of world, because it is stronger than the version he gives.

In the treatise of 1929 and in the course of the preceding winter, Heidegger distinguishes from the concept of world of the older metaphysics the concept that Kant reaches in the first *Critique*. The older concept means the totality of created things. Kant keeps the relation to finite things on hand, but he interprets their finitude from the fact that they are possible objects for a finite knowing, one that must first let them be given. The world is then "that idea in which the absolute totality of the objects accessible in finite knowledge is represented a priori", the "sum of all appearances" or of "all objects of possible experience" (GA 9, 150–51). As an idea it surpasses appearances, "yet in such a way that, as *their* totality, it is precisely related back to them" (GA 9, 152). Heidegger calls this a "more original ontological interpretation" of the concept of world (GA 9, 151–52), and the lecture course, in the translator's English, gives the section the title "World as the idea of the totality of appearances: Correlate of finite human knowledge" and says of this idea that it has "a Janus face": it is the totality of beings themselves, "and nevertheless belonging to the nature of the finite subject" (GA 27, § 34 e).

Idea, correlate, totality of what can be experienced, belonging to the subject: these are the words of Husserl's § 28 in the *Meditations*, whose title names the world as "correlate-idea of a complete evidence of experience" (Hua I, 96). The lectures from which the *Meditations* grew were given in February 1929, in the same winter as Heidegger's course, and the correlation is already asserted in 1913; I infer nothing from the dates except that neither text could have been written against the other. If Heidegger had wished to place Husserl's world in his history, this is where the texts would have allowed him to place it, and not under the "natural concept" which the treatise rejects. On such a placing Husserl's world would be the Kantian world purified of the thing in itself: the totality of possible objects of experience, thought as an idea and referred to the knowing that it completes. The placing would be respectful, and it would still make of the world something that belongs to knowledge.

Is the placing correct? For one strand of Husserl's concept it is. The world as a whole is for him an idea, and he says so. But the strand that parts 1 and 2 followed is another. An idea lies ahead of experience as what its completion would yield. The general thesis, the world-belief and the ground of validity lie behind every experience as what it already stands on, and Husserl says of this ground that it precedes knowing no less than valuing and acting. Kant's idea of the world, on Heidegger's account of it, regulates a knowledge that advances. Husserl's world-certainty is not advanced towards. It is there before the first step. There are thus, on my reading, two determinations of the world in Husserl, the world as that towards which experience is under way and the world as that from which it sets out, and the Kantian placing reaches only the first. That the two are not brought together in the passages read here is a gap I can only record.

The reading, corrected in this way, does not disappear. It changes its place. The dispute between the two authors does not concern whether the world is a totality of natural things or a context of use; Husserl does not hold the first, and Heidegger, by 1929, calls the second a hopeless starting point (GA 9, 155 n.). Nor does it concern whether the things of the world are first present or first ready to hand, a question about what is in the world on which Husserl hesitates and Heidegger is clear. It concerns the bond itself. Heidegger's reading was right to sense that for Husserl the world is, in the last analysis, something taken to be, and wrong to identify what is so taken with nature. If this is correct, the comparison must be made between a world that holds and a world for the sake of which, and no longer between a world of things and a world of tools.

## 5. Validity and possibility

We have not yet answered the question under which the two authors were to be compared: if the world is subjective, is it so as what a subject takes to be, or as what a subject is? Part 4 has cleared away the form in which the comparison is usually made. This part makes it in the form that remains. It proceeds in four steps: the agreements, the differences, the root of the differences, and the objections to which the result is exposed. The method is comparative throughout; where I restate one author in the words of the other, the restatement is mine.

### 5.1 Four agreements

The first agreement is that the world is not the sum of what is in it. For Husserl the world is "always there" when anything in it is struck out, and only its form is prefigured (Hua III/1, 57, 61). For Heidegger "the world is not the sum of what is on hand" (GA 24, 237). Each of them distinguishes from the world a loose sense of the word in which it means a region of objects, and each illustrates the loose sense by mathematics (Hua III/1, 59; SZ 64–65).

The second agreement is that the world is not a being. Heidegger says so in 1928: the world is "no being", "nothing that is" (GA 26, 252). Husserl says so in the *Crisis*: the world does not exist "as a being, as an object exists" (Hua VI, 146). The dates should be stated, since the two sentences are easily set side by side as if they were contemporaries. Heidegger's is the earlier by about eight years, and three years before it he had himself still spoken of "this being 'world'" (GA 20, 230). Husserl's sentence of 1936 is prepared, as part 1 showed, by what he says of the form and the horizon in 1913, but in 1913 he does not say that the world is no being; he says that the world of things, beyond its being for consciousness, "is a nothing" (Hua III/1, 106), which is a different statement. I draw no conclusion about who learned from whom. The texts read here give no evidence on it, and a resemblance is not an influence.

The third agreement is the one from which the title is taken. Both authors hold that the world is subjective, and both add at once that the subject in question is not the one the word suggests. Husserl calls the world a validity that has sprung up in subjectivity (Hua VI, 99) and rejects in the same book in which he first says such things the charge of a "Berkeleyan idealism": nothing is taken from the being of the world, only its absolutising is removed (Hua III/1, 120–21). Heidegger writes that world is "subjective" and "more objective than any possible object" (SZ 366), and that being-in-the-world "transforms the concept of subjectivity and of the subjective from the ground up" (GA 26, 252). The letter of 1927 states the common ground from which both transformations start: what is in the world cannot be clarified by going back to something else that is in the world (Hua IX, 601).

The fourth agreement follows from the third. Neither author allows the question how a subject gets out of itself to a world. Husserl's version of the refusal is that an absolute reality is worth as much as a round square (Hua III/1, 120). Heidegger's is that the problem of transcendence cannot be reduced to the question "how does a subject come out to an object", in which the totality of objects is identified with the idea of the world (SZ 366). In 1925 Heidegger even gives Husserl's principle its due in his own words: what the principle of immanence sees is that "world is encountered" (GA 20, 298).

These agreements are considerable, and they are sufficient to show that the two authors are speaking of the same thing. A comparison that began from the totality of things on one side and the workshop on the other would never have found them.

### 5.2 Four differences

The first difference is in the name of the bond. Husserl's words for what there is between the subject and its world are thesis, positing, belief, certainty and validity. Heidegger's are familiarity, understanding, projection and binding. The two series do not overlap. No text of Husserl read here calls the world something projected, and no text of Heidegger read here calls it something believed. I state this as a finding about the passages listed in the register and not about the two bodies of work.

The second difference concerns number. For Husserl the world is one. It is "the one and the same world" through all changes of apprehension (Hua IX, 57); possible worlds are variations in thought of which the actual world is a special case, and a second actual world outside this one is a material absurdity (Hua III/1, 101–03); in the *Crisis* the world exists "in a uniqueness for which the plural is senseless" (Hua VI, 146). Heidegger, by contrast, reserves the word for the sense in which a factical Dasein lives "in" a world, which may be the public we-world or one's own nearest surroundings (SZ 65), and he speaks of Dasein giving itself to understand from "its" world (GA 9, 157). He sees the difficulty and puts it as a question: does every Dasein then first of all have its world, and how is a common world still possible? (SZ 64). In the passages I have read, the question is not answered in so many words. The course of 1928 says that in the for-the-sake-of Dasein binds itself to a potentiality of being that is at once being with others (GA 26, 247), which places the others inside each world, but that is not yet the statement that there is one world. Husserl's world is one because what holds, holds as the same for everyone who can experience. Heidegger's world is in each case that of a Dasein, and its unity with the worlds of others is a problem he names.

The third difference concerns the relation of the world to what is actual. For Husserl the world as a whole is "an infinite idea", the correlate of a complete synthesis of harmonious experiences (Hua I, 97). Every actual experience falls short of it, and falls short in the direction of more confirmation: the horizon is one of "unfulfilled anticipations that need fulfilment". For Heidegger the world "surpasses every actual being", and whatever is actual shows itself as a restriction, "one possible realisation of the possible" (GA 26, 248). In both cases the world exceeds what is given. But Husserl's world exceeds the given as that which the given, if experience continues, will bear out; Heidegger's exceeds it as that from which the given is understood and against which it always remains less. The notes that prepared this study observe the same reversal in the two authors' accounts of surplus, in perception and in Dasein. I extend the observation to the world as a whole, and the extension is mine.

The fourth difference is the one in which the others are gathered, and it concerns the nothing. Both authors use the word of the world, and they mean by it two things. For Husserl the world would be "a pure nothing" if the belief in it were not borne out; this is the transcendental illusion, a possibility that nothing speaks for and that cannot be excluded (Hua VIII, 55). Nothing is here what the world turns out to be when its validity fails. For Heidegger the world "is" nothing in the sense that it is no being, and this nothing is no failure; it is what the world positively is, a nothing that temporalises itself and from which beings are first met (GA 26, 272). The two thought experiments in which the difference appears are closer than their vocabularies suggest, and they should be set beside each other. In § 49 of *Ideas I* the ordered connection of experience dissolves; things can no longer be held as things; "there is no longer a world", and what remains is consciousness, modified but untouched in its existence. In § 40 of *Being and Time* the whole of involvements collapses into itself; nothing within the world matters any longer; and what remains is "the world as such", which now obtrudes itself alone. In each case an ordered whole of things gives way. In the one case the world goes with it and a subject is left. In the other the things sink and the world is left, and with it the being whose world it is.

This comparison needs one caution. The two collapses are not the same event. Husserl imagines the failure of concordance in perception, so that nothing identical can be perceived any longer. Heidegger describes a state in which everything is still there and is of no consequence. Anxiety is therefore not Heidegger's version of the annihilation of the world, and I do not present it as such. My point is only that the two descriptions answer one question, what there is of the world when the things in it no longer support it, and that they answer it in opposite ways.

### 5.3 The root

Why does the world go with the things in the one case and stay in the other? I believe that the texts allow an answer, and that it lies in the first difference. The step that follows is not found as such in either author.

A world that is a validity is a world in the mode of being-certain. Husserl is explicit that this certainty is no single judgement, that it precedes thinking, valuing and acting, and that it bears on the whole before the parts. But a certainty, however deep it lies, is the certainty that something is, and its content is that there is one identical world which further experience will confirm. It has, as Husserl says of the certainty of things, its possible "modalisations" (Hua VI, 146): it can pass into doubt, into illusion, into cancellation. The possibility that the world is not is thus built into the concept. It is the limiting modalisation of a certainty that Husserl, in his last texts, comes close to declaring unshakeable in fact. And because the content of the certainty is a concordance among things experienced, the world cannot outlast the dissolution of that concordance. What holds, holds of something.

A world that is a for-the-sake-of is a world in the mode of being-possible. What Dasein has in having a world is, on Heidegger's last account, the whole of what it can be, held out by a freedom that binds itself to it. A possibility of this kind is neither confirmed nor refuted by what is actual. It can only be taken up, or missed, or despaired of, and Heidegger says of despair that the one who despairs "sees the impossibility of the possible" and so bears witness to the possible once more (GA 26, 249). This is why the insignificance of everything within the world leaves the world standing. The table may cease to matter; that for the sake of which there are tables does not thereby cease, since it never depended on the table.

The difference between the two concepts of world is therefore a difference between two modalities of the bond, and each modality has its own way of failing. A world that is believed can prove illusory. A world that is projected can become insignificant or impossible to take up. Husserl's subject, at the limit, survives its world, because what is certain and the one who is certain can be told apart. Heidegger's Dasein cannot survive its world, because what it projects is itself.

Each position can be stated in terms the other could accept, and the statement shows how much each has of what the other makes fundamental. In Husserl's terms, Heidegger's for-the-sake-of is the horizon of ends. Husserl knows this horizon and says of the pregiven world that it "includes all our goals, all our ends, fleeting or lasting" (Hua VI, 147). But for him the ends lie in a world that is pregiven, and its pregivenness is a matter of certainty: one can pursue an end only on a ground that holds. In Heidegger's terms, Husserl's world-certainty is the familiarity with the world in which Dasein always already moves. Heidegger knows this familiarity and says that it "co-constitutes Dasein's understanding of being" (SZ 86). But for him it is a mode of understanding, and holding something to be on hand is a late and narrowed form of it. Each author thus has a place for what the other puts first, and assigns it the second rank. To this extent the result agrees with what the notes say of the two authors' concepts of transcendence, that the phenomena are largely shared and the order of founding is reversed. What the comparison of the two collapses adds is that the reversal is no mere matter of arrangement. It decides whether a subject without a world can be thought.

### 5.4 Objections

Three objections must be faced, and I take them in order of increasing weight.

(1) *Is this not the old contrast of theory and practice under new names?* Belief belongs to knowing and purpose to acting, and so, it may be said, the validity and the for-the-sake-of merely repeat the opposition that part 4 claimed to leave behind. It is true that Husserl's model of the bond is taken from the judgement, as I granted there. However, neither author accepts the division for his own concept. Husserl's world-certainty is the ground of validity for "every valuing and practical activity" as much as for every positing of being (Hua VI, 400). Heidegger says in 1928 that transcendence can be assigned neither to intuition in the theoretical sense nor to practical comportment, "whether in the sense of the use of the craftsman or any other", and that the task is to go back behind these divisions "into the common root"; freedom must not be taken in the narrow sense in which it belongs to practice as opposed to theory (GA 26, 236). The contrast I have drawn is therefore one between two candidates for that root. A certainty that precedes thinking and acting, and a freedom that precedes them, are not theory and practice. They are two views of what theory and practice both presuppose.

(2) *Does not Heidegger's world also hold?* The projection of world, Heidegger says, is not at Dasein's discretion in every respect. That beings are discovered along with one's existence "is not left to the choice of Dasein"; only what and how far it discovers is a matter of its freedom, "though always within the limits of its thrownness" (SZ 366). The treatise of 1929 says that it does not lie in the power of freedom that transcendence happens at all, and that "all projection of world is therefore *thrown*" (GA 9, 174–75). And the world of 1928 is called a "binding" and a "free counter-hold" (GA 26, 247–48). Is a world that binds, and that one has not chosen to have, not a world that holds? In a certain way one must answer yes, and the answer brings Heidegger nearer to the Husserl of the *Crisis*, for whom the world is a ground one has not laid. However, what binds in Heidegger's world is a possibility of one's own being, and what one is thrown into is the having to be it. The bindingness of an end is not the validity of a fact. Husserl's ground holds in that it is; Heidegger's counter-hold binds in that it is to be. The two authors meet in the thought that the world is not at the subject's disposal, and they still differ in what it is that is withheld from disposal.

(3) *Does not the late Husserl give up the very feature on which the difference turns?* This is the hardest case for the thesis, and it lies in the texts quoted at the end of part 2. If my ego can a priori be only a world-experiencing ego (Hua I, 166), and if the certainty of the world is apodictic in its way and cannot be destroyed at will (Hua VI, 408), then the subject of the last texts no more survives its world than Dasein does, and the contrast of the two collapses describes only the Husserl of 1913. I grant the first half of this. The separability of consciousness from the world, which the first book of the *Ideas* asserts, is not asserted in the passages of 1931 and 1936 that I have read, and one of them comes close to denying it. On the question whether a subject without a world can be thought, Husserl's texts give two answers, an early one and a late one that is tentative, and I have not found the place where he chooses.

The second half does not follow. Even where the bond is called apodictic, it remains a certainty: the appendix says "apodictic as world-certainty". What has changed is the modality of the belief, from presumptive to something like necessary. Its kind has not changed. A subject that must believe in a world and a Dasein that must project one are both inseparable from their world, and they are inseparable from it in different ways. The late Husserl therefore narrows the difference at one point, that of separability, and leaves it where it was at the other, that of what the bond is. There is something here that the thesis cannot explain. If the certainty of the world cannot be destroyed, what is the suspension of it with which the *Crisis*, like the *Ideas*, begins? Husserl distinguishes putting out of action from doubting and from destroying, and the distinction is as old as § 31. But a validity that can be put out of action by a free decision, and that is nonetheless the ground on which every decision stands, is the same strange thing that part 1 found in the thesis that is not an act. The texts read here give it new names. They do not make it less strange.

### 5.5 What the question has received

Is the world something subjective? Both authors answer yes, and the study has tried to show that the yes means two things. For Husserl the world is subjective as a validity is: it is what holds for a subject, as the one identical ground of everything that can hold or fail to hold, and it is the subject's own in that the holding springs from it. For Heidegger the world is subjective as a possibility is: it is that from which a being understands what it can be, and it is this being's own in that the being exists as it. The first answer keeps a distance between the subject and its world, however small, since one who is certain can be distinguished from what he is certain of. The second removes it. The alternative of the opening, between what a subject takes to be and what a subject is, has thus been decided once on each side, and the usual contrast of a theoretical and a practical world has proved to be an inexact report of this decision.

The table with its books may serve a last time. For Husserl it stands there, and its standing there is one case of a certainty that reaches past it into the room, the garden and the mist beyond; were that certainty to give way altogether, there would be no table and no world, and someone for whom there was none. For Heidegger it stands there for writing, and what it is for leads back to someone who can write and who is, in being able to, already beyond every table; were the table to lose all its point, the world would be the only thing left. Neither description refutes the other, and I have not asked which is true.

The limits of the result are these. It rests on five years of Heidegger, and the texts before 1925 and after 1929, which I have not read for this study, may show a concept that the opposition of validity and possibility does not fit. It rests, for Husserl, on published works, lectures and two appendices, and not on the research manuscripts in which the home-world and the plurality of surrounding worlds are treated; what I have said of the one world may need to be qualified there. I have not been able to determine the layer of the second book of the *Ideas* to which its statements about the surrounding world belong, so that their place in the sequence of part 2 is uncertain. And the question how Heidegger's world, which is in each case a Dasein's, can be common has been stated and left as he leaves it in the passages read.

It remains to give each author what the other's criticism does not take from him. Heidegger was right that the first book of the *Ideas* describes a subject for the world without asking what manner of being it has, and the *Crisis* concedes the point when it asks how a component of the world can constitute the world. But it was Husserl who first wrote, in 1913, that the world is there in its form before any of its contents, that it survives the striking out of whatever is in it, and that to make it absolute is absurd; and it was Husserl who, in 1936, found for the difference between the world and the things in it the wording that Heidegger's own texts of 1928 approach from the other side: every plural, and every singular, presupposes the world-horizon. The two concepts are nearest where each says of the world that it is nothing among the things that are. They part where one adds that it holds, and the other that it is for the sake of someone.

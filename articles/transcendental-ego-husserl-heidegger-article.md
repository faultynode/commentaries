# An I Only by Equivocation

## The Transcendental Ego in Husserl and What Becomes of It in Heidegger

It is generally accepted that Heidegger replaces Husserl's transcendental ego with Dasein. On this view the ego is a subject without a world, reached by reflection and purified of everything human, and the analytic of Dasein begins by setting it aside in favour of a being whose basic constitution is being-in-the-world. The view has texts on its side. *Being and Time* says that the ideas of a "pure I" and of a "consciousness in general" pass over the facticity of Dasein (SZ 229), and that a bare subject without a world never "is" and is never given (SZ 116). In 1973 Heidegger still says that the *ego cogito* is an enclosed space without windows. Yet the view leaves three facts unexplained. In October 1927 Heidegger writes to Husserl that the two of them agree about transcendental constitution, and he asks only in what sense the "absolute ego" is the same as the factical I and in what sense it is not. In 1928 and 1929 he calls transcendence "the basic structure of subjectivity" and speaks, in his own name, of the "egoity" of Dasein and of its "metaphysical isolation". Husserl, for his part, writes in 1917 or 1918 that the I for which time is constituted "should properly not be called the I", and in 1936 that the ego of the *epoché* is called "I" only by equivocation.[^1]

In what follows I wish to show that the transcendental ego holds two things together in Husserl, and that Heidegger separates them. I shall call them the office and the form of the ego; the two words are mine and are found in neither author. The office is to be that for which there is a world and a time, and which for that reason cannot be counted among the things that are in the world and in time. The form is that of an identical pole of acts, which reflection finds and names "I". I wish to show that Heidegger keeps the office and assigns it to the transcendence of Dasein, and that he refuses the form its rank as something given first, deriving it from the structure of care. I also wish to show that Husserl's own texts loosen the tie between office and form from the other side, and that they do so before Heidegger's objection is made.

The comparison has a measure because both authors ask one question. Heidegger asks it in the second enclosure to his letter of 22 October 1927: "What is the mode of being of this absolute ego: in what sense is it the same as the ever factical I; in what sense *not* the same?" (Hua IX, 602). Husserl asks it in § 54 of the *Crisis*, where the paradox that human beings are subjects for the world and objects in it leads him to the question who "we" are, and to the answer that the ego of the *epoché* bears its name equivocally (Hua VI, 186–88). The shared question can be put as an alternative. Is the sameness of the I that of something which stands under the change of its acts, is found there by reflection, and is afterwards apprehended as a human being? Or is it a sameness that the factical human being has to achieve in existing, so that the I which reflection finds is a late and unreliable report of it?

The article rests on a closed body of texts, read in the files of one working archive. On Husserl's side these are § 8 of the Fifth Logical Investigation in its two editions (1901, 1913), §§ 57 and 80 of *Ideas I* (1913), §§ 22–29 of *Ideas II*, text no. 14 of the Bernau manuscripts on time (1917/18), §§ 11, 15 and 31–33 of the *Cartesian Meditations* (1931), and § 54 of the *Crisis* (1936). On Heidegger's side they are § 13 of the lecture course of the summer of 1925, the enclosures to the letter of 1927, §§ 10, 25, 44 and 64 of *Being and Time* (1927), § 15 of the course of the summer of 1927, §§ 10 and 11 of the course of the summer of 1928, the treatise *On the Essence of Ground* (1929), § 34 of *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* (1929), and the protocol of the Zähringen seminar (1973). I leave aside the constitution of other egos in the Fifth Meditation and in the three volumes on intersubjectivity, and I touch the question of who performs the reduction only where the ego's name depends on it. I also leave aside Heidegger's account of the subject in the history of being after 1930, except for the one late verdict on Husserl. This restriction needs a word of explanation. The archive holds no secondary literature that I was free to use, and it does not hold Husserl's late manuscripts on the living present; the received view is therefore stated above without names, and where the archive is silent I claim no absence.

## 1. The pole denied, found and emptied: Husserl, 1901–1913

Husserl first meets the pure ego as another author's doctrine, and he rejects it. § 8 of the Fifth Investigation reports Natorp's view that all content of consciousness is related to an I as its "unitary point of relation", and that this I cannot itself become content, since to be an I is to be that for which something is an object. Husserl calls these statements impressive and then declines them: "I must frankly confess, however, that I am quite unable to find this primitive I as the necessary centre of relation" (Hua XIX/1, 374). What he can perceive, he says, is the empirical I and its empirical relation to its own experiences. Reduced to what is phenomenologically given, this I yields a complex of experiences, and the complex stands to the whole mental I as the perceived side of a thing stands to the thing. In 1901, then, the sameness of the I is the unity of a connection of experiences, and nothing stands behind it.

The second edition keeps the paragraph and contradicts it in a note: "Since then I have learned to find it, or rather learned not to let myself be led astray, in the pure grasping of the given, by worries about the degenerations of the metaphysics of the I" (Hua XIX/1, 374 n.). The note tells us two things. It tells us that the pure ego is, for Husserl, a matter of finding, that is, of intuition and not of inference from the conditions of experience. And it names the fear that had stood in the way. The fear did not concern the datum; it concerned what a philosophy might make of it. Both points stay with the concept, and the second explains the caution of everything that follows.

*Ideas I* introduces the ego as a difficulty "at a limit". The human being as a natural being and as a person has fallen under the reduction; what of the pure I? Husserl answers by describing what reflection finds. After the reduction we shall nowhere come upon the pure I "as one experience among other experiences", nor as a proper piece of an experience, arising and vanishing with it. Its "ray of regard" changes with every *cogito*. "The I, however, is something identical" (§ 57; Hua III/1, 123). Since it remains absolutely identical through every actual and possible change of experiences, it can in no sense count as a real part or moment of them, and Husserl appeals to Kant: "The 'I think' must be able to accompany all my representations". He then gives the pure I the name that fixes its place. If it remains as residuum of the exclusion of the world, there presents itself with it "a transcendence of a peculiar kind, one that is not constituted, a *transcendence in immanence*" (§ 57; Hua III/1, 124). The section ends on a restriction. The pure I is to count as a phenomenological datum only as far as its immediate, evident givenness reaches, and all doctrines about it that go further are to fall under the exclusion.

What this datum contains is stated in § 80, and the statement is negative. Every act is characterized as an act of the I; it "goes forth from the I", and no exclusion can cancel the form of the *cogito* and strike out the pure subject of the act. Yet the experiencing I is nothing that could be taken for itself and made into an object of investigation in its own right. "Apart from its 'modes of relation' or 'modes of comportment', it is completely empty of essential components, it has no explicable content at all, it is in and for itself indescribable: pure I and nothing more" (§ 80; Hua III/1, 179). The I that Husserl has learned to find is thus described only by what it does and by what it is not. It is not an experience, not a part of one, not constituted, and not describable.

The second book of the *Ideas*, which Husserl did not publish, gives this I the name it keeps: the pure I is the "ego-pole" (*Ichpol*). Every *cogito* requires a *cogitatum*, and so we find in every act a remarkable polarity, "on the one side the ego-pole, on the other the object as counter-pole" (§ 25; Hua IV, 105). Three determinations follow, and each is won by contrast with the human person. The first concerns time. Every *cogito* arises and passes away in the flow of experiences, but the pure subject "does not arise and does not pass away", although it steps forward and steps back, begins to function and ceases (§ 23; Hua IV, 103–04). The second concerns change. The pure I changes in its activities, but these changes do not change it: "in itself it is unchangeable", and it has no original or acquired dispositions of character, no abilities (§ 24; Hua IV, 104–05). The third concerns givenness. To know what I am as a human personality I must enter into the infinity of experience, and it remains possible in principle that I am not this personality at all. To know that the pure I is and what it is, a single simple *cogito* suffices. "As pure I it harbours no hidden inner riches; it is absolutely simple and lies absolutely open to view" (§ 24; Hua IV, 105).

The relation of this pole to the human being is settled in the same sections, and it is a relation of constituting to constituted. The whole world is the surrounding world of the pure I, and this includes the human being whom I also call "I". "I as the human being am a component of the real surrounding world of the pure I", which, as the centre of all intentionality, also carries out the intentionality in which I, the human being and the personality, am constituted (§ 27; Hua IV, 109–10). By 1913, then, the question of sameness has received a first answer. The I for which there is a world is identical, unchangeable, unborn and wholly evident, and the factical I is one of its objects. The answer has a price, which Husserl does not hide. An I with no properties and no history has nothing by which it could be this I and no other, except the stream to which it belongs. How such a pole can have convictions, a past and a character is the question that the following texts take up.

## 2. The pole filled and the name withdrawn: Husserl, 1913–1936

Husserl's later texts answer this question in two directions at once, and the two answers do not converge. The first makes the pole richer. Already in *Ideas II* he sees "a law of essence of the pure I": every new position-taking founds an abiding "opinion", so that from now on, as often as I grasp myself as the same who I was, I also hold to my themes (§ 29; Hua IV, 111–12). The *Cartesian Meditations* make this a doctrine. § 31 notes "a great gap" in the exposition so far, which had looked only at the streaming *cogito*: the ego grasps itself also as the I who lives through this and that *cogito* "as the same", and this is a second polarization beside that of objects. § 32 then corrects the description of 1913 in so many words. This centering I "is not an empty pole of identity (any more than any object is such)". By a law of transcendental genesis it gains, with every act that radiates from it, "a new abiding property" (Hua I, 100).

Husserl's example is a decision, and I shall carry it through the rest of the article because Heidegger's account of the self turns on the same phenomenon. "I resolve: the act-experience flows away, but the resolve persists." Whether I sink into dull sleep or live through other acts, it remains in force; "correlatively: I am henceforth the one who is thus resolved, and for as long as I do not give up the resolve". If the resolve aims at a deed, it is not cancelled by being fulfilled: "I continue to stand by my deed" (§ 32; Hua I, 101). Two things are joined in this description. The resolve outlasts the act, and so there is an abiding of the I that is no filling of immanent time with experiences. And the abiding belongs to someone who was there before it: the I is "identical substrate of abiding I-properties", and by constituting itself as such a substrate it constitutes itself further as a "standing and abiding personal I" (*stehendes und bleibendes personales Ich*). The pole is thus presupposed by its habitualities (*Habitualitäten*). They determine it; they do not make it one.

§ 33 adds a third concept. From the I as identical pole and as substrate of habitualities Husserl distinguishes "the ego taken in full concreteness", which he calls with Leibniz's word the monad. It includes that without which the I cannot be concrete, namely the streaming manifold of its intentional life and the objects meant in it, so that the problem of the self-constitution of this monadic ego "must include all constitutive problems whatsoever" (Hua I, 102–03). The richer answer therefore ends by giving the ego everything. I, taken in full concreteness, comprise my surrounding world as it is for me.

The second answer runs the other way, and it is the earlier of the two in date. In 1917 or 1918 Husserl asks at Bernau what the stream of experiences does not contain. What we have in the stream is something that is, something temporal; but not everything subjective is temporal. What we above all do not have in the stream is "the I itself, the identical centre, the pole". The I as pole for all experiences is the pole for all time-series, and as such it is necessarily "supra"-temporal: it is "the I for which time is constituted", and which is not itself temporal. "In this sense it is therefore not 'a being', but the counterpart to all that is, not an object (*Gegenstand*) but the primal stand (*Urstand*) for all objectivity" (Hua XXXIII, 277). Husserl draws the consequence for the name at once. The I "should properly not be called the I, and should not be called at all, since it has then already become objective. It is the nameless beyond everything graspable", not standing, not hovering, not being, but "functioning" (Hua XXXIII, 277–78). He then puts the difficulty as a question that the text does not dissolve: how can that become objective which is no object, and how can that be grasped which is non-temporal and can yet be found in grasping only as something temporal?

The *Crisis* returns to this point by another road. § 54 asks who "we" are, as the subjects that accomplish the constitution of the world, and denies that we can be human beings, since human beings are themselves phenomena and poles of transcendental questioning. Husserl is not satisfied with the result and names his own mistake: the "self-forgetfulness of ourselves, the ones philosophizing". It is I who perform the *epoché*, and in my *epoché* all other human beings are included in the world-phenomenon. The consequence concerns the pronoun. "I am not *an* I, who still has his thou and his we and his total community of co-subjects in natural validity. The whole of humankind and the whole distinction and ordering of the personal pronouns has become a phenomenon in my *epoché*, together with the priority of the I-human-being among other human beings" (Hua VI, 188). And then: "The I that I attain in the *epoché* … is actually called 'I' only by equivocation, although it is an essential equivocation, since, when I name it in reflection, I can say nothing other than: it is I, I who practise the *epoché*" (Hua VI, 188). Husserl calls this I the "primal I" (*Ur-Ich*), which can never lose "its uniqueness and personal indeclinability" (Hua VI, 188).

One receives the impression, then, that Husserl gives two answers to the question what the transcendental ego is. On the one hand it is ever more determinate: a pole with convictions and resolves, a person in the widest sense, a monad that comprises its world. On the other hand it is ever less so: non-temporal, not a being, nameless, an I only by equivocation. The *Crisis* does not choose. In the very sentence that withdraws the name, Husserl describes the primal I as "the ego-pole of the transcendental life" and as the one who, "taken in full concreteness, comprises all that" (Hua VI, 188). Nor does the text say how the two descriptions belong together; it says how the primal I is related to others and to the human being. It "makes itself declinable" for itself by a constitutive accomplishment of its own, and each human being "bears a transcendental I in himself", not as a real part or a layer of his soul, but in so far as he is "the self-objectivation (*Selbstobjektivation*) of the transcendental I in question" (Hua VI, 188–90).

In the terms proposed above, the two answers concern different things. The richer answer elaborates the form: it tells us how an identical pole comes to have a history. The poorer answer concerns the office: it tells us that what functions as the ground of all objectivity and all time cannot be described in the terms that hold for what is grounded, and that "I" is one of these terms. This sorting is mine; Husserl does not make it, and his word "essential" forbids making it too quickly. The equivocation is essential because the one who holds the office has no other access to it than reflection, and reflection finds an I. The texts of 1917 and 1936 thus say two things together: that the functioning is wrongly named when it is named "I", and that it cannot be named otherwise. What they do not ask is what kind of being belongs to something of which one must say that it is "not a being". Heidegger's letter of 1927 asks exactly this.

## 3. The I as formal indication: Heidegger, 1925–1927

Heidegger's first extended report of Husserl's ego is brief and exact. In the lecture course of the summer of 1925 he examines the "personalistic attitude" of the second book of the *Ideas*, which he says was carried out in one draft with the first, and he grants that it must be taken positively. He then states what it leaves as it was. Access to the person remains the immanent reflection on experiences. It is said expressly that the connection of experiences is always that of a determinate I-subject, "but the mode of being of the acts remains undetermined. Acts are performed; the I is the pole of the acts, the subject that maintains itself" (GA 20, 172). Heidegger adds a concession that the received view tends to forget. This, he says, is "not the last step" that Husserl takes in clarifying the unity of the stream of experiences, and he defers the last step to the analysis of time. The reproach of 1925 is therefore limited. It does not say that the pole is a fiction. It says that Husserl describes what the I does and leaves undetermined what it is to be an I that does it.

The letter of 22 October 1927 states the same reproach to Husserl himself, and it begins from an agreement. "There is agreement that the being in the sense of what you call 'world' cannot be clarified in its transcendental constitution by a return to a being of the same mode of being." Heidegger thus accepts the office: that for which there is a world cannot be one more thing in the world. He continues: "But this does not mean that what makes up the place of the transcendental is not a being at all; rather, precisely the *problem* arises: what is the mode of being of the being in which 'world' is constituted?" (Hua IX, 601). His own answer follows in one sentence: transcendental constitution is "a central possibility of the existence of the factical self". The human being is never a "worldly real fact", because the human being is never merely present-at-hand (*vorhanden*) but exists. The enclosure ends with a formula that reads like a reply to the Bernau text, which the letter does not mention and which I do not claim Heidegger knew: "That which constitutes is not nothing, hence it is something and in being, although not in the sense of the positive" (Hua IX, 602).

*Being and Time* carries out the question, and it does so first as a prohibition. § 10 says of Descartes that he investigated the *cogitare* of the *ego* and left the *sum* wholly undiscussed, and it draws a consequence that goes further than a criticism of Descartes. Every idea of a "subject", unless it has been purified by a prior ontological determination, still posits the *subjectum* ontologically, "however vigorously one may defend oneself ontically against the 'soul-substance' or the 'reification of consciousness'" (SZ 46). The sentence does not name Husserl, but the next page does. The interpretations of personality in Husserl and Scheler "agree in the negative": the person is no thing, no substance, no object. "They no longer pose the question of 'being a person' itself" (SZ 47–48). What Heidegger holds against Husserl is thus the list of negations that we met in *Ideas I* and *Ideas II*. He does not dispute any item on the list. He disputes that a list of what the I is not amounts to a determination of its being.

§ 25 turns the prohibition against the form itself. Heidegger first states what the tradition understands by the "who" of Dasein, and the statement fits Husserl's pole word for word: the who is "that which maintains itself as something identical through the change of comportments and experiences and thereby relates itself to this manifold" (SZ 114). He then says what such an identical something implies. One may reject the soul-substance and the thinghood of consciousness; ontologically one still posits something whose being keeps the sense of presence-at-hand, since the indeterminacy of its being always implies this sense. The use of Husserl's vocabulary in a paragraph that does not name him is, I think, not by chance. The paragraph goes on to speak of the "givenness of the I", of "the simple, formal, reflective awareness of the I", of the "I of acts", and of the instruction to disregard, for the sake of this givenness, an existing "world" and the being of other "I's" (SZ 115).

Is this to say that Heidegger denies the evidence on which Husserl's ego rests? In a certain way one must answer no, and Heidegger says so himself. "Perhaps what this kind of giving gives … is in fact evident", and this insight even opens the way to an independent phenomenological problematic, which as "formal phenomenology of consciousness" has its fundamental significance (SZ 115). What he denies is that this evidence discloses Dasein in its everydayness, "if it discloses it at all". His reason is a possibility that Husserl's sections on the pure I exclude in principle: "It could be that the who of everyday Dasein is precisely *not* I myself." Perhaps Dasein says "it is I" loudest when it is "not" this being (SZ 115). The methodical conclusion is that the "I" may be understood only as a non-committal "formal indication" (*formale Anzeige*) of something that may reveal itself, in the phenomenal context of its being, as its "opposite". This "not-I" does not mean a being that lacks I-hood. It means "a determinate mode of being of the 'I' itself, for example, having lost oneself" (SZ 116).

The difference from Husserl can now be stated in his own terms. For Husserl the pure I is the one thing about which deception is impossible: it would be absurd to suppose that I, the pure I, am really not, or am something wholly other than what functions in this *cogito* (Hua IV, 104–05). For Heidegger the I is the thing about which Dasein is deceived first and most. The two claims do not contradict each other as flatly as they seem to, since they do not concern the same matter. Husserl's concerns whether the one who thinks is. Heidegger's concerns who the one who says "I" is, and whether that one is itself. Heidegger concedes the first, and what he draws from the concession is the verdict of § 44: the ideas of a "pure I" and of a "consciousness in general" contain so little of the *a priori* of "actual" subjectivity that they "pass over the ontological characters of the facticity and the constitution of being of Dasein, or do not see them at all" (SZ 229). The verdict is negative. It tells us what the pole cannot be; it does not yet say what the sameness of the self consists in if it is not the sameness of a pole.

## 4. Self-constancy and egoity: Heidegger, 1927–1929

§ 64 of *Being and Time* gives the positive account, and it reaches it through Kant. Heidegger begins from saying "I". The content of this expression counts as absolutely simple: "It means in each case only me and nothing more" (SZ 318). Kant, he says, holds fast to this phenomenal content more strictly than his predecessors. He shows that the ontic theses about a soul-substance are without right, and he grasps the I as "I think". He nevertheless takes this I again as subject, and "the ontological concept of the subject characterizes *not* the selfhood of the I *qua* self, but the sameness and constancy of something always already present-at-hand" (SZ 320). Why does Kant fall back? Because the I is not only "I think" but "I think something", and Kant leaves the mode of being of this accompanying undetermined, so that the I is pushed back to an isolated subject. Against this Heidegger sets his own reading of the pronoun: "Saying I means the being that I in each case am as: 'I-am-in-a-world'" (SZ 321).

The step that matters for our question comes next. If the self can be traced back neither to an I-substance nor to a subject, it does not follow that the self is the constantly present ground of care. Selfhood can be read off only from the authentic potentiality-for-being-a-self. Heidegger then names what takes the place of the pole. The constancy of the self, in the double sense of steadiness and steadfastness, is the authentic counter-possibility to the non-self-constancy of irresolute falling. "Self-constancy (*Selbst-ständigkeit*) means existentially nothing other than anticipatory resoluteness" (SZ 322). And the order of grounding is reversed: "Care does not need to be founded in a self; rather, existentiality as constitutive of care gives the ontological constitution of the self-constancy of Dasein, to which, in accordance with the full structural content of care, the factical being-fallen into non-self-constancy belongs" (SZ 323).

We can now return to Husserl's resolve. For Husserl I resolve, the act flows away, the resolve persists, and I am henceforth the one who is thus resolved; the resolve is a property that accrues to an I which was already identical and which would be identical without it. For Heidegger there is nothing under the resolve to which it could accrue. The "standing" of the self is the having-taken-a-stand, and where no stand is taken the self is not thereby absent: it is there in the mode of the they-self, which "says I-I loudest and most often because at bottom it is *not authentically* itself" (SZ 322). The two descriptions share a good deal. In both, the abiding of the self is no continuous filling of time with experiences; in both it is tied to standing by something; and the German of both turns on the same word, Husserl's "standing and abiding" I and Heidegger's *Ständigkeit*. What they do not share is the direction. Husserl's I can become untrue to itself only by becoming another through new motives, and he adds that in truth "I am not untrue to myself, I am continually the same" (Hua IV, 112). Heidegger's self is for the most part untrue to itself, and its sameness is the exception that has to be won.

The lecture course of the summer of 1927 draws the consequence for access. Dasein is there for itself in existing even when the I does not expressly turn back upon itself in what phenomenology calls inner perception. "The self is there for Dasein itself without reflection and without inner perception, *before* all reflection" (GA 24, 226). Reflection in the sense of turning back is only one mode of self-apprehension and not the way of primary self-disclosure; Dasein finds itself first in the things it is concerned with. The form of the ego, as *Ideas II* describes it, was adequately graspable in the reflective turn toward the centre of functioning. Heidegger does not deny the turn. He denies that it comes first, and § 64 has said why: what the turn finds is "the constantly selfsame but indeterminately empty simple" of the self-forgetful "I am concerned with" (SZ 322). Husserl's words of 1913, completely empty and nothing more, are here accepted as a description and explained as a symptom. This reading of the echo is mine; § 64 does not name Husserl.

So much for the form. What of the office? The texts of 1928 and 1929 show that Heidegger does not let it go, and that he is willing to use the old names for it. *On the Essence of Ground* says: if one chooses the title "subject" for the being that we ourselves are, then "transcendence designates the essence of the subject, is the basic structure of subjectivity". The subject never first exists as subject in order then also to transcend; "to be a subject means: to be a being in and as transcendence" (GA 9, 137–38). In the surpassing Dasein first comes toward the being that it is, toward it as "itself": "Transcendence constitutes selfhood" (GA 9, 138). The world, in turn, is that for the sake of which Dasein exists, and "to selfhood belongs world" (GA 9, 157). The world is thus "subjective", that is, belonging to Dasein, and for that very reason does not fall as a being into the inner sphere of a subject. That for which there is a world is still a self. But the self does not precede the world as a pole precedes its acts; it is first given to itself from the world toward which it surpasses.

The same treatise states how this selfhood (*Selbstheit*) is related to the I. The proposition that Dasein exists for the sake of itself contains no egoistic purpose and no solipsistic isolation; it gives the condition under which a human being can behave egoistically or altruistically at all. "Only because Dasein as such is determined by selfhood can an I-self relate itself to a thou-self. Selfhood is the presupposition for the possibility of I-hood, which is only ever disclosed in the thou." Selfhood itself is "neutral with respect to being-I and being-thou" (GA 9, 157–58). The lecture course of 1928 gives this neutrality a name that the received view would not expect. Heidegger calls the neutral I-hood, which also underlies the thou, "egoity" (*Egoität*), and says that he mostly uses for it the expression selfhood, since "self" can be said of I and thou alike (GA 26, 243). The guiding statements of the same course say that neutral Dasein "is never what exists", since Dasein exists only in its factical concretion, and that the approach in neutrality means "a peculiar isolation of the human being", not a factical one, as though the one philosophizing were the centre of the world, but "the *metaphysical isolation* of the human being" (GA 26, 172).

One further text concerns the relation of this self to time, and it uses the same Kantian words as Husserl's § 32. In § 34 of the Kant book Heidegger asks what Kant means when he calls the I of pure apperception "standing and abiding". These are, he says, no ontic statements about the unchangingness of the I. They mean that the I holds before itself in advance something like standing and remaining, and so forms the horizon of sameness within which what is objective can be experienced as the same. This forming is the essence of time. That the I is not "in time" is granted; "but does it follow from this that the I is not temporal, or does it turn out that the I is so 'temporal' that it is time itself?" (GA 3, 192). Hence the formula: time and the "I think" "no longer stand over against each other as incompatible and heterogeneous; they are the same" (GA 3, 191). The statement is made of Kant and not of Husserl. Whether it also answers the question that Husserl had put at Bernau can be decided only by setting the two side by side.

## 5. Office and form

The texts allow us to state four agreements before any difference, and they are closer than the received view allows. First, neither author takes the I for a thing, a substance or a real part of experience. Husserl says so in § 57 of *Ideas I*, and Heidegger grants in § 10 of *Being and Time* that Husserl says so. Secondly, neither is content with an empty identity. Husserl corrects his own "empty" pole in 1931 by the abiding of a resolve, and Heidegger explains the emptiness of the I by the irresoluteness of the one who says it. Thirdly, both deny that what carries the office is in time, and both bind it to the origin of time: for Husserl it is "the I for which time is constituted", for Heidegger the I that is "time itself". Fourthly, both arrive at a neutrality that precedes the difference of I and thou, and both describe it as a solitude that is not a social fact. Husserl writes that the whole ordering of the personal pronouns has become a phenomenon and that the *epoché* creates "a unique philosophical solitude" (Hua VI, 187–88). Heidegger writes that selfhood is neutral with respect to being-I and being-thou and speaks of metaphysical isolation. Each adds that this neutral one does not exist apart from the factical human being: every human being "bears a transcendental I in himself", and neutral Dasein "is never what exists".

Naturally there are also weighty differences, and they can be stated in three points. The first concerns access. Husserl's pure I "lies absolutely open to view" for reflection and harbours nothing hidden. Heidegger's self is there before all reflection and is covered over by the very saying of "I". The second concerns sameness. For Husserl the identity of the pole precedes its habitualities and carries them; for Heidegger constancy is achieved in resoluteness and lost in falling, and no identity lies beneath both. The third concerns the relation to the human being. For Husserl the human being is constituted by the transcendental I as its self-objectivation; the relation is that of an accomplishment to the one who accomplishes it. For Heidegger transcendental constitution is a possibility of the existence of the factical self; the relation is that of a way of being to the one who is in that way.

Where do these differences come from? Here lies, I think, the real reason for all three. Husserl determines the ego from what it does for objects. It is the pole from which acts radiate, the counter-pole to every object-pole, the "primal stand" for all that stands over against it; even its richer determinations, the habitualities, are correlates of what abidingly holds for it. Because it is defined by this function, its own being can be stated only by negation of what it functions for: not constituted, not temporal, not "a being". Heidegger accepts the function and refuses the negation as a last word. "That which constitutes is not nothing." The demand is that the being of the one for whom there is a world be determined positively, and the determination he gives, existence as care, brings the three differences with it. A being whose being is at issue for it can be hidden from itself, can fail to be itself, and can be the factical human being without being a fact in the world.

It may help to restate each position in words the other could have accepted. Heidegger's self-constancy is, in Husserl's terms, a habituality without a substrate: a standing by my resolve that makes me the same, where Husserl has a same I that stands by its resolve. Husserl would object that someone must be there to take the resolve, and Heidegger would agree; he would add that this someone is "there" as care and for the most part not as itself. Husserl's primal I, in Heidegger's terms, is Dasein taken in its metaphysical neutrality: unique, prior to the pronouns, the source of the inner possibility of each factical I, and never existing as such. Heidegger would object that Husserl reaches it by a reflection that brackets its being, and Husserl would not agree; he would answer, as he does in the *Crisis*, that nothing human shows itself there because everything human belongs to what is constituted. These restatements are mine. They show, I hope, that the two authors are closer on the office than on the form, and that each could have recognized the other's description of the office as a description of the same thing.

Is this to say that Dasein is the transcendental ego under another name? In a certain way one must answer yes, since Heidegger himself offers the equation when he calls transcendence the essence of the subject and neutral selfhood "egoity", and since he tells Husserl that the mode of being of human Dasein harbours "the possibility of transcendental constitution". However, this is not to say that only the name has changed. What Heidegger keeps is that for which there is a world. What he does not keep is that this is first of all an I: an identical something, found by reflection, for which the world is a correlate. And the office changes its sense when it changes its bearer. For Husserl it is exercised in acts and their sediments. For Heidegger it is a surpassing that happens "before all comportment" (GA 9, 137) and that first gives Dasein a self to which acts can be ascribed.

A second objection comes from Heidegger's own late verdict. At Zähringen in 1973 he says that *Being and Time* no longer speaks of consciousness, that consciousness is rooted in Dasein and not the other way round, and that Husserl, despite intentionality, "remains trapped in immanence", with the *Cartesian Meditations* as the consequence. One cannot shatter the sphere of consciousness as long as one starts from the *ego cogito*, for it belongs to its basic constitution, as to that of Leibniz's monad, "to have no windows"; it is "an enclosed space" (GA 15, Zähringen seminar, first and second sessions). Heidegger is certainly right about one of Husserl's two answers. The monad of § 33, which comprises its world as what is meant in its intentional life, is described by Husserl himself in terms of inclusion, and the *Crisis* still says that the primal I, taken concretely, "comprises all that". But the verdict does not reach the other answer. An I that is "not a being", that is in no time, that should not be called an I and should not be called at all, is not a space, and nothing is enclosed in it. The Zähringen protocol speaks of the form and of its richest version. It is silent on the texts in which Husserl takes the form away from the office.

What becomes of the transcendental ego in Heidegger is therefore a division. Its office passes to the transcendence of Dasein, and passes with the old titles of subject and egoity still attached for a few years. Its form is taken from the beginning and placed at the end: the I that reflection finds is either the loud "I-I" of the one who is not itself, or the reticent constancy of the one who has taken a stand and does not say "I" at all. Husserl had reached the first half of this result by himself. He saw in 1917 that the name fits the functioning badly, and he said it again in 1936. What he did not do, and what Heidegger asks of him, is to say positively what it is to be the one who functions.

Three limits of this result should be stated. I have not shown that Heidegger's "egoity" is more than the form returning under a neutral name; that mineness and the "for the sake of itself" belong to the essence of Dasein may be thought to keep the pole in everything but the word, and the note in which the course of 1928 comments on the title "I" is broken off in the file I read. I have not decided whether the supra-temporal I of Bernau and the I that is "time itself" are two descriptions of one thing or two things. Heidegger grants in 1925 that Husserl's last step lies in the analysis of time, and the comparison would have to be made there, on a wider basis than one manuscript and one section of the Kant book. And I have set aside the other egos, although Husserl's uniqueness and Heidegger's neutrality are both stated against them. It remains to give Husserl the last word on the name, since on this point Heidegger does not get beyond him. The equivocation, Husserl says, is essential: when I name in reflection the one for whom there is a world, I can say nothing other than "it is I". Heidegger's authentic self keeps silent and does not say "I-I". Yet the analytic that describes it has to say of this being, as the first formal indication of its constitution, that its being is "in each case mine" and that it is the being "which I myself in each case am" (SZ 114).

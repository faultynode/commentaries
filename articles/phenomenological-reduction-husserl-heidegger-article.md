# Who Performs the Reduction?

## Husserl and Heidegger, 1907–1936

It is generally accepted that the phenomenological reduction is the point at which Heidegger parts from Husserl. On this view Husserl's phenomenology stands or falls with a method that suspends belief in the world in order to reach pure consciousness, and Heidegger's analytic of Dasein begins by declining that method, since a being whose basic constitution is being-in-the-world cannot bracket the world. The view has a fact on its side. In the file of *Being and Time* on which this article rests, the word "reduction" (*Reduktion*) does not occur once. Yet the view leaves three other facts unexplained. In the lecture course of the summer of 1927 Heidegger names "phenomenological reduction" as a basic component of his own method. In October of the same year he edits, for Husserl's article in the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, a description of the reduction, and he writes to Husserl that the two of them agree on what it must not do. Husserl, for his part, warns in 1924 against the words "residuum" and "exclusion", and in 1936 he names "the great disadvantage" of the way to the reduction that he had taken in *Ideas I*, in terms that come close to what Heidegger had objected in 1925.[^1]

In what follows I wish to show that the two authors are divided neither by the question whether phenomenology requires a reduction nor, in the first place, by the question where the reduction leads, but by the question who performs it. For Husserl the one who reduces is an I that the reduction itself first brings to light, and of which the human being is the worldly appearance. For Heidegger the one who reduces is the factical human being, and the reduction is one of the possibilities of its existence. I also wish to show that each author's texts contain what the other demands. Husserl describes the reduction as a change of the whole form of life and, late, as an "existential" transformation. Heidegger keeps the leading back of the gaze from beings to that which lets them be understood, and he admits that this leading back is negative and needs a guide.

The comparison has a measure because both authors ask one question, and each asks it of the other's theme. Husserl asks it in the *Crisis*: "Who are we, as the subjects accomplishing the sense and validity of universal constitution? … Can 'we' mean 'we human beings', human beings in the natural-objective sense, that is, realities of the world?" *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 186). Heidegger asks it in the letter of 22 October 1927: "what is the mode of being of the being in which 'world' is constituted?" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 601). The shared question can therefore be put as an alternative. Is the one who performs the reduction the human being, exercising a possibility that belongs to its way of being? Or is it an I that is not human, and that the human being who began the meditation turns out to have been all along?

The article rests on a closed body of texts, read in the files of one working archive. On Husserl's side these are *The Idea of Phenomenology* (1907), the opening chapters of the "Fundamental Phenomenological Consideration" in *Ideas I* (1913), lectures 40 to 42 of *First Philosophy II* (1923/24) with one supplement of 1924, the second draft of the *Britannica* article (1927), §§ 11 and 15 of the *Cartesian Meditations* (1931), the lecture "Phenomenology and Anthropology" (1931), and §§ 35, 39, 43, 53 and 54 of the *Crisis* (1936). On Heidegger's side they are the lecture courses of 1923/24, 1925, 1927 and 1928, *Being and Time* (1927), his contributions and marginal notes to the *Britannica* draft, and the letter that accompanied them. I leave aside the eidetic reduction except where Heidegger makes it his target, the reduction to the sphere of ownness in the Fifth Meditation, and the further reductions of *Ideas I* §§ 56–62. This restriction needs a word of explanation. The archive holds neither Heidegger's lecture "What Is Metaphysics?" nor his courses before 1923 and after 1928, and it holds no secondary literature that I was free to use. The received view is therefore stated above without names, and where the archive is silent I claim no absence.

## 1. The exclusion of the transcendent and what remains: Husserl, 1907–1913

Husserl first studies the method under an epistemological name. In the lectures of 1907 he announces "the epistemological reduction, whose methodical essence we want to study here *in concreto* for the first time" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 43). Its motive is a confusion to which the Cartesian starting point is exposed. The evidence that a *cogitatio* is must not be mistaken for the evidence that my *cogitatio* is, the experience of this person in the world. The lecture draws the consequence without softening it: "The I as person, as thing of the world, and the experience as experience of this person, set into objective time, however indeterminately: all these are transcendences, and as such they are epistemologically null" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 44). Only through a reduction, "which we will already call *phenomenological reduction*", do I gain an absolute givenness that offers nothing of transcendence (Hua II, 44).

Two features of this first statement stay with the concept. The first is that the reduction is from the outset more than a retreat into what is inside. In the summary that Husserl wrote of the lectures' train of thought, the concept receives "a closer, deeper determination": it means the exclusion "of the transcendent in general as an existence to be accepted", that is, of everything that is not evident givenness in the genuine sense *(my translation)* (Hua II, 9). The fourth lecture repeats the point. The reduction does not restrict the investigation to what is really contained in the *cogitatio*; it restricts it "to the sphere of *pure self-givennesses*" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 60–61). The second feature is that the one who performs the reduction is among the things it excludes. The human being in world-time, "the thing among things", is no absolute givenness, and Husserl concludes: "We *leave* definitively the ground of *psychology*, even of *descriptive* psychology" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 7). In 1907 the I that had asked the question of knowledge is thus struck by its own method, and the lectures do not say who is left to do the seeing.

*Ideas I* gives the method the form in which it became known. Husserl begins from what he calls the "general thesis" of the natural attitude. I find the one spatiotemporal actuality constantly "on hand" (*vorhanden*) over against me, and I take it as it gives itself, as being there. Doubt and rejection of particular data change nothing in this thesis: "'the' world is always there as actuality" *(my translation)* (*Ideas I* § 30). The method consists in a change of this thesis that is none of the changes we know. Husserl takes his clue from the attempt to doubt, which "belongs to the realm of our *perfect freedom*", but he separates from it the one component he needs. The thesis is not turned into its negation, nor into conjecture or doubt. What happens is "something wholly peculiar": the thesis is "put out of action", "bracketed" *(my translation)* (§ 31). For this he takes the Greek word *epoché* (ἐποχή), "a certain abstention from judgment which is compatible with the unshaken … conviction of truth" *(my translation)* (§ 31).

An example will be useful, and I take one that Husserl uses ten years later because we shall need it again. I see a house in bright sunshine. Nothing gives me the slightest reason to doubt that it is there. To exercise the *epoché* is not to pretend that I have such a reason. It is to stop making use of the conviction that the house is there, while the conviction stays what it was. Husserl states the difference in a sentence that answers in advance most of what was later objected: "If I do so, as is my full freedom, then I do not negate this 'world', as though I were a sophist, I do not doubt its existence, as though I were a sceptic; but I exercise the 'phenomenological' ἐποχή, which completely closes off for me every judgment about spatiotemporal existence" *(my translation)* (§ 32). The sentence tells us what the method is not, and it tells us who acts: "I", in "my full freedom".

What the method yields is described in *Ideas I* with a word that Husserl will later regret. Consciousness, the text says, has "a being of its own which in its absolute own essence is not touched by the phenomenological exclusion", and it therefore remains as the "phenomenological residuum" *(my translation)* (§ 33; original pagination 59). The argument for this claim is the thought-experiment of § 49. Since a thing can always turn out not to be, the being of consciousness "would indeed be necessarily modified by an annihilation of the world of things, but would not be touched in its own existence" *(my translation)* (§ 49; 91–92). The next section draws the result, and it shows that "residuum" was never meant as a remainder after subtraction. We have "excluded" the whole world with all things, living beings and human beings, "ourselves included". Yet: "We have properly lost nothing, but have gained the whole of absolute being which, rightly understood, harbours all worldly transcendences in itself, 'constitutes' them in itself" *(my translation)* (§ 50; 94). Husserl adds later that nothing is thereby taken from "the fully valid being of the world"; what is removed is an absurd interpretation of it (§ 55; 107).

The text of 1913 thus contains two statements about the performer that it does not bring together. We ourselves are excluded along with the world. And I exclude, freely. In the sections read here Husserl does not ask how the I that exercises its full freedom is related to the human being that falls under the brackets. The house, we may say, has been accounted for: it is there as before, and its being there is no longer a premise. The one who looks at it has not been accounted for. The lectures of ten years later make this their theme.

## 2. The one who brackets: Husserl, 1923–1936

In the winter of 1923/24 Husserl approaches the reduction from the side of the one who performs it, under the heading of a theory of reflection. Reflection, the fortieth lecture says, is a "splitting of the I" (*Ichspaltung*). When I pass from "I perceive this house" to "I am aware that I perceive this house", a reflecting I comes on the scene above the perceiving one, and it is itself forgotten in its turn until a further reflection catches it (Hua VIII, 89–90). Ordinarily the upper I shares the interest of the lower: it believes with it that the house is there. Husserl's point is that this need not be so. The reflecting I can grasp the act of the lower I and its interested directedness "but be uninterested in that in which this observed I is interested". In that case, he says, I am as the one reflecting "no longer the I for which the house is existing actuality" *(my translation)* (Hua VIII, 96–97). I have become the "non-participating onlooker" (*unbeteiligter Zuschauer*) of myself.

Husserl guards this description at once against a misreading. The lack of interest "cannot mean a mere privation, a mere not-doing"; I do not drop my belief in the house as I drop my activities in sleep. Reflecting simply, "I cannot do otherwise than sympathize with myself". A special motivation must first free me from this sympathy, and the attitude of the onlooker can come about "only through the free deed of the *abstention from judgment*, of willingly detaching oneself from the original co-interest". The paragraph ends on a question that Husserl puts to himself: "What can the *motive* be here?" *(my translation)* (Hua VIII, 98). The performer of 1913, who acted in full freedom, has thus acquired a structure and a problem. The structure is that the one who brackets is a second I above the first, in the same streaming present. The problem is what could move a life that sympathizes with itself to split in this way.

A supplement of 1924 turns this into a criticism of his own earlier writing. Its title is "Critique of the two stages in which I had gained the idea of the reduction in 1907 and 1910". Its first point concerns words: "talk of a phenomenological '*residuum*' is better avoided, as is talk of the '*exclusion* of the world'", because it easily misleads one into thinking that the world now drops out of the phenomenological theme *(my translation)* (Hua VIII, 432). Its second point concerns the performer. It might seem self-evident that the subjectivity gained through the reduction is my own pure subjectivity, the subjectivity of the one who is phenomenologizing. Husserl answers that my pure psychic life is still something existing in the world, and that to reduce to it "would only be to abstract out the stratum of the psychic within the existing world" *(my translation)* (Hua VIII, 433). The subject who carries out transcendental experience is described in this text by a phrase that shows the difficulty: it is "I myself", as "the phenomenologizing and thereby anonymous I" *(my translation)* (Hua VIII, 432).

From here on Husserl's texts give two answers to the question who this anonymous I is, and they give them side by side. The first answer denies that it is the human being. The *Cartesian Meditations* say that the I which necessarily remains for me through the *epoché* "is not a piece of the world, and when it says 'I am, *ego cogito*', this no longer means: I, this human being, am" *(my translation)* (Hua I, 64). The second draft of the *Britannica* article says of the one who brackets every transcendent validity: "If I do so for myself, then I am not a human I" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 275). The lecture of 1931 speaks of "a true revolution" that has taken place with my philosophizing I, and of a new loneliness: out of human solitude there has come "the transcendental solitude, namely the solitude of the ego" *(my translation)* (Hua XXVII, 171). The *Crisis* is the most severe. In the *epoché*, and in the pure regard to the functioning I-pole, "nothing human shows itself, not soul and life of the soul, not real psychophysical human beings; all that belongs in the 'phenomenon'" *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 187).

The second answer says that this I is nevertheless none other than myself. The same sentence of the *Britannica* draft continues: I am not a human I, "although I lose nothing of the own-essential content of my pure soul", and the reduced ego "is, to be sure, my I, in the whole concretion of my life" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 275). The *Meditations* use the image of the splitting again. Above the naively interested I "the phenomenological I establishes itself as *uninterested onlooker*" (Hua I, 73), and this onlooker is an onlooker "of itself" (Hua I, 75). The *Crisis* joins the two answers in a single sentence, and its wording should be noticed. The I that I reach in the *epoché* "is actually called 'I' only by equivocation, though it is an essential equivocation, since, when I name it in reflection, I can say nothing other than: it is I, I who practise the *epoché*" *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 188).

One receives the impression that Husserl hesitates here, and the hesitation is his own. He calls the situation a paradox and states it without sparing himself. Mankind is a component part of the world; how can a component part of the world constitute the whole world? "The subject-part of the world swallows, so to speak, the whole world and thereby itself. What an absurdity" *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 183). The resolution he offers keeps the first answer for the order of method and the second for the order of being. Methodically everything must start from the unique I of my *epoché*, which "can never lose its uniqueness and personal indeclinability" *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 188). But every transcendental I must necessarily be constituted as a human being in the world, so that every human being "bears a transcendental I within himself", not as a real part or a layer of his soul, but in so far as he is "the self-objectivation" of that I *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 190). Husserl ends with this formula. He also says that the uniqueness of the ego must be upheld "for the deepest philosophical reasons, which cannot be gone into further" *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 190). The text therefore leaves the equivocation standing as essential, and I do not remove it.

What Husserl does not leave standing is the suggestion that the performer is simply the human being. The lecture of 1931 calls this the almost overpowering temptation. It is all too natural to say: "I, this human being, am after all the one who practises the whole method of the transcendental change of attitude, and who thereby withdraws to his pure ego; so what is this ego other than an abstract stratum in the concrete human being?" The reply is immediate. Whoever speaks in this way "has fallen back into the naive-natural attitude", for "to take oneself as a human being is already to presuppose the validity of the world" *(my translation)* (Hua XXVII, 173). The conclusion for a philosophy that begins from the human being follows a few pages later: it "falls back into that naivety which it is, as we think, the whole sense of modernity to overcome" *(my translation)* (Hua XXVII, 179).

Two further observations complete the picture, and both concern the vocabulary in which the late Husserl describes the act. The first is that he describes it as an event in a life. The *Britannica* draft calls the transcendental reduction "a kind of change of the whole form of life, which wholly surpasses all previous life-experience" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 276). The *Crisis* compares the phenomenologist's *epoché* to that of a vocation, which "has its times" and leaves the person's other interests intact; and it then denies that being a phenomenologist is in the end like being a shoemaker. The total phenomenological attitude may be called to bring about "a complete personal transformation, which would be comparable at first to a religious conversion", and which bears within itself "the significance of the greatest existential transformation that is set as a task to mankind as mankind" *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 140). The word "existential" does not occur in the file of *Ideas I*. Its appearance in 1936, in the description of the method that was to lead away from the human being, is at the least an indication that the question of the performer had not been closed.

The second observation is that Husserl now faults the way of 1913 for what it leaves empty. The "much shorter way" of *Ideas I*, which he calls the Cartesian way, "has the great disadvantage that, while it leads to the transcendental ego as if in one leap, it brings this ego into view, since every preceding explication must be lacking, in an apparent emptiness of content", so that one is at a loss to know what has been gained by it *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 157–58). The new way begins from the natural life of the world and asks how the world is pregiven in it. What is required for this is "a *total change* of the natural attitude, a change in which we no longer live, as heretofore, as human beings of natural existence in the constant accomplishing of the validity of the pregiven world" *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 151). Husserl has thus reconstructed his own method twice over: as the act of a second I, and as a transformation of the life from which that I splits off. The next section must ask how the same method looked to the reader who knew it best.

## 3. The reduction reported and measured: Heidegger, 1923–1925

Heidegger's first extended treatment of the reduction is a defence. In the course of 1923/24 he compares it with Descartes's way of doubt, and he starts from the opinion that the two are the same. "At first glance", he concedes, the two paths have so much in common that one could almost say that the reduction is at bottom the same as the Cartesian removal: both begin from the I in its surroundings, both press forward to the *cogito*, both proceed by suspension (GA 17 § 46 a). He then separates them on the very point on which Husserl had insisted in § 32. For Descartes the sciences are set aside as uncertain. For Husserl they are taken up into the theme: "The reduction has the positive sense of bringing the sciences and what is grasped in them into the new science's thematic domain". Descartes's search ends before nothing. Husserl's reduction, by contrast, "develops the possibility that every merely possible being comes into view; hence, not nothing but instead the entirety of being, with a specific modification, is supposed to become thematically present" (GA 17 § 46 a). Heidegger even credits Husserl with the tendency "to see the surrounding world itself in its original givenness", and he calls it secondary whether Husserl is able to carry this out.

The criticism of 1923/24 is placed elsewhere, and it concerns a presupposition. Although the reduction differs in its methodical sense from the way of doubt, "it presupposes for itself precisely the result of the path of doubt in the sense of something self-evident". The *cogito sum* is neither discussed nor earned: "Consciousness is the point of departure towards which, without being further questioned at all, the entire reduction is oriented" (GA 17 § 47 a). Heidegger traces this to what he calls care about certainty, and he states its effect on the maxim of phenomenology in one sentence. "To the matters themselves" has come to mean "to them insofar as they come into question as the theme of a science" (GA 17 § 48 c). In this first text, then, Heidegger does not object that the reduction loses the world. He objects that it knows in advance where it is going.

The course of 1925 contains the most careful report of the reduction that Heidegger gave, and the report is more favourable than the critique that follows it would lead one to expect. His example is the perception of a chair, and what he says of it holds equally of Husserl's house. In reflection I do not live in the perception of the house; I live in the apprehension of the perceiving, and I do not "go along with" the positing of the material world. This not going along is the *epoché*. Heidegger then describes what bracketing does to the thing perceived: "This bracketing of the entity takes nothing away from the entity itself, nor does it purport to assume that the entity is not. This reversal of perspective has rather the sense of making the being of the entity present." And again, more strongly: the suspension of the transcendent thesis "has but the sole function of making the entity present in regard to its being" (GA 20 § 10; GA 20, 136). The epoché performed uniformly through the whole sphere of acts is the reduction; a second, eidetic reduction then disregards whatever makes this stream of experiences mine, and the two together yield the pure field of consciousness (GA 20, 137).

It is striking that this report already contains the formula with which Heidegger will define his own reduction two years later. In 1925 it is offered as the sense of Husserl's method. The suspension, Heidegger says, is "always misunderstood" when one thinks that phenomenological reflection then has nothing more to do with the entity: "in an extreme and unique way, what really is at issue now is the determination of the being of the very entity" (GA 20, 136). We shall return to this sentence when we come to the course of 1927.

The critique that follows asks one question of the field so secured: is the being of the intentional, of the acts themselves, ever inquired into? Heidegger examines the four determinations that *Ideas I* gives of pure consciousness, as immanent, as absolutely given, as needing no thing in order to exist, and as pure. He finds that each characterizes consciousness as apprehended, as given, as constituting or as essence, and none as the being that it is. The reason is stated without polemic. "Husserl's primary question is simply not concerned with the character of the being of consciousness." It is guided by another concern: "*How can consciousness become the possible object of an absolute science*?" (GA 20 § 11; GA 20, 147). Heidegger then turns to the way by which the field is reached, and here his judgement of the reduction changes sign. The reduction starts from the real consciousness of the factually existing human being, but only in order to disregard it. Hence: "In its methodological sense as a disregarding, then, the reduction is in principle inappropriate for determining the being of consciousness positively" (GA 20 § 12; GA 20, 151).

Is this to say that Heidegger contradicts in § 12 what he reported in § 10? In a certain way one must answer yes, since the same operation is said first to make being present and then to be a mere disregarding. However, the two statements have different objects, and the difference is the whole point. What the bracketing makes present is the being of the house, of the entity that is perceived, in so far as it is an object of intentionality. What the reduction disregards is the being of the perceiving, the fact that there is someone for whom the house is there. Heidegger grants Husserl the reply that he would make: the reduction disregards reality "*at first*", in order then to consider it as it manifests itself in pure consciousness. And he grants more. Since the psychic too is constituted in pure consciousness as a reality, "*The question of being is thus raised, it is even answered*" (GA 20, 155). The objection survives these concessions in a narrower form. The answer that Husserl's method gives treats the being of the performer exactly as it treats the being of the house, as something that shows itself to a consciousness. This narrowing is not stated in these words by Heidegger, but his text supports each step of it.

Two sharper formulations give the objection its force, and both look ahead. The first concerns the eidetic reduction. It regards the what of the acts and disregards their individuation and existence, in the belief that the what of any entity can be defined in this way. "But if there were an entity *whose what is precisely to be and nothing but to be*, then this ideative regard of such an entity would be the most fundamental of misunderstandings" (GA 20, 152). The question of the being of the acts is thus not merely left unraised in the reductions: "it *gets lost precisely through them*" (GA 20, 151–52). The second concerns the starting point, and it turns on a word. The attitude from which the reduction sets out takes the human being as a living thing that occurs in the world, with experiences attached to it. Heidegger asks whether one experiences oneself, in the natural way of experience, "zoologically", and he answers that this is "an experience which is totally *un*natural", since it includes a definite theoretical position. It is rightly called an attitude (*Einstellung*) only because one must first place oneself into it (*hineinstellen*). "Man's natural manner of experience, by contrast, cannot be called an attitude" (GA 20, 155–56).

By 1925 Heidegger has thus measured the reduction three times. It is not the Cartesian doubt, and it loses nothing of the entities it brackets. It makes their being present. But it starts from a description of the performer that is not natural, and it ends by disregarding the performer's being. His result is stated in one sentence: in the reduction the question of the being of the intentional "is expressly deferred" (GA 20, 157). What Heidegger does with the method once he has his own answer to that question is the subject of the next section.

## 4. The word kept and the word withheld: Heidegger, 1927–1928

In the summer of 1927 Heidegger takes over Husserl's term by name. Ontological investigation, he says, always turns first to some being, but it is then led away from that being and led back to its being. "We call this basic component of phenomenological method—the leading back or re-duction of investigative vision from a naively apprehended being to being—*phenomenological reduction*." The next sentence fixes the relation to Husserl: "We are thus adopting a central term of Husserl's phenomenology in its literal wording though not in its substantive intent" (GA 24 § 5; Hofstadter, 21; GA 24, 29). For Husserl the reduction leads the gaze from the natural attitude of the human being back to the transcendental life of consciousness. "*For us* phenomenological reduction means leading phenomenological vision back from the apprehension of a being, whatever may be the character of that apprehension, to the understanding of the being of this being" (GA 24, 29).

The disclaimer is firm, yet it should not be taken at its word without a look at the report of 1925. There Heidegger had said of Husserl's bracketing that its sole function is to make the entity present in regard to its being. The reduction of 1927 has the same direction, from the entity to its being. What has been altered is the place where the leading back arrives. Husserl's arrives at a consciousness in which the entity is constituted, Heidegger's at an understanding of being. The "substantive intent" that Heidegger declines is therefore narrower than the sentence suggests. He declines the destination, and with it the performer that the destination implies, while the movement of the gaze he keeps. This is my comparison of the two passages; Heidegger does not refer back to the earlier course.

Heidegger at once limits what his own reduction can do, and the limits repeat his criticism of Husserl's. The leading back "is not the only basic component of phenomenological method; in fact, it is not even the central component". Taken by itself it is a turning away: "Pure aversion from beings is a merely negative methodological measure which not only needs to be supplemented by a positive one but expressly requires us to be led toward being." Being is not found lying before us as a being is. It "must always be brought to view in a free projection", which he calls phenomenological construction; and since every projection starts from the experience of beings that is open to a factical Dasein in its historical situation, construction requires a critical dismantling of inherited concepts, which he calls destruction (GA 24 § 5; Hofstadter, 21–23). A reduction that is nothing but disregarding was the charge against Husserl in 1925. In 1927 Heidegger says the same of the reduction in general, his own included, and assigns the positive work to two other components.

What becomes of the word in the published work? Here an observation about vocabulary is in place. *Being and Time* does not use "reduction" at all. It does use, twice in passages that bear on our question, the German word with which Heidegger glosses the term in the lecture course, "leading back" (*Rückführung*). The first step in understanding the problem of being, the book says, is not to determine beings as beings "by leading them back to another being in their origin" *(my translation)* (SZ 6). And idealism, if it means "the leading back of all beings to a subject or a consciousness" whose own being remains undetermined, is "methodically no less naive than the crudest realism" *(my translation)* (SZ 208). The word that names Heidegger's method in the lecture hall names, in the book, the two forms of leading back that are to be avoided. I take this as an indication that the term was withheld from print because, used without commentary, it would have been read in the sense refused at SZ 208. That is an inference from usage, and the texts read here do not confirm it in so many words.

The documents of October 1927 show that the term was not withheld from Husserl. The second draft of the *Britannica* article has an introduction and a first part of which the editor of the critical edition notes that they were edited by Heidegger (Hua IX, 256–63). The introduction presents the whole of phenomenology as a "regress to consciousness" and asks whether this "turning of the gaze from beings to consciousness" is accidental "or in the end demanded by the peculiar character of that which, under the title being, is constantly sought as the problem-field of philosophy" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 256). The first part describes the psychological reduction in the vocabulary of the lecture course: the "leading back (reduction)" of the tendency of apprehension out of the perception alters the perception so little "that the reduction precisely makes the perception accessible as what it is, namely as perception of the thing" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 261). In a text written for Husserl, then, Heidegger motivates the turn to consciousness by the question of being, and he describes the reduction as a disclosure.

The letter that accompanied his notes states the agreement and the difficulty in two consecutive paragraphs. The agreement: "There is agreement that the being in the sense of what you call 'world' cannot be clarified in its transcendental constitution by a going back to a being of just the same mode of being." The difficulty: "But this is not to say that what makes up the place of the transcendental is no being at all; rather, precisely the *problem* arises: what is the mode of being of the being in which 'world' is constituted? That is the central problem of *Being and Time*" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 601). The first paragraph grants Husserl everything that the reduction is meant to secure against psychologism. The second refuses the inference from "not a being like the world" to "not a being". Heidegger's own thesis follows: transcendental constitution "is a central possibility of the existence of the factical self", and this self, the concrete human being, is as such never a "worldly real fact", because the human being "is never merely present-at-hand but exists" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 601–2).

The marginal notes apply this to Husserl's sentences one by one, and three of them concern the performer directly. Where Husserl writes that as the one who brackets "I am not a human I", Heidegger notes: "Or perhaps precisely such, in its ownmost, 'wondrous' possibility of existence", and he refers Husserl to his own phrase about a change of the form of life. A second note on the same passage reads: "Why not? Is this doing not a possibility of the human being, but, precisely because the human being is never present-at-hand, a comportment, that is, a mode of being, which of itself procures itself for itself and so never belongs to the positivity of the present-at-hand" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 275 n.). Where Husserl writes that the reduction embraces the whole of life "in one universal theoretical will", the note is four words long: "And this will itself?" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 275 n.). And where Husserl sets the transcendentally pure ego over against the soul, a word that presupposes a world, Heidegger asks: "Does not a world in general belong to the essence of the pure ego?" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 274 n.).

The note on the will touches the place where Husserl had himself asked, in the lectures of 1923/24, what the motive of the free deed could be. *Being and Time* contains a description that can be read as Heidegger's answer, although the book does not present it as a method and does not mention Husserl in this connection. In anxiety, innerworldly beings lose all relevance. This complete insignificance "does not mean absence of world"; it means that the world in its worldliness is all that still obtrudes *(my translation)* (SZ 186–87). Anxiety "individualizes and so discloses Dasein as '*solus ipse*'", but this "existential 'solipsism'" does not put an isolated subject-thing into the harmless void of a worldless occurring; it brings Dasein "in an extreme sense before its world as world" *(my translation)* (SZ 188). And anxiety "fetches Dasein back out of its falling absorption in the 'world'" *(my translation)* (SZ 189). Each of the three features has its counterpart in Husserl's account of the *epoché*: beings stay and lose their hold, the one concerned is singled out, and an absorption in the world is undone. The difference is the one that the marginal note marks. Husserl's suspension is a free deed of the will, whereas Heidegger's comes over Dasein as a mood and is no act at all. That anxiety stands in *Being and Time* where the *epoché* stands in Husserl is my reading; I state it because it explains why the note asks after the will.

Two last texts show that Heidegger's objection of 1925 was kept after the term had been redefined. *Being and Time* says of its own procedure that the mode of being of Dasein demands of an interpretation "that it *conquer* the being of this being *against its own tendency to cover things up*", so that the analysis constantly has, for everyday understanding, "the character of a *violence*" *(my translation)* (SZ 311). The method goes against the grain of ordinary life, as Husserl's does. And in 1928 Heidegger repeats, in passing, the complaint about essence. In the consideration of essences, "especially in Husserl", the what is brought out by the exclusion of the actual in the phenomenological reduction; "but with the exclusion of the actual, actuality, that is, the *modus existendi* … is not excluded" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 228–29). The mode of existing remains to be asked about after the reduction has been performed. With this we have both authors' accounts before us, and we have as yet not answered the question from which we began.

## 5. Two answers to one question

The parallels must be stated first, and they are more exact than the received view allows. Both authors deny that the reduction is doubt and that it loses the world. Husserl says so in *Ideas I* § 32 and § 50 and again in the supplement of 1924; Heidegger says so on Husserl's behalf in 1923/24 and in 1925. Both hold that the life from which the method sets out conceals from itself what the method uncovers, and that the method therefore has to be won against that life. For Husserl the naive I is forgotten in its acts and sympathizes with itself; for Heidegger everyday Dasein is absorbed in the world and covers itself up. Both describe what the method does to its performer as a singling out: "transcendental solitude" in Husserl, "existential solipsism" in Heidegger. Both judge that a way to the reduction which only disregards is insufficient. Husserl says in 1936 that the Cartesian way shows the ego "in an apparent emptiness of content"; Heidegger says in 1927 that pure aversion from beings is "merely negative". And both agree, in Heidegger's words to Husserl, that the world cannot be clarified by going back to a being of the same mode of being as the world.

Naturally there are also weighty differences. The first concerns the point of departure. Husserl's natural attitude is defined by a thesis, the constant positing of the world as on hand. Heidegger holds that the description from which the reduction of *Ideas I* in fact sets out, the human being as a living thing with experiences, is a theoretical construction and no natural experience at all. The second concerns the manner of the act. Husserl's reduction is a free deed, carried by a will that binds the phenomenologist "once and for all" (Hua XXVII, 172). Heidegger's note asks what this will itself is, and his published description puts a mood in its place. The third difference concerns the result. Husserl's reduction ends at an I and its life of consciousness, in which everything that is has its sense and validity. Heidegger's ends at the understanding of being, and it is expressly not self-sufficient.

Here lies, however, a temptation to locate the root of these differences where it is usually located, in the destination. The texts do not support this. Heidegger motivates the regress to consciousness by the question of being in the very draft he edits for Husserl, and he calls transcendental constitution a central possibility of existence. Husserl, for his part, never held that the reduced field was a consciousness without world; the reduced ego harbours all worldly transcendences in itself. The root lies in the third member that every account of a reduction needs besides a point of departure and a destination, namely the one who goes from the one to the other. Husserl's first difference with Heidegger, on the natural attitude, is a difference about what the performer is before the reduction. The second, on will and mood, is a difference about how the performer comes to it. And the third follows from these, since what one finds at the end depends on who has arrived.

Let us return a last time to the house in the sunshine. For Husserl there are, once I reflect in the required way, two of me in one present: the I for whom the house is there, and the onlooker for whom it is the meant house of a perceiving. The onlooker is not a human being, since "human being" is one of the senses that the onlooker watches being constituted; yet it is I. For Heidegger there is one of me throughout. The one who stands back from the house and asks how it is given is the same factical self who lives in houses, and standing back is something this self can do because its way of being is never that of a thing on hand. Each account has to pay for what it gains. Husserl's protects the performer from every worldly presupposition and must then say that it is called "I" by equivocation. Heidegger's keeps the performer whole and must then show that a being which exists factically, here and at a time, can be that in which world is constituted, without becoming once more a fact within the world.

Is this to say that each has simply refuted the other from his own ground, so that nothing is decided? In a certain way one must answer yes, since Husserl's reply of 1931 measures every doctrine of the human being by the validity of the world, and Heidegger's question of 1927 measures every talk of an ego by the meaning of "is". However, this is not to say that the two replies meet their targets equally. Husserl's reply holds against any philosophy that takes the human being as a reality in the world: such a philosophy does presuppose what the reduction suspends. It is true that the lecture of 1931 describes its opponents in just these terms, as seeking the foundation of philosophy in a doctrine of the essence of the human being's "concrete-worldly existence" (Hua XXVII, 164). But the letter of 1927 had denied in advance that Dasein is a "worldly real fact". The lecture does not name Heidegger, and I do not know from the texts read here whether Husserl had the letter in mind; if he had, the lecture answers a position that the letter does not hold. Heidegger is right, then, that the alternative between a worldly human being and an unworldly ego is not complete.

Heidegger's objection of 1925, on the other side, does not reach the Husserl of the lectures given a year and a half earlier, which remained unpublished. That the reduction disregards the performer is true of the way of *Ideas I*, and Husserl says as much in 1936. It is not true of the theory of the splitting of the I, whose entire purpose is to say what the one who brackets is and does. Husserl is right, too, on the point that Heidegger concedes in his letter: whatever the performer is, it cannot have the mode of being of what it constitutes. What Husserl's theory does not supply is what Heidegger's last line in the letter demands: "The question about the mode of being of that which constitutes cannot be evaded. The problem of being is therefore universally related to what constitutes and what is constituted" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 602). Husserl's texts come close to granting this. An I that is "I" only by an essential equivocation, and that bears the sense "human being" as its own self-objectivation, is an I whose "am" has two meanings. To say what holds the two together is to ask about being in a sense that covers both. Husserl does not draw this consequence. He draws the opposite one, that the question can only be asked inside the *epoché* and therefore of the constituted alone.

The result can now be stated in its sharpest form. The question in the title has two answers in the texts, and they are divided by a single point. For Husserl the reduction is performed by an I that is mine and not human; for Heidegger it is performed by the human being, in a possibility that is its own and not worldly. Each denies of the performer the predicate that the other most fears: Husserl denies that it is human, Heidegger that it is merely present. Everything else that is usually listed as dividing them on the reduction, the fate of the world, the comparison with Descartes, the charge of emptiness, turns out to be common ground or a matter on which the criticized author had already criticized himself.

Three limits should be stated. I have not shown that Heidegger's reduction is free of the difficulty he finds in Husserl's. The course of 1927 says that ontology "has an ontical foundation" in a being, Dasein (GA 24 § 5), and the leading back from beings to being is carried out by a factical Dasein in its historical situation; whether the performer of this reduction can be described without a paradox like Husserl's is a question the texts read here raise and do not answer. Nor have I followed Heidegger beyond 1928. A protocol of the seminar of 1973, which is not from his own hand, reports him as saying that *Being and Time* "no longer speaks of consciousness. Consciousness is plain and simply set aside—for Husserl this was a pure scandal!" If the report is accurate, the later Heidegger described his difference from Husserl by the destination after all, and the texts of 1927 are evidence of a moment and not of a settled position. Finally, the dispute over the performer is one that Husserl, and not his critic, first put into words. The question "And this will itself?" was written in a margin in 1927. Husserl had asked in the lectures of 1923/24 what the motive of the free deed could be. He named the paradox of the subject that swallows the world and itself, and he said in his last work that the deepest reasons for his own answer could not be gone into. It is in the patience with which he kept this question open against his own method that Husserl gave the phenomenological reduction more than its name.

## 3. Steps that are mine and not the texts'

- That *Being and Time* withholds the word "reduction" because of the sense refused at SZ 208 (section 4).
- That anxiety stands in *Being and Time* where the *epoché* stands in Husserl (section 4).
- That Heidegger's objection of 1925 narrows, after his own concessions, to the claim that the performer's being is treated like that of any constituted object (section 3).
- That the lecture of 1931 applies to Heidegger; it names Dilthey and Scheler only (section 5).
- That Husserl's "essential equivocation" calls for a question of being covering both senses of "I am" (section 5).

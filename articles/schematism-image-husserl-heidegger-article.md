# The Look and the Rule

## Schema and Image in Husserl and in Heidegger's Reading of Kant

It seems to be generally accepted that Husserl has nothing to set beside the interpretation of the Schematism chapter that stands at the centre of Heidegger's Kant book of 1929. Husserl dismissed Kant's doctrine of the schematism in his first publication, he later counted it among the constructions of a philosophy that deduces where it ought to exhibit, and the word hardly occurs in him. On this view the comparison is either impossible, since the two authors share no term, or decided in advance. Heidegger's reading rests on the claim that time is a "pure image" and that every concept needs an image of its own kind. Husserl holds that perception is not a consciousness of images and that the intuition of the past "cannot itself be a rendering in images". The dispute would then come down to the word "image", and Husserl's old argument against the image-theory of consciousness would settle it. This picture leaves two things unexplained. In *Ideas I* Husserl describes the givenness of a thing as governed by "an a priori rule" that pre-forms the type of infinitely many possible appearances, and he gives this rule a Kantian name. Heidegger, for his part, says that a photograph is only a "transcription" of what shows itself directly, and that the primary sense of "image" is the look a being itself offers.[^1]

In what follows I wish to show that the two authors are not divided where the word "image" places their difference. Three steps are needed. The first is that "image" (*Bild*) names one thing in Husserl and three in Heidegger, and that Husserl's objection reaches only the one sense that Heidegger himself treats as derivative. The second is that both authors describe the same structure in the perceived particular: a look that counts for less than the bounded range of possible looks to which it belongs, and a rule that draws this range. The third is that they part over the order of founding between the look and the rule. For Husserl the straightforward look of a thing founds every grasp of what is general, and a concept is an identical object that no procedure can replace. For Heidegger the look of this house already rests on a schematizing preview, and the concept is nothing beyond the rule. I also wish to show that this root of the difference is already stated in the two sentences that Husserl wrote against Kant in 1887.

The comparison has a measure because both authors ask one question, which Heidegger puts in a phrase: how does a look show "the one that holds for many"? A house that I see is this house, and yet it shows me what a house looks like. Five points drawn on paper are these five points, and yet they are, in Kant's words, "an image of the number five". The question is what the single look contributes to the general, and what the general has already contributed to the look.

The article rests on a closed body of texts: the passages listed in one topic index of a working archive, each opened and read in the archive's files. On Heidegger's side these are §§ 19–23 of *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* (1929) and nothing else. On Husserl's side they are the Habilitationsschrift *On the Concept of Number* (1887) and the *Philosophy of Arithmetic* (1891); the Second, Fifth and Sixth *Logical Investigations* (1901); the lectures *Thing and Space* (1907); *Ideas I* (1913); the lectures on internal time-consciousness with their supplementary texts; and one page of the lectures *First Philosophy* (1923/24). The dates are the index's. I leave aside Heidegger's lecture courses of the 1920s, among them the course on the *Critique of Pure Reason* of 1927/28, which will contain a longer treatment of the Schematism than the book does. I also leave aside Husserl's lectures on phantasy and image-consciousness, to which *Ideas I* itself refers for the correction of the *Logical Investigations*, and his account of how an empty intention is made intuitive, which occupies the place of Heidegger's "sensibilization" without doing its work. These omissions have a reason. The article makes a claim about an order of founding, and for such a claim a short series of passages that has been read is worth more than a long one that is remembered. One further restriction may surprise. I make no claim of influence in either direction. Most of the Husserlian texts are older than Heidegger's book by sixteen to forty-two years, and the one that comes nearest to it, the lectures of 1907, was not in print until 1973. The comparison is of problems. Where I set a sentence of 1891 beside a sentence of 1929, the pairing is mine.

## 1. Sensibilization and the three senses of "image": Heidegger, 1929

Heidegger's interpretation begins from finitude. A finite being does not create the beings it knows. It has to receive them, and it can receive them only if it has turned toward them in advance in such a way that they can stand over against it. Heidegger calls this prior turning "transcendence", and he adds a requirement that carries the five sections. The horizon within which beings can be encountered must itself be perceivable, and perceivable means "immediately receivable in intuition". The finite being must therefore "form" the look of this horizon from out of itself. Since finite intuition is sensibility, the conclusion follows: "The horizon of transcendence can form itself only in a sensibilization" (GA 3, 91). The term is Heidegger's own. Sensibilization (*Versinnlichung*) means "the way in which a finite being can make something intuitable for itself, that is, can provide itself with a look (image) of something" (GA 3, 92). The pure sensibilization of the pure concepts is what Kant, on this reading, treats under the title "schematism".

Everything now depends on what a look or image is, and Heidegger distinguishes three meanings and says that Kant uses all three without marking them off. "Image" can mean, first, the look of a particular being in so far as it is manifest as present: the landscape offers a look, and it is called an image "as though it looked at us" (GA 3, 93). It can mean, second, a likeness (*Abbild*): something present, such as a photograph, which offers a look of its own and in doing so shows something else. It can mean, third, a look of anything whatever, where nothing is said about whether a being or a non-being becomes intuitable in it (GA 3, 92–93). The order of the first two is stated without hesitation. The second meaning is "derived" from the first, and every likeness, a photograph for instance, "remains only a transcription of what shows itself immediately as 'image'" (GA 3, 93). To provide oneself with an image in the second sense is no longer to intuit a being directly. It is to buy or to make a photograph.

Heidegger then builds a chain of likenesses in order to reach a function of the look that neither of the first two meanings covers. A death mask is a likeness of a dead man's face, and a photograph of the mask is a likeness of a likeness. It is still an image of the dead man, "but only because it gives the 'image' of the dead man, shows him as he looks or looked" (GA 3, 94). The photograph can also show something else, namely how a death mask in general looks. The mask can show how the face of a dead human being in general looks, and so can a single dead man. In this function the looks of this dead man, this mask and this photograph show "how something looks 'in general', in the one that holds for many" (GA 3, 94). They serve the sensibilization of concepts. This cannot mean that they give a direct look of the concept, since a concept is a general presentation and an intuition is a singular one. For the same reason, Heidegger says, a concept "cannot be depicted" (GA 3, 94).

The example that carries the analysis from here on is a house. This perceived house shows how a house in general looks. Heidegger asks in what way it does so, and his answer has three parts. First, we are not absorbed in the look of this house in order to learn how just this house looks. The house shows itself as something that need not look as it does in order to be a house: it shows "'only' the how in which a house can look" (GA 3, 95). Second, this house has decided for one among a compass of possibilities, and the result of the decision "interests us as little" as the decisions made by the factual look of other houses. What we are after is "the compass of possible looking as such, more exactly what draws this circle, that which regulates and pre-delineates how something must look in general in order to be able, as a house, to offer the corresponding look" (GA 3, 95). Third, this pre-delineation is "no list in the sense of a mere enumeration of the 'marks'" found on a house. It is a marking-out of the whole of what is meant by something like "house".

From the house Heidegger draws a definition of conceptual presenting. If the concept is what serves as a rule, then to present conceptually is "the giving in advance of the rule of a possible provision of a look", and such presenting is by its structure referred to a possible look (GA 3, 95–96). Two things about this definition should be noticed. The unity of the concept is not grasped for itself. It stands in view as regulating only when one looks away from it to its determining of the rule. And what used to be called the concept, the presenting of a unity valid for many, was "only a detached element" of it (GA 3, 96). Kant's sentence then receives its place: the presentation of "a general procedure of the imagination for providing a concept with its image" is the schema of that concept (A 140/B 179–80; GA 3, 97). The schema is to be distinguished from an image and is nevertheless necessarily referred to something like an image. This image is neither a simple look nor a likeness. Heidegger gives it a name of his own, the schema-image (*Schema-Bild*).

The relation of the schema-image to the schema is worked out on Kant's remark that an object of experience or an image of it never "attains" its empirical concept. Heidegger grants that this means, to begin with, that it does not present the concept adequately. He then turns the remark. The empirical look can have no function of depicting the concept at all, and it contains everything the concept contains, "if not more"; only it does not contain it as the concept presents it, as one that holds for many (GA 3, 98). The single case has taken leave of arbitrariness and is thereby "a possible example" of the one that regulates the arbitrary many. Hence the non-attainment "expresses the positive structural relation of the schema-image to the schema", and the consequence is drawn at once: "the concept is nothing beyond the presentation of this regulating unity of the rule. What logic calls a concept is grounded in the schema" (GA 3, 98).

The mathematical cases confirm this. A drawn triangle must be acute, right-angled or obtuse, so that it too has "the face of a single thing", while the schema intends the unity of the general rule of manifold possible presentations (GA 3, 99). Five points set in a row may be called an image of the number five only in the sense of a schema-image. The row shows the number "not because it can be surveyed and we can seemingly read the number off it, but because it coincides with the presentation of the rule of the possible presentability of this number" (GA 3, 100). We do not first grasp the number on the ground of this coincidence either. We already have every number in what Kant calls the presentation of a method for presenting a multitude, "a thousand for instance", in an image. Whether a row of points can be surveyed or not is therefore "of no account" for the seeing of the schema-image.

The section closes with a reversal of the order from which it started, and this reversal is the thesis that the confrontation below will have to weigh. Schema-forming sensibilization cannot be understood by analogy with ordinary pictorial presentation, nor traced back to it. On the contrary, sensibilization in the first sense, "the immediate empirical looking at things and the making of present likenesses of them", is possible "only on the ground of the possible sensibilization of concepts in the manner of schematism" (GA 3, 100–101). The reason is given in two sentences. "All conceptual presenting is by its essence schematism", and all finite knowing, as thinking intuition, is necessarily conceptual. So the immediate perception of this house "necessarily contains the schematizing preview of something like house in general", and only from this presenting-in-advance can what is encountered show itself as a house (GA 3, 101). The look of a being came first in the order of the three meanings. In the order of founding it now comes second. What the look of a being presupposes in the case of the pure concepts is the matter of the next two paragraphs of Kant's chapter and of our next section.

## 2. The pure image and the formation of concepts: Heidegger, 1929

Kant says that the schema of a pure concept of the understanding is something "which can be brought into no image at all" (A 142/B 181). If it belongs to a schema to bring itself into an image, this sentence seems to deny that the pure concepts have schemata. Heidegger meets the difficulty with the distinction he has prepared. "Image" in Kant's sentence can only mean a certain kind of image, the schema-images that belong to empirical and mathematical concepts. The refusal says that the look regulated by the schema of a pure concept can never be taken from the compass of what is empirically intuitable, nor from the constructions of mathematics, which are restricted to one region of objects. It is worth noticing the word Heidegger uses here. What Kant rejects, he says, is a possible "rendering in images" (*Verbildlichung*) of the schema of the notions (GA 3, 102). We shall meet the same word in Husserl.

The image that remains is time. As pure intuition time is "something that provides a look prior to all experience", and the pure look that gives itself in it must therefore be called a pure image. Heidegger quotes Kant's own sentence that time is the pure image of all objects of the senses in general (A 142/B 182). The thesis is then stated in its full strength: "As 'pure image', time is the schema-image and not merely the form of intuition standing over against the pure concepts of the understanding" (GA 3, 104). Time is, moreover, the only pure look available to the pure concepts, and so this one image must be capable of being formed in several ways. The schemata regulate themselves into time and thereby articulate it; they are, in Kant's words, "a priori time-determinations according to rules". In a parenthesis Heidegger marks whose time this is. The pure look in question is "for Kant the pure succession of the sequence of nows" (GA 3, 103).

One schema is interpreted, that of substance, and Heidegger says why he offers the interpretation: it is to make visible that Kant's doctrine "is no baroque theory but is drawn from the phenomena themselves" (GA 3, 106). Substance as a pure concept means lying-at-the-ground. Its schema must present this in the pure image of time. Now time as a sequence of nows is now at every time: "In every now it is now." Time thereby shows its own constancy. Heidegger sharpens the point: time "is not one abiding thing among others", but on the ground of this character it gives "the pure look of something like abiding in general" (GA 3, 107). And since each now is also another now, time as the look of abiding offers at the same time "the image of pure change in abiding". In the prior view of this pure image a being that is unchangeable through change can show itself for experience.

Heidegger calls this interpretation "short and rough", and the limits he sets to it are part of his position. He observes that Kant's four characters of time "are not so much developed systematically out of time itself by an analysis of it as fixed in it 'according to the order of the categories'" (GA 3, 105). He declines a concrete exposition of Kant's definitions of the single schemata and postpones the reason to a later section. And he says of his own reading of substance that it "cannot by far penetrate into the more original structures" (GA 3, 107). The text thus claims that the doctrine is drawn from the phenomena, and it concedes in the same pages that the articulation of the pure image is taken from the table of categories. Heidegger does not remove this tension in the sections read here, and I leave it standing.

The last of the five sections draws the consequence for the concept of a concept. Kant introduces the schematism as a problem of subsumption: how can appearances be brought under pure concepts with which they are wholly heterogeneous? Heidegger grants the formulation "a certain right" as a first introduction and denies that it is the solution. He recalls Kant's distinction between bringing objects under concepts and bringing the pure synthesis to concepts. The second happens in the transcendental schematism, and there "the categories first form themselves as categories". If the categories are the original concepts, then the transcendental schematism is "the original and proper formation of concepts in general" (GA 3, 110). With this the generality of ontological concepts becomes a question. Since the categories are not homogeneous with empirical objects and their concepts, their generality cannot be "that of a merely gradually higher level of generality of a higher or highest ontic 'genus'" (GA 3, 111). The section ends with a judgement on the chapter itself. There is "not the slightest occasion to keep complaining of a lack of unity and a confusion in the Schematism chapter" (GA 3, 112). Heidegger names none of those who complained. One of them had been Husserl.

## 3. Number is not the presentation of counting: Husserl on Kant's schema, 1887–1924

Husserl's discussion of the Schematism chapter stands in his Habilitationsschrift of 1887 and is taken over, with slight changes, into the *Philosophy of Arithmetic* of 1891. It belongs to a criticism of the view that the concept of number rests on the intuition of time. Husserl first cites Kant's statement that number is the pure schema of magnitude. He finds the passage dark and hard to square with Kant's explanations of what a schema does, and he adds that these explanations themselves "are admittedly not exactly uniform". He then sets two of them side by side. According to the one, the schema is the formal and pure condition of sensibility to which the concept of the understanding is restricted in its use. According to the other, which comes "a few lines further", it is the presentation of a general procedure of the imagination for providing a concept with its image (Hua XII, 32–33).

The second definition is put to a test on the category it ought to fit best. If we apply it to the schema of quantity, Husserl writes, we should have to say that number is the presentation of a general procedure of the imagination for providing the concept of quantity with its image. The objection follows in two sentences: "But by this procedure only counting can be meant. Is it not clear, however, that 'number' and 'presentation of counting' are not the same?" (Hua XII, 33). Two further difficulties are added. It is not easy to see how one should get a priori from the category of quantity, by way of the presentation of time, to the single determinate concepts of number. And it is still less evident what necessity determines us to ascribe to a concrete multiplicity one number and always the same, the number of which we say that it belongs to it. The verdict is general: the doctrine of the schematism "seems here, as elsewhere, to miss the purpose for which it was specially created" (Hua XII, 33).

What does this early criticism commit Husserl to? The index reads the passage as the one place where the two authors work on the same lines of Kant, and it is right about that. The definition Husserl tests is the sentence on which Heidegger closes his § 20, and the non-uniformity Husserl notes is the charge Heidegger rejects at the end of his § 23. But the objection is narrower than a rejection of schemata, and what it presupposes matters more than what it rejects. Husserl takes for granted that a number is one thing and the presentation of the way to produce an instance of it another. The number belongs to the multiplicity, and the procedure belongs to the one who counts. A concept that could be exchanged for the presentation of a procedure would on this premise be no concept of the thing. This premise is what Heidegger's § 21 denies when it says that the concept is nothing beyond the presentation of the regulating unity of the rule.

The same book gives the premise its positive form in the distinction between authentic and symbolic presentations. The word "authentic" (*eigentlich*) here has no connection with Heidegger's later use of it. A symbolic or inauthentic presentation is a presentation through signs: a content is given not directly as what it is but indirectly through signs that characterize it unambiguously. Husserl's example is a house. "We have, for example, an authentic presentation of the outward appearance of a house when we actually look at it; a symbolic presentation when someone gives us the indirect characterization: the corner house of such and such streets" (Hua XII, 193). The symbolic presentation serves as a surrogate, provisional or lasting, for the actual one. The house actually looked at is here the standard, and what is not actually seen is measured against it.

For numbers the distinction is decisive, and its ground is one that Heidegger will also name. If we had authentic presentations of all numbers as we have of the first few, Husserl says, there would be no arithmetic, because it would be superfluous. In fact our capacity for presentation is highly limited, and that limits are set here at all "lies in the finitude of human nature. Only of an infinite understanding can we expect the authentic presentation of all numbers" (Hua XII, 191–92). A small multiplicity can be collected in one act, each member apprehended for itself. For sets of a hundred, a thousand or a million members this is beyond us, although an extension of our capacity that would make it possible is "quite conceivable" (Hua XII, 219). In such cases we rely on the sensuous character of the whole configuration, for which Husserl proposes the term "figural moment" (Hua XII, 209), and on signs. The thousand, which is Kant's own example, is thus for Husserl a number that we do not authentically have. For Kant as Heidegger reads him we have it exactly as we have the number five, in the presentation of a method.

Husserl did not take up the Schematism chapter again in the texts read here. One page of the lectures of 1923/24 touches it and gives the criticism a methodical form. Kant's procedure, Husserl says there, is regressive. It asks how sensibility must be conceived if geometrical judgements are to be possible, and it demands and carries out deductions of the forms of intuition and of the categories; "the schematism too is deduced". This is "a constructive procedure of thought, which intuition follows after", and not one that ascends from below and advances intuitively "from exhibition to exhibition" (Hua VII, 197–98). The index sets this page against Heidegger's assurance that the schematism is drawn from the phenomena and calls the pair the sharpest opposition it records. The opposition is real, but both texts qualify it. Husserl writes on the same page that Kant saw, "with an unparalleled intuitive power", essential structures of transcendental subjectivity that no one before him had suspected. And Heidegger, as we have seen, concedes for the single schemata what Husserl charges against the whole: their order is taken from the categories and not from an analysis of time. If the schematism is to be measured by what an exhibiting analysis finds, the next section must ask what Husserl's own analyses find at the place where Kant put the schema.

## 4. The rule of possible appearances: Husserl, 1901–1913

Husserl's first answer to the question of how something general is given is negative, and it is directed against images. In the Second *Logical Investigation* he takes up Locke's universal triangle, the idea of a triangle that is neither right-angled nor acute nor obtuse. Geometry proves a priori that every triangle is one of these, and what is incompatible a priori is incompatible in a picture too: "The adequate picture of a triangle is itself a triangle" (LI II, § 11; Findlay). The universal idea is the idea of what every triangle has, and it is not for that reason the idea of *a* triangle. The doctrine of vague "generic images" fares no better, since vagueness is a definite feature of a concrete content. Husserl states the positive point in one sentence: "The essence of the matter does not reside in changing contents, but in the unity of an intention directed to constant attributes" (LI II, § 12; Findlay). Heidegger's triangle and Husserl's are the same triangle, and so far the two agree. A drawn figure has the face of a single thing, and the general is not a faint or composite picture.

They agree on a further point, which the *Logical Investigations* state in the form of a doctrine of founding. Sense-perception, Husserl says in the Sixth Investigation, makes the thing appear "in one blow"; its manner is "straightforward", and "it requires no apparatus of founding or founded acts" (LI VI, § 47; Findlay). The acts in which something general or something categorial is given are founded on such straightforward acts. This is the order from which Husserl never departs in the texts read here, and it is the reverse of the order with which Heidegger's § 21 ends. Before the two orders are confronted, however, we have to see how much of Heidegger's rule Husserl finds inside straightforward perception itself.

The lectures of 1907 find a great deal. Husserl starts from the indeterminacy of what is not properly seen. I do not know exactly what the back of the thing is like. But indeterminacy "is never absolute"; it is delimited, and to its essence belongs "determinability within a firmly delimited general sphere", such as spatial shape or colouring (Hua XVI, 59). Husserl adds at once what this determinability is not. It is not the possibility of applying the general words "shape" and "colour" to the appearance, nor that of predicative synthesis. It is a determinability in further perceptions. The same lectures state the law of this range. Infinitely many appearances are possible, but not every appearance is possible: "The unity of a possible perceptual synthesis in general, into which the appearance in question is to fall, prescribes rule and law to the appearances possible in such a unity" (Hua XVI, 130).

The section in which this sentence stands is headed "Direction of interest and concept-formation", and its example is the concept "flower". What the concept ultimately means, Husserl says there, does not refer to a single phase of appearance. It refers to "a distinguished circle of appearances, which is itself variable within a sphere of irrelevance" (Hua XVI, 130). The lectures draw two conclusions that Heidegger does not draw. The first concerns the limit of givenness. Since the rule always opens further possibilities of determination, no appearance and no finite series of appearances gives the thing adequately, and the perception of a thing is, in the title of the summarizing section, "a process that in principle cannot be concluded" (Hua XVI, 135). The second concerns the privileged appearance. That there are best views of a thing belongs, Husserl decides, "not to the proper essence of the appearance as such but to the interest that governs it" (Hua XVI, 135). The rule is a priori, and the circle that an empirical concept distinguishes within it depends on an interest.

*Ideas I* gives the doctrine its published form and its Kantian name. Complete givenness of a thing is impossible in any closed consciousness, and yet it is pre-delineated "as 'Idea' (in the Kantian sense)", as a system of endless processes of continuous appearing that is absolutely determined in its essential type (*Ideas I* § 143; Hua III/1, 331). Where the giving intuition is a transcending one, the object cannot come to adequate givenness; what can be given is only the idea of such an object, "and with it an a priori rule for the lawful infinities of inadequate experiences" (§ 144; Hua III/1, 332). From this rule one cannot read off how the further course of experience must run. "On the contrary, infinitely many possibilities remain open, which are however pre-formed as to their type by the very rich a priori regulation" (§ 144; Hua III/1, 332). Husserl's example is geometry, whose system of rules fixes all the possible shapes of motion that could complete the stretch of motion observed here and now, and "singles out not a single actual course of motion" of the thing that actually moves (Hua III/1, 333).

One more distinction of *Ideas I* belongs here, because it concerns the very phrase on which Heidegger's § 21 turns. The geometer, Husserl says, is not interested in the factual shapes given to the senses. Geometrical concepts are ideal concepts and express "something that one cannot 'see'". The ideation that yields them gives ideal limits which are in principle not to be found in any sensuous intuition, and which the vague, morphological essences of description "approach" more or less "without ever reaching them" (§ 74; Hua III/1, 155–56). Husserl thus has a non-attainment of his own. He uses it to separate two kinds of concept with two origins, the exact and the descriptive, and he insists that neither can stand in for the other. Heidegger's non-attainment is one relation, the same for the dog and for the triangle, differing only in the width of the compass.

We can now say what Husserl's analyses find where Kant put the schema. They find a rule that prescribes to possible appearances, a bounded range that the rule draws, a single appearance that matters less than the range, and an a priori that leaves the actual course open. The index calls this the deepest systematic affinity between the two authors, and the passages bear it out. Husserl does not call the rule a schema, and he does not call it a concept. The first omission is terminological. The second is his position, and the confrontation will turn on it. Before that, the word "image" has to be cleared, since it is with this word that the received view decides the matter.

## 5. Image-consciousness and the intuition of time: Husserl's two refusals

Husserl refuses the image twice, once for perception and once for time. The first refusal is prepared in an appendix to the Fifth *Logical Investigation*. The image-theory holds that the thing is outside and that an image in consciousness does duty for it. Husserl objects that to be an image is no real predicate of the object that functions as one: "Resemblance between two objects, however precise, does not make the one be an image of the other." Only the power of a presenting ego to use one similar thing as the representative of another "makes the image *be* an image" (LI V, appendix to §§ 11 and 20; Findlay). An image is an image for a consciousness that takes it as one, and this consciousness cannot in turn be explained by an image.

*Ideas I* applies the result to the transcendence of the thing. One is misled, Husserl writes, by the thought that the transcendence of the thing is that of an image or a sign. The image-theory is often combated and a sign-theory put in its place, but both are "not merely incorrect but countersensical". The decisive sentences are these: "The spatial thing that we see is, with all its transcendence, something perceived, given to consciousness in its bodily presence. An image or a sign is *not* given in its place. One should not slip a consciousness of signs or images underneath perceiving" (*Ideas I* § 43; Hua III/1, 89–90). Between perception and presentation by image or sign there is, Husserl says, an unbridgeable difference of essence. In the latter we intuit one thing in the consciousness that it depicts or indicates another. We have the one in the field of intuition and are directed through it at the other.

What an image is for Husserl can be read from his example of the engraving by Dürer. He distinguishes the perception of the sheet of paper in the portfolio; the perceptual consciousness in which small grey figures appear in the black lines; and the depicted realities, the knight of flesh and blood, to which we are turned in looking at the picture. The consciousness of the image-object, the little figures in which something else is depicted through resemblance, is a modified perception. The image-object "stands before us neither as being nor as not being" (§ 111; Hua III/1, 252). Images can be nested, as in the painting of a gallery remembered from the Dresden gallery, and then an image is characterized in itself as the image of an image (§§ 100–101; Hua III/1, 236). In every case the word names what Heidegger calls a likeness: something that appears and through which something else is meant.

The second refusal concerns the past. In the lectures on time Husserl denies that the just-past phase of a tone is present in retention as a weaker tone that would depict the earlier one. A present tone can remind us of a past one and can depict it, but that already presupposes another presentation of the past. "The intuition of the past itself cannot be a rendering in images (*Verbildlichung*). It is an originary consciousness" (*Time-Consciousness* § 12; Hua X, 32). Where Husserl does speak of an image of time, in a supplementary text on the transfer of spatial concepts to temporality, he means the conception of time "in the image of a line", and he classes it as such a rendering (Hua X, Text No. 37).

Do these two refusals reach Heidegger's image? The index answers that they do. It calls § 43 of *Ideas I* the most direct collision with Heidegger's § 20, and it sets the sentence on retention against the sentence that time is the schema-image. In a certain way one must agree. Had Heidegger's Kant held that the thing is given through a likeness, or that the past is given through a present content that resembles it, Husserl's argument would be fatal. However, this is not what the three senses of "image" say. The first sense is the look that a being itself offers when it is manifest as present, and this is what Husserl calls the thing given in its bodily presence. The second sense, the likeness, is derived from the first and remains its transcription. Heidegger's order of the first two senses is Husserl's order of perception and image-consciousness, and Heidegger does not slip an image-consciousness underneath perceiving any more than Husserl does.

The same holds for the two images that carry the schematism. The schema-image is expressly "neither a simple look nor a likeness", and a concept "cannot be depicted". Heidegger even gives Husserl's reason for this in his own terms. The schema-image has its character as a look "not only and not first" from the content that can be seen in it, but from the way it springs out of the possible presentation whose rule is presented (GA 3, 99). The five points are an image of five through the presenting that takes them as a possible case of the rule, and resemblance has no part in it, since the number never looks like five points. This is the thesis of Husserl's appendix moved from the likeness to the example. As for time, the pure image is a look given prior to all experience and not a picture of anything, and what Kant rules out for the pure concepts is, in Heidegger's word, their *Verbildlichung*. Both authors, then, use this word for what they exclude. The consequence that there is no collision over the image is mine; neither author draws it, and Heidegger's sections do not mention Husserl. It removes the dispute from the place where it is usually put. It does not remove the dispute.

## 6. Look and rule: the confrontation

The parallels can now be stated, and they are closer than the absence of a common vocabulary suggests. Both authors deny that the given particular is what is meant when it is seen as a house or a flower. Both describe what is meant as a bounded range, Heidegger as a "compass of possible looking", Husserl as a "circle of appearances" or a "firmly delimited general sphere". Both attribute the bounds to a rule, and both use the verb "to pre-delineate" (*vorzeichnen*) for what the rule does. Both make the particularity of the particular indifferent: the factual look "interests us as little" in Heidegger, and the circle varies "within a sphere of irrelevance" in Husserl. Both refuse to equate the rule with a list of marks or with the applicability of general words. Both hold that no single appearance attains what the rule prescribes. And both begin from finitude. Husserl derives symbolic presentation from "the finitude of human nature" and contrasts it with an infinite understanding; Heidegger derives sensibilization from the finitude of a being that must receive.

Naturally there are also weighty differences, and I take three in turn. The first concerns what finitude makes necessary. For Husserl it makes signs necessary. Beyond the smallest multiplicities we cannot actually collect what we mean, and a sign or a figural moment stands in for the collection we cannot carry out. An actual look remains the measure, and an enlarged capacity that could collect a thousand members is conceivable. For Heidegger finitude makes the look itself necessary, and no enlargement would change this, because the look in question was never a matter of how much can be surveyed. Surveyability is for him of no account. For Husserl it marks the line between what we authentically have and what we have only through a surrogate.

The second difference concerns what the non-attainment shows. Husserl takes it as a thesis about givenness: the thing is never adequately given, its complete givenness is an Idea, and the exact concept names a limit that the seen shape approaches. Heidegger takes it as a thesis about the concept: the look does not attain the concept because the concept is the rule of possible looks and nothing besides. It would be too simple to say that the one finds a defect where the other finds a positive structure. Husserl's inadequacy is no shortcoming of human perception. It belongs to the essence of the thing, and the Idea that regulates it is as positive as Heidegger's rule. The two authors use one finding as the premise of two different conclusions, one about how far givenness reaches and one about what a concept is.

The third difference concerns what the rule is a rule of. Husserl's rule governs the further appearances of one thing that is already apprehended as a thing. It says how this same object can go on showing itself, and its end, never reached, is the object's complete determination. Heidegger's rule governs the possibility that anything shows itself as a house at all, and in the pure case as an object at all. Its terms are the "standing-against" of objectivity and the horizon in which objects can be encountered. In Husserl the rule runs along the series of appearances, and in Heidegger it runs ahead of the first of them. This description of the difference is not found as such in either text; I take it over, with a change of wording, from the index, which speaks of a horizontal and a vertical rule.

Here lies the root of the differences, and it is an order of founding. For Husserl the straightforward perception of the thing comes first. It needs no apparatus of founding acts, and whatever is general in it, the pre-delineated sphere within which the unseen side is determinable, belongs to the sense of the apprehension and is expressly distinguished from conceptual determination. Concepts arise in founded acts, with an interest directing their formation, and what they mean is an identical something, a number or a species, which is not the procedure by which an instance is produced. For Heidegger the order is reversed in the last lines of § 21. Finite knowing is thinking intuition and therefore conceptual throughout; the immediate perception of this house already contains the schematizing preview of house in general; and the concept is nothing beyond the presenting of the rule. Husserl's two sentences of 1887 state the same alternative from the other side. Number and the presentation of counting are not the same, he says, and he means the remark as a reduction to absurdity of Kant's second definition. Heidegger asserts the identity that Husserl finds absurd, for every concept, as the meaning of that definition. I do not claim that Heidegger's sections are an answer to Husserl's objection. They are a reading of the chapter on which the objection would no longer count as one.

Is this to say that the two orders are simply opposed and that nothing can be decided between them? Each side has something to ask of the other, and the questions are not of equal weight on every point. From Husserl's side three things are missing in Heidegger's five sections. There is no account of how a rule is acquired, confirmed or corrected; the schematizing preview never fails, whereas Husserl's perceptual sense can be disappointed and revised in the further course of experience. There is nothing that selects the relevant range, where Husserl has the interest that governs the formation of an empirical concept. And the house, which carries the analysis of §§ 20 and 21, is left behind when the pure concepts are reached, so that the reader is not told whether the concept of a house is formed in a schematism in the same sense as the category of substance. Husserl's third objection of 1887 also remains without reply in these sections. A rule of the possible presentability of the number five does not yet say why this multiplicity has this number and always the same.

From Heidegger's side the question to Husserl is what makes the thing a thing in the first place. The rule of the lectures of 1907 presupposes the apprehension under which what appears already counts as a spatial thing with some shape and some colour. Husserl says that "one glance at the thing, and it stands there as a thing" (Hua XVI, 59), and he says that the pre-delineation involved is not a predicative one. He does not say in these texts what it is, if it is general and yet no concept. One receives the impression that two answers are available to him. On the one hand perception is straightforward and founds everything general. On the other hand its sense prescribes rule and law to all possible appearances and pre-forms their type a priori. The texts read here hold both and do not relate them, and the passages in which Husserl relates the pre-delineation of a horizon to passive synthesis lie outside what I have read. Heidegger's thesis that all perception is schematizing can be understood as the decision to call this prescribing sense a concept. Whether that decision explains the sense or only names it is a question his sections do not settle either.

The disagreement over time has the same form and can now be put more exactly than the opposition of "image" and "no image" allows. Heidegger's pure image is the look of abiding that the sequence of nows gives because in every now it is now, and it is this look that lets something unchangeable show itself in experience. Husserl agrees that time and objectivity belong together. Objective time, he writes in a supplementary text, "is a form of 'persisting' objects", and persistence "is a unity that constitutes itself in the flow" (Hua X, 113). In another he calls the time-form "only a categorial form", a form of individual objects, and time-consciousness an objectivating consciousness (Hua X, Text No. 44). But he places something beneath this form. It belongs to the essence of the flow that there can be no persistence in it, and "in the original flow there is no duration" (Hua X, 113). In Husserl's order, therefore, the look of abiding belongs to constituted, objective time, and what constitutes it offers no such look. Heidegger marks the time of his § 22 as Kant's and says that his interpretation does not reach the more original structures. Both authors thus point beneath the sequence of nows. Whether they point to the same thing cannot be decided from five sections of the Kant book and a handful of Husserl's time-texts, and I do not decide it.

What the received view took for a dispute about images has turned out to be a dispute about whether a look can be had before its rule. On the image the two authors are nearer to each other than either is to the theories they both reject: a likeness presupposes the direct look, a general presentation is no faint picture, and something is an image through the way it is taken and never through resemblance alone. On the rule they describe one structure in two vocabularies. They part where Husserl holds that the house actually looked at is the standard by which every other presentation of it is measured, and Heidegger holds that this house shows itself as a house only from a preview of house in general. The sharpest form of the result is that Husserl's sentence of 1887 and Heidegger's sentence of 1929 are one sentence with opposite signs. Number is not the presentation of counting; the concept is nothing beyond the presentation of the rule. I have not shown which of them is right, and the texts read here do not allow it. They do allow one credit to the author who wrote first. The criticism that the schematism is deduced and not exhibited is usually held against Husserl as a failure to see what Kant had found. Yet it was Husserl who, without the name and from below, exhibited in the perception of a thing the rule that pre-forms a range of possible looks, and who thereby supplied the phenomenon on which Heidegger's defence of the chapter depends.

# What Transcendence Surpasses

## Husserl and Heidegger, 1907–1936

It is generally accepted that Heidegger reverses the reference of the word "transcendence". For Husserl, on this view, the transcendent is the object, the thing that is not a part of the experience in which it is perceived. For Heidegger the transcendent is the one who perceives: "Objects and things are never transcendent," as the lecture course of 1927 puts it. The received view then draws one of two conclusions. Either the two authors share a word and no concept, so that there is nothing to compare, or their concepts are mirror images, immanence grounding transcendence in the one and transcendence grounding immanence in the other. Both conclusions leave the same facts unexplained. Husserl himself assigns transcendence to the pure I in 1913, to the stream of consciousness in the lectures of the 1920s, and in 1931 to an ego that "transcends its own being". He calls the transcendence of one's own conscious past a "primal transcendence" (*Urtranszendenz*), and this is the compound Heidegger uses in 1928 for being-in-the-world. Heidegger, for his part, says in 1927 that intentionality is that in which transcendence consists, and as late as 1929 he still defines transcendence as the essence of the subject.[^1]

In what follows I wish to show that the two concepts are divided neither by the side of the subject–object relation on which the word is placed nor by an order of founding, but by what the transcending is toward. In Husserl, every transcendence, whether of the thing, of the I, of the stream or of the other, is the transcendence of something identical that lies beyond the experience in which it is given, and its measure is possible confirmation. In Heidegger, from 1928 on, that toward which Dasein transcends is world, which is no being at all, and the measure of transcendence is freedom. I also wish to show that each author's development moves toward the other's point of departure without arriving there. Husserl's word migrates from the thing to the stream of consciousness. Heidegger's concept withdraws from intentional comportment to a constitution that precedes all comportment.

The comparison has a measure because both authors ask one question. Both reject, in nearly the same words, the question of how a subject gets out to an object. Husserl calls it absurd to ask "How do I get out of my island of consciousness?" *(my translation)* (*Cartesian Meditations* § 41; Hua I, 117). Heidegger writes that "the 'problem of transcendence' cannot be reduced to the question how a subject gets outside to an object" (*Being and Time* § 69c; SZ 366). The question they share is therefore this: once the question of the way out has been refused, what does "transcendence" still name? What is surpassed, and toward what?

The article rests on a closed body of texts: the passages listed in one topic index of a working archive, each opened and read in the archive's files. On Husserl's side these are *The Idea of Phenomenology* (1907), *Ideas I* (1913), the lectures on internal time-consciousness in the edition Heidegger published in 1928, the lectures on passive synthesis (1920–26), one supplement to the psychology lectures of 1925, the Paris Lectures (1929), the *Cartesian Meditations* (1931) and the *Crisis* (1936). On Heidegger's side they are the lecture courses of 1923/24, 1925, 1927, 1928 and 1928/29, *Being and Time* (1927), *On the Essence of Ground* (1929), and the Kant book of 1929 with the Davos disputation. The dates are the index's. I leave aside what the index has not mined: the *Logical Investigations* and everything of Husserl's before 1907, *Ideas II*, *First Philosophy II*, the three volumes on intersubjectivity, Heidegger's courses before 1923 and his writings after 1929, and Husserl's marginal notes on Heidegger. This restriction needs a word of explanation. A claim about how a concept develops is only as good as the series of texts it rests on, and I prefer a short series that I have read to a long one that I remember. Where the index is silent I therefore claim no absence. Sartre, Marion and Henry, whom the index names as third positions without indexing them, do not appear below.

## 1. The transcendent as what is not really contained: Husserl, 1907–1913

Husserl begins from the problem that both authors will end by refusing. In the lectures of 1907 transcendence is "the riddle", the "initial and guiding problem of the critique of knowledge" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 36). But the same lectures already separate two things under the word. Transcendence can mean, first, that the object of knowledge is not "really contained" (*reell enthalten*) in the act of knowing as one of its parts, and then the question is: "how can the experience get beyond itself, so to speak?" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 35). It can mean, second, whatever goes beyond absolute and evident self-givenness. Husserl says where the distinction comes from: the two senses run together "before the critical reflection has gone deeper", and to treat only the really contained as unproblematically given is "a fateful error" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 36).

The picture of an inside and an outside is thereby assigned to a stage that the lectures leave behind. In the summary Husserl wrote of their train of thought, it is "the beginner" who says that the immanent is "in me" and the transcendent "outside me" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 5). What is unquestionable about the really immanent is described in a phrase that will matter for everything that follows: it is unquestionable because it "means nothing beyond itself" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 5). Conversely, then, there is transcendence wherever an experience means something beyond itself. In 1907 this is still a problem of knowledge. A supplement to the lectures states the premise in one sentence: "How immanence can be known is intelligible; how transcendence can be known is unintelligible" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 84).

The same lectures show that the premise will not hold. Husserl's example is a tone that lasts. After the reduction the tone seems a simple given, yet the *cogitationes* "harbour all sorts of transcendences" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 11). When I hear the tone, the phase now sounding is really there in the experience. The phases that have just sounded are no longer there, and still they belong to what I hear, since what I hear is one tone with its duration. The past phases, Husserl writes, "are still objective now and yet not really contained in the appearance" *(my translation)* (Hua II, 11). Hence a distinction between appearance and what appears has to be drawn "within immanence" itself. We shall return to this tone more than once, because Husserl does, and because Heidegger later holds it against him. For the moment one result is enough. As early as 1907, and by way of time, the transcendent is found inside the sphere that was to be free of it, and what transcends there is the unity of one tone across its phases.

*Ideas I* turns the riddle into a doctrine of two kinds of being. The physical thing is transcendent to the perception of it, and "not merely in the sense that the physical thing cannot be found in fact as a really inherent component of consciousness" (*Ideas I* § 42; Kersten, 89–90; Hua III/1, 87).[^2] The point is one of essence: a thing can be given only through adumbration (*Abschattung*), an experience cannot be so given, and the thing is therefore "unqualifiedly transcendent" (§ 42; Kersten, 91). What Husserl means by the thing in these sections is stated with care. It is "the intentional unity", the thing "intended to as identical and unitary" in the flow of perceptual multiplicities (§ 41; Kersten, 88–89), and the spatial thing "is nothing other than an intentional unity which of essential necessity can be given only as the unity of such modes of appearance" (§ 42; Kersten, 93). Transcendence in *Ideas I* is thus a predicate of what is one and the same over against the many experiences in which it appears.

Two consequences follow in the text, and they pull in different directions. On the one hand, Husserl refuses every theory that would put a picture or a sign between perception and the thing. To suppose that perception "does not reach the physical thing itself" is "fundamentally erroneous", and the view that only a divine intuition would have the thing itself "is a countersense" (§ 43; Kersten, 92–93). On the other hand, the difference in modes of givenness becomes a separation in being. Consciousness is a nexus "out of which nothing can slip", and the world is a being posited by consciousness that can be determined "only as something identical" belonging to motivated multiplicities of appearances: "beyond that it is nothing" (§ 49; Kersten, 112–13; Hua III/1, 105). This is the section in which Husserl writes that an annihilation of the world of things would modify consciousness without touching its existence, and that between consciousness and reality there yawns an abyss of sense. It should be noticed that the closure is expressly not spatial. Husserl denies in the same sentence that anything is external to consciousness in space and time (§ 49; Kersten, 112–13). The inside of 1913 is a self-sufficiency of sense and of being, which is no longer the beginner's inside.

It is against this background that *Ideas I* gives the word to the subject side for the first time in the texts read here. After the reduction the pure I is nowhere to be found in the flux of experiences as one experience among others. It is something identical through every actual and possible change of experiences, and for that reason it cannot count as "a really inherent part or moment" of them (§ 57; Kersten, 132–33; Hua III/1, 123). If it remains as a residuum, Husserl concludes, it presents "a transcendency of a peculiar kind", one which "is not constituted", "a transcendency within immanency" (§ 57; Kersten, 133; Hua III/1, 124). The next section adds a third member: the transcendence of God, which stands "in polar contrast to the transcendency pertaining to the world" (§ 58; Kersten, 133–34) and is bracketed.

What does this first subject-side transcendence show? The index reads § 57 as the first of three places where Husserl puts transcendence "at the heart of the subject". That is right as far as the location goes. But the reason Husserl gives for calling the I transcendent is the reason he gave for the thing. The I is identical, the experiences are many, and the I is not really contained in any of them. Husserl has not reached a different concept of transcendence here. He applies the one concept of 1907 to a new instance, and the instance embarrasses the division of being he has just drawn, since something not really contained now belongs on the side of consciousness. He registers the embarrassment by a hyphenated qualification, "not constituted", and by postponing the matter to the second book. *Ideas I* itself leaves no doubt which transcendence comes first. Consciousness enters the real world, Husserl says, only by a certain participation "in transcendence in the first, the originary sense; and this is obviously the transcendence belonging to Nature" (§ 53; Kersten, 124–25; Hua III/1, 117). In 1913, then, the transcendence of the thing is first, and that of the I is a peculiar exception. The next section must ask whether this order survives.

## 2. The first transcendent displaced: Husserl, 1920–1936

It does not survive, and the text that displaces it is again a text about time. The lectures on passive synthesis first restate the transcendence of the thing as an excess of claim over performance. No appearance gives the object completely; each carries a *plus ultra* in its empty horizon, so that perception "in fact constantly claims more than it can achieve by its own essence" *(my translation)* (Hua XI, 11). The lectures then put a question that *Ideas I* had not put: what makes something an object for me at all? Husserl answers that an immanent perception constitutes "a self" (*ein Selbst*), but that this is something identical for the I only by virtue of possible recollections. "A self, an object", he says, is there only for the active I, as something "abidingly available, identifiable again and again" *(my translation)* (Hua XI, 203).

The word "self" needs fixing here, because English invites a misreading. In these lectures *Selbst* does not name the ego's selfhood. It names what is given when something is itself given: the identical item that can be returned to and confirmed. Husserl speaks in the same pages of the "self" of a house or a symphony. I shall therefore write "a self" where Husserl's sense is meant, and "selfhood" for Heidegger's *Selbstheit*, which we shall meet below.

With this sense in hand Husserl draws a consequence that he himself calls remarkable. Recollections occur in the immanence of the present, but what they bring back, the past, is transcendent to the present experience and to everything constituted in the present. The lecture goes on: "every self is transcendent in an original and good sense—which admittedly yields a curious paradox. The first transcendent, the one at the primal source, is the stream of consciousness and its immanent time" *(my translation)* (Hua XI, 204–5). Two further sentences complete the reordering. The stream "lives in streaming and at the same time becomes objective for its I". And what holds of this "primal transcendence of recollection" holds also for transcendence in the usual sense: "This transcendence of the spatial world is a transcendence of the second level" *(my translation)* (Hua XI, 205).

Set beside *Ideas I* § 53, these sentences date a shift. In 1913 the transcendence of nature is transcendence "in the first, the originary sense". In the lectures of 1920–26 the first transcendent is my own conscious past, and the world is transcendent at the second level. The tone of 1907 has become the model: what was there a surprise inside immanence is now the origin of all transcendence. Yet the concept has not changed. The stream is the first transcendent because it is the first identical something that outlasts the experience in which it is given and can be confirmed in others. Husserl says so in terms of a goal. To the stream there corresponds "the idea of a true self, of the true conscious past, as the idea of complete self-giving" *(my translation)*, approached without end by clearer and wider recollection (Hua XI, 205). The index calls this passage Husserl's strongest subject-side transcendence, and it does not notice that the passage contains Heidegger's word. On the first point I shall have to narrow the index's reading. The stream is transcendent here as an object for its I, and Husserl calls the result a paradox because the stream is also what the I lives. Of this second aspect the passage says only that the stream "lives in streaming".

Husserl had already said something about that second aspect, and Heidegger was the one who put it into print. The time lectures as Heidegger edited them in 1928 contain a section, resting according to the editor of the critical edition on a note written probably not before the end of 1911, in which the time-constituting flow is denied the predicates of what it constitutes. The flow "is not 'something in objective time.' It is absolute subjectivity", and Husserl adds: "For all of this, we lack names" (*Time-Consciousness* § 36; Brough, Hua X [75]). A later section admits how "shocking (when not initially even absurd)" it seems that the flow should constitute its own unity (§ 39; Brough, Hua X [80]). The texts thus contain two answers to the question of what the stream is. It is the first transcendent, an identical self for recollection. It is also a flow that is no object in time and for which the vocabulary of objects fails. Husserl does not unite the two answers in the passages read here, and I do not supply the unity. What can be said is that the word "transcendence" goes, in these texts, only to the first.

A supplement to the psychology lectures of 1925 gives the concept its plainest statement and its only verbal form in the texts read. Every perception points beyond itself, Husserl writes, and "transcending" then means being referred from the perception to ever new perceptions which "agree in the same object" and would confirm it *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 430). The true object does not lie "in a mystical outside and in-itself"; it lies in the experiences "as what is identically and persistently meant" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 431). The same page states the price. An absolute confirmation is "not only in fact but a priori an impossibility", since every confirmation has "an infinite horizon of possible further confirmation" *(my translation)* (Hua IX, 431). The date of this supplement is uncertain, and I build nothing on its position in the sequence. Its content agrees with the lectures on passive synthesis: to transcend is to be referred onward to further experience of the same.

The texts of 1929 and 1931 draw the systematic conclusion. In the Paris Lectures, and again in the *Cartesian Meditations*, the old riddle is declared absurd. Transcendence "in every form is an immanent character of being, constituting itself within the ego" *(my translation)* (*Cartesian Meditations* § 41; Hua I, 117; the Paris Lectures have the sentence without "in every form", Hua I, 33). Husserl now also says why the ego is called transcendental. The world is no really contained part of my conscious life, and "this transcendence of irreal inclusion" belongs to the sense of everything worldly; the I that bears this sense in itself is for that reason called transcendental *(my translation)* (§ 11; Hua I, 65). The term thus keeps the meaning of 1907 exactly, while its relation to immanence has been turned around. Not being really contained used to be the mark of what knowledge could not reach. It is now a sense that the ego constitutes.

The Fifth Meditation adds the last instance, and with it a third ordering of what is first. To experience another I, the ego must have intentionalities "with a sense of being through which it wholly transcends its own being" *(my translation)* (§ 48; Hua I, 135). Below this lies the primordial world, "in itself the first" transcendence, which is still a determining part of my own concrete being because it is the "synthetic unity of an infinite system of my potentialities"; the objective world is transcendent at a higher, founded level *(my translation)* (§ 48; Hua I, 136). In 1913 the first transcendence was nature, in the 1920s my conscious past, and in 1931 it is the primordial world. Husserl gives no account of how the second and third orderings fit together, and the texts read here do not settle it. The criterion again stays the same, for the primordial world is a synthetic unity over against a multiplicity, and the other is that unity's limit, a being whose own experiences can never be really contained in mine.

One late text goes a step further, and it is the one that comes nearest to Heidegger. In the *Crisis* Husserl distinguishes the consciousness of things from the consciousness of world. Things are given as objects in the world-horizon. The world, however, "is not in being as a being is, as an object is, but is in a uniqueness for which the plural is senseless" *(my translation)* (*Crisis* § 37; Hua VI, 146). This was written, by the index's dating, eight years after Heidegger's lectures on transcendence, and it can only converge with them. It should also be read together with the sentences that lead up to it. The world-horizon, Husserl has just said, is conscious "only as horizon for existing objects" and cannot be actual without objects of which we are specially conscious *(my translation)* (Hua VI, 146). Husserl does not use the word "transcendence" for this horizon in the passage. With that observation the Husserlian series is complete, and the question for the next two sections is what Heidegger makes of the same word.

## 3. From target to concept: Heidegger, 1923–1927

The index treats Heidegger's concept as a product of the years 1927 to 1929, and the texts bear this out. In the course of 1923/24 the word does not occur as a term; the one relevant passage defends Husserl against the charge of an "act-phenomenology", on the ground that phenomenological reflection concerns "the way of comporting oneself to the objective world" *(my translation)* (GA 17, 262). Whether the courses before 1923 hold more I do not know, since they were not searched.

In the course of 1925 the word belongs to Heidegger's exposition of *Ideas I*. He reports Husserl's position faithfully: consciousness is one with nature in the concrete living being and yet "separated from it by an absolute gulf, as every perception of a thing shows in the distinction of immanence and transcendence" (GA 20 § 10; Kisiel; GA 20, 134). He then puts the question that the later concept will answer by dissolving its premise: "How is it possible that lived experiences constitute an absolute and pure region of being and at the same time occur in the transcendence of the world?" (GA 20 § 10; Kisiel; GA 20, 139). Two concessions made in the same course deserve to be kept in view. Heidegger reads Husserl's term "constituting" without any suggestion of manufacture: it "does not mean producing in the sense of making and fabricating; it means *letting the entity be seen in its objectivity*" (GA 20 § 6; Kisiel; GA 20, 97). And he does not reject the so-called principle of immanence, that beings are only ever for a consciousness. He says what it registers: "The phenomenal finding in this proposition is rather that *a world is encountered*" (GA 20 § 24; Kisiel; GA 20, 298). In 1925, then, Heidegger has a target and a phenomenon, and no concept of transcendence of his own.

*Being and Time* announces the concept and defers it. The word stands at three joints of the book. At the first, being itself is called "the *transcendens* pure and simple", and the transcendence of Dasein's being is said to be a distinctive one (§ 7C; Welch; SZ 38).[^3] At the second, the thematizing of things by science is said to require that Dasein transcend the beings thematized: "Transcendence does not consist in objectivization; rather, it is presupposed by it" (§ 69b; Welch; SZ 363). At the third, the world receives the predicate: "Grounded in the horizonal unity of ecstatic temporality, world is transcendent" (§ 69c; Welch; SZ 366). The world so understood is "subjective", Heidegger adds, and yet "'more objective' than any possible 'object'" (SZ 366). The book also contains, in two clauses, a verdict on transcendental idealism that bears on Husserl without naming him. If idealism means that being can never be explained by beings and is for every being already what is "transcendental", then it holds the only possibility of a philosophical problematic. If it means the reduction of all beings to a subject or consciousness whose own being remains "indeterminate", then it is "no less naïve than the most crudely hewn realism" (§ 43a; Welch; SZ 208). The second clause states in advance the limit that the lectures of 1928 will assign to Husserl by name.

What the book does not do is determine the relation of this transcendence to intentionality. It promises to. A footnote, the only place in the book where the index finds the word, credits Husserl's intentional analysis of perception with having suggested the temporal character of the phenomenon, and continues: "How the intentionality of 'consciousness' is *grounded* in the ecstatic temporality of being-there will be shown in the following Division" (SZ 363 n.; Welch). The division did not appear.

The lecture course of the summer of 1927 contains two statements that are hard to hold together, and the difficulty is instructive. In its first part, intentionality and transcendence are all but identified. Against the question of how an immanent experience acquires transcendent validity, Heidegger says that "it is precisely intentionality and nothing else in which *transcendence* consists" (GA 24 § 9b; Hofstadter; GA 24, 89). The summary formula follows two pages later: "Intentionality is the ratio cognoscendi of transcendence. Transcendence is the ratio essendi of intentionality in its diverse modes" (GA 24, 91). In the second part of the same course the reference of the word is reversed in so many words: "The Dasein is the transcendent being. Objects and things are never transcendent" (GA 24 § 20e; Hofstadter; GA 24, 425–26). Transcendence is now the "overstepping" that belongs to Dasein as being-in-the-world, and it "is founded" on "the original ecstatic-horizonal unity of temporality" (GA 24, 429).

Does the course of 1927 then simply identify transcendence with intentional comportment, as the index's reading of § 9b suggests? In a certain way one must answer yes, since the sentence about what transcendence "consists" in says so. However, the paragraph that contains the formula already says more. The being that exists as intentional is said to exist so "on the basis of transcendence", and the next paragraph calls intentionality "the essential though not the most original structure of the subject itself" (GA 24, 91–92). The identification and the relation of grounding stand side by side in 1927, on the same pages. I conclude that the course uses one word for two things without yet having names for the difference, and the index says as much. The names come a year later.

## 4. The doctrine of 1928–29 and its retraction

The lecture course of the summer of 1928 supplies them. Intentionality, Heidegger now says, is related to beings themselves "and in this sense an ontically transcending comportment", but it does not itself originally constitute this relation *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 9b, 168). The decisive sentences follow: "The problem of transcendence as such is not identical with the problem of intentionality. As ontic transcendence, intentionality is itself possible only on the ground of original transcendence: being-in-the-world. This primal transcendence makes possible every intentional relation to beings" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 170). Husserl is named in the same pages, with credit and with a limit. He freed intentionality from Brentano's psychology and showed that it determines the essence of consciousness as such. But with his doctrine of the immanent intentionality of the *cogitationes* the question of the being that is constituted as consciousness "is not pursued further" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 167).

Heidegger then clears the ground by a numbered distinction of his own. The philosophical tradition has two concepts of the transcendent. The epistemological concept opposes it to the immanent and rests on a picture of the subject "as a capsule, with an inside, a capsule wall and an outside" *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 11a, 205). Heidegger grants at once that nobody holds the crude opinion that consciousness really is a capsule. What matters in the analogy is only that a barrier between inside and outside has to be crossed. The theological concept opposes the transcendent to the contingent: it is the unconditioned that lies beyond all conditioned beings. The two concepts couple, Heidegger observes, so that the problem of the external world and the problem of the knowledge of God are entangled (GA 26, 206). Against both he sets four determinations, of which three carry the doctrine. Transcendence is "the original constitution of the subjectivity of a subject", so that "to exist originally means to step over" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 211). What is stepped over is not a barrier but "beings themselves", including the nature in whose midst Dasein finds itself through its body (GA 26, 212). And that toward which the subject transcends "is not an object, and not this or that being at all"; it "is what we call world" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 212).

The third determination is the one on which this article turns, and Heidegger develops it in two steps. The first is freedom. World has the basic character of the for-the-sake-of (*Umwillen*), a for-the-sake-of is possible only where there is will, and so "the transcendence of Dasein and freedom are identical" *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 11b, 237–38). World is the whole of the possibilities that a free being holds before itself, and as such it "surpasses everything actually in being": every being, however it is met, shows itself as a restriction, as one possible realization of the possible *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 11c, 248). The second step is the nothing. When Dasein exists, beings have the occasion of what Heidegger calls entry into world (*Welteingang*), and in this entry nothing happens to them. Is the world itself then nothing? "The world: a nothing, no being—and yet something; nothing that is in being—but being" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 252). It is, in the title phrase of the following section, a *nihil originarium*, the nothing that temporalizes itself with temporality (GA 26 § 12, 272). For the "inner possibility of transcendence, so I claim, is time as original temporality" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 252).

Two qualifications in the course keep this doctrine from being read as an idealism of world-making, and both deserve notice because they answer worries a reader of Husserl would have. The first is that "being-in-the-world" is a statement of essence. It does not say that Dasein in fact exists, and Heidegger compares it to the statement that a body is extended, which holds whether or not this or that body is on hand (GA 26 § 11a, 217). He thus keeps, for his basic term, the distinction between essence and fact on which Husserl's own method rests. The second is that entry into world adds nothing to beings. What is on hand is what and how it is "even if it does not become something within the world"; being within the world is only the condition under which it can show itself as it is in itself *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 11c, 251). Where *Ideas I* § 49 has the world depend on consciousness for its sense, Heidegger has world depend on Dasein and beings on neither.

The same course settles the relation to intentionality on its own terms and says how its author wishes to be read. The intentional relation "is only the particular factical way of appropriating what, on the ground of transcendence, has already been leapt over, and that means unveiled" *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 12, 253). Even the formula that Dasein is "always already outside" is now called crooked, because "the 'already outside' presupposes that it was at some time inside" *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 11a, 214). Heidegger insists that transcendence "is nowhere equated or identified with intentionality" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 215), and he reports, in the one first-hand notice of Husserl's reaction that the texts read here contain, that neither Bergson nor Dilthey nor Husserl sees the phenomenon, and that Husserl "two years ago vehemently opposed it from the outset" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 214–15). The report is Heidegger's. Husserl's side of it is not among the texts.

The treatise that Heidegger published in 1929, in the volume honouring Husserl's seventieth birthday, states the doctrine in fewer words and changes it on four points. First, the relation to intentionality is fixed in print: if all comportment toward beings is called intentional, then intentionality "is possible only on the ground of transcendence, but is neither identical with it nor, conversely, itself what makes transcendence possible" *(my translation)* (GA 9, 135). To equate comportment with transcendence is "a misrecognition of the essence of transcendence" *(my translation)* (GA 9, 168). The sentence of 1927 about what transcendence "consists" in is thereby withdrawn, without being mentioned. Second, transcendence is expressly placed before all comportment, as "the basic constitution of this being, occurring before all comportment", and it reaches the one who transcends: what is surpassed is "solely beings themselves", and so "also and precisely the being as which 'it itself' exists". "Transcendence constitutes selfhood" *(my translation)* (GA 9, 137–38).

Third, the treatise adds a counterweight to the surplus of 1928. Transcendence grounds in two ways at once. It projects world, and it has always already taken ground in the midst of beings by which Dasein is attuned and taken in. Neither of these is "a comportment toward beings"; together they are "the transcendental enabling of intentionality" *(my translation)* (GA 9, 168). Because Dasein finds itself amid beings, certain possibilities are withdrawn from it, and only this withdrawal makes the remaining ones binding. Transcendence is therefore "at once over-swinging and withdrawing", and this is "a transcendental document of the finitude of Dasein's freedom" *(my translation)* (GA 9, 167). Fourth, the treatise is silent where the lectures spoke. Husserl's name does not occur in it, although the argument is the one that named him repeatedly in 1928. The index adds that the treatise omits the grounding in time. Here the text is a little less reticent than the index reports. A note says that the temporal interpretation of transcendence is set aside "throughout the present consideration, and deliberately", but the sentence to which the note is attached asserts that transcendence "is rooted in the essence of time, that is, in its ecstatic-horizonal constitution" *(my translation)* (GA 9, 166). In 1929 Heidegger still asserts the thesis and leaves out only its working out.

Two further texts of these years confirm the direction. The Freiburg course of 1928/29, which the archive holds only in English, describes transcendence as play: "'World' is the term for the play played by transcendence", and what is so characterized "is not our respective factical comportment" but "that which enables it" (GA 27 § 36).[^4] The Kant book of 1929 gives the doctrine in Kant's idiom and puts finitude first. A finite knower, who has not created the beings it knows, must let them stand over against it, and "to hold oneself in advance in such a play-space, to form it originally, is none other than the transcendence which marks all finite comportment to beings" (*Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* § 16; Taft, 50; GA 3, 71). What it lets stand against itself "cannot be a being. But if not a being, then just a nothing" (§ 16; Taft, 51; GA 3, 72). At Davos in the same year Heidegger calls the liberation of the inner transcendence of Dasein the basic character of philosophizing, and glosses it as becoming free "for the finitude of Dasein" *(my translation)* (GA 3, Davos disputation; pagination not located in the file).

Here the evidence stops, and its last layer has to be kept apart from the rest. The edition of the 1929 treatise that the archive holds prints, as lettered notes, remarks that Heidegger later wrote into his own copies of the first and second editions. They are undated in the file. They are not the doctrine of 1929 and I do not cite them for its content. For its history they say one thing clearly, namely that their author no longer stands behind the concept. One speaks of "the erroneous determination of the relation of 'distinction' and transcendence"; another answers the treatise's reading of Plato's "beyond" with "No! Da-sein not grasped at all"; the last says that freedom "has nothing in common with grounding and with ground" *(my translation)* (GA 9, marginal notes to 159, 160 and 175). How and when Heidegger gave up transcendence as a leading word is a question these notes raise and cannot answer. Any further statement about it is **[UNSUPPORTED — needs GA 65 ff.]**.

## 5. What the two concepts share

We have followed two series of texts. Before the differences are named, the agreements must be stated exactly, since they show that the two authors are speaking of the same matter. There are five.

The first is the refusal from which this article set out. Both authors reject the question of the way out, and both trace it to the same picture. Heidegger's "capsule" has a counterpart in Husserl's own vocabulary: the *Crisis* describes Descartes' problem of transcendence as the question of how *cogitationes* "in the encapsulated soul" could ground a being outside the soul *(my translation)* (*Crisis* § 22; Hua VI, 87). The index is therefore right to say that the capsule of 1928 does not describe Husserl's stated view from 1907 on, and Heidegger had said the same himself in 1923/24 and in 1925.

The second agreement is that neither author lets the object's transcendence be the first. Each finds a transcendence that precedes it and makes it possible, and each uses the same compound for it. For Husserl the "primal transcendence" is that of my conscious past for recollection, and the transcendence of the spatial world is of the second level. For Heidegger the "primal transcendence" is being-in-the-world, and intentionality is transcendence in a derived, ontic sense. The word is the same and the terms are two. I infer no influence from the coincidence. The lectures on passive synthesis were unpublished when Heidegger lectured in 1928, and nothing in the texts read here shows that either man knew the other's usage.

The third agreement is that both look for this prior transcendence in time. For Husserl the first transcendent arises in the "originally streaming present" *(my translation)* (Hua XI, 204–5). For Heidegger temporality is the inner possibility of transcendence. Both, moreover, deny that original time is something in time. Husserl's flow "is not 'something in objective time'". Heidegger's horizon of the ecstases "'is' not at all; it temporalizes itself" *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 12, 269). It is striking that Heidegger does not register the agreement. His one paragraph on the time lectures in 1928 credits Husserl with having been the first to see these phenomena with the help of the intentional structure, objects that time is still "taken as something immanent", and ends with a sentence that concedes more than the objection allows: "What Husserl still calls time-consciousness, that is, consciousness of time, is precisely, in the original sense, time itself" *(my translation)* (GA 26, 264). The words "retention" and "flow", in Husserl's sense, do not occur in the course. The index reports that Bernet made this observation about Heidegger's silence, and the file of the course confirms it.

The fourth agreement is a structural excess. For Husserl perception "constantly claims more than it can achieve". For Heidegger Dasein is "in itself in surplus", and every being shows itself as a restriction of the possible *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 11c, 249). In both, what is open exceeds what fills it.

The fifth is the status of world. In *Ideas I* § 27, as the index reports it, the world is already a horizon whose form alone is prefigured, and in 1936 Husserl says that the world is not in being as an object is. In 1928 Heidegger says that the world is no being. One can add Heidegger's own reading of 1925, on which the principle of immanence registers that a world is encountered. On each of these five points a reader who wished to minimize the opposition would find support, and would be right as far as the phenomena go.

## 6. The root of the difference

The strongest account of what nevertheless divides the two authors is the index's own, and it deserves to be stated at full strength. On this account the two concepts answer different questions, and they differ in which term is the ground. Husserl holds that transcendence is constituted in immanence. Heidegger holds that immanence is a derivative abstraction from transcendence. Every parallel is then a case of the same figure with its founding order reversed, and the index adds that the reversal is complete on freedom and finitude and only partial on time. This account has three merits. It respects the difference of reference, so that no Husserlian sentence about the transcendent thing is mistaken for a Heideggerian one. It takes its key term from Heidegger himself, who describes intentionality as "founded" in a prior being-alongside beings. And it agrees with what the index reports of Bernet's reading of 1990, that Heidegger always views the relation between Husserlian intentionality and his own transcendence as a relation of foundation. The texts of 1928 and 1929 confirm that report without exception.

The account is right about Heidegger's relation to intentionality. It is, however, not a sufficient account of the relation between the two concepts of transcendence, for two reasons that the texts supply. The first concerns Husserl. The formula "transcendence is constituted in immanence" is his, from 1929. But by his own analyses the immanence in question is not a ground free of transcendence. Since 1907 the *cogitationes* harbour transcendences, in 1913 the pure I is one, and in the 1920s the stream itself is the first transcendent. If immanence means what is really contained in the present experience, then almost nothing of consciousness is immanent, and Husserl draws exactly this conclusion when he calls the result a curious paradox. An order of founding in which the ground turns out to be the first case of the grounded cannot be the last word on his position.

The second reason concerns Heidegger. In the texts read, he does not say that transcendence grounds immanence. He says that it grounds intentionality, and he treats immanence as a counter-concept that falls together with the capsule. Nor does he give the word to the object side and take it from the subject. He keeps the subject, on condition: "If one chooses the title 'subject' for the being that we ourselves are", then transcendence is the essence of the subject, and whoever misses it has "only a rump subject, as it were" *(my translation)* (GA 9, 137–38). The mirror image of Husserl's formula is thus not to be found as such in Heidegger's text. It is a construction of the comparison, though one that Heidegger's talk of founding invites.

Where, then, does the difference lie? I locate it in the whither. The step is mine and neither author takes it, but each supplies half of it in so many words. For Husserl, to transcend is to mean beyond the present experience something that is one and the same. This holds of the tone across its phases, of the thing across its adumbrations, of the I across its acts, of the stream across its recollections, and of the primordial world as the synthetic unity of my potentialities. What is transcended toward is in every case an identical something, a self in the sense fixed above, and it is what it is as the goal of possible confirmation. For Heidegger, what Husserl calls the transcendent is what is surpassed. "The object, or the being that can have the character of something encountered, is what is stepped over, not the whither" *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 11a, 212). The whither is world, and world is no identical something. It is the whole of possibilities for the sake of which a free being exists.

This relocation lets each position be stated in terms the other could have accepted as a description. Put in Husserl's language, Heidegger claims that every consciousness of an identical object presupposes a horizon which is not the horizon of that object's further determinations and which is not itself meant in any act. It is the horizon of what I can be. It is not there to be confirmed, since it is held open by the one whose possibilities it contains. Husserl could have recognized the first half of this claim. His world-horizon of 1936 is not an object, and no single act intends it. He could not have recognized the second half, because for him a horizon is conscious "only as horizon for existing objects". Put in Heidegger's language, Husserl holds that all transcendence is ontic, including the transcendence that Dasein has toward itself. Even my own past is for him a being that I have at my disposal, "abidingly available", to be identified again. Heidegger could have recognized this as the phenomenon he calls coming back to beings, and he would have denied that it is first.

The other differences follow from this one, and I take them in order. If the whither is an identical something, then the excess of what is meant over what is given is a shortfall, and the measure of transcendence is fulfilment. The shortfall can never be made good, and so the complete givenness of the transcendent is an idea in the Kantian sense, as the index reports from *Ideas I* § 143. Husserl's transcendence is in this sense infinite. If the whither is the possible as such, then the excess is a surplus of what I can be over what is, and its measure is freedom. This surplus is not on the way to any completion. In the treatise of 1929 it has its limit in the withdrawal of possibilities, which belongs to transcendence itself. Heidegger's transcendence is in this sense finite. The index notes this contrast as one of meaning over fulfilment against possibility over actuality, and I take it over. What I add is its derivation from the difference of the whither.

It may be objected that freedom is not foreign to Husserl's account. The objection has a text on its side. In the lectures on passive synthesis the recollections by which a self is confirmed are called "a realm of freedom, of free producibility", and a self that has once been instituted is for the active I "a constant possible telos" *(my translation)* (Hua XI, 203). It is true, then, that Husserl's transcendence also involves a free I and a goal. However, the freedom in question is the freedom to go back to what is already there and to make it clearer. It serves the confirmation of an identical self. Heidegger's freedom projects that for the sake of which there are goals at all, and he denies that it is first a spontaneity or a power of beginning (GA 9, 164–65). The two authors do not place freedom at the same point, and the difference is again that of the whither.

The same holds for the I. Husserl's first subject-side transcendence is the pure I, which is transcendent because it is identical. Heidegger's temporality has no such pole. The unity of the ecstases is itself ecstatic, and one may not "smuggle in" a person-centre or an "I-kernel" *(my translation)* (GA 26 § 12, 269). The index observes that the treatise of 1929 applies Husserl's verb to the relation that *Ideas I* excluded: there the I is the one transcendence that "is not constituted", here "transcendence constitutes selfhood". The observation is exact as to the wording, and I have checked both passages. Whether Heidegger intended the echo cannot be shown, since he does not cite § 57. The doctrinal point does not depend on it. An identical I can be transcended toward, in reflection and recollection, like any other self. A selfhood that first arises in the surpassing of beings cannot be an identity given beforehand to the one who surpasses.

Is this to say that Husserl knows only what Heidegger calls ontic transcendence, so that Heidegger's verdict of 1928 stands? In one respect it does stand. Wherever Husserl uses the word in the texts read here, that toward which consciousness transcends is something identical, and Heidegger would count every such something among beings. However, this is not to say that Husserl lacks the phenomena for which Heidegger reserves the word. He has a flow that is no object in time and for which he says the names are lacking, and he has a world that is not in being as an object is. What he does not do is call either of them transcendence. It is true that this is in part a matter of terminology. But the terminology records a decision. For Husserl the word stays with what can be identified and confirmed, and what cannot be so identified is described as absolute subjectivity or as horizon. Bernet's remark of 1987, which the index reports, that the double intentionality of the flow is more properly "transcendence", is thus right about the phenomenon and goes beyond Husserl's usage. The one text in which Husserl applies the word to the stream applies it to the stream as recollected.

One point remains on which the texts do not decide, and I leave it open. Both authors call the world subjective in a sense that each is at pains to protect. For Heidegger the world is subjective because it belongs to Dasein, and for that very reason it does not fall "into the inner sphere of a 'subjective' subject" *(my translation)* (GA 9, 158). For Husserl everything worldly draws its sense from my own experiencing, and the world is nonetheless no part of my I. Is Husserl's world-horizon of 1936 then a first step toward a whither that is no being, or is it the last refinement of the horizon of objects? The sentence that the world is not in being as an object is supports the first answer. The sentence that the horizon is conscious only as horizon for existing objects supports the second. Husserl writes both within a few lines of each other. The paragraph ends on the difference between the mode of being of an object and that of the world, and does not say which of the two sentences governs the other.

Only now, with both positions set out, do I raise two objections, one against each author. Heidegger is right that the intentional analysis of *Ideas I* leaves undetermined the being of the one who intends, and right that a closure of sense reproduces the logic of the capsule without its spatial imagery. But his statement that Husserl does not see the problem and the phenomenon rests on a narrow textual base. It passes over the flow of the lectures he had himself just edited, and it treats as immanent in the capsule's sense a time-consciousness that he concedes to be time itself. His assurance that transcendence was "nowhere" identified with intentionality is, further, not true of his own course of the year before. The distance between the positions is real. In 1928 Heidegger overstates it at the two points where it is smallest.

Husserl is right, against the Heidegger of 1928, that no barrier separates consciousness from the thing, and he said so two decades earlier. But *Ideas I* § 49 and the lectures on passive synthesis cannot both stand as written. A consciousness "out of which nothing can slip", self-sufficient over against everything transcendent, is not the stream whose own past is the first transcendent and which is given to itself only incompletely and approximately. Husserl names the paradox and does not resolve it in the passages read. He does not draw the consequence that the immanence to which transcendence is referred back is itself already beyond itself in time. That consequence would not have made him a Heideggerian, since his stream transcends toward its own identical past and not toward a world. It would, however, have removed the best ground for Heidegger's charge.

We began from the view that Heidegger reverses the reference of a word. The texts show a reversal, and they also show that it is not the root of the matter. Husserl reverses the reference himself, three times, without changing his concept, and Heidegger keeps the subject while changing his. In its sharpest form the result is this. Husserl's transcendence is always toward a self, an identical something that further experience can confirm, and it is infinite because such confirmation has no end. Heidegger's transcendence is toward what can never be confirmed because it is no being, and it is finite because what it holds open is the possible, and possibilities are withdrawn from a being that exists amid beings. The result has a limit that I do not wish to hide. It rests on the texts of one index, and it says nothing about Husserl before 1907 or about Heidegger after 1929, where his own marginal notes suggest that the word was given up. Within these limits one thing remains to Husserl's credit that the confrontation of 1928 conceals. He found, in a lasting tone, that consciousness is beyond itself before it is with any thing, and he found it twenty years before Heidegger made transcendence the name for what a subject is.

## 6. What the primary texts changed

- **Index:** Hua XI `:2768` is Husserl "locating transcendence at the heart of the subject", "every self is transcendent". **Text:** *Selbst* here is the identical, identifiable item of any self-giving ("Ein Selbst, ein Gegenstand", `:2756`); the stream is transcendent as "gegenständlich, objektiv" for its I. Narrowed.
- **Index:** no mention that Husserl uses *Urtranszendenz*. **Text:** § 45's title and the phrase "Urtranszendenz der Wiedererinnerung" (`:2768`); and "Diese Transzendenz der Raumwelt ist eine Transzendenz zweiter Stufe". Widened; the article's second parallel and its dating of a shift against *Ideas I* § 53 rest on this.
- **Index:** GA 24 § 9b says intentionality "simply is" transcendence. **Text:** the same paragraph has the being exist as intentional "(auf ihr als Grund)", and EN `:907` calls intentionality "the essential though not the most original structure of the subject". Narrowed.
- **Index:** *Krisis* § 37 is "Heidegger's world-is-no-being thesis in Husserl's words". **Text:** the world is "seiend in einer Einzigkeit", and the horizon is conscious "nur als Horizont für seiende Objekte". Narrowed.
- **Index:** CM § 48, the ego "transcends its own being entirely". **Text:** it does so "mit einem Seinssinn, durch den", that is, through the sense of being of what it intends; and the primordial transcendence is a "synthetische Einheit". Narrowed.
- **Index:** Paris Lectures `:1028` for "Transzendenz ist ein immanenter … Seinscharakter". **Text:** CM § 41 has the stronger "Transzendenz in jeder Form", and the phrase "aus meiner Bewußtseinsinsel heraus" (`:2465`), which the index does not cite and which gives the article its shared question.
- **Index:** Hua IX Beilage XVI for "no mystical outside". **Text:** the same supplement defines "das Transzendieren" (`:8053`), not noted by the index.
- **Index:** the "subject" formulas of GA 9. **Text:** `:100` is conditional ("Wählt man … den Titel 'Subjekt'"), and `:102` says a subject-concept has been warded off.

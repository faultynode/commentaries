# Genesis without History?

## The Genetic Method at Work in Husserl's Lectures on Passive Synthesis

It is generally accepted that the lectures printed in the eleventh volume of the Husserliana are the place where Husserl first put his genetic phenomenology to work. The volume also prints the short text of 1921, "Static and Genetic Phenomenological Method", in which the distinction is drawn, and the lectures are read as its execution: static analysis describes finished constitutive systems with the object as its clue, genetic analysis asks how these systems and the habits of the ego came to be. Bernet, Kern and Marbach give the classic statement of the view; Anthony Steinbock, in the introduction to his translation, calls the lectures "exemplary" of the genetic method and places the matter of that method in "monadic individuation and its genesis"; Donn Welton calls them Husserl's "attempt to apply his newly found genetic analysis to both perception and theory of judgment".[^1] Yet the volume does not support the view in the form in which it is held. Its main text is not one lecture course but the last of three versions of one, given in the winter of 1920/21, the summer of 1923 and the winter of 1925/26, and printed "in the version of 1925/26", with pages of the earlier versions left standing in it.[^2] The sentences that name the genetic method are not all of one year; several of them were written before the text of 1921 that is supposed to state the method they apply. And at the place where the lectures go furthest towards a genesis, in their analysis of affection, Husserl states a principle that ties every motive of a genetic theory to what is evident in "the, so to speak, finished living present".[^3] A method bound by that principle cannot be the narration of a history, of the monad or of its objects. The question is what it is instead.

In what follows I wish to show that in these lectures a genesis is never narrated but exhibited, and that it is exhibited in two ways, by seeing it in a case and by constructing it, which both versions of the course use but which they put in opposite orders. In the leaves of 1920/21 the analysis begins from an occurrence in which a genesis is seen, one memory awakening another or a blended memory coming apart, and goes back from it, by steps Husserl for the most part marks, to what must already hold in the impression; its own rule is that association must be approached from recollection. In the version of 1925/26 the order is turned round, into the ascent "from below" that the course had announced in 1920/21 and the first version had not carried out: the living present is built up as a "systematic genesis", under an "as if", and the seen case comes last; the principle just quoted states the condition under which such a construction may be asserted, and notes written in February 1926 ask how the construction is to be had from the finished present at all. The difference between the layers is a difference of order and of the standard to which a genetic claim is first held, not a clean division of kinds: each version also does what the other puts first. "Genetic", in these lectures, names first a kind of evidence, what the text of 1921 calls "explanatory" knowledge; the "history" of which the closing reflection of the course speaks names the idea that this evidence serves, not a procedure that the lectures carry out.

The study rests on the Husserliana edition of the lectures (Hua XI, 1966), read in German, with three parts of the volume that the received view seldom uses together: Margot Fleischer's introduction, her text-critical appendix with its table dating the leaves of the main text, and the reconstructed first version of §§ 12–40. Of the supplementary texts I use the treatise of 1921 and the notes of February 1926 printed as Beilage XIX. Four texts outside the volume are used for the dates before and after: the companion text of 1921 printed as Beilage I of Hua XIV, the opening of the course printed in Hua XVII, a note of 1916/17 in Hua XIII, and *Cartesianische Meditationen* §§ 37–38. Of the readers I take up Holenstein (1972), Bégout (2000), Steinbock (2001) and Welton (2000), each at the level at which I have read them. I leave aside the active part of the course (Hua XXXI), which was not read in 1923 or 1925/26, except where it bears on the sense of the word "genesis"; the Bernau manuscripts, which on Bernet and Lohmar's account already contain the breakthrough the lectures are usually credited with, since the question here is what the lectures do and not where the doctrine first appears; and *Erfahrung und Urteil*, whose Introduction was drafted, on its editor's account, only after 1935 and cannot tell us what the lectures did a decade earlier. Most of §§ 19–22 and 43–47 and most of the supplements were read only at the lines where the vocabulary of genesis occurs. The method of the study changes with its stages and I say at each stage which is in use: the first part is textual and historical, the second and third re-perform Husserl's descriptions on a carried example, the fourth and fifth confront his statements of method and his readers around one question, what makes a genetic claim evident.

## 1. The word and the layers

The received view takes "genetic" to have one sense in Hua XI and the lectures to have one date. Neither holds, and before any analysis of what the lectures do the two assumptions must be replaced by what the volume shows. This part is textual and historical. It separates the senses of the word as the volume uses it, dates the layers of the main text from the editor's apparatus, places the vocabulary of genesis in those layers, and asks how the treatise of 1921 stands to the course. Its result is a reformulation of the question: what the lectures "apply" has to be asked of each layer separately.

### 1.1 Four senses of "genetic"

The word occurs in the volume in four senses, and they must be kept apart because the received view slides between the second and the fourth.

The first sense is the one Husserl excludes. When association is introduced in the version of 1920/21 he says that it names for us "a form and lawfulness of immanent genesis belonging constantly to consciousness as such", and not, as it does for the psychologists, "a form of objective, psychophysical causality" (115–16/161). Genesis in this sense is the coming-about of a mental event in the causal order of nature; the phenomenological reduction brackets it. It is the sense that the treatise of 1921 still had to correct in its own manuscript: on the leaf where Husserl writes that the apperceptions of real things "have a history, a genesis according to the primal laws" (339/627), the manuscript first had "causation" and was corrected to "genesis" (Anh. 510). The correction is a small fact about a word; I take it as evidence that the second sense was being separated from the first under the pen.

The second sense is necessary succession in the stream of consciousness. The treatise opens with it: "laws of genesis in the sense of the demonstration of laws for the successions of single events in the stream of experience", such as "the necessary attachment of retentions to events that have run off" (336/623). Hua XIV Beilage I, written in 1921, gives the same sense its most compact form: the phenomenology of genesis "shows how consciousness becomes out of consciousness", and its example is "the necessary passage from impression into retention" (Hua XIV 41). In the lectures of 1925/26 this is the sense of the "basic law of passive genesis" by which every empty presentation attaches to a preceding intuition (73/114), and of the "primal genesis of the time-stream" (74/115). A genesis in this sense is not a history. It is a law of the form: if this, then necessarily that, next.

The third sense is the order of levels in which objects of different types are founded on one another. Beilage I says that a systematic phenomenology "as I thought of it" follows "the levels of possible constitutions", the time-stream at the bottom, "then the genetically higher levels", and adds: "These are therefore genetic considerations" (Hua XIV 38). Here "genetic" is said of an order that can be described without asking whether or how anything came to be in that order.

The fourth sense is history: the history of an apperception, of an object type, of the ego, of the monad. The treatise speaks of the "'history' of consciousness (the history of all possible apperceptions)" and immediately restricts it: it does not concern "the demonstration of factual genesis for factual apperceptions", and what is given with the "essential genesis" is "only the mode of genesis in which any apperception of this type must originally have arisen in an individual stream of consciousness" (339/627). This is the sense the received view has in mind, and the treatise's restriction is the first thing it tends to forget.

I do not claim that this list is complete; the contrast of a "static" and a "kinetic" consideration in the lectures, to which I come in Part 3, will add a further use of the paired word "static". But the four are enough to show that the question "does Husserl apply the genetic method here?" has no answer until one says which sense is meant. The thesis of this study can now be put more exactly: in the lectures, the fourth sense names an idea; the second sense names what is actually exhibited; and the third names the order in which the version of 1925/26 sets out what it exhibits.

### 1.2 One course in three versions

The history of the text is soon told, and the editor has told most of it. Husserl lectured on "Logic" in Freiburg in the winter of 1920/21, repeated the course in the summer of 1923 and again in the winter of 1925/26, and reworked it at both repetitions; Fleischer printed the main text "in the version of 1925/26" (Einl. XIII). On the wrapper of the first of the three convolutes of the manuscript, F I 37, Husserl wrote a judgement on the result: "Lectures transcendental logic 1920/21. Repeated and (unfortunately reworked) 1923 summer and again (partly improved, partly spoiled) 1925/26" (Einl. XXII). The leaves he removed during the revisions he kept in a separate wrapper with the note "Discarded leaves, not always discarded for good reasons", and inside it a further bundle marked "It seems that the presentation here is better in detail: so go through it again exactly" (Anh. 480). From these leaves Fleischer reconstructed the first version of §§ 12–40 and printed it as the first of the supplementary texts.

The apparatus does more than tell the story. It dates the leaves of the main text one by one, from Husserl's original paginations and the look of the paper, and sets the result out in a table (Anh. 446). I give the table in a condensed form, by pages of Hua XI, because everything that follows depends on it.

The first column gives pages and the second the year in which the leaves were written; the third, which is mine, says which sections the pages carry. The table does not date thoughts. A leaf of 1920/21 may carry later insertions, and Fleischer notes that in the parts of 1920/21 the reworking "goes far beyond what is usual with Husserl" (Anh. 446). Nor does it say what was said aloud in 1923, since a leaf written in 1920/21 and kept was presumably read again. What it does show is which wording of a passage stood in the manuscript of the first course and which was written for the last.

Two facts in the table matter most. The first is that the whole theory of association and affection as Hua XI prints it, from the new opening of § 26 on p. 118 to p. 187, is written on leaves of 1925/26. Husserl's own note on the wrapper of the second bundle says as much: "1925/26 newly worked out. Association from 134a on" (Anh. 445). The second is that the closing reflection, § 48, together with the fourth section that leads up to it and two pages of the analysis of expectation in §§ 40–41, stands on leaves of 1920/21. The course thus begins and ends in the first version; its middle, where the received view looks for the execution of the genetic method, is the last.

Holenstein, whose monograph remains the most careful reading of these sections, saw the first of these facts and missed its extent. He counted that the first version treats association on "around fifteen book pages", that the revisions made it five times as long, and that the new version runs from 118,14 to 187,5; and he regretted that "in the Husserliana edition it cannot be made out how the working-out is distributed over the lectures of 1923 and 1925/26".[^4] The table answers him: every leaf of that stretch is dated 1925/26, and the pages of 1923 lie elsewhere, in the analysis of modalization and of evidence. Holenstein's page count is right, and so is his sense that the stretch is new. What he could not use is the date that turns the count into an argument.

The third part of the course, on activity and judgment, Husserl did not read at either repetition (Anh. 443). Breeur, who edited it as Hua XXXI, takes the omission to have been "presumably for lack of time" and notes that Husserl kept the part in the new pagination of 1925 (Hua XXXI IX). For the question of this study that part matters in one respect only, to which I come in Part 5: in it "genesis" means an a priori order of building up, a sense close to the third of those listed above.

### 1.3 Where the word stands

With the table in hand one can place the vocabulary of genesis. I searched the main text and the first version for "genetisch", "Genesis" and "Genese"; the lecture text has about fifty occurrences, against fifty-four in the nine pages of the treatise. Their distribution over the layers is not even, and the difference is itself evidence of what each layer is doing.

In the leaves of 1920/21 the word occurs mostly at the joints of the course and only in a few places inside an analysis. The Introduction ends by naming two tasks. "I can only say in a word", Husserl writes, that all these intentional analyses belong "in a universal genesis of consciousness", and then he divides: if the theme of the constitutive analyses is to make understandable how perception brings about its sense-giving, it is the theme of the genetic analyses "to make understandable how, in the development that belongs to the essence of every stream of consciousness and is at once a development of the ego, those complicated intentional systems develop" through which an outer world can appear (24/61–62). The genetic task is announced in a word and for the outer world. The end of the second section, on a leaf of the same winter, opens the way to the theory of association with the problem of error: what is still needed is "the clarification of the origins of error in passivity", and "this problem leads us to a radical piece of the analysis of passive consciousness, namely as genetic analysis: to the phenomenology of association" (116/161). The closing reflection, finally, describes consciousness as "a never-ending history" and assigns "static and genetic" treatment to all the transcendental disciplines it lists (219–20/270–72). Between these joints the first version uses the word sparingly and mostly in the second sense: the phenomena of expectation receive "enrichment and clarification" from "our present genetic analyses" (289–90/424), the mixing of memories is "genetically possible" (194/245), reproductive association is followed "in its genetic development" (196/247), and an absence of what was expected weakens expectation "in the further course of the genesis of consciousness" (189/240). The last two uses are inside an analysis, and Part 2 takes them up.

In the leaves of 1925/26 the word moves inside the analysis and multiplies. The lecture on evidence after Christmas 1925 promises "the most universal lawfulnesses of genesis" alongside the "most universal structural lawfulnesses" (70/112), calls retention a "basic law of passive genesis" (73/114) and protention "the second side of the genetic primal lawfulness" (74/115), and says that intuitive memories do not "precede in genesis", that "the essentially earlier" are empty presentations (74/117). The new opening of the theory of association speaks of the access to "a universal theory of the genesis of a pure subjectivity" (118–19/163), of the "genetic structure of unity of pure subjective life" (119/164) and of a subjectivity "which is thinkable only in genesis" (125–26/171). The living present becomes "the most universal genetic phenomenon" (137–38/184), and the affective analysis is carried out "in our consideration of the lowest genetic level" (150–51/198).

The distribution is not an accident of vocabulary. In 1920/21 "genetic" names a direction the analysis takes when a problem of truth requires it, and the analysis that follows describes what it finds. In 1925/26 it names the frame within which the whole middle of the course is set out. Part 2 examines the first of these uses on the pages where it occurs; Part 3, the second.

### 1.4 The course's own account of its task

If "genetic" names a frame only in the last version, one would expect the first course to describe its task in other words, and it does. The opening lecture of 1920/21, which Husserl later used for the introduction of *Formal and Transcendental Logic* and which is printed among the supplementary texts of Hua XVII, ends by setting the task of the lectures to follow: to open up "this wide, great world of the interiority of consciousness", under the guiding viewpoint of a theory of science, "and, ascending from below, to show how genuine thinking emerges there in all its levels, how it is motivated and built in its founded accomplishment" (Hua XVII 374). The sentence promises an ascent, an order of levels and a motivation. It does not use the word "genetic". A search of the opening lecture for "genetisch" and "Genesis" finds two occurrences, both in a passage that sets aside "the questions of the historical genesis of the spiritual formations of the cultural world" and the "genesis of the stock of words" of languages (Hua XVII 360–62): genesis in the first sense, which the course excludes. The ascent from below is the order that the version of 1925/26 will give the theory of association; the first version, as Part 2 shows, approached that theory from the other end. When Husserl summed up the course for his students after the Christmas break of 1920, he described its method in still other terms: "Our method was in essence the Socratic-Platonic" (253/387). Guided by vague thoughts about the sense of logic, the lectures had gone to concrete analyses and ordered them so that determinate general thoughts could arise from them. The lower level of passivity was studied first because it is "the ground on which the free activity of the ego moves, and without knowledge of which the higher accomplishments of this activity must remain wholly unintelligible to us" (252–53/386).

The texts of the same winter that Fleischer printed as treatises say the same. Before the main text begins, the course restricts itself to what it calls a transcendental aesthetic: "We further undertake the restriction of the transcendental aesthetic, we exclude all judgmental knowledge" (295/582), and keep to intuition, and within it to perception, so that the world-phenomenon is considered only so far as it is a phenomenon of perception. A note in the second treatise explains why a course on logic must begin there: perception and its parallel modes of intuition are the first basic shapes of consciousness that come into question for the building of logical consciousness. "So we are not digressing, but in doing this we are already logicians without knowing it" (319 n./607). The order is an order of foundation, from the lowest level of evidence up to the evidence of judgment, and its title is aesthetic, not genetic.

The editor's introduction frames the volume in the same way. Fleischer does not describe the lectures as an exercise of the genetic method; in her eleven pages the word "genetic" occurs twice, both times as the name "genetic logic": in her report that Husserl so referred to the course (Einl. XIV n. 1), and in a note on Landgrebe's use of the same title (Einl. XXI n. 1). The analyses, she writes, "are united into a whole by the problem that keeps the lectures in motion, that of the in-itself of the stream of consciousness" (Einl. XVII), and she follows that problem through to its result: the past is shown to have its in-itself, while "the future of consciousness keeps the character of the problem" (Einl. XXI). Steinbock's introduction, by contrast, describes the course as a "regressive, archeological movement from the active cognitive dimensions to the passive kinaesthetic ones", followed by the inverse movement of ascent (*Analyses*, xxii). The two editors of the same text thus read it under two different titles, truth and genesis. The course's own opening gives the ascent and the motivation that Steinbock's account requires, and the problem of truth that Fleischer's requires; it does not give the regress.

The difference is not a matter of emphasis. If the course is governed by the problem of the in-itself, then genesis enters it as a means, and the question to put to each genetic step is what it contributes to the evidence of the past. If it is governed by genesis, the problem of truth is an occasion, and the question is how far the lectures get with the history of consciousness. The first question can be answered from the text; the second, as § 48 will show, receives from the text the answer that the lectures got no further than the past. I take the first as my guide, and keep the second for Part 5.

### 1.5 The texts of 1921 as a reflection on the course

The received view reads the treatise as the programme and the lectures as its execution. The dates reverse the order of at least half of the relation. The treatise stands in a convolute headed "Genesis. 1921. New supplements" (Anh. 510); Beilage I of Hua XIV belongs to the same convolute; its first leaf is dated June 1921, and Kern takes the remaining leaves to be in all probability of the same year (Hua XIV, textual notes to Beilage I).[^4a] The treatise is dated only by the year, but it speaks of the course in the past tense, and so do its margins. After drawing the line between "explanatory" phenomenology, the phenomenology of lawful genesis, and "descriptive" phenomenology, the phenomenology of essential shapes in pure consciousness "however they came to be", Husserl adds: "In the lectures I said not 'descriptive' but 'static' phenomenology" (340–41/629). A marginal note on the preceding page speaks of "constitutive descriptions, which I used to call static" (Anh. 510). The treatise is thus, in part, a reflection on a vocabulary the lectures had already used, and the lectures it reflects on are those of 1920/21.

This does not settle what the treatise is a reflection on. It might be catching up with a genetic analysis that the course had practised without naming it, which is the reading Steinbock gives when he says that Husserl had undertaken genetic "analyses implicitly without phenomenology having been explicitly cognizant of itself as having this genetic methodological dimension" (*Analyses*, xxix). Or it might be reflecting on a course that had used the word at its joints and had not yet decided what a genetic analysis was. The treatise's own text inclines to the second. It distinguishes laws of genesis in two senses, the necessary succession of events and the laws that "regulate the formation of apperceptions" (337–38/623); it leaves open "whether one can bring about a systematic phenomenology of static connections such as that of noesis and noema, whether the genetic is to be excluded entirely from it" (344/633); and it ends, in a later addition, with Husserl's resolution that "I must now go through the *Ideas* once more, in order to become clearer" (345/629). A footnote adds a third discipline that the main text did not name: a universal phenomenology of the general structures of consciousness, a constitutive phenomenology, and a phenomenology of genesis (345/629). Beilage I, of the same year, likewise distinguishes three kinds of inquiry, though not the same three: static inquiries that follow the correlations of constituting consciousness and constituted object "and exclude genetic problems altogether"; inquiries into "the types of the various shapes of experience and of genesis" according to their essential possibilities, "without considering the problems of individuals in their connection"; and the phenomenology of monadic individuality, which includes "the phenomenology of a connected genesis" (Hua XIV 38). It adds: "I can also describe isolated geneses, and laws of genesis, without taking up systematically the problem of the universal genesis of a monad and the essence of its individuality" (Hua XIV 38).

This middle term is the place where the lectures stand, and the received view has no room for it. Bernet, Kern and Marbach, Steinbock and Welton all read the lectures with the monad's history in view: genetic phenomenology asks for the history of the ego and of its objects, it "concerns monadic individuation and its genesis", it treats transformation as "a process of development in which the earlier gives rise to the later".[^5] Husserl's own texts of 1921 say that one can describe geneses and laws of genesis without taking up that question at all, and the lectures, as I shall show, never take it up. Whether what they describe under the title of genesis is the second kind of inquiry of Beilage I, or something that that text's list does not yet contain, is the question of Parts 2 and 3.

The reformulated question is therefore this. In the leaves of 1920/21, which the treatise calls "the lectures", what is a genetic analysis, and on what evidence does it stand? In the leaves of 1925/26, written four years after the treatise, how is the same analysis rebuilt, and what does the rebuilding cost? Only when both are answered can one say what the lectures "apply", and whether the method of 1921 is what they apply.

## 2. Genesis seen: the version of 1920/21

The leaves of 1920/21 contain the first theory of association of the course: the end of the second section and the opening of the third in the main text (pp. 103–118), the end of the third and the whole of the fourth (pp. 187–222), and the stretch of the first version that the revision of 1925/26 replaced (pp. 268–290). Read together, they show what a genetic analysis was in the course that the treatise of 1921 calls "the lectures". This part first sets out the question that brings association into the course, then re-performs the analysis on Husserl's own example, and then follows the steps by which the analysis goes back from what it sees to what it must assume. Its method is descriptive: I report what the text says and, where I vary its example, say that the variant is mine.

### 2.1 A question of truth

Association does not enter the course as the next topic in an order of levels. It enters as the answer to a question about the evidence of the past, and the question has to be set out first, because it decides what kind of claim the genetic analysis that answers it is.

The second section of the course had shown that passive experience has its own evidence. A perception that runs on without break fulfils its anticipations; a protention is confirmed; there is, below all judgment, a self-giving that measures intention. But the same section had also shown that this evidence can be modalized: a perception can turn into doubt, an expectation can be disappointed. If every self-giving can be struck out by another, the passive sphere seems to offer no standard at all. Husserl asks the question in the leaves of 1920/21: can it be that every self-giving can be devalued by conflict with others, and so on without end, or does an in-itself correspond to what a presentation means? The answer begins with what is evident in the living present. Immanent being in its living present is not only given as being, it cannot be struck out; the supposition that it is not cancels itself against the given. "But what good is it, since it is only momentary? The immanent flows off and is gone" (109–10/155). Wherever one speaks of a true self and of a presentation that confirms itself once and for all, one goes beyond the momentary consciousness, through recollections in which one comes back to the same presentation and assures oneself of the confirming self as identical. And so the question of how objectivity, "objectivity existing in itself", is constituted and can originally show itself as such "leads everywhere and in principle first to the problem of the constitution of an in-itself of recollection" (111–12/156).

Husserl added to this passage an undated note that begins "I am not wholly satisfied with this" (112/155) and anticipates the method of the later course. It says that the object is constituted from the very beginning as something temporal, and that the momentary phase is an abstraction one must first form; it ends: "The impossibility of striking out the moment is therefore not a first" (112/155). The evidence of the moment, from which the argument started, is thus not given at all but formed by abstraction from a temporal object, and what the analysis treats as its starting point is not what comes first in the matter. I return to the note in Part 3, where the abstraction becomes the procedure.

The question of the in-itself of the past now narrows to the question of error in recollection. A recollection is a self-giving consciousness; how can it turn out to be an illusion? Husserl's "guiding thought" (114/159) is that a clear distant recollection which can be repeated and identified has, with respect to its objectivity, "only one possible way of passing into doubt and then turning out to be void: namely that of the mixing up of recollections" (114/160). A false memory is a blend: pieces of two pasts have run together in one image, and the error consists in taking the blend for one past. If this is so, then error in this sphere has a determinate origin, and so does its correction: the blend can be split, and each piece shown to belong to its own past. What is still needed, the lecture goes on, is "the clarification of the origins of error in passivity, namely of error in its most original shape, that of mixing. This problem leads us to a radical piece of the analysis of passive consciousness, namely as genetic analysis: to the phenomenology of association" (116/161).

This is the first time in the course that an analysis is called genetic, and the occasion is telling. The genetic analysis is asked to explain the origin of an error, through it the possibility of correcting the error, and through that the in-itself of the past. It is not asked to narrate how the ego came by its memories or how memory develops. Its claims will be claims about how a blend can come about, and they will be answerable to the question whether the blend so understood can be undone. The genesis it studies is the genesis of a false memory, and its test is the truth of the true one.

The opening of § 26, on a leaf of the same winter, fixes the sense of the word. "For us the title association designates a form and lawfulness of immanent genesis belonging constantly to consciousness as such, and not, as it does for the psychologists, a form of objective, psychophysical causality" (115–16/161). The reduction has bracketed the world with its animals and its causalities; within the frame of pure consciousness we find a streaming present and, entering it, recollections; and between what is present and what is recollected we find a peculiar character of connection, which is expressed in the words: "The present recalls the past" (118/163). The genesis that the genetic analysis studies is this connection as it is given. It is the second of the senses distinguished in Part 1, and it has, as the next sections show, an evidence of its own.

### 2.2 The example and its variants

The first version carries the analysis on one example, and the example is worth giving in full because it does the work of the analysis. "For example, the memory of Sils Maria rises up, and I see a young writer before me, and we are in a lively conversation. It concerned Gundolf's 'Shakespeare'" (269/403). Then a doubt stirs, and if I follow it a second image rises: "I am now with the same young man in a small farmhouse room in the Fex valley" (269/403), his lodgings, where he read to me from Gundolf's book and we discussed it there. If I go back into the first image, I notice that part of its continuity remains purely self-giving and unbroken; but where I heard the young man speak there "was a small, formerly unnoticeable jump in the continuity; unnoticed, the reproduction jumped there into the other image" (269/403). The one conversation had been laid under the other, and the same person in the one situation under the same person in the other. And, Husserl adds, it is evident that this doubleness lay already, unnoticed, in the image that first gave itself as one.

The example has the two parts that an example needs if it is to carry a distinction: a member that is the same in both pasts, the young man and his talk of Gundolf, and surroundings that differ, the open air of Sils and the farmhouse room. Let me vary it, as the method requires, before going on with Husserl's text.

First, suppose the memory of Sils rises and, when I follow a doubt, nothing splits off: I make it clearer and clearer and the conversation remains where it was, under the sky of the Engadine, without a jump. Then the memory has not been shown to be true; it has only failed to show itself false in the one way the guiding thought allows. Husserl draws the consequence himself in the first version: the process of splitting "cannot go on in infinitum"; since it is a confusion of discrete pieces, there must be an end.[^6] The variant shows what the analysis promises: not a proof of each memory, but the assurance that the only way a clear memory can be false is a way that can be uncovered.

Second, suppose the doubt arises and nothing comes of it: no second image, only an empty unease. Then I have no evidence of illusion, only of doubt, and the memory keeps its claim. The variant shows that the evidence of error is itself a self-giving, the self-giving of the two separate pasts, and not a mere loss of confidence.

Third, and this is the variant that breaks the case, suppose two past scenes that have nothing in common: an evening at a concert and a morning on a glacier, with no person, word or object in both. Can they blend into one false memory of the kind described? It seems not. Nothing would hold the pieces together; the image of the concert could be interrupted by the glacier, but they would not run into one another so that the one is taken for the other. The variant shows where the necessity lies: blending requires a member in which the two pasts coincide. That is the condition Husserl names next, and it is the point at which the description of an error becomes a description of association.

The variants are mine; the result is Husserl's. "The two are 'associating'; the one situation recalls the other, and that is not the expression of some objective psychological fact, but a purely phenomenological essential situation" (270–71/404). The bridge between the two pasts is a similar member, the young man in the one scene recalling the young man in the other; from it the awakening spreads over what coexists with it and then along the course of time that followed. In every association there is thus a primary association by similarity, and dependent on it an association by contiguity, first in coexistence and then in succession. The result holds for all three variants: it explains why the first can stay unbroken, why the second yields no evidence of illusion, and why the third cannot produce the error at all.

[^6]: Hua XI 282–83/417 (first version, no. 22).

### 2.3 "No theory"

What kind of claim is this? The first version answers in a sentence that I take to state the method of the whole stretch. "The coming apart into two separate recollections is no theory, but a state of affairs intelligible in its intentionality" (269–70/404). The splitting is not inferred from the fact that the memory was false; it is lived through, and in living through it one sees what the blend was. "It is evident above all that they do not arise anew, but were already there in consciousness" (269–70/404); they have only taken on a changed mode of intuition and connection. The second memory was not added to the first. It was in the first, suppressed and covered, and the splitting brings it out.

This is a peculiar kind of evidence and it needs to be stated exactly. It is evidence of a genesis: of the blend's having come about by the overlaying of one past on another. It is also evidence of a relation between two moments of time, the earlier state of the memory, in which the doubleness was unnoticed, and the later, in which it is seen. But it is evidence obtained wholly in the present of the one who remembers and corrects. Nothing in it reaches beyond what the clarifying recollection itself shows. The past state it speaks of is given as a past state of this memory, and the claim that the doubleness "already lay" in the first image is made good by the way the first image gives itself once it has been split: as having concealed what it now shows.

Husserl does not yet name this evidence. The new opening of § 26, written five years later, comes close to naming it when it says: "Further, the phenomenon gives itself a genesis, the one member as awakening, the other as awakened. The reproduction of the latter gives itself as brought about by the awakening" (121/165). I return to that sentence in Part 3. Here it is enough to say what the first version shows: a genesis can be seen. The awakening of one memory by another, and the formation of a blend from two, are not hypotheses about hidden processes but relations given with the phenomena themselves, on the side of the object as a "recalling" and on the side of the experience as an awakening.

The two sides must be kept apart, as Husserl's own formulation does. On the side of what is experienced, the Fex valley scene is characterized as recalled by the Sils scene: it stands there as the one the other points to. On the side of the experiencing, there is a process in which a reproduction that was weak and covered gains force and breaks through. The first is a character of the object as meant, "the one situation recalls the other"; the second is a happening in the stream, the awakening. The genetic claim is a claim about the second, and it is made on the evidence of the first.

Is this to say that the genetic analysis of 1920/21 is nothing more than a description of what recollection shows? In one respect it is: its first evidence is the evidence of a clarified recollection, and it claims nothing about the past of consciousness that a recollection could not in principle exhibit. But this does not make it static in the sense the treatise gives the word. A static description, on the treatise's account, describes the possible shapes of consciousness "however they came to be" (340–41/628); the analysis of the blend describes precisely how one shape came to be out of two others. What distinguishes it from the history of the treatise is not that it ignores becoming but that the becoming it describes is one it can see.

### 2.4 The steps back

If the analysis stopped here, it would be a phenomenology of recollection and its errors, not a genetic theory of passivity. It does not stop. From the seen genesis of the blend it goes back, by steps, to what must already be in place in the impression for such a genesis to be possible, and the way it marks these steps is the best evidence of how the first version understood the difference between seeing a genesis and inferring one.

The first step widens the concept. If association is the bridge that a similar member builds between two pasts, the same kind of connection must already be at work where there is no reproduction at all, in the present itself, wherever like is joined to like. "In fact one would have to extend the concept of association phenomenologically" (271–72/406), and speak not only of a connection between present and sunken consciousness but of analogous connections within one present: likeness, contrast, everything that in passivity makes a prominent plurality conscious as a unity. The conditional "would have to" marks the step as required by the analysis, not as yet carried out.

The second step names what the widening finds. A tone sounds, then a second like it, then a third; the second takes up the first, which was already sinking retentionally, and gives it a lift of affective force; when the series a b c is repeated, the second a awakens the first, and a rhythm arises as the unity of a periodic plurality. "These are, it seems to me, primal forms of association, so to speak original associations, impressional ones" (272–73/407). The same lecture, written out again a few pages later, puts it more strongly: "So I am compelled to see in this primal forms of association, so to speak of original association, association within the impressional sphere" (286/421). The first formulation stands on a single leaf from another convolute, which Husserl marked "probably not read" and which the editor inserted into the first version (Anh. 482); the second is in the lecture text. The two formulations mark the status of the step. What is seen is the tone series: the lift that the new tone gives to the sinking one, the repetition that binds the past series to life. What is claimed is that this is association in the same sense as the awakening of the Fex valley by Sils; and that claim is presented first as an appearance ("it seems to me") and then as a compulsion ("I am compelled"). It is neither seen in the way the splitting of the memory is seen nor merely supposed. It is forced on the analyst by the likeness between the two cases.

The third step formulates laws. "It is a primal law that like enters with like into an original connection of 'fusion'" (287/421), and a further law says that every awakening of a member raises the affective force of all the members bound up with it. These laws generalize what the tone series shows, and they are presented as laws of essence: the "primal law" is not an empirical regularity but what must hold for any such series.

The fourth step goes to the limit of what can be seen. If the present awakens a distant past by similarity, how can a past that is nothing any more determine the course of reproductive awakening? "How is it to be understood that the present by similarity fetches the distant sunken past, and that the past, which after all is nothing any more ..." (288–89/422): the question is put in the form of a request for understanding, and the answer runs through the law of retention. Sinking back, every retained content loses affective force; "to the temporal perspective, the phenomenal drawing-together of the just-pasts, there corresponds, I would say, an affective perspective" (288–89/423); in the end everything retentional flows into the one far horizon that extinguishes all difference. "But to this is added, as a supplement, the law that this far horizon, of the already dead far retention, can be awakened again from the actual present" (289/423), and the awakening then propagates according to the force of what was awakened. This is the first statement in the course of what the version of 1925/26 will build out into the theory of the "null sphere". Its status is again marked: "I would say", "as a supplement, the law". And it is followed at once by a confession: "Here new problems certainly arise that urgently await working out. A phenomenology of tendencies is needed" (289/423), and, the lecture adds, the association of mere simultaneity and succession, pure time-association, "is missing".[^7]

The four steps go from what is seen to what is compelled, from what is compelled to what is lawful, and from what is lawful to what is postulated. At each step the text marks the change. And at the end of the stretch the first version states the rule by which its order is determined: "We have tackled the phenomenology of association from recollection, and in my view it must be tackled from there" (289–90/424). The rule is not a matter of convenience. Recollection is the place where association is seen, in the awakening of one past by another and in the splitting of a blend. Expectation and free phantasy are not to be drawn in at once. Primal association in the impression is reached from recollection by analogy and compulsion; it is not the place where the theory starts.

The first version does not, however, stay within what it sees, and it does not always mark the step. In the analysis of expectation, on leaves of the same winter, an absence of what was expected acts "in the further course of the genesis of consciousness" as a weakening of the force gathered by repetition, and in the empty retentional sphere "the forces sum and inhibit each other", "blind" (189/240); in § 42 reproductive association is followed "in its genetic development" (196/247), with retentions that do not reach intuition, are pushed down and break through. These are accounts of what is not seen, and they are given without an "it seems to me". What distinguishes them from the constructions of 1925/26 is their place: they come after the seen case, and they are offered to explain it. The first version's order, not its abstinence, is what Part 3 will find reversed.

This, I think, is what a genetic analysis was in the leaves of 1920/21. It begins from a genesis that is given, an awakening and a blend; it describes it on both sides; it goes back from it to conditions in the present and in retention that must hold for it to be possible, and it marks most of its steps beyond the given. The analysis is regressive in the plain sense that it goes from what is later in the order of constitution, recollection, to what is earlier, impression and retention. But it is not the regress of an archaeology from finished deposits to an unseen origin. It is the regress from a seen genesis to the conditions of that same genesis, and its results are always tied back to the evidence from which it started: the first version uses its laws of awakening at once to answer the question of error and of the in-itself of the past.

### 2.5 The retrospect of § 48

The closing reflection of the course, on leaves of the same winter, looks back on the whole and says what has been done and what not. It is the passage in which the received view finds its strongest support, and it deserves to be read at length.

"The great theme of transcendental philosophy is consciousness as such as a storied structure of constitutive accomplishments" (217–18/269), in which ever new objectivities constitute themselves at ever new levels, each with its own self-givings and its own ways of verification. "All the other levels are taken up into the higher ones, but not lost in them; rather they are themselves at any time ready for corresponding directions of regard and demonstrations" (217–18/269). Then the "guiding thought": "Nothing can become conscious in a stream of consciousness or its ego without this consciousness having accomplished, according to laws of essence, that is, according to laws that cannot be cancelled at all, and out of its material of hyletic contents, the corresponding intentional genesis" (217–18/270), whose sediment is the retentional system "in which lie the preconditions for the in-itself of an intentional objectivity of such a type and for its norming" (217–18/270). "Consciousness is an incessant becoming" (217–18/270), not a mere succession of experiences but an incessant constituting of objectivities in an incessant progress of levels. "It is a never-ending history. And history is a constituting of ever higher formations of sense, by levels, governed throughout by an immanent teleology" (219/270). History in the ordinary sense, related to human culture, is only a highest level, and it too has its in-itself prefigured.

Read on its own, this is a programme for the history of the monad. But the passage is not on its own. It is called a "guiding thought", a principle that orders the investigations, not a result of them; and it is followed at once by an account of what the investigations have in fact done. "The way of radical phenomenological investigation must follow the storied structure of constitution" (219/270), and it can only start from the objective world as it presents itself. An obvious and already difficult work is "the study of finished intentionality within the self-giving of nature" (219/270), and this is the work done before Christmas, in the many demonstrations "in which we are offered a so to speak static understanding of the noematic contents of external perception and so of the finished phenomena of external nature" (219/271). The general theory of belief then led further, to modalization and verification. What is to be achieved here for every kind of objectivation "became and becomes clear to us only in the self-objectivation of consciousness with respect to its past, and at the same time in this most fundamental and primitive sphere the problem of genesis also became sensible to us, which we had also taken up there" (220/271). "The same would now have to be achieved for the constitution of nature" (220/271): the whole system of harmonious and inharmonious givings of nature would have to be inserted "into the history of constituting consciousness, and it must be made genetically intelligible how the readinesses grow in consciousness" (220/271) that make such constitutive connections possible. It must suffice to have worked out the problems and, "in particular in carrying out the work for the most universal and at the same time most primitive constitution of immanent temporality, to have gained a clear understanding of the style of such investigations" (220/271).

The apparatus qualifies the date of this account. The sentence "The general theory of belief showed us the way further" and the whole stretch from "What is to be achieved here" to "would now have to be achieved for the constitution of nature" are Husserl's insertions on the leaf (Anh. 478–79), undated; the tense of "became and becomes clear" suggests a later reading of the course, of 1923 or 1925/26. The retrospect is therefore not simply the voice of the first winter. On either date, however, it says the same thing about the course: that the problem of genesis was taken up for the past of consciousness and has still to be taken up for nature.

The retrospect thus makes three claims about the course, and they are the claims of this part. The first is that the analysis of external perception before Christmas was static. The second is that the problem of genesis became "sensible" in one sphere only, the self-objectivation of consciousness with respect to its past, that is, in the question of the in-itself of recollection, and was taken up there. The third is that the genetic understanding of nature, the history of constituting consciousness as it bears on the outer world, is a task that "would have to" be done. The "history" of the guiding thought is therefore not what the course has given. It names the frame within which the analysis of the past stands, and the direction in which the work would have to go on.

The apparatus records two further marks on the leaf. The sentence on history in the ordinary sense, from "History in the ordinary sense" to "prefigured", stands in blue-pencil square brackets (Anh. 478); the sentences on consciousness as a never-ending history do not. And a sentence that followed the assignment of the task for nature was struck out in blue: "But everything constituted is something constituted in consciousness, in becoming, and so it is a necessary problem and one of a complementary dimension, how consciousness and its ego builds up its objectivities by levels" (Anh. 479), and how ideas answer to each new type of objective sense. The deleted sentence stated the genetic programme in its most general form. The marks are not dated, and I infer nothing from them about what Husserl meant to do with the passage, except that the programme stands on the leaf as a task, and was stated there once more and then removed.

What the first version leaves undone is thus stated by the first version. It has seen a genesis in the sphere of recollection; it has gone back from it to primal association and to the law of retention by steps that it marks as compelled or postulated; and it declares that a phenomenology of tendencies, of the laws of awakening in the dark sphere and of pure time-association is urgently needed. These are exactly the questions that the revision of 1925/26 takes up, and it takes them up, as the next part shows, by turning the order of the analysis round.

## 3. Genesis built: the version of 1925/26

When Husserl took up the course for the third time, in the winter of 1925/26, he wrote the theory of association anew. The leaves of the new version replace the first version's pages on association and affection with some seventy pages that begin in the middle of § 26 and run to the opening of § 40; together with the lectures on evidence given after Christmas 1925 (§§ 16–20), they are the part of Hua XI on which the received view rests. This part reads them in the order of the printed text and asks the question put to the first version: what is a genetic claim here, and on what evidence is it made? The answer is that the order of the analysis has been reversed. Where the first version went from a seen genesis back to its conditions, the new version begins with the conditions and builds upward, and it has to say, as the first did not, under what rule a genesis that is not seen may be asserted.

### 3.1 The new opening of § 26

The revision begins in the middle of a paragraph. The leaves of 1920/21 run to 118,13; the next words, "From the expositions of the last lectures it follows", were inserted when the new leaves were joined on (Anh. 462). What follows sets the theory of association in a new frame. "From the expositions of the last lectures it follows that association is a possible theme of purely phenomenological research" (118/163). Penetrating deeper, one sees that from here "the access opens to a universal theory of the genesis of a pure subjectivity, and first with respect to its lower layer of pure passivity" (118–19/163); the phenomenology of association is "so to speak a higher continuation of the doctrine of original time-constitution" (118–19/163). Kant saw this: that the connections which first meet us under the title of association express an absolutely necessary lawfulness, without which a subjectivity could not be, "Kant really already saw; but his brilliant doctrine of the transcendental necessity of association is not underpinned by a phenomenological analysis of essence" (119/164), one that would exhibit what is really there under this title and thereby make understandable "the genetic structure of unity of pure subjective life" (119/164). Husserl does not claim that phenomenology has solved the genetic problems. "But it is far enough to be able to exhibit these problems determinately and to prescribe the method of solution" (119/164).

Three things have changed against the first version. The question of truth, which brought association into the course in 1920/21, is no longer the frame. The frame is now the transcendental question of the conditions without which a subjectivity could not be, and it is set out in Kant's terms. The phenomenology of association is placed in a series, as the continuation upward of the doctrine of time; its place is assigned before it is reached. And the claim of the section is a claim of method: the problems can be exhibited and the method of their solution prescribed.

The new opening keeps the seen case and puts it in its place. The facts of reproduction "result, when we practise phenomenological reduction, first as transcendental facts. At the beginning we stand, then, still before eidetic intuition of essence" (119/164). Then the Aristotelian distinction is introduced to order the inquiry: one can recall "the Aristotelian distinction between the first in itself and the first for us, namely the first from the standpoint of explanatory (of making-understandable) knowledge" (120/165). What is first for us, "and not by accident but essentially", are the facts of association in the reproductive sphere, awakening experiences and awakened reproductions "in which objects are conscious as objects for themselves" (120/165). The first version had said that association must be tackled from recollection; the new opening says that recollection is what is first for us, and implies that it is not first in itself.

The implication is drawn at once. The analysis of associative phenomena draws attention to the fact that consciousness need not be consciousness of an object for itself, "and accordingly a new problem becomes sensible here" (120–21/165): how a consciousness of single things and of explicit pluralities becomes possible at all. For the time being, however, "we can bring associations into view only thus, and only thus directly, that we have single objects given phenomenologically" (121/165), unities set off for themselves which recall other unities as past. "We do not say that we see the 'recalling something' everywhere, but only that we see it in certain cases, that is, that we have grasped it in original grasping" (121/165). The example is now impersonal: "A head of a valley that we perceive at a bend of the way recalls to us another head of a valley" (121/165). And then the sentence that names the evidence of the first version: "Further, the phenomenon gives itself a genesis, the one member as awakening, the other as awakened. The reproduction of the latter gives itself as brought about by the awakening" (121/165).

The new opening thus says in so many words what the first version had shown: a genesis is seen in certain cases, and these cases are the reproductive associations between objects already set off. But it says it in order to go beyond it. The seen cases are first for us; they are not where the theory will begin. A few pages later the question that the first version had asked of the past, how the ego can know that it has an endless field of past experiences behind it, is turned into the question of the "genetic conditions of this possibility (the conditions of possible awakening)", to be brought out in insight into essence (124/169); and the whole problem becomes "the fundamental problem of clarifying the basic conditions of the possibility of a subjectivity itself" (124/169). Syntheses are called transcendental in a special sense, "as apodictically necessary for the genesis of a subjectivity (which, after all, is thinkable only in genesis)" (125–26/171).

The revision of the manuscript shows the change in a deletion. Where the first version, after naming the theory of association, had added that there is also a parallel theory of association, "one presupposing the lawfulness of reproductive genesis", Husserl struck the phrase (Anh. 462). In 1920/21 the theory of primal association presupposed reproductive association, because it was reached from it. In 1925/26 it is to be developed for itself, as first in itself.

### 3.2 The laws of passive genesis

The lectures on evidence that Husserl gave after Christmas 1925, earlier in the printed order but written in the same winter, show what a genetic claim now is when it concerns the living present. They speak the language of genesis throughout, and in each case the genetic statement turns out, on inspection, to be a structural statement read in the direction of time.

The analyses will lead, Husserl announces, to the most universal "structural lawfulnesses of the unity of transcendental inner life, but also to the most universal lawfulnesses of genesis" (70/112). The two are named side by side, and the next paragraph explains why one cannot be had without the other: perception, recollection, co-presentation and expectation are not to be set side by side as types; "only if we understand them as a structural connection can we also understand how they function in synthetic connections" (71/113). The first law is retention. Every intuition, once it has run off, is still conscious in an unintuitive way; every such empty presentation is retention, "and its necessary attachment to preceding intuitions designates a basic law of passive genesis" (73/114). The law reaches further, since in the inner becoming of every intuition "every now-phase arising primally-impressionally modifies itself retentionally in essential necessity" (73/114). Protention is the counterpart: "the title protention designates the second side of the genetic primal lawfulness, which rigidly governs the life of consciousness as a time-constituting stream of unity" (74/115).

The genetic sense here is the second of those distinguished in Part 1: necessary succession in the stream. The statement that every now-phase modifies itself into retention is a statement about what follows what, necessarily; it is genetic because it concerns becoming, and it is seen because it is part of the structure of every present that can be inspected. When Husserl then says that intuitive memories do not "precede in genesis", that "the essentially earlier are the corresponding empty presentations" (74/117), the earlier is said to be earlier by essence, not in fact; it is read off the structure of recollection, which presupposes an empty presentation that it fills. And when he says, of the empty presentation of the hall next to the room I am looking at, "Genetically we also say with reason that the perception has awakened the presentation, but awakening means at the same time the arising of a synthesis of direction" (75–76/117), the genetic statement is immediately restated as a statement about the structure of the connection between the two presentations: a ray of direction that springs from the perception and passes through the empty presentation to what it presents. The genesis is the arising of that structure, and the structure is what is seen.

This is the second way of making a genetic claim, and it is not the way of the first version. In 1920/21 a genesis was seen in an occurrence, the awakening of a past by a present, and the analysis went back from it. In the lectures on evidence of 1925/26 a genesis is seen in a structure, the necessary attachment of retention to impression, and the analysis can state it as a law without going back from anything. The association theory is announced in this setting as "a most important and wholly universally functioning shape of passive genesis" (76–77/119), whose general doctrine is "provisionally deferred". When it comes, in § 26, it is placed under the title of these laws.

### 3.3 The present built from below

The new theory of association begins in § 27 with what the first version had reached last: the present before reproduction. Its first move is to set aside what the first version had started from. "Let us remain in the continuous synthetic unity of a streaming present, let us at first draw on no functions of recollection, which we are to take into account only afterwards in their genesis and new accomplishment" (129/173), and no expectation that leaps beyond the continuous protention, no phantasy, no act of thought or valuing. Recollection, which in 1920/21 was where association had to be tackled, is now to be treated "only afterwards in its genesis"; the starting point is the present without it.

The second move states what is presupposed. Time-consciousness is the primal site of the constitution of identity and of the forms of coexistence and succession, but "mere form is admittedly an abstraction, and so the intentional analysis of time-consciousness and its accomplishment is an abstract one from the outset" (128/173); what gives an object its unity of content, and what makes differences of content, "the analysis of time alone does not tell us, since it abstracts precisely from the content" (128/173). The analysis must therefore add content to form. But it cannot begin where content first forms unities, because it cannot see that. "We presuppose that immanent objects already set off" (129/173), single things or closed groups, are constituted. "We can only get to see, grasp directly, where we have something set off for itself" (129/175). The present is built from below, but the building begins on the second floor, with prominent unities already given.

On this floor the method is the one the first version had used on memories, now applied to coexistent data. "If already in the so to speak static consideration of a coexistence of two or more objects related in content their unity from homogeneity comes out ... its deeper peculiarity discloses itself in so to speak kinetic consideration" (130/176), in the comparing passage from related to related, in which one consciousness is overlaid on the other and comes to coincidence with it. "What is present statically as a connection of likeness or mere similarity of the separated contents is itself already a mode of the one or the other synthesis of coincidence. It is coincidence par distance" (131/177). "If we use overlaying as a method of disclosing" what lies in the phenomena of likeness and similarity (131–32/177), one finds that in every synthesis of homogeneity a fusion at a distance is at work.

This is the further use of "static" that Part 1 promised. Here "static" does not mean the correlation analysis of the treatise nor the study of finished intentionality of § 48. It means the consideration of a coexistence at rest, as against the "kinetic" consideration that moves from one member to another. And the kinetic consideration is not genetic in the sense of the treatise either. It is a procedure of the analyst, overlaying, that makes visible a coincidence which, on Husserl's account, is already at work in the static coexistence "par distance". The analyst's movement discloses what the present already contains; it does not trace a becoming.

Having described the unities of homogeneity and the sense-fields, Husserl looks back at the end of the next lecture and says what he has been doing. "In order to draw a natural way to a phenomenology of genesis and, in passivity, to a phenomenology of association, we considered the structure of a living immanent present, which is itself the most universal genetic phenomenon" (137–38/184). The sentence carries the tension of the whole version. The living present is the most universal genetic phenomenon, because in it everything comes to be: impressions arise, sink into retention, are anticipated. But what the lectures have studied is its "structure", and they have studied it, by their own account, by presupposing unities already formed and disclosing their connections by overlaying. The genesis is what the present is; the structure is what has been described.

### 3.4 "We act as if"

The next step is affection, and with it the version reaches the point at which the first version had been "compelled" to see primal forms of association in the impressional sphere. The new version does not wait for compulsion. It sets the stage by an abstraction and says so. "We, in our consideration of the lowest genetic level, pose the problem in the abstraction necessary for a systematic genesis: we act as if the world of the ego were only the impressional present" (150–51/198), and as if nothing of apperceptions reaching further, "nothing of the knowledge acquired in the life of the world, of aesthetic and practical interests, valuations and the like" (150–51/198) played a part.

The sentence is the methodological centre of the version, and its terms must be taken exactly. The "lowest genetic level" is not reached by going back in time to an earlier state of the ego. It is reached by an abstraction from the actual present, which is always already full of acquired apperceptions, and the abstraction is stated as a fiction: "we act as if". The fiction is "necessary for a systematic genesis": the order of a systematic exposition, which goes from lower to higher, requires that the lower be isolated, and the lower can be isolated only by pretending that the higher is absent. A "systematic genesis" in this sense is an order of exposition modelled on an order of becoming. It is genetic in the third sense distinguished in Part 1, and it is built.

Husserl's note to the first version, written about the momentary phase, had already said that it "is an abstraction that one must first form", and that the impossibility of striking out the moment "is therefore not a first" (112/155). The version of 1925/26 makes the same point about the impressional present as a whole and draws the opposite consequence: since the lowest level must be formed by abstraction, the analysis forms it, and begins there. Later in the same version Husserl calls the result "that living, originally constituting present which we have so far thought, in a necessary abstraction, purely as a process of immanent hyletic experience" (184/234). The abstraction is lifted when recollections arrive, and the present is enriched by them like "torn-off scraps" of an earlier experience. The version thus has a determinate structure: it builds the present up under an abstraction, and then it lifts the abstraction step by step, and at each step what was set aside returns.

What the abstraction yields is stated with equal care. The forms of time and place and the conditions of their filling, which the analysis has set out as essential necessities, have only "the mere significance of *conditions* of the possibility of hyletic unity-formations, while the formation of unity itself ... would still depend on the factor of affection that has remained unconsidered" (152/200). The same paragraph adds that a suitable experimenting, "not inductive-objectively directed", would be possible here, with the task of producing "favourable conditions" for pure cases of the kind in question (150–51/198). Husserl thus does not take the abstracted level to be a mere fiction; he thinks cases approaching it could be produced. The lectures do not produce them. A condition of possibility is not a genesis. It says what must be in place if a unity is to form, not how it forms. The question of how unities actually form in the impressional present, which the first version had answered by the tone series, is now open, and it is opened in a form in which no case can be seen: the case would be a present that is only impressional, and no such present is given, though Husserl, as just quoted, thought one might approach it by experiment.

### 3.5 The rule of § 34

Section 34 is the place where Husserl faces this problem, and its procedure is that of a disputation. "Essential conditions of affection can mean two things" (159/207). Either the objective structure of the living present is presupposed, and affection runs along the connections it prescribes; or one tries another conception, in which affinity and contrast are only relations, not yet actual fusion, and one distinguishes "a) unconditionally necessary fusions" that take place in rigid lawfulness whatever affection does, from "b) fusions, unity-formations, which are owed only to affection. Let us see whether such a theory could be carried out" (160/208).

The theory is tried at full strength. The unconditionally necessary fusions would be those that constitute the rigid form of the field of presence, time and place; within each sense-field, special unities would form by the rigid lawfulness of continuity and contrast, "but does not affection here too presuppose accomplished work" (160–61/208), the special fusion that occurs when the conditions are fulfilled? And at the higher level of pluralities, the fusion at a distance of like with like might be owed to affection: like and like, affecting the ego at once, would gain a special unity first for the ego and then as an objective connection. "However, on closer reflection on the possible interpretations the untenability of the one which we have just let speak as far as possible results" (161–62/209). The cut it makes between lower and higher objects is unjustified, "because it is an unintelligibility that fusion should first be produced by unity of affection" (161–62/209); if fusion is intelligible from itself at the lower level, it is so at the higher, and the reverse.

The answer begins from what is given. "What is constituted in consciousness is there for the ego only in so far as it affects" (162/210). Unities for themselves constitute themselves intelligibly by the principles of concrescence and contrast, and "as unities for themselves they are eo ipso also for the ego, affecting it" (162/211). But is not this too bold? We do not always have noticeable affection. Here Husserl turns to what can be seen. "If we have once seen the phenomenon of the propagation of affection, we shall say: it meets us in evidence as a phenomenon of the modification of existing affection" (163/211). A weak affection becomes strong when an awakening affection radiates into it; a strong one becomes weak when its conditions change. "But that something should become an active something for the ego, a pure affective nothing, that is not exactly intelligible" (163/211). "If we follow the gradualness lying in the essence of affection, we remain in intelligibility and insight into essence, and we then have no reason to make unintelligible substructions (*Substruktionen*), with which the sphere of essence would eo ipso be overstepped" (163/211). "According to this methodological principle we therefore ascribe to every datum constituted as set off for itself an affective stimulus on the ego" (163/211).

I keep the German word once because a reader of the literature will meet it in two opposite senses (Part 4). A "substruction" is something put underneath what is given, to carry it, without being given itself. The rule of § 34 forbids substructions in this sense: units that form without any affection at all and only then affect the ego. It does not forbid going beyond what is noticed; the unnoticed is admitted, as the zero of a gradation whose other degrees are seen. The difference is the one the first version marked with its "it seems to me" and "I am compelled": the step from the seen to the unseen is allowed where the unseen is the limit of a series of which the seen members are given, and forbidden where it is a different kind of thing posited to explain the given.

The section then states what is at stake. "Decisive insights into the essence of association we can gain only if we bring to lawful understanding the function of affection" (163–64/211). "We had studied the unbreakable objective structure of the impressional living present without regard to differences of affection" (163–64/212); but "it is not said that these objectivities for their part already are before all affection" (164–65/213). The possibility is to be weighed that all the fusions and separations through which unities become in the field of the present need affective liveliness in order to become at all. "A radical theory which does justice in the same way to the concrete construction of the living present and to the construction of the single concretions themselves out of constitutive elements can solve the riddle of association and with it all riddles of the 'unconscious' and of the changing 'becoming conscious'" (165/214). The elements of such a construction would no longer be objects but phases of objects, "sensory points, so to speak". And then the rule in its general form: "On the other hand, all motives of a theory spring from the evidences of the first level, from those which for us are necessarily the first: the evidences that lie in the phenomena of the, so to speak, finished living present" (165/214). Husserl added a note to the result: "But under the presupposition that we moved precisely in the living present and had constituted concrete objects in it! Compare the new lecture and the clear results there" (165–66/213). The lecture ends: "Let us therefore begin systematically with this living present, whose concrete objective structure according to shape and content we already know" (166/214). A new lecture probably began a page earlier, at 163,27 (Anh. 469).

Two remarks qualify what I shall call the rule of § 34. The sentence on the motives of theory is stated as a fact about where theory gets its motives, not as a prohibition; the explicit "methodological principle" of the section, two pages earlier, says only that an affective stimulus is to be ascribed to every datum set off for itself. To read the two together as one norm, that a construction is admissible only where its motives come from the finished present and it stays within the gradation that is seen, is my step, and I mark it as such. And the version does not give up seeing: "hyletic objects constitute themselves, we said and proved, they are in constituting becoming" (165/213), and the propagation of affection "meets us in evidence". The becoming of a datum is seen here too, in the second sense; what is not seen is the formation of unities in a present without acquired apperceptions.

The section is the hinge of the version, and the hinge turns two ways. On the one hand it gives the version what the first version had not needed: a rule under which a genesis that is not seen may be asserted. The radical theory is allowed to construct the living present out of elements that are not objects and cannot be seen; but its motives must come from what is evident in the finished present, and its constructions must stay in the sphere of intelligibility that the gradation of affection opens. On the other hand the rule concedes that the evidence of the version is the evidence of the finished. The present it builds from below is known only as finished; the construction is answerable to the finished; and the theory that would solve the riddles of the unconscious is a theory, a word the first version had used for what the splitting of a memory is not.

### 3.6 Gradation, the null and the theoretical conception

Sections 35 to 39 carry the rule into the past. Retention is described as a process in which affective force sinks continuously: fresh retention passes steadily into empty retention, "one can call it the genetic primal form of empty presentations" (170/218), and the empty retention loses its inner differences until it presents what it presents without any. The following lecture opens with a description of what the last one did: "In the last lecture we began to sketch a theoretical conception which is called to make understandable to us how, in the living present ... an effect of the objectivities that are inexorably veiling themselves, and finally of those already wholly veiled, comes about" (173/222). The first version had said of the splitting of a memory that it is "no theory"; the new version calls its own account of the null a "theoretical conception".

The conception is presented with its history. "Formerly I thought that this retentional streaming and constituting of being-past went on incessantly even in full darkness. But it seems to me that one can do without this hypothesis. The process itself ceases" (177–78/226). What remains is a "never-ceasing typic": at the head of the living present "the primally instituting process" goes on for ever new objects (177–78/226); the retentional modification always leads into the one null; and every accomplishment of the living present is deposited in the dead or rather sleeping horizon "in the manner of a fixed order of sediments" (178/227). How what is so deposited can become effective again "is the problem of association" (178/227).

The answer to that problem returns, at the decisive point, to the seen case. "One knows of sleep only by waking; so also here, and wholly originally" (178/227). The sleeping sense is known only through its awakening, and its awakening is what recollection shows. "The motives must lie in the living present" (178/227). The three levels of association are then summed up: the first, primal association, "that systematic or systematizing affective awakening which makes possible the objective structure of the living present" (180/230); the second, the backward-radiating awakening that clarifies darkened empty presentations; the third, the passage of awakened empty presentations into recollections. "In any case the law holds: recollections can arise only through awakening of empty presentations" (181/231).

The structure of the version is now visible, and it is the reverse of the first. The first version went from recollection, where association is seen, back to primal association and the law of retention, which it reached by compulsion and postulate. The new version goes from primal association, which it builds under an as-if and a rule, up through the gradation of affection and the null to recollection, which it reaches last; and at the point where it has to say how the null can be known it says that it is known only by waking, that is, from the case with which the first version began. The genesis of the new version is constructed from below; its evidence still comes from above.

Is this to say that the new version is a mistake, and the first version right? Husserl's own verdict on the revision was that it had "partly improved, partly spoiled" the course (Einl. XXII). The improvement is plain: the new version has a theory of the present that the first lacked, and it states, in § 34, a rule of method that the first had followed without stating. What was spoiled, if anything was, is the order. The first version's rule, that association must be tackled from recollection, kept the theory at every point within reach of a seen genesis; the new version's order puts the seen genesis at the end, and needs a rule to keep the construction answerable to it. The new order is not wrong. It is the order of a system, and it costs what a system costs: its first steps are the ones that can least be seen.

### 3.7 February 1926

On 12 to 14 February 1926 (Anh. 524), in the semester in which the new version was given, Husserl wrote five leaves on fusion and put them in a wrapper marked "ad Logik von 1920". A keyword on the first leaf reads: "On genetic logic 1920 and the Bernau manuscripts on fusion etc." (Anh. 524). Fleischer printed them as Beilage XIX. They are the only text in the volume that uses the word *Abbau*, and they ask, in so many words, the question that the version of 1925/26 had left open.

The notes begin from the construction. Every concrete unity of the impressional present has connection and unity of liveliness, and in succession its building up is a continuous becoming; "but presupposed in this is the 'timeless' building-itself-up, the not-becoming one, in every momentary present" (413/515). Then the doubt: "Born blind and the moment of becoming seeing: is an ordered present already constituted in the moment?" (413/515). Could one not say instead that unities first concresce in the becoming continuity, and that only later can one see a structured impressional world at a glance, merely by waking and opening one's eyes? The "chaos" of the impressions organizes itself, "the impressions not yet objects, elements of reduction, genetic primal elements, to which the dismantling (*Abbau*) of intentionality and its genesis leads back" (413/516). "The syntheses make the objects according to principles that can be won only through 'analysis'. Given first and evident is always the 'finished' structure of the impressional world" with its unities and the affectivities that condition them (413/516). "How can one draw from this an ideal genesis out of chaos?" (414/516).

The notes go on to work out such a genesis: chaotic successions of momentary lines, sense-fields that need not care about one another, the conditions under which retention can hold anything at all. Their conclusion is a demand: "Such analyses, constructions of all the ideal, abstractive possibilities belonging here, must be carried out, in order to understand the structure of the affective formation of impressional present 'worlds'" (415/517). And then a correction: "A mistake in the lectures and also in the above" (415/517): the sense-fields are not, or need not be, chaotically connected, since they fuse in one time-shape.

The notes say three things that the lectures had not said. First, the given is always finished: the evidence from which any genetic theory of the present starts is the evidence of a structured present, as § 34 had said, and the elements to which the dismantling leads back are not themselves given but are "elements of reduction". Second, a genesis of the present can only be "drawn", as an "ideal genesis", and the procedure that draws it is "construction" of "abstractive possibilities". Third, this procedure is still to be carried out; the lectures, which have been building the present from below for three months, contain a mistake about its structure. The word *Abbau* here names the reverse of construction, the taking-apart of the finished structure into the elements that a construction would put together; it is introduced together with construction and as its correlate. And the case that tests the construction is a case of becoming that might be seen, the moment of a blind person's first sight, which the notes cannot settle.

The version of 1925/26 thus ends where the first version had begun, with the question of how the evidence of a genesis is related to its construction. The first version had answered it by staying close to seen geneses and marking every step beyond them. The second answered it by a rule, and the rule, as February showed, does not decide what a construction of the present is answerable to. To say what a genetic claim is and what makes it evident, the lecturer needed an account of genetic phenomenology as such. He had written one in 1921.

## 4. The programme of 1921 and its readers

The two versions of the course have now been read for what they do. This part sets them against Husserl's statements of method of 1921 and against four readers of the volume, and it does so around one question that all of them face: what makes a genetic claim evident? The question is not the one the received view asks. It asks whether the lectures carry out a history of the monad; this part asks what kind of evidence a claim of genesis can have, and whether the statements of 1921 and the readers give an answer that fits what the lectures do. The method is confrontation. Each text is first stated in its own terms; the parallels with the result of Parts 2 and 3 are given before the differences; and each reader is credited with what the reading has right before the step that does not hold is named.

### 4.1 The treatise

The treatise begins, as Part 1 noted, by distinguishing two senses of "laws of genesis". The first concerns the succession of single events in the stream of experience, for instance "the necessary attachment of retentions to events that have run off" (336/623), or the mediate succession governed by the laws of association. The second concerns the "lawfulnesses that regulate the formation of apperceptions" (337–38/623). An apperception is a consciousness that has something conscious as perceived which is not itself given in it; every present consciousness has retentional and protentional horizons and is in this sense apperceptive; and so, wherever in the stream something "arises from something", apperception arises from apperception. Some apperceptions must arise at every point of the stream; others can arise wherever their conditions are fulfilled; and types of complex apperceptions, once they are there, repeat themselves through the whole stream, "but they themselves according to their essence have a history, a genesis according to the primal laws" (339/627).

This history is then restricted, in the sentence the received view usually omits. "This 'history' of consciousness (the history of all possible apperceptions) does not concern the demonstration of factual genesis for factual apperceptions" (339/627), nor anything like the evolution of plant and animal species. Every shape of apperception is a shape of essence and has its genesis according to laws of essence, and what is given with the essential genesis is "only the mode of genesis in which any apperception of this type must originally have arisen in an individual stream of consciousness" (339/627). The stream of consciousness is "a stream of a constant genesis, not a mere succession but an out-of-one-another, a becoming according to laws of necessary succession" (339/628).

Against this the treatise sets the constitutive descriptions, in which "there is no question of an explanatory genesis" (340/628). "In a certain way, then, 'explanatory' phenomenology as the phenomenology of lawful genesis separates itself from 'descriptive' phenomenology as the phenomenology of the possible shapes of essence in pure consciousness, however they came to be" (340–41/628), and it adds that in the lectures Husserl had called the latter "static". In the sphere of the active ego, however, no shape of active apperception can be described "without there being constant talk of genesis" (342/630). The "primal laws of genesis are the laws of original time-consciousness, the primal laws of reproduction and then of association and associative expectation" (344/633). The question whether a systematic static phenomenology can be achieved, "whether therefore the genetic is to be excluded entirely from it" (344/633), is left open, and so is the order of the investigations; the types of object offer themselves as guiding clues, and "another guiding clue is the unity of a monad as unity of a genesis" (344/633). A later paragraph restates the main point in the plainest form: "Necessary successions in the open sphere of experience: what is coming is then not only coming but necessarily ensuing according to an evident law of necessary succession. One can of course call that a law of genesis" (344–45/634). In the "static" consideration we have "finished" apperceptions, which emerge and are awakened as finished "and have a 'history' lying far back" (344–45/634); "another 'constitutive' phenomenology, that of genesis, follows the history, the necessary history of this objectivation" (344–45/634).

Read for its answer to the question of this part, the treatise says two things, and they do not coincide. On the one hand a law of genesis is "evident": it is a law of necessary succession, seen in insight, and its examples are the laws of time-consciousness, retention and association that the lectures of 1925/26 call laws of passive genesis. On the other hand there is a "necessary history" of every apperception, which lies "far back" and is given only as the "mode" in which an apperception of a type "must have" arisen. The first kind of claim is evident in the way the attachment of retention to impression is evident: one sees it in every present. The second kind is a claim about what must have happened in a past that no present shows. The treatise does not say how the second is made evident. It says what it is, and that it is essential and not factual.

The lectures, read with the treatise in hand, answer the question the treatise leaves. In the first version the "necessary history" is approached from a seen genesis: the blend of two memories is seen to have arisen, and the conditions of its arising are reached by marked steps. In the second version the laws of the first kind are stated as structural laws of the present, and the history of the second kind is constructed under an as-if and checked by the rule of § 34. The treatise's "explanatory" knowledge is the lectures' "making-understandable" knowledge, and the lectures say how it is had.

### 4.2 Beilage I of Hua XIV

The companion text of 1921 puts the same distinction in a different order and asks the question of this part directly. "I always speak there of static and genetic phenomenology. What was the actually guiding point of view?" (Hua XIV 41). The answer starts from a case. I can take external perception, hold fast the meant object, set against its meant features the sensations and adumbrations in the perception, pursue the possibility of further perceptions of the same, follow the correlation of the unity of the appearing object and the multiplicity of appearances. "I do not ask here about genesis in the monad, about the way such phenomena arise" (Hua XIV 41). These are constitutive questions, and "to follow constitution is not to follow genesis, which is precisely genesis of constitution and moves as genesis in a monad" (Hua XIV 41). Static phenomenology is "precisely the phenomenology of guiding clues" (Hua XIV 41). Its correlations are essential belongings, "but that is no conditionedness of genesis; here the conditioned does not come to be from the conditioning" (Hua XIV 41). The phenomenology of genesis, by contrast, follows the original becoming in the time-stream and the "motivations" that function genetically, and "shows how consciousness becomes out of consciousness, how in becoming constitutive accomplishment is constantly also carried out" (Hua XIV 41), for instance in "the necessary passage from impression into retention" (Hua XIV 41).

The text also complicates its own criterion. "However, statically I describe not only the constitutive possibilities in relation to an object as guiding clue, I also describe the typic of the connections in consciousness of any stage of development" (Hua XIV 41), as in the *Ideas*; and a note asks "whether one must regard the structures of pure consciousness from the start as constitutive occurrences" (Hua XIV 41). Earlier in the same text the order of levels of a systematic phenomenology, "as I thought of it" (Hua XIV 38), from the time-stream up to the "genetically higher levels" of transcendence, nature and animals, is called genetic: "These are therefore genetic considerations, and, as description of finished constituted formations and their constitutions, placed in the connection of genetic research" (Hua XIV 38), and "in genesis their becoming from constitutive lower levels becomes intelligible" (Hua XIV 38). Beside the laws of compatibility there are laws "which do not merely concern compatibilities, but necessities of succession" (Hua XIV 40), and the primal law of genesis "is the law of original time-constitution, the laws of association and reproduction" (Hua XIV 39).

Beilage I thus offers a criterion of the genetic and then states a case that the criterion does not fit. The criterion is that in genesis the conditioned comes to be from the conditioning; in a correlation it does not. The case is the order of levels, which is described as finished and called genetic because in genesis "its becoming from constitutive lower levels becomes intelligible". The order of levels does not meet the criterion by itself: a description of the time-stream below and the thing above does not show the thing coming to be from the time-stream. It meets it only if it is "placed in the connection of genetic research", that is, if one shows how the higher comes to be from the lower. Husserl leaves the two statements side by side. I leave them so too; they are the fourth of the tensions this study does not resolve. Two further sentences of the text tell against too sharp a use of the criterion. The order of levels, Husserl goes on, also lays the ground "for a systematic doctrine of levels of monads", and "every higher monad is developed from a lower monad" (Hua XIV 38): the order is not only an order of foundation but, in principle, of development. And of static analysis itself he says: "I construct here essential possibilities" (Hua XIV 41). Construction is the ordinary procedure of eidetic analysis, static as well as genetic. What distinguishes the construction of 1925/26 is not that it constructs, but what it constructs: a becoming of the present no case of which is given.

### 4.3 Where the lectures stand

With these texts one can place the two versions of the course more exactly than the received view allows.

The first version stands where Beilage I puts its middle inquiry: it describes "isolated geneses" and "laws of genesis" without taking up the problem of the universal genesis of a monad (Hua XIV 38). The blend of two memories is an isolated genesis; the laws of awakening and of retention reached from it are laws of genesis. Their evidence is that of the first sense of the treatise's laws: necessary succession seen in a case. The question of the monad's history does not arise in it at all, and § 48 says that it would arise only when the same work is done for nature.

The second version stands where Beilage I puts systematic phenomenology. It follows the levels of constitution from the present upward and calls its order genetic; it presents its descriptions of the finished present as placed in the connection of genetic research; and it faces the problem the criterion of Beilage I raises, by constructing a genesis of the present from below under an as-if and requiring that the construction answer to the finished. The rule of § 34 is, read this way, the lectures' version of the criterion: a construction may be asserted only if what it says comes to be can be made intelligible from what is seen to be there.

Neither version takes up the history of a monad, though Beilage I says that the order of levels lays its ground. The treatise names that history as "another guiding clue" (344/633), Beilage I as the third kind of inquiry. The lectures do not take up the clue, and they do not claim to. What they "apply", if the word is to be kept, is the first sense of the treatise's laws of genesis, seen in recollection in 1920/21 and in the structure of the present in 1925/26; and what they aim at, in the guiding thought of § 48 and in the "universal theory of the genesis of a pure subjectivity" of the new § 26, is the second.

### 4.4 Holenstein

Holenstein's monograph is the reading of the volume that comes nearest to this result, and it should be credited first. He saw the rule of § 34 and read it as I have read it: units that form for themselves and only afterwards affect the ego "are substructions, with which the sphere of phenomenology is undercut" (*Phänomenologie der Assoziation*, 38), and Husserl puts pre-affective unity-formation "only up for discussion" in the lectures (38). He saw that the abstraction by which the passive sphere is reached "means, however, no constructing or postulating speculation" (80). He noted that Husserl operates "at least hypothetically" with the assumption of connections that have not become affective in some supplements to the lectures (38). And he drew from the treatise and from manuscripts of B III 10 the conclusion that "Husserl did not quite succeed in separating static and genetic phenomenology from each other without overlappings" (28), and that the two are "two abstractive or reductive modes of access to the same genetically growing phenomenon of manifold consciousness" (29).[^8]

The parallels with this study are plain. Holenstein has the rule, the non-speculative character of the abstraction, the overlap between static and genetic. The difference lies in one fact and one consequence. The fact is the dating. Holenstein regretted that the edition did not show how the new theory of association was distributed over 1923 and 1925/26 (12 n. 32); the editor's table shows that the whole of it belongs to 1925/26 (Anh. 446), and the first version is of 1920/21. The consequence is that the overlap he found between the static and the genetic is not only an overlap of definitions. It is also a difference within the genetic: a genesis seen, in the first version, and a genesis built, in the last. His "two abstractive modes of access to the same genetically growing phenomenon" are, in the lectures, not two modes but three, and the genetic mode has two forms that the course gave in two different winters. Holenstein did not draw this distinction because his question was the structure and function of association, not the evidence of genetic claims; on his question the layers matter less. On mine they decide.

### 4.5 Bégout

Bégout's *La Généalogie de la logique* is the fullest reading of the lectures in French and the nearest precedent to the thesis of this study, since it too finds that the genetic analysis of passivity works by abstraction and reconstruction. Bégout is right that "abstraction is 'necessary'" to the genetic analysis, as Husserl says of the abstraction to the impressional present; he is right that the theory of graded affection serves Husserl as a "guarantee of phenomenality", a means of keeping every level of constitution within reach of consciousness; and he is right, I think, that the gradation does not solve the problem it was introduced to solve but "only pushes it back to its ungraspable limit", the null of affection.[^9] This last point is a real objection to § 34, and it holds against the rule as I have read it: the rule allows the unnoticed as the zero of a series, but the zero is itself not seen, and to call it the limit of a gradation is to give it a place, not an evidence. I take the objection up again in Part 5 as the second of the tensions the lectures leave standing.

Three steps in Bégout's account do not hold against the text. The first is a matter of a word. Bégout says that the constructions by which the analysis exhibits its first elements are what Husserl "elsewhere" calls "substructions", and that they operate in the genetic phenomenology of passivity as unconditioned elements, "at once unearthed and produced" by the genealogical analysis.[^10] In Hua XI the word occurs, as far as the file shows, once, and it names what the methodological principle of § 34 excludes: "unintelligible substructions, with which the sphere of essence would eo ipso be overstepped" (163/211). Bégout quotes this sentence correctly later in the book, in his account of affection; the earlier use reverses it. The word Husserl uses in February 1926 for the procedure that produces such elements is "construction", and he says that the constructions "must be carried out" (415/517); the elements are "elements of reduction" (413/516), not substructions. The difference is not verbal. A substruction, in Husserl's sense, is a posit put under the given without being made intelligible from it; a construction of abstractive possibilities is answerable, by the rule of § 34, to the finished present. Bégout's "necessary conjecture" falls between the two, and the text of the lectures places it on the side of construction under a rule.

The second step is the date. Bégout reads the text as "the Lessons on genetic logic of 1921" throughout.[^11] The sections on which his account of abstraction rests, §§ 27–38, are written on leaves of 1925/26; the "as if" is of the same winter. Dated so, they cannot be read as the first application of a method formulated in 1921; they are a rebuilding of the course four years after the method was written down, and the rule of § 34 is part of the rebuilding.

The third step is the method. Bégout describes the regress of the genetic analysis as a "dismantling" (*démantèlement*, *Abbau*), and he describes it from manuscripts of the 1930s, in particular the manuscript C 6 of August 1930 "devoted precisely to the method of the *Abbau*".[^12] In Hua XI the word occurs once, in the notes of February 1926, where it names the taking-apart of the finished present into elements of reduction, together with the construction that would rebuild it. Welton, from another direction, attributes the opposition of a regressive "Abbau-Analyse" to a progressive "Aufbau-Analyse" to Fink's outline of 1930. The two readers together suggest a date for the vocabulary: it is the late Husserl's and his assistant's, and the lectures contain only its first appearance, in a question.

### 4.6 Steinbock, Welton, and the received view

Steinbock's introduction has two things right that the received view usually lacks. He sees that "Husserl had undertaken genetic analyses implicitly" before phenomenology was explicitly aware of its genetic dimension, and that Husserl "was provoked by the very matters themselves to catch up reflectively with his own descriptions" (*Analyses*, xxix); and he sees that in genetic phenomenology static phenomena are grasped as "'finished,' as abstractions from temporality" (xxxvi). Both observations agree with what the layers show. But he identifies the matter of genetic phenomenology with the monad: "The matter of genetic phenomenology, then, concerns monadic individuation and its genesis" (xxxii), and "it is not until we get to the problems of association and affection, and the individuation of the monad that the problem of genesis really comes into play" (xxxii). He knows the history of the text: "Parts 1 and 2 of the Main Text date back to 1920/21" (xxvi), Husserl reworked them in 1923 and, in his words, "partly improved, partly spoiled" §§ 12–40 in 1925/26, and the translation prints the first version for that reason. But his account of the genetic method does not use the dating of the leaves; it is drawn from the texts of 1921 and read into the lectures as a whole. The lectures he introduces do not take up the individuation of the monad, and the stretch on which his account of the method relies is of 1925/26.

Welton's reading is more careful on the first point. He places constitutive analysis between static and genetic analysis, and says that "Husserl's account of passive synthesis moves through his constitutive to his genetic analysis" (*The Other Husserl*, 253); the Hua XIV sentence that to follow constitution is not to follow genesis "locates the difference between constitutive and genetic phenomenology" (252). He also says that the transcendental aesthetic of the period "was itself a piece of genetic analysis" (220). The first two claims come close to the distinction of this study, and Welton deserves credit for seeing that the lectures do two things and not one. But he states the genetic as development: genetic phenomenology schematizes transformations "as a process of development in which the earlier gives rise to the later" (254). That is the treatise's "necessary history", and the lectures, on my reading, give it only as the idea of § 48 and as a construction under a rule. Welton's own observation that the regressive and progressive analyses are Fink's contrast of 1930 (227) supports the study's dating of the vocabulary; he does not apply it to the lectures.

Bernet, Kern and Marbach close their chapter with the judgement that "Husserl did not elaborate clearly enough the methodology of genetic" constitutional analysis (*Introduction*, 196). The judgement is true, and the lectures show where the methodology was unclear: in the relation between a genesis seen in a case and a genesis built from below, which the course handled differently in two winters and which Husserl, in February 1926, put as a question.

### 4.7 A test on a text not yet read

The plan of this study named one text in the holdings that had not been read when the thesis was fixed: §§ 40–41, on the motivation of expectation and on its strengthening and inhibition (pp. 184–191). If the thesis holds, Husserl should present the genesis of an expectation as seen, as a motivation given with the expectation itself, and should mark any step beyond it into conditions that are not seen. The sections are of two layers: their opening is of 1925/26, the analysis of the induction of expectation from the distant past (187,6–190,33) of 1920/21.

The opening of 1925/26 places expectation after the structure of the present: the future "does not create experiential unities in the original sense, it presupposes them" (185/235), and "with expectation we enter at the same time the genetically most original source-domain of modalization" (185–86/236). The protention of a continuing tone is "a necessary motivation" (185–86/236). The leaves of 1920/21 then take a case of induction: circumstances U and an event q in the distant past, similar circumstances now, and the expectation of a similar event. If the awakening has become a clear recollection, the coming of the new event is given as to be expected "in original evidence. We can here see directly the causality of motivation as a necessity; we can say in evidence: I expect q' here because I have experienced q in similar circumstances, and this because-so is evidently given" (188/238). Where the past is not clearly recollected, things are otherwise: "In the empty retentional sphere, however, the forces sum and inhibit each other, and with them also the forces of expectation, blind like those drives" (189/240). And the fourth section of the course, written in the same winter, begins: "It is questionable how far insight into essence reaches into this dark sphere of retention. It is otherwise in the realm of memories already developed reproductively" (191–92/242).

The prediction is confirmed, and more literally than it was stated. The leaves of 1920/21 say that the motivation of an expectation can be seen as a necessity where the motivating past is clearly recollected, and they set against this the empty sphere, in which the forces are "blind" and the reach of insight is "questionable". The genesis of an expectation is seen where it is seen in recollection, and only there; beyond it the text marks its own limit. The pages of 1925/26 that open the section speak of the genetic origin of modalization and of necessary motivation in the protention of a tone, that is, in the second sense, a structure of the present. The pages that follow come closest to the history of apperceptions that the treatise names. The force of expectation grows "with the number" of inductive "instances" (187–88/238), "or with habit, which is the same" (190/240); past configurations, awakened, are overlaid on the present, and new ones like them are "'known', not individually but according to their type" (190/241); "the apperceptive unity that has come to be" is surrounded by a horizon of the long familiar (191/241). This is the formation of types, the treatise's second law of genesis, carried out in general form, on leaves partly of 1920/21 and partly of 1925/26. It is stated as a law of what happens wherever instances accumulate, not narrated as the history of an ego; but it shows that the lectures do give the formation of apperceptions a place, at the level of law, and the claim that they leave the history of apperceptions wholly undone has to be narrowed to this: they give its law, not its course.

The confrontation thus gives one answer to the question of this part. A genetic claim in these lectures is evident when it is seen in a case, an awakening, a blend, a motivated expectation, and the seeing always takes place in recollection or in the structure of the present; it is admissible, without being seen, when it is a construction answerable to what is seen. A "necessary history" lying far back is neither seen nor, in the lectures, constructed. But there is a reading on which all this is history after all, and the next part gives it its due.

## 5. History after all? The opposing reading and what remains

The result of the first four parts can be stated against the received view in one sentence: the lectures do not narrate a history of the monad or of its objects; they exhibit geneses, seen in recollection and in the structure of the present, and construct a genesis of the present under a rule that ties it to what is seen. There is a reading of the volume on which this sentence is true and changes nothing, because the "history" of the received view was never a narrative and the exhibition of geneses is exactly what it means. That reading is the strongest objection to the thesis of this study, and it must be given at full strength before it is answered. This part does so, says what is right in it, says what does not follow, tests the thesis on the case the opposing reading would choose, and then turns to what the lectures leave undone.

### 5.1 The opposing reading

The opposing reading starts from Husserl's own restriction of the word. The "history" of consciousness, the treatise says, "does not concern the demonstration of factual genesis for factual apperceptions" (339/627); what is given with the essential genesis is "only the mode of genesis" in which an apperception of a type must have arisen (339/627). History in the phenomenological sense was never the narration of what happened, to whom and when; it was always the essential order in which shapes of consciousness presuppose and arise from one another. The lectures of 1925/26 say the same of the "essentially earlier" (74/117): the empty presentation precedes the intuitive memory not in fact but by essence. The closing reflection defines history as "a constituting of ever higher formations of sense, by levels" (219/270). Beilage I calls the order of levels of a systematic phenomenology "genetic considerations" (Hua XIV 38). If history means this, then the stratification of the living present in §§ 27–35 is not a substitute for history; it is a piece of it, the lowest.

The opposing reading then reads the as-if as an ordinary step of eidetic method. Every analysis of a dependent moment isolates it by abstraction; the analysis of time-consciousness, Husserl says himself in 1925/26, "is an abstract one from the outset" (128/173), because it abstracts from content; the momentary phase, he says in 1920/21, is "an abstraction that we must first form" (112/155). The "we act as if" of § 32 is no different. It is the abstraction by which the lowest level is isolated so that it can be described, and the "systematic genesis" it serves is the essential history of the present described level by level. Nothing is constructed that is not seen; the abstraction only holds apart what is given together.

Third, the opposing reading points to concepts in the lectures that are historical in the plain sense. At the head of the living present, Husserl says, "the primally instituting process" goes on for ever new objects (177–78/226), and every accomplishment of the present is deposited "in the manner of a fixed order of sediments" (178/227). Primal institution and sedimentation are the concepts by which the later Husserl describes the history of sense, and they are at work here, in 1925/26. "One knows of sleep only by waking" (178/227) says that the history of the ego is known from its traces, as any history is.

Fourth, the opposing reading cites the later texts. *Formal and Transcendental Logic* (1929) says that static analysis is guided by the unity of the meant object, whereas genetic intentional analysis "is directed at the whole concrete connection in which every consciousness and its intentional object as such stands" (Hua XVII 316), and that a category of objects is first instituted for us "in an earlier primally instituting genesis" (Hua XVII 317). The *Cartesian Meditations* (1929) say that the phenomenology first developed "is merely static" (Hua I 111), that every synthesis has "its history announcing itself in it" (Hua I 112), and that this history can be found "without our putting ourselves back on the ground of passivity" (Hua I 112), from the intentional references of what is finished. On this reading the later texts state the method that the lectures practise: the history is read off the finished phenomenon by its references, and the lectures do precisely this when they tie every motive of theory to the finished present. Steinbock's remark that genetic phenomenology grasps static phenomena as "'finished,' as abstractions from temporality" (*Analyses*, xxxvi) says the same. The received view, rightly understood, stands.

### 5.2 What is right in it

Much of this is right, and it changes the thesis in one respect.

It is right that "history" in Husserl's sense is essential and not factual, and that the received view, when it speaks of the history of the monad, means at best this essential history. It is right that the genesis seen in the first version, the formation of a blend from two memories, is a piece of essential history: it shows how a shape of consciousness, a false memory, arises from others, and it shows this as a necessity of essence. It is right that the construction of the present in 1925/26 is meant to be an essential history of the lowest level, and that its as-if is an abstraction of a dependent moment. And it is right that primal institution and sedimentation are at work in the lectures.

The thesis must therefore be stated more carefully than a first reading of the layers suggests. Seeing and constructing occur in both versions: the first version gives blind forces and a genetic development of awakenings that no one sees, the second claims to have proved that hyletic objects constitute themselves in becoming and thinks its lowest level might be approached by experiment. What the versions differ in is which way of exhibiting a genesis comes first and serves as the standard for the other. The lectures are not without history in the sense of essential history; what they lack is a history narrated, a "necessary history" lying "far back" (344–45/634) that is set out as a course from earlier to later in a monad. Essential history is what they aim at, in the guiding thought of § 48 and in the "universal theory of the genesis of a pure subjectivity" of the new § 26. What they exhibit, to that end, is geneses of two kinds, and it is the difference between the two kinds that the opposing reading cannot accommodate.

### 5.3 What does not follow

The opposing reading treats the seen genesis and the constructed genesis as one, the essential history of the present taken at different levels. Husserl does not. Three passages separate them, and a fourth asks how they are related.

The new opening of § 26 says that one sees the "recalling something" "only in certain cases", the cases of reproductive association between objects already set off (121/165), and it puts these cases, as what is first for us, before the theory that is to reach what is first in itself. Section 34 says that the theory of the lowest level is a theory, that its elements are not objects, and that "all motives of a theory spring from the evidences of the first level", those of the "finished living present" (165/214). Beilage I gives a criterion by which a correlation, however much it orders levels, "is no conditionedness of genesis" (Hua XIV 41). And the notes of February 1926 ask, of the finished structure of the impressional world, "how can one draw from this an ideal genesis out of chaos?" (414/516). If the stratification of the present were simply the essential history of the present, seen level by level, none of these sentences would be needed. They are needed because the construction of the present from below is not seen in the way the awakening of a memory is seen, and Husserl knew it.

The opposing reading's second step fails for the same reason. The abstraction of § 32 is indeed the abstraction of a dependent moment. But the abstraction of a dependent moment, in ordinary eidetic method, isolates something that is given in the concrete whole and can be pointed to in it: the colour of a surface, the form of time. The "lowest genetic level" of § 32 is not given in the concrete present; it is the present as it would be if it contained no acquired apperceptions, and no present is like that. The as-if does not isolate a moment of the present; it posits a present. That is why § 33 can say of its result only that it gives "conditions of the possibility" of unity-formation (152/200), and why § 34 has to ask whether unity-formation itself needs affection, a question that no inspection of the abstracted level can settle.

The third step concerns the concepts of primal institution and sediment. They are at work in §§ 37–38, and they are historical in form: something is instituted at a time and deposited for later. But what the lectures say with them stays within the two kinds of genesis already distinguished. The "primally instituting process" at the head of the living present (177–78/226) is the arising of ever new impressions and their crystallizing into objects, a genesis in the second sense that can be seen in any present. The "fixed order of sediments" (178/227) is the null into which retention flows, and of it the lectures say only that it is known by waking. Neither concept is used to describe how an ego acquires a habit, a type or a category; the acquisition of apperceptive types is named in the new § 26 among the tasks of the theory of association, as the genesis of expectations and apperceptions, and is given in §§ 40–41 only as a law of the formation of types (Part 4.7). The historical form of the concepts is real; their content, in these lectures, is the present and its limit.

The fourth step concerns dates. The passages from *Formal and Transcendental Logic* and the *Cartesian Meditations* are of 1929, three years after the last version of the course and eight after the first. They state a method of reading history off the finished phenomenon, and the lectures of 1925/26, with their rule, come close to it. But they cannot tell us what the lectures did, and the first version, which tackled association from recollection and saw a genesis in the splitting of a memory, does not read a history off a finished phenomenon; it watches a phenomenon come apart. The later texts may describe the method that the version of 1925/26 was working towards. They do not describe the method of the course.

The hardest case for the thesis is the one the opposing reading would choose: the living present itself, which Husserl calls "the most universal genetic phenomenon" (137–38/184). Here the two kinds of genesis seem to meet. The attachment of retention to impression is a genesis seen, in every present, as a necessary succession; the formation of unities in the present is a genesis constructed, under the as-if and the rule. The living present is both: it is where one sees genesis happen, and it is what the construction builds. If the thesis were that the seen and the constructed are always in different places, this case would refute it. The thesis is that they are different kinds of evidence, and in this case they lie on top of one another. What the case shows is that the version of 1925/26 can call the living present genetic in two senses at once and that it does not separate them: the sentence of § 31 says that the present "is itself the most universal genetic phenomenon" and goes on to describe its "structure". I do not think the text resolves this. It is the first of the tensions the study leaves standing: whether the living present is a genesis, or the finished ground of every genetic claim, or both in senses the text does not distinguish.

The second tension follows from it, and it is Bégout's objection. The rule of § 34 allows the unnoticed as the zero of a gradation of affection, and in §§ 36–37 the zero becomes the "null" into which retention flows and from which awakening draws. But the null is not seen; it is known, as Husserl says, only by waking. The rule makes it intelligible as a limit; it does not give it an evidence of its own. Husserl himself, on Holenstein's report, operates in some supplements "at least hypothetically" with connections that never became affective, the very assumption the rule excludes. The lectures end the stretch with the law that recollections arise only from awakened empty presentations, and leave the status of what lies below the awakening as they found it: a postulate made intelligible by a series, not a seen genesis.

### 5.4 The vocabulary, dated

The opposing reading draws strength from a vocabulary of genesis that seems to run through Husserl's work from the *Logical Investigations* to the *Crisis*. Dated, the vocabulary tells a more articulated story, and the lectures have a definite place in it.

The pair "static" and "genetic" is older than the course. A text of 1916/17 printed in Hua XIII carries a note in which Husserl speaks of what he had "elsewhere also called (unsuitably) static phenomenological constitution", and then of "a first attempt at the doctrine of genesis according to general principles. Genetic constitution" (Hua XIII 351). The note is not dated and may be later than the text; but the text itself, which Husserl afterwards retitled "Thoughts towards a genetic phenomenology", shows the question four years before the course. Bernet and Lohmar, in their introduction to the Bernau manuscripts, place the breakthrough to genetic phenomenology in 1917/18, not in the course of 1920/21.[^13] The course does not invent the pair. It uses it, in 1920/21, at its joints.

The texts of 1921 reflect on the course. The treatise says that "in the lectures" Husserl had said "static" for what it now calls "descriptive"; its manuscript corrects "causation" to "genesis"; it ends with the resolution to go through the *Ideas* again. Beilage I asks what the guiding point of view had been. Both texts state the method in terms that fit the first version only in part: they speak of laws of genesis in a sense the first version used, and of a history of apperceptions it did not undertake.

The version of 1925/26 rebuilds the course under the title of genesis and states a rule of method for the construction it undertakes. The notes of February 1926 name the procedure "construction" and its correlate *Abbau*, and ask how the one is to be had from the other.

The texts of 1929, *Formal and Transcendental Logic* and the *Cartesian Meditations*, speak of a history that announces itself in the finished phenomenon and of a primal institution of every category of objects. *Erfahrung und Urteil*, finally, was assembled by Landgrebe from manuscripts of the course and others, and its Introduction, with its account of the *Abbau* of all the sediments of sense in the world of present experience, "was only now drafted", after 1935, partly from manuscripts "of the years 1929–1934" (EU VIII–IX).[^14] Its § 16 repeats, in Landgrebe's redaction, the sentence of 1925/26 on association as a phenomenon that "gives itself in itself as genesis" (EU 77). The vocabulary on which the opposing reading draws is thus, in large part, later than the course; and where it is not later, as in § 16 of *Erfahrung und Urteil*, it is the vocabulary of the version of 1925/26, not of the course of 1920/21.

### 5.5 What the lectures leave undone

The lectures leave undone what they say they leave undone, and the list is itself evidence of what kind of genetic phenomenology they practise.

They leave the future undone. Fleischer's summary is exact: the analyses of awakening and of the overlaying of memories allow Husserl to solve the task for the past of consciousness, while "the future of consciousness keeps the character of the problem" (Einl. XXI). The analysis of expectation in §§ 40–41 shows why. An expectation is seen to be motivated where its motivating past is clearly recollected; in the empty sphere its forces are blind. A genetic phenomenology whose evidence lies in recollection can reach the future only through the past it recollects.

They leave nature undone. Section 48 says that what has been achieved for the self-objectivation of consciousness with respect to its past "would now have to be achieved for the constitution of nature" (220/271); the analyses before Christmas had given only a static understanding of the finished phenomena of external nature. The genetic task that the end of § 4 announced "in a word" for the outer world (24/61) is not carried out in the course.

They leave the active part undone, in the sense that the version of 1925/26 did not read it. In the part that Husserl gave in 1920/21 and Breeur edited, "genesis" has the third of the senses distinguished in Part 1. Insights into "the structure of judgments as finished formations of determining action and insights into the a priori genesis" (Hua XXXI 66) go together; "our principle, to understand objectivation as a genetic sequence of levels of thematizing accomplishments" (Hua XXXI 67), must be carried through to the last. Genesis here is the a priori order in which higher forms of judgment are built on lower; it is neither seen in a case nor constructed under an as-if, but stated as the order of a system. Whether this part would have been rebuilt, as the passive part was, under the rule of § 34, the text does not say.

And they leave the monad undone. Neither the treatise's "other guiding clue", the unity of a monad as the unity of a genesis, nor Beilage I's phenomenology of monadic individuality is taken up in the course. The question whether the genetic can be excluded from a static phenomenology at all, which the treatise leaves open (344/633), is the third tension the study leaves standing; the lectures neither exclude it nor take it up as the history of a monad. The fourth, Beilage I's two statements on the genetic character of the order of levels, was shown in Part 4. The fifth is Husserl's own: whether the revision improved the course or spoiled it. He wrote both, "partly improved, partly spoiled" (Einl. XXII), and of the leaves he removed, that they had "not always" been removed "for good reasons" (Anh. 480). On the reading of this study the improvement was the rule of § 34 and the theory of the present; what was spoiled, if anything, was the order in which the seen came first.

The title of this study asked whether the lectures give a genesis without history. The answer is yes, in a precise sense. The lectures give no history narrated, of the monad or of its objects, and they claim none: the "never-ending history" of § 48 is a guiding thought, and the formation of types in §§ 40–41 is given as a law, not as a course. What they give is genesis made evident in two ways, by seeing it, in the awakening and the splitting of memories and in the necessary succession of the present, and by constructing it, from below, under an as-if and a principle that binds every motive of the construction to what is evident in "the, so to speak, finished living present". Both versions use both ways; the first puts the seen case first, the second the construction. "Genetic" in these lectures is therefore first a title for a kind of evidence, the evidence of making understandable how something comes about, and only second a title for a domain. The limit of the result is the null of affection and the future, where neither kind of evidence reaches and the lectures postulate what they cannot see. But the credit belongs to Husserl, and it is not small. In the winter of 1920/21 he wrote that the coming apart of a false memory "is no theory, but a state of affairs intelligible in its intentionality"; in the winter of 1925/26, rebuilding the course as a theory, he wrote the principle by which the theory stays answerable to such states of affairs; and in February 1926 he asked, of his own construction, the question that a later reader would have to ask of it. A genetic phenomenology that knows the difference between what it has seen and what it has built is not the history of consciousness its programme promised. It is the method by which such a history, if it can be had, would have to be made evident.

### 12. The prediction

Text: Hua XI §§ 40–41 (pp. 184–191), not read before the plan was fixed. Predicted: the genesis of an expectation would be presented as seen, a motivation given with the expectation, and steps beyond it marked. Result: confirmed, more literally than predicted. The leaves of 1920/21 say "We can here see directly the causality of motivation as a necessity ... this because-so is evidently given" (188/238), where the motivating past is clearly recollected; in the empty sphere the forces are "blind" (189/240), and how far insight reaches there "is questionable" (191–92/242). The pages of 1925/26 speak of necessary motivation in protention, a structure of the present. Effect on the thesis: it confirms that the seen genesis of the first version is tied to recollection, and it supplies the clearest single statement of the distinction in Husserl's own words (Part 4.7).

### 13. The review

A reviewer in a fresh context, given the study, the plan, the evidence table and the source files, argued the opposing reading, re-checked eleven quotations, and named the weakest claim.

**Opposing reading.** The reviewer argued that seeing and building occur in both layers: on leaves of 1920/21 the blind summing of forces (189) and the "genetic development" of reproductive association with pushed-down retentions (196); on leaves of 1925/26 the propagation of affection given "in evidence" (163), "we said and proved" (165), and the proposal of an experiment in the very paragraph of the "as if" (151). It pointed to pp. 190–91, where the formation of types with habit is carried out as a law; to Hua XIV 38 (the order of levels as the ground of a doctrine of monadic development) and 41 ("I construct here essential possibilities"); and to the course's announced ascent "from below" (Hua XVII 374) in 1920/21.

**Weakest claim named:** that "the layers of the text keep apart" the two ways of genesis. **Revision:** granted. The thesis now says that both versions use both ways and put them in opposite orders; the difference is one of order and of the standard to which a genetic claim is first held (opening; 2.4, new paragraph on unmarked constructions in 1920/21; 3.4, the experiment; 3.5, "we said and proved", and the reading of § 34's two sentences as one norm, marked as mine; 4.2, the two omitted sentences of Hua XIV 38 and 41; 4.7, pp. 190–91 and the narrowed claim "they give its law, not its course"; 5.2; close). The announced ascent of 1920/21 is now used: the reversal of 1925/26 brings the theory of association into the order the course had announced.

**Quotations checked:** C3 (116), M6 (270), M10 (272–73), G1 (151), G19 (165), L5 with R12 (219; Anh. 478), L9/L10 (220), R11 (Anh. 479), S1 and S12–S15 (Hua XIV 38, 41), Y1 (Holenstein 12 n. 32), and in addition ST7 (Steinbock xxvi) and Bégout. Wording correct in all. Problems found and revised: (1) C3 closes the second section, it does not open the fourth (fixed in 1.3, 2.1 and the introduction to Part 2); (2) M10 stands on a single D 19 leaf marked "probably not read" (Anh. 482; now said in 2.4); (3) G1's paragraph also proposes an experiment (added in 3.4); (4) G19 is descriptive, and the explicit "methodological principle" is at 163 (3.5 now says so and marks the combination as mine; "rule" replaced by "principle" in the opening and the close); (5) **the blue bracket covers only 219,5–7, "History in the ordinary sense ... prefigured", not "a never-ending history"** (Anh. 478, notes to 219,4 and 219,5): the first draft's claim that Husserl bracketed the never-ending history, and its gloss on the meaning of blue brackets, were false and are removed (2.5, close); (6) the retrospect sentences 219,33 f. and 219,38–220,6 are Husserl's insertions, undated (Anh. 478–79; now said in 2.5); (7) R11 continues as a statement of the genetic programme, and "ergänzend" is now rendered "complementary" (2.5); (8) only the first leaf of Hua XIV Beilage I is dated June 1921, the leaves from 38,39 are "in all probability" of 1921 (note 4a; "June 1921" removed for them); (9) Steinbock's p. xxvi does state the reworking of §§ 12–40, so the charge that he dates the stretch to 1920/21 was unfair (4.6 rewritten).

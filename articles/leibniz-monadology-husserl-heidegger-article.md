# Why the Monad Needs No Window

## Leibniz's Anticipation of Phenomenology in Husserl and Heidegger

It is commonly held that the phenomenologists take a word from Leibniz and little else. On this view Husserl's "monad" is a name for the concrete ego, chosen for its associations, and Heidegger's pages on the *Monadology* belong to his history of the concept of substance. Husserl himself gives the view its strongest form. At the end of the Fifth Cartesian Meditation he reports the objection that his step to other subjects is possible only through "a secret adoption of Leibnizian traditions", and he answers that his monadology, "for all its deliberate echoes of Leibniz's metaphysics", draws its content purely from phenomenological explication (Hua I, 174, 176–77). Yet both authors say more than the view allows. In the lectures on First Philosophy Husserl calls the doctrine of monads "one of the most magnificent anticipations in history" and says that Leibniz "grasped the fundamental properties of intentionality" (Hua VII, 197). Heidegger says in 1927 that Leibniz "undoubtedly had a genuine phenomenon in view" with the windowlessness of the monads (GA 24, 428), and in the winter of 1928/29 that the monad is "one of the boldest ideas ever to emerge in philosophy since Plato" (GA 27, § 19). An anticipation is more than a word, and a genuine phenomenon is more than a chapter in the history of substance.[^1]

In what follows I wish to show what Leibniz anticipated and where the anticipation ends. I wish to show that three theses of the *Monadology* are recognised by Husserl and by Heidegger as descriptions of what phenomenology later exhibits: that perception is the representing of a manifold in a unity and of what is not really contained in the one who perceives; that each monad has the one universe from a point of view; and that nothing enters a monad from outside. I also wish to show that the two authors agree about the first two theses and divide over the third. Husserl holds that the monad is closed only as regards its real contents and that it has windows; Heidegger holds that it has none, and that Leibniz gave the wrong reason. Finally I wish to show that this division has one root, which is the judgement each author passes on the Cartesian ego in Leibniz.

The comparison has a measure because both authors ask one question, and it is Leibniz's own. § 7 of the *Monadology* says that the monads have no windows through which anything could enter or leave. Why does the monad need no window? Husserl asks the question in a manuscript of 1920 and answers against Leibniz: "every soul-monad has infinitely many windows" (Hua XIII, 473). Heidegger asks it in three lecture courses and answers with Leibniz, for another reason: the monad needs no window "because it is already outside" (GA 24, 427). The alternative can be stated plainly. Either the relation of a subject to its world and to others is added to a sphere that is first its own, and then the sphere needs an opening. Or the relation belongs to what the subject is, and then there is nothing to open.

The article rests on a closed body of texts. For Leibniz I rely on the paragraph-by-paragraph commentary on the *Monadology* held in the same archive as this article, and on the passages from the letters to de Volder and des Bosses that Heidegger cites. For Husserl I use the lectures on First Philosophy of 1923/24 with their Appendix XVIII (Hua VII), four texts from the manuscripts on intersubjectivity, dated between 1920 and 1925 (Hua XIII and XIV), the Paris Lectures, and §§ 33, 55–56, 60 and 62 of the *Cartesian Meditations* (Hua I). For Heidegger I use § 20 of the course of the summer of 1927 (GA 24), § 5 and the end of § 12 of the course of the summer of 1928 (GA 26), and §§ 19–20 of the course of the winter of 1928/29 (GA 27). I leave aside Husserl's third volume on intersubjectivity and Heidegger's treatment of Leibniz after 1930, in the Nietzsche lectures and in *The Principle of Reason*. The restriction has a reason and a cost. The reason is that the question of the window is asked in the texts named, and within one decade. The cost is that Leibniz is read here at one remove, and that I can say nothing of what Heidegger later made of the monad as the beginning of the modern metaphysics of the subject.

## 1. Three theses of the *Monadology*

The first thesis concerns what a monad has within it. The monad is simple, that is, without parts (§ 1), and yet it changes continually (§ 10). Since it has no parts among which a change could take place, it must contain "a multiplicity within the unity" that is a multiplicity of affections and relations and not of parts (§ 13). Leibniz gives this its name: "The passing state that envelops and represents a multiplicity in the unity or in the simple substance is nothing other than what is called *Perception*" (§ 14). Two things are said of perception so defined. It is distinguished from apperception or consciousness, and the Cartesians are blamed for having "counted for nothing the perceptions of which one is not aware" (§ 14). And it cannot be explained "by figures and movements": if we could walk through a thinking machine as through a mill, we should find parts pushing one another and never a perception (§ 17). To perception Leibniz adds appetition, "the action of the internal principle that brings about the change or passage from one perception to another". Appetite does not always reach the whole perception at which it aims, "but it always obtains something and arrives at new perceptions" (§ 15). Each state follows from the one before, so that "the present is pregnant with the future" (§ 22).

The second thesis concerns what is perceived. Each simple substance has relations that express all the others, and is therefore "a perpetual living mirror of the universe" (§ 56). The example by which Leibniz explains this will be carried through the present article. "Just as the same city viewed from different sides appears entirely different and is as if multiplied perspectively", so there are, through the multitude of simple substances, as many different universes, "which are however only the perspectives of a single one, according to the different points of view of each monad" (§ 57). § 60 states the limit that belongs to a point of view. No monad represents only a part of things; each goes "confusedly to the infinite, to the whole". The monads are limited "not in the object but in the modification of the knowledge of the object", by the degrees of their distinct perceptions, and what each perceives distinctly is what is nearest to it, above all its own body (§ 62).

The third thesis is negative. There is no way to explain how a monad could be changed in its interior by another creature: "Monads have no windows through which anything could enter or leave" (§ 7). The sentence has a definite opponent. Accidents cannot detach themselves from substances and wander about outside them, "as the sensible species of the Scholastics once did". What Leibniz excludes, as the commentary says, is any theory that has something literally passing from one substance into another. From this the first thesis receives its ground, since the natural changes of a monad must then "come from an internal principle" (§ 11).

The three theses together raise a difficulty that Leibniz sees and answers. If nothing passes between monads, how do their perspectives agree, so that they are perspectives of one city? The influence of one monad on another is "only an ideal influence", which has its effect "only through the intervention of God", who in regulating each from the beginning had regard to all the others (§ 51). The agreement of the points of view is thus assured from outside all points of view. One further paragraph must be recorded, because both phenomenologists return to it. It is by reflexive acts, Leibniz says, that we think of what is called "I", and "in thinking of ourselves, we think of Being, Substance, the simple and the composite, the immaterial, and God himself" (§ 30). The concepts of metaphysics are drawn from the self. We must now ask which of these theses Husserl recognises, and what he puts in the place of the answer that Leibniz gave to the difficulty.

## 2. Intentionality and the windows of empathy: Husserl, 1920–1931

Husserl's fullest judgement on Leibniz stands in the lectures on First Philosophy, in a passage on the lack of method in the theories of knowledge of the eighteenth century. Leibniz and Kant, he says, do not study the phenomena of knowing; they construct conditions under which knowledge would be possible, and the structure of sensibility and of thinking is "not really investigated but postulated". The sentence on Leibniz follows as a concession. An intuitive thinker of this kind invents nothing for which his imagination could not anticipate a fitting intuition, "and so his whole doctrine of monads is one of the most magnificent anticipations in history. Whoever fully understands it cannot but accord it a great content of truth" (Hua VII, 197). Husserl then says what is anticipated. Under the titles of perception, of the "striving transition from perception to perception", and in particular of the "representation of what is not really present and is yet perceptually conscious", Leibniz "grasped the fundamental properties of intentionality and worked them up metaphysically". The three titles are those of our first thesis. The many-in-one of § 14 is read as the relation of an experience to what it means, appetition as the tending of one experience towards the next, and representation as the having of something that is not a real part of the one who has it.

Appendix XVIII to the same lectures, which the editor dates to about 1924, extends the recognition to the second thesis. Leibniz, Husserl writes there, holds fast to the Cartesian demonstration that all nature is there for the ego only through its own ideas, and he rejects the inference to a second kind of substance. The being of minds suffices. "The reality of the physical receives the mere sense of an identical *ideatum*, of the same intentional object" of the representations that the different monads have of something physical (Hua VII, 365). This is the city of § 57, described in Husserl's vocabulary: one object, which is one as what is meant in many perceivings and is nothing behind them. Yet the same appendix states the limit. "To the greatness of this anticipation there corresponds no scientific execution." Leibniz has no notion that intentionality could be a theme of science, or of how a reality could "constitute itself as an intentional unity". His procedure is "naively objectivistic": the world is given in the natural sense, science knows its truth, and the monadology "only reinterprets" all of it. The plurality of the monads and their commerce are taken over "naively dogmatically" from the empirical intercourse of human beings, and what is taught of the monad is not drawn "from the study of the pure ego" (Hua VII, 366). The verdict is severe: any monadology that precedes the pure science of the *ego cogito* is "evidently absurd" (Hua VII, 367).

An earlier lecture of the same course shows how exactly this limit is meant. Husserl there compares Leibniz with Berkeley, and the comparison goes against Leibniz. Berkeley's theory, however primitive, is "a first theory of transcendence on the ground of immanence", the first attempt to determine the sense of the experienced world from the experiences themselves. Leibniz's doctrine "has the style of a metaphysical interpretation of mathematical natural science", undertaken to reconcile the truth of that science with the truth of theology; it is "an aperçu of genius" (Hua VII, 153–54). The two are close in their result and far apart in their grounding. For Husserl, then, Leibniz saw the right structures from the wrong place. He saw them as an interpreter of a world already known, and not as one who describes how a world comes to be given.

The *Cartesian Meditations* put the word to work under this condition. § 33 distinguishes the I as identical pole and as substrate of its habitualities from "the ego taken in full concreteness (which we shall call by the Leibnizian word monad)", that is, the I together with the streaming multiplicity of its intentional life and the objects meant in it (Hua I, 102). The monad is the whole of which the I-pole is the centre, and the many-in-one of Leibniz's § 13 returns as the unity of a life. The Paris Lectures add a remark which, as I read it, answers to § 30. The ego has "an immense innate a priori", and the whole of phenomenology is its disclosure. "This is the genuine sense of innateness, which the old naive concept as it were sensed but could not grasp"; and this a priori belongs to the concrete ego, "to speak with Leibniz: to my monad" (Hua I, 28). Leibniz drew being and substance from the reflexive thought of the self. Husserl draws the forms of every possible object from the self-explication of the ego, and credits the older doctrine with having sensed this.

The third thesis is taken up where the other subject enters. §§ 55 and 56 describe the other as constituted "in me", yet as another monad that is for itself exactly as I am for myself. Husserl then states the relation of the two in terms that are a commentary on § 7. The monads are really (*reell*) separate, since no real connection leads from the experiences of one to those of the other. "On the other hand this original community is not a nothing." If every monad is really an absolutely closed unity, the "irreal intentional reaching" of the others into my sphere is still not irreal in the sense of something dreamt. "A being is in intentional community with a being", and this community is what "makes transcendentally possible the being of a world" (Hua I, 157). The world so constituted is constituted in each monad "as the same, only in another subjective mode of appearance" (Hua I, 158). § 60 draws the consequence for the difficulty of our first section. Two communities of monads without any actual connection would still have their two surrounding worlds as "mere aspects of a single objective world common to them", because both stand in necessary community with me, the "primal monad" (*Urmonade*) that conceives them. There can be only one community of monads and only one world. "Leibniz is of course right", Husserl adds, that infinitely many monads are conceivable and that not all of them are compossible (Hua I, 167). The unity of the city is here derived from the reference of the points of view to one another, and God is not named. That this takes the place of the pre-established harmony is my reading; Husserl says only that the results are "metaphysical" in a sense that excludes all "metaphysical adventures" (Hua I, 166).

The research manuscripts answer the question of the window in so many words, and they do not answer it quite as the *Meditations* do. In a text of the summer of 1920 Husserl attacks the psychology that treats the soul as a closed monad, between which and other monads no relations of motivation could be founded. Such a psychology is "a pure fiction". "Leibniz said that monads have no windows. But I hold that every soul-monad has infinitely many windows; for every understanding perception of another's body is such a window." When I address a friend and the friend answers with understanding, an act of my I has passed into the friend's I, and "a real unity, indeed truly a real unity" has been established (Hua XIII, 473). A text of 1921/22 is more guarded. "Every I is a 'monad'. But the monads have windows. They have no windows or doors in so far as no other subject can really enter, but through them (the windows are the empathies) it can be experienced as well as one's own past experiences through recollection" (Hua XIV, 260). An appendix of the same group says that a monad has windows "to receive foreign influences. They are the windows of empathy" (Hua XIV, 295), and a text from the years 1923 to 1925 speaks of "windows of specifically monadic causality" by which the monads form "a real-causal unity" (Hua XIV, 360).

Two answers therefore stand side by side in Husserl, and I do not think they can be reduced to one. On the one answer the bond between monads is intentional and "irreal": the other is mirrored in me and never present in the original. On the other answer the bond is real: one will acts on another, and the monads owe what they are in part to the influence of others. What the two answers share is the distinction by which Husserl divides § 7. Leibniz is right that nothing of another subject can become a real part of my stream of experience. He is wrong if this is taken to mean that no relation is founded between us, since motivation and understanding are such relations and they are experienced. Husserl's monad is closed in its contents and open in its acts. It needs windows because it has a sphere of its own from which the other is at first absent, and empathy (*Einfühlung*) is the name of the act that opens it. Whether Leibniz's denial of windows was simply an error that this act corrects is the point at which Heidegger takes the question up.

## 3. Drive, point of view and transcendence: Heidegger, 1927–1929

Heidegger's interpretation is given in the course of the summer of 1928, where the monadology is treated as the metaphysical foundation of Leibniz's doctrine of judgement. Two things are said of it before it begins. The monadology is general metaphysics: it is to determine the being of every being, whether physical nature, living thing, human being or God (GA 26, 89). And much in it is "only indicated" by Leibniz, so that an interpretation must be ventured that "goes beyond Leibniz, or better: goes back into him more originally; even at the risk of departing far from what he in fact said" (GA 26, 87, 88). Heidegger thus announces at the outset what Husserl charged Leibniz with, a construction, and he practises it knowingly on Leibniz himself.

The first step concerns the guiding thread of the monadology, and it brings § 30 into view. Heidegger collects the passages in which Leibniz says that the monad is conceived by analogy with what we call "I", and that the principle of activity is intelligible to us because it is an analogue of what is in us, namely representing and striving (GA 26, 106–08). He then cites the *New Essays*: how could we have the idea of being if we were not beings ourselves and did not find being in us? Leibniz means more by this, Heidegger says, than that we must exist in order to have the idea. It belongs to our essence that we cannot be what we are without the idea of being: "the understanding of being is constitutive for Dasein" (GA 26, 109). This is the first anticipation that Heidegger credits, and it is not the one Husserl named. But it is credited with a reservation. Leibniz keeps, with Descartes, the self-certainty of the I as what is first, and in the whole of this procedure "the subject itself remains ontologically unclarified" (GA 26, 110). The monadology is therefore not an anthropomorphism, as it may appear, but an ontology that has not asked about the being of the being from which it takes its measure.

The second step concerns the structure of what Leibniz calls active force, which Heidegger renders as drive (*Drang*). A unity that is not the result of a collection must give unity in advance; it must reach out beyond the manifold and embrace it beforehand. This, Heidegger says, is what Leibniz means when he calls the monad representing. "Representing is here to be taken quite broadly, structurally, not as a particular faculty of the soul." The monad is in its essence not soul; "conversely, soul is a possible modification of monad" (GA 26, 113). Perception in Leibniz's sense is "the expression of many in one", as a letter to des Bosses has it, and appetite is the tendency to pass from one perception to the next, so that drive is in itself a representing striving. Two words in this analysis are Heidegger's own and show where it is going. The structure of the drive is called "reaching out" and "ecstatic", and of the succession that belongs to it he says: "Time arises from the drive itself" (GA 26, 113, 115). The first thesis of the *Monadology* is here read as Husserl read it, as a relation to what is not contained; but it is ascribed to the being of the monad and not to its experiences.

The third step reaches the second thesis. Because the drive unifies with a view to a unity held in advance, there lies in it a point of view (*point de vue*), and this is what individuates. Each monad unifies the universe from its own point, according to its possibility, and is thus a "concentrated world" (*mundus concentratus*), as Leibniz writes to de Volder (GA 26, 117–19). Heidegger insists on the consequence. The monads "are not isolated pieces that would yield the universe only in their sum"; each is the universe in its own manner, and the universe is in a certain way multiplied as many times as there are monads, "just as the same city is represented differently according to the different situations of the several observers" (GA 26, 120). The mirror, too, is given its sense. "Mirroring is not a rigid copying"; the monad lets something be seen in representing, and in this doing the world is first unveiled (GA 26, 121). From here Heidegger states the third thesis as Leibniz meant it. Every monad stands, as a perspective on the universe, in agreement with the others. "That is why the monads have 'no windows' (*Monadology* § 7): because they need none. There is no influx, not only because it would be inexplicable but because it is superfluous." He cites Leibniz's own question to des Bosses: why should a monad give a monad what it already has? (GA 26, 123).

Up to this point Heidegger has expounded, and he says afterwards how. At the end of § 12, having set out his own account of transcendence and temporality, he looks back: "You now see a certain correspondence to Leibniz's monadology, but also what distinguishes them." His interpretation, he says, was "intentionally exaggerated" in two respects: it took representation and appetite "as intentional structures", and it made them clear as transcendental structures, in their relation to a universe or world (GA 26, 270). The difference lies in the reason for the missing window. Leibniz "takes the *ego cogito* of Descartes as the basis" of his conception of the monad; he takes the monad as a substance "enclosed in its sphere", only that he incorporates the whole world into this immanence. "That is why Leibniz can say: the monads need no windows, because they already have everything in their interior. We would say, conversely: they have no windows, not because they have everything inside, but because there is neither an inside nor an outside." Time is therefore "no *mundus concentratus*, but the converse": it is essentially a self-opening into a world (GA 26, 271).

The course of the summer before had said the same of Dasein and had named the phenomenon. Dasein as monad needs no windows, "not, as Leibniz thinks, because all beings are already accessible within the housing", but because the monad, Dasein, is by its own being "already outside, that is, with other beings". Leibniz "undoubtedly had a genuine phenomenon in view. Only his orientation to the traditional concept of substance prevented him from grasping the original ground of the windowlessness." He could not see that the monad, "because it essentially represents, that is, mirrors a world, is transcendence" and not something present like a substance (GA 24, 427–28). For Heidegger, then, § 7 is the place where Leibniz comes nearest to what phenomenology has to show, and the pre-established harmony is the price of his not having understood his own sentence.

The course of the winter of 1928/29 applies this to the relation between one Dasein and another, and here a third party appears without being named. Monads, Heidegger says, have no windows because they have everything within themselves and need no commerce. "It is 'empathy', however, that gives the monad windows; empathy indeed is, as it were, the window." His own interpretation agrees with Leibniz that the monad needs none, for the other reason. "The task is not to supplement the monadological approach and improve it by way of empathy but to radicalize it" (GA 27, § 19). The next section gives the ground. Empathy can have a meaning only on the presupposition that the I is at first within its own sphere and must from there enter the sphere of the other. "Because it is already outside, the 'I' neither first breaks out of itself (out of its window) nor breaks into the other" (GA 27, § 20). The formula of the window of empathy is Husserl's, as our second section has shown, and I take the passage to be directed at him; the text does not say so. We are now in a position to set the two readings of Leibniz side by side.

## 4. The window and the city

There are three points of agreement, and they are more than incidental. First, both authors find in the monad's perception and appetite what they call intentional structures. Husserl says that Leibniz grasped the fundamental properties of intentionality; Heidegger says that he took the two determinations of the monad "as intentional structures", and he reads the many-in-one as a reaching out. Second, both find the same defect of execution. For Husserl Leibniz remains "stuck in the occasional aperçu, in anticipation and construction" (Hua VII, 197); for Heidegger much "remains at the stage of the first conception" and the *Monadology* is characteristically ninety theses set one after another (GA 26, 87–88). Third, both name Descartes as the source from which Leibniz takes his concept of the I. Appendix XVIII says that Leibniz takes over the evidence of the *ego cogito* in the form of a thinking substance; the course of 1928 says that he takes the *ego cogito* as the basis of his conception of the monad.

There are also three differences. The first concerns what is anticipated. For Husserl it is a property of experiences, their being of something, and with it the sense of a reality that is one as the identical object of many experiences. For Heidegger it is a determination of the being of the one who experiences, which he calls transcendence, and with it the understanding of being that § 30 ascribes to the I. The second difference concerns § 7. Husserl divides the sentence and corrects it: no real entry, but windows of empathy. Heidegger keeps the sentence whole and replaces its ground: no windows, because no housing. The third concerns the others. For Husserl the other monad is constituted in me and mirrored there, and the community of monads is the result of this constitution, even if it is a result that the sense of my own world already requires. For Heidegger being with one another belongs to each Dasein beforehand, and what Husserl calls a window presupposes it.

I believe that the three differences have one root, and that it lies in the third point of agreement. Both authors say that Leibniz builds on the Cartesian ego, and they mean opposite things by it. Husserl's complaint is that Leibniz did not go far enough into the ego: what is taught of the monad is not drawn from the study of the pure ego "within the frame of the Cartesian evidence, which has first to be rightly grasped and interpreted", and any monadology that precedes this study is absurd (Hua VII, 366–67). The remedy is a more radical return to immanence, from which the other monads and the one world are then to be won by explication. Heidegger's complaint is that Leibniz stayed within the ego: the monad is a substance enclosed in its sphere, and the world is brought inside it. The remedy is to give up the sphere. Husserl, then, finds in Leibniz a monadology without a reduction, and Heidegger finds in him a transcendence still thought as immanence. Whether the monad needs a window depends on which of these is the fault.

Each position can be stated in the terms of the other, and the statement shows how near they are. In Heidegger's terms, Husserl's windows are an admission that the being of the monad is a being with others; the admission is made, however, in the form of an act that is added to a sphere of one's own, and so it comes too late. In Husserl's terms, Heidegger's "already outside" says that intentionality is no real relation between two things and that the horizon of others belongs to every experience of a world. Husserl could reply that he has said this. § 60 holds that my ego "can a priori be a world-experiencing ego only by being in community with others like it", and it speaks of structures in me "that imply the being-with (*Mit-sein*) of the other monads" (Hua I, 166–67). What remains between them after this translation is the order of the exposition. Husserl begins with what is my own and shows that it implies the other; Heidegger denies that what is my own can be described first.

Two objections must be faced. Is the anticipation not simply the product of the reading? Heidegger concedes that his interpretation was exaggerated on purpose and guided from the start by his own account of temporality, and Husserl grants that the true sense of Leibniz's philosophy has become accessible only "in our days" (Hua VII, 199). The concession must be made in part. Neither author claims that Leibniz possessed the concept of intentionality or of transcendence, and Heidegger's "ecstatic" drive is found in no letter to de Volder. But the objection does not reach the three theses themselves. That perception is a many-in-one which no inspection of parts will find, that the one universe exists for each monad from a point of view and is limited in the mode of knowing and not in the object, and that nothing passes from one substance into another: these stand in §§ 14, 17, 57, 60 and 7. And they are recognised by two readers who share no guiding thread. Husserl reads from the pure ego and Heidegger from the being of Dasein, and both find the same paragraphs. What Leibniz does not anticipate is equally plain. He ascribes perception to every simple substance, including the "bare monads" that never wake, and neither phenomenologist follows him there by description. Heidegger says of the question whether his own interpretation of Dasein could be extended to all beings, as the monadology was, that it is one "which I myself am unable to decide, which is still completely dark to me" (GA 26, 271).

The second objection concerns Heidegger's criticism. Does it reach the Husserl of the manuscripts? The manuscript of 1920 calls the psychology of the closed monad a fiction and makes of every human being who is treated so "a solipsist in the midst of the living reality of community" (Hua XIII, 473). Its target is the target of Heidegger's § 20. It even says of the perception of a thing that "the thing is in immediate relation to me", which leaves little for a housing to enclose. Two answers must again be left standing. In so far as Husserl's windows are relations of motivation that are always already established between persons, Heidegger's objection that the I must first break out does not apply. In so far as the *Cartesian Meditations* reach the other by abstracting to a primordial sphere and proceeding from it, the objection describes the procedure accurately. I cannot decide from the texts read here which of the two Husserl held to be fundamental, and I doubt that the published *Meditations* settle it.

We may now return to the city. For Leibniz the many views are views of one city because the city and its viewers were chosen together; the agreement of the perspectives is established by one who has no perspective. For Husserl the views are of one city because each view carries the others in its horizon as views that I could have from there, and that another has. The city is the identical object of all of them and is nothing beside them. For Heidegger the question how the views come to agree is put too late, since those who view are in the city with one another before any of them looks. This third statement is mine; Heidegger cites the example only to expound Leibniz. In each case § 57 is kept, and in each case it is § 51 that is given up.

The result can now be given its sharpest form. Leibniz anticipated phenomenology least where he is most easily compared with it, in the system of the harmony, and most in the sentence that seems furthest from it. To say that the monad has no window is to say that perceiving is not the reception of something sent, and that a subject's relation to its world cannot be understood as a transaction between two things. Husserl and Heidegger both hold this, and both learned to say it against the theories of knowledge of their own time. Three limits of the result should be stated. I have read the *Monadology* through a commentary and through Heidegger's quotations, and a study of Leibniz's own texts might show that he knew more, or less, than his two readers allow. I have not followed Husserl into the manuscripts after 1929, where the monad is carried further, nor Heidegger into the later lectures, where the monad as representing drive becomes a stage in a history that he no longer tells in order to find himself anticipated. And I have left undecided whether a community that is implied by my own sphere and a being-with that precedes it are two things or one thing described in two orders. The last word belongs to Leibniz, since on one point neither reader improves on him. Asked why there is no influence of monad on monad, he does not say only that it would be inexplicable. He asks why a monad should give to another what the other already has. The monadology of phenomenology is not a borrowed word, and it is not a secret adoption either; it is what remains of that question once it is no longer God who is asked to answer it.

## 2. Points where the files limit the claims

- **Leibniz at one remove.** No text of Leibniz was read directly. The *Monadology* is quoted in the English of the commentary, which paraphrases and quotes by turns; phrases in quotation marks are the commentary's wording and may be its paraphrase. The letters to de Volder and des Bosses and the *New Essays* are known only from GA 26. Boutroux's notice at the head of the commentary (`:11`–`:73`) was not read.
- **Page numbers.** Pages are taken from the page markers in the files. In Hua VII the marker for p. 198 is missing, so the passage cited as p. 197 may run onto p. 198, and "in our days" (cited as p. 199) may stand on p. 198. In GA 24 the markers for pp. 426–28 fall close together; the passage is cited as 427–28 for the reply and 426–28 in the register. In GA 26 the sentence on windows stands after the marker for p. 123, whose running head already names § 7.
- **Dates.** Summer semester 1920 (Hua XIII, Beilage LIV), 1921/22 (Hua XIV, Text no. 13 and Beilage XXXVII) and 1923–1925 (Text no. 18) are read from the files' running heads; "c. 1924" for Appendix XVIII is the editor's note. The dates of the three Heidegger courses are confirmed by the cross-references at GA 26 `:579` and GA 27 `:1308`, except GA 24, whose date is the standard one. The dates 1923/24 for the lectures on First Philosophy, 1929 for the Paris Lectures and 1931 for the *Meditations* are standard and were not checked in the files.
- **GA 27.** Read in English only; § numbers are the translation's. A second file of the same translation (`…-b.md`) was not compared.
- **Not read.** Hua XV; the other occurrences of "Monade" in Hua XIV (over two hundred) and Hua IX; *Ideas II*; the *Crisis*; Heidegger's *Nietzsche II*, *The Principle of Reason*, GA 22, GA 49, GA 66 and the Leibniz essay in *Wegmarken* (not in the folder). ## 3. Steps that are mine and not the texts'

- The sorting of the *Monadology* into three theses and one difficulty (section 1).
- That Husserl's three titles at Hua VII, 197 correspond to §§ 14–15, and the "identical *ideatum*" of Appendix XVIII to § 57 (section 2).
- That the "innate a priori" of the Paris Lectures answers to *Monadology* § 30; Husserl names Leibniz there but not the paragraph (section 2).
- That the community of monads in CM § 60 takes the place of the pre-established harmony (section 2).
- That Husserl gives two answers on whether the bond between monads is real or "irreal", and that they are not reducible (section 2).
- That GA 27 § 19 is directed at Husserl's formula of the windows of empathy; Husserl is not named there (section 3).
- That the differences have one root in the opposite judgements on the Cartesian ego (section 4).
- The restatement of each author in the other's terms, and the third reading of the city ascribed to Heidegger (section 4).
- The closing claim that Leibniz anticipates phenomenology most in § 7 and least in the harmony.

## 4. Style-guide notes

- The article follows the research-article form of the Bernet style guide (A1–A13): opening without a heading, four numbered sections with noun-phrase titles, no section headed "Conclusion", close on the sharpened result, three limits and a credit to the author discussed.
- The shared question (A7) is that of *Monadology* § 7. Section 4 gives parallels, then differences, then their root (A8), and restates each author in the other's terms (A9). Criticism is held until the reconstructions are complete (A10).
- The carried example (M15) is the city of § 57. Two places show two answers left standing (M9): Husserl on the real or irreal bond, and the reach of Heidegger's criticism of empathy.
- Originals are kept for terms of art at first use (*Drang*, *Einfühlung*, *Urmonade*, *mundus concentratus*, *point de vue*, *ideatum*, *ego cogito*) and where the argument turns on the wording (*reell*; *Mit-sein* in Husserl's § 60).
- Notes are held to one, on the citation convention (N1, N3); file and line references are in this register.

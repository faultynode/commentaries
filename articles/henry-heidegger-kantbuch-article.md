# The Borrowed Lever

## Heidegger's *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* in Michel Henry's *L'essence de la manifestation*

*A note on citation and on translation.* This article is written against a
working archive of source texts rather than against the printed editions.[^1]
I therefore cite by the handles the texts themselves supply — Henry's numbered
sections (§) and his own footnote numbers, Heidegger's section numbers — and,
where a passage has no section-level handle, by the archive's line pointers, in
the form `GA 3:6980`. Those pointers are not pagination; they will drift on
re-conversion. They are given so that every claim below can be checked against
the same text I checked it against.

Henry cites Heidegger's Kant book in the 1953 de Waelhens–Biemel French
translation, under the siglum *K*, and his page numbers are that translation's.
The archive holds the German original (GA 3). All the alignments below therefore
cross a translation boundary in the direction French → German, and I say so at
each point where the direction matters. Renderings into English are my own
working translations unless otherwise noted; one recurring choice needs flagging
in advance, because it carries the argument. De Waelhens and Biemel render
Heidegger's coinage *Gegenstehenlassen* [letting-stand-over-against] as
*objectivation*, and *Vernehmbarkeit* [perceivability, capacity to be taken in]
as *perceptibilité*. Both French words are ordinary; both German words are not.
That asymmetry is not a pedantry here — it is one of the things this article is
about.

---

## I. The question

*L'essence de la manifestation* (1963) is usually read as a book against
Heidegger. Its epigraph is from *Sein und Zeit*; its polemical target is what
Henry calls *le monisme ontologique* [ontological monism], the presupposition
that to appear is to appear in the ek-static opening of a horizon; and its
positive thesis — that the essence of manifestation is a self-revelation which
never places itself at a distance from itself, an *immanence radicale* — is
formulated as the exact negation of Heideggerian transcendence. On this reading
the relevant Heidegger is the Heidegger of *Sein und Zeit*, and Henry's move is
to accept the Daseinsanalytic's question about the being of the ego while
rejecting its answer.

That reading is not wrong, but it locates the decisive textual work in the wrong
book. The claim of this article is narrower and, I think, more interesting:
**the concept of radical immanence is not extracted from *Sein und Zeit*. It is
extracted, by an immanent critique carried out almost line by line, from
*Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik*.** Henry's Section II — the section
titled *Répétition de l'élucidation du concept de phénomène: transcendance et
immanence*, which is where the word *immanence* acquires its content — is a
sustained reading of GA 3's doctrine of the transcendental imagination, of
schematism, and above all of time as *reine Selbstaffektion* [pure
self-affection]. Henry's key term, *auto-affection*, is the French Kantbuch's
word. His operative term for the structure he is attacking, *objectivation*, is
the French Kantbuch's rendering of a word Heidegger coined in GA 3 and never
used in *Sein und Zeit*. The lever with which he prises immanence out of
phenomenology is borrowed, and it is borrowed from one book.

Three questions follow. How heavily does Henry actually use the Kantbuch? How
far does the borrowing extend past the explicit citations into the vocabulary in
which Henry states his own positive doctrine? And does the resulting position
hold up as phenomenology — that is, is Henry entitled to the conclusion he draws
from a text that says something else?

## II. What the citation record shows

Henry's footnotes can be counted. The abbreviation table at the head of the book
assigns *K* to *Kant et le problème de la métaphysique*, and *SZ* to *Sein und
Zeit*.[^2] Resolving the *ID.* and *Ibid.* chains — Henry's footnotes run in
sequences where only the first names the work — gives the following distribution
across the whole book:

| Work | Citations |
| --- | --- |
| Eckhart, *Traités et Sermons* | 150 |
| Heidegger, *Sein und Zeit* | 81 |
| **Heidegger, *Kant et le problème de la métaphysique*** | **68** |
| Scheler, *Le formalisme en éthique* | 57 |
| Fichte, *Initiation à la vie bienheureuse* | 53 |
| Hegel, *Phénoménologie de l'Esprit* | 47 |

The Kantbuch is the third most-cited work in *L'essence de la manifestation* and
the second most-cited Heidegger. That alone would be worth noting. But the
aggregate understates the case badly, because the citations are not spread
evenly. Broken down by the book's divisions, the Kantbuch citations fall like
this: three in Section I, **fifty-one in Section II**, one in Section III, two in
the continuation of Section III, fourteen in Section IV, none in the
Introduction and none in the Appendix.

Set against *Sein und Zeit* within Section II alone, the ratio is stark:

| Section II citations | Count |
| --- | --- |
| *Kant et le problème de la métaphysique* | 51 |
| Heidegger, *Holzwege* | 13 |
| Hegel, *Phénoménologie de l'Esprit* | 11 |
| Sartre, *L'Être et le Néant* | 5 |
| **Heidegger, *Sein und Zeit*** | **3** |

Seventeen Kantbuch citations for every one of *Sein und Zeit*, in the section
where the concept of immanence is produced. And within Section II the citations
concentrate further still: the great majority fall in §§22–24 and §31 — that is,
in "the internal possibility of the receptivity of being and the problem of
schematism," "the ontological interpretation of time as auto-affection," and
"the fundamental ambiguity in the concept of auto-affection." Those four
sections are the hinge of the book, and across them Henry is reading one text.

## III. The uncited stratum: an operating vocabulary

Citation counts establish attention, not dependence. The stronger evidence is
lexical, and it can be tested with a control.

Henry's exposition runs on a small set of technical terms which he uses in his
own voice, without quotation marks, hundreds of times: *objectivation* (about a
hundred occurrences), *réceptivité* (231), *opposition* (266), *finitude* (126),
*auto-affection* (100), *perceptibilité/perceptible* (39), *offre* (29),
*imagination transcendantale* (7). The question is where that vocabulary comes
from. The control is to ask how much of it is available in *Sein und Zeit*.

Counting the German originals in the archive:

| German term | GA 3 | *Sein und Zeit* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Einbildungskraft* [imagination] | 334 | 0 |
| *reine Anschauung* [pure intuition] | 61 | 0 |
| *Gegenstehenlassen* [letting-stand-over-against] | 34 | 0 |
| *Angebot* [offering] | 8 | 0 |
| *Vernehmbarkeit* [perceivability] | 2 | 0 |
| *Selbstaffektion* [self-affection] | frequent (§34 passim) | 0 |

Every term in the apparatus Henry works with is a Kantbuch term with no presence
whatever in *Sein und Zeit*. The point is not that Henry could not have got such
notions elsewhere; it is that these particular words, in these particular
functions, exist in exactly one of the two Heidegger books he had in front of
him. The table should not be read as saying that *Sein und Zeit* is silent about
being affected — it is not: *Befindlichkeit* [attunement, findingness] is
precisely an account of how Dasein is affected, and §68b has the sentence that
only a being which «je schon gewesen ist» [has in each case already been] can be
affected at all.[^a] But *that* vocabulary is not the vocabulary Henry runs his
argument in, and where he does engage *Befindlichkeit*, in §65, he engages it
through the Kantbuch's *Achtung* analysis (below, §VI).

The clinching case is *objectivation*, Henry's most-used technical term. That it
translates *Gegenstehenlassen*, and not the ordinary French sense of
objectification, is fixed by Henry's own quotation. In §31 he quotes: «Affecter
purement l'acte d'objectivation, en tant qu'il est orientation pure vers… veut
dire qu'on lui suscite quelque opposition» [to affect the act of objectivation
purely, insofar as it is pure orientation toward…, means to raise up against it
some opposition].[^3] The German is:

> Das Gegenstehenlassen als solches, d. h. als reines Sich-zuwenden-zu . . .,
> rein affizieren, bedeutet: ihm überhaupt so etwas wie ein „Gegen-es", das
> Dawider, entgegenbringen.

[To affect purely the letting-stand-over-against as such — that is, as pure
turning-toward — means: to bring against it something like a "counter-to-it",
the *Dawider*.][^4]

*l'acte d'objectivation* = *das Gegenstehenlassen*; *orientation pure vers* =
*reines Sich-zuwenden-zu*; *opposition* = *das Dawider*. The correspondence is
exact, and it means that every one of Henry's hundred uses of *objectivation* —
including those in sections that cite no Heidegger at all — carries GA 3's
coinage inside it.

The same holds for a term Henry does not translate at all. In §15, expounding
the originary concept of finitude, he writes in his own voice that beings
considered in themselves are «aussi bien le noumène, l'*Enstand* de l'intuition
infinie que le *Gegenstand* de la connaissance réceptrice» [as much the noumenon,
the *Enstand* of infinite intuition, as the *Gegenstand* of receptive
knowing].[^5] *Ent-stand* against *Gegen-stand* is a GA 3 coinage — Heidegger
splits the words with hyphens precisely to make the contrast audible.[^6] Henry
carries it over untranslated, unglossed, and at that point uncited, into the
statement of a concept (finitude) that his whole critique of monism rests on.

## IV. §§22–24: the immanent critique

What Henry does with this material is a single, patient argument, and it is
worth reconstructing because the article's assessment depends on it.

Heidegger's Kantbuch argues that ontological knowledge is finite; that finite
knowing is receptive; that pure intuition and pure thought are both grounded in
the transcendental imagination, which is «die ursprüngliche und nicht erst
zusammengesetzte Einheit von Rezeptivität und Spontaneität» [the original and
not first composed unity of receptivity and spontaneity];[^7] and that the
imagination, so understood, is at bottom original time, which is *reine
Selbstaffektion*.

Henry accepts the first steps and then asks a question Heidegger does not ask.
If the imagination *forms* the pure horizon of being, how can it also *receive*
what it forms? Henry's §23 is the demonstration that GA 3 has no answer.

He first blocks the obvious reply — that reception is explained by formation.
Heidegger says of the pure intuitions that «cette réception est *en elle-même*
un acte formateur qui se donne à lui-même ce qui s'offre» [this reception is *in
itself* a formative act which gives to itself what offers itself].[^8] Henry's
objection is phenomenological, not logical: that a content is produced by the
essence explains nothing about its being *received* by the essence, and in fact
the dependence runs the other way, since forming a horizon has phenomenological
sense only insofar as what is formed shows itself. He states the reversal
flatly: «*L'essence de la spontanéité réside dans la réceptivité*» [the essence
of spontaneity resides in receptivity].[^9]

He then tracks Heidegger's own reversal. GA 3, at exactly this pressure point,
stops saying that intuition is imagination and starts saying that imagination is
intuition: «die Einbildungskraft ist ja doch auch und gerade ein Vermögen der
Anschauung» [the imagination is indeed also and precisely a faculty of
intuition].[^10] Henry notices that the reversal occurs where the perceptibility
of the horizon can no longer be deferred, and he assembles the chain from GA 3
§19 in Heidegger's own words:

> Damit aber das Seiende sich als solches anbieten kann, muß der Horizont seines
> möglichen Begegnens selbst Angebotcharakter haben. […] Damit aber der Horizont
> des Gegenstehenlassens als solcher fungieren kann, bedarf dieser
> Angebotcharakter einer Vernehmbarkeit. Vernehmlich heißt: unmittelbar in der
> Anschauung hinnehmbar.

[But in order that beings can offer themselves as such, the horizon of their
possible encounter must itself have the character of an offering. […] But in
order that the horizon of letting-stand-over-against can function as such, this
character of offering requires a perceivability. Perceivable means: immediately
receivable in intuition.][^11]

Henry's three consecutive quotations reproduce this passage almost
continuously.[^12] His conclusion is that intuition has been made to render the
horizon perceptible, that receiving the horizon therefore coincides with forming
it, and that Heidegger's own summary — «L'imagination appartient à la faculté
d'intuition» — completes a circle: «La pensée qui demande à l'intuition la
réalisation de ce qu'elle conçoit comme la condition de cette intuition même se
meut manifestement dans un cercle» [the thought that asks intuition to realize
what it conceives as the condition of that very intuition is plainly moving in a
circle].[^13]

§24 then follows the same circle through time. Here Henry's quotations track GA
3 §34 in sequence. He cites «Le temps… prête d'emblée à l'horizon de la
transcendance le caractère d'une offre perceptible» — *Die Zeit also ist es, die
als a priori gebende von vornherein dem Horizont der Transzendenz den Charakter
des vernehmbaren Angebotes verleiht*.[^14] He cites the thesis itself,
«l'imagination transcendantale est le temps originel» — *die transzendentale
Einbildungskraft ist die ursprüngliche Zeit*.[^15] He cites the inner sense
which «ne reçoit rien du dehors» — *Der innere Sinn empfängt nicht „von außen",
sondern vom Selbst her*.[^16] He cites time as «par nature, pure affection de
lui-même» — *Die Zeit ist ihrem Wesen nach reine Affektion ihrer selbst*.[^17]
He cites «Le sens interne pur est l'affection pure de soi, c'est-à-dire le temps
originel» — *Der reine innere Sinn ist die reine Selbstaffektion, d. h. die
ursprüngliche Zeit*.[^18] And he cites the sentence on which everything will
turn: «L'affection pure de soi… détermine l'essence profonde de la
transcendance» — *Die Idee der reinen Selbstaffektion, die […] das innerste
Wesen der Transzendenz bestimmt*.[^19]

This is not allusion. Across §§23–24 Henry is quoting one continuous stretch of
GA 3 — §§19, 23, 26, 32, 34 — in something close to Heidegger's own order, and
building his exposition in the gaps between the quotations.

Then, at the end of §24, the whole apparatus is turned. Heidegger's positive
determination of intuiting is *Anschauen heißt Hinnehmen des Sichgebenden* [to
intuit means: to take in what gives itself].[^20] Henry quotes it and comments:
«*L'intuition n'est qu'un nom pour la réceptivité qui assure la manifestation de
l'horizon pur de l'être*. *Loin de résoudre le problème de la réceptivité de
l'essence, la détermination de l'essence du temps comme intuition le pose
seulement avec plus d'urgence*» [intuition is only a name for the receptivity
that secures the manifestation of the pure horizon of being. Far from resolving
the problem of the essence's receptivity, determining the essence of time as
intuition only poses it more urgently].[^21] The Kantbuch's answer is
re-described as a restatement of the question.

## V. §31: the fork

The critical work of §§23–24 produces one result, and §31 cashes it. If the
essence must receive itself before it can receive anything it has set over
against itself, then there is an affection more original than the affection of
transcendence by its horizon — and that original affection cannot itself be
horizonal, since it is the condition of the horizon being formed at all:

> *Plus originaire que l'affection de la transcendance par l'horizon est
> l'affection de la transcendance par elle-même.* […] *L'essence originaire de
> l'affection réside dans l'immanence.*

[More original than transcendence's affection by the horizon is transcendence's
affection by itself. […] The original essence of affection resides in
immanence.][^22]

That sentence is the birth of radical immanence in the book, and it is reached
by pressing a Kantbuch thesis until it splits. Henry then makes the split
terminological. *Auto-affection* has been used equivocally, he says, to cover
two things: *affection par soi* [affection *by* oneself], where what affects is
the horizon one has oneself produced, and *affection de soi* [affection *of*
oneself], where the essence receives itself without any horizon at all. Only the
second deserves the name. And with the distinction in hand he rejects the
sentence he had quoted seven sections earlier, in Heidegger's own words:

> Pour cette raison, il est faux de dire que «l'affection pure de soi… détermine…
> l'essence profonde de la transcendance» si, comme il convient de le faire et
> comme le fait Heidegger, on entend par affection pure de soi l'auto-affection
> elle-même.

[For this reason it is false to say that "pure affection of self… determines…
the deep essence of transcendence" if, as one ought and as Heidegger does, one
understands by pure affection of self auto-affection itself.][^23]

The structure of the gesture is worth stating precisely, because it is the whole
argument in miniature. Henry does not reject *Selbstaffektion*. He keeps it,
insists it is the ultimate concept, and denies only that it can be predicated of
transcendence. The Kantbuch is not a target he refutes and discards; it is a
text he takes to have found the right thing and misassigned it.

## VI. §§52 and 65: the return, and where the commentary stops

The fourteen Kantbuch citations in Section IV matter because Section IV is where
Henry states his positive doctrine — the essence of revelation as *affectivité*
[affectivity]. If the Kantbuch were merely a polemical target, it would have
been left behind by now. It is not.

§52, the section that introduces affectivity and ipseity, opens on a Kantbuch
citation: «Toute affection, dit Heidegger, est une manifestation par laquelle
s'annonce un étant déjà donné» [every affection, says Heidegger, is a
manifestation by which an already-given being announces itself].[^24] The
paragraph then rebuilds the full GA 3 apparatus — pure affection independent of
beings, the inner sense, *la forme de toute autosollicitation*, the
transcendental imagination — as the background against which affectivity will be
set. Henry's positive doctrine is introduced by way of Heidegger's Kant.

More striking is §65, *Le pouvoir de révélation de l'affectivité selon
Heidegger*, whose Kantbuch citations cluster on GA 3 §30, the analysis of
*Achtung* [respect]. That section of the Kantbuch contains the closest thing in
Heidegger to Henry's own thesis:

> Gefühl ist Gefühlhaben für . . ., so zwar, daß hierin zugleich das fühlende Ich
> sich selbst fühlt.

[Feeling is having-a-feeling-for…, in such a way that in this the feeling I at
the same time feels itself.][^25]

Feeling as that in which the self is revealed to itself: this is, in outline,
Henry's doctrine. And Heidegger continues in terms Henry will quote one by one —
«le respect pour la loi… est en soi un dévoilement de moi-même comme soi
agissant» (*ist in sich ein Offenbarmachen meiner selbst als des handelnden
Selbst*); «le respect à l'égard de la loi est respect à l'égard de soi-même»
(*Achtung vor dem Gesetz ist Achtung vor sich selbst*); «je suis moi-même dans
cet acte de soumission à moi-même» (*In diesem Mich-mir-unterwerfen bin ich als
ich selbst*); the self revealed «dans sa dignité» (*in seiner „Würde"*); respect
as *l'être-soi véritable* (*das eigentliche Selbstsein*).[^26]

Henry's objection is that all of this is routed through the representation of
the law, so that the self's revelation to itself is *indirecte* — mediated by a
transcendence that presupposes the very ipseity it claims to constitute. His
verdict is that the analysis «fait éclater celui-ci en une pluralité impensable
de moi différents et irréductibles» [makes the I burst into an unthinkable
plurality of different and irreducible I's] and that «l'ontologie défaillante,
une fois de plus, cède la place à l'enthousiasme moral» [a failing ontology
gives way once more to moral enthusiasm].[^27]

The point for the present argument is not whether the objection lands. It is
that Henry's most important positive thesis — affectivity as the self-revelation
constitutive of ipseity — is worked out in direct, sustained engagement with the
Kantbuch's *Achtung* chapter, which had already stated a version of it. Radical
immanence is not only levered out of GA 3 negatively, by pressing its circle; it
is also defined positively against the one passage in GA 3 that comes nearest to
saying what Henry wants to say.

**And this is where the existing commentary stops.** The paragraph-by-paragraph
commentary on *L'essence de la manifestation* in this repository runs from the
Introduction through §57.[^28] §58 (the Kantian problematic of respect) and §65
(affectivity according to Heidegger) — the two sections where the Kantbuch
returns most decisively — fall outside its coverage.

Two further observations about the commentary, offered as corrections rather
than criticisms, since its handling of the argument is accurate throughout.
First, it never names the Kantbuch. Across §§22–31 it renders Henry's citations
faithfully — "Heidegger's formula, that imagination is the original and
uncompounded unity of receptivity and spontaneity"; "transcendence, Heidegger
says, must in objectivation render the horizon it forms intuitable"; "it is
therefore false to say that pure affection of itself determines the deep essence
of transcendence, if by pure affection of itself one means, as Heidegger does,
auto-affection" — without once identifying *which* Heidegger text is being
quoted.[^29] It does name *Sein und Zeit* where Henry cites it.[^30] A reader of
the commentary alone would reasonably infer that the Heidegger under attack in
Section II is the Heidegger of *Sein und Zeit*, which is precisely the inference
the citation record refutes. Second, and relatedly, the commentary inherits
Henry's French *objectivation* directly into English as "objectivation" without
flagging that it stands for *Gegenstehenlassen*, so the term's GA 3 provenance
disappears at the point where it does most work.

## VII. Does it hold as phenomenology?

Three things can be asked separately: whether Henry reads GA 3 accurately,
whether the inference to immanence is valid, and whether the resulting position
is phenomenologically warranted.

**On accuracy, Henry is scrupulous.** Every alignment I checked between his
French quotations and the German holds. He does not truncate to advantage, he
quotes the strongest available formulation of the position he is attacking, and
he marks his own emphases (*souligné par nous*) consistently. Where he presses
hardest — the claim that GA 3's account of receptivity is circular — the circle
is genuinely in the text, and Henry's reconstruction of it is the one the text
supports: the horizon requires perceivability; perceivability is immediate
receivability in intuition; intuition is grounded in the transcendence which the
horizon constitutes. Heidegger does not treat this as vicious, because for him
the "circle" is the structure of transcendence rather than a defect in the
account of it. But Henry is entitled to ask what phenomenological content the
account has, and Heidegger's own §31 — *Kants Zurückweichen vor der
transzendentalen Einbildungskraft* [Kant's shrinking back before the
transcendental imagination] — concedes that the ground laid here is one the
*Critique* itself recoils from. That gives Henry's question a foothold in the
text it interrogates.

**On the inference, the case is weaker than Henry's presentation suggests.** The
argument has the form: reception cannot be explained by formation; therefore
there is an affection prior to the horizon; therefore that affection is
non-horizonal; therefore its essence is immanence. The third step is the one
that does not follow. From "the affection that grounds the horizon cannot itself
be constituted by that horizon" it does not follow that it has no ek-static
structure whatever — only that it is not constituted by the horizon it
constitutes. Heidegger's own answer is that original time is the ek-static
structure that makes the horizon possible, which is a different thing from being
inside it; Henry's argument does not engage that answer so much as decline it.
The move from "not derived from transcendence" to "immanent, and immanent in a
sense that excludes all distance whatever" is a strengthening for which §31
gives an assertion rather than a demonstration.

There is a second and sharper difficulty, which Henry sees and does not fully
answer. His own criterion, stated in §31, is strict phenomenological fidelity:
«*Car la réalité ontologique n'est pas dissociable de la forme dans laquelle
elle se montre, étant elle-même cette forme comme telle*» [for ontological
reality is not dissociable from the form in which it shows itself, being itself
that form as such].[^31] He deploys this against Heidegger to deny that the
essence shows itself "under the form of" a horizon. But the criterion cuts both
ways. If reality is not separable from the form in which it shows itself, then a
self-revelation which is by definition invisible — which never places itself at
a distance, has no *face*, and is knowable in no intuition — owes an account of
what its showing consists in. Henry's answer is affectivity: the essence's
self-revelation *is* its self-suffering, the *s'éprouver soi-même*. That is a
real answer and not an evasion; Section IV is its elaboration. But it is an
answer that changes the standard of evidence rather than meeting the one
inherited from Husserl, and Henry does not argue for the change so much as
enact it.

**On warrant, the verdict is mixed, and the Kantbuch dependence is what makes it
so.** The strongest thing to be said for Henry is that his central concept is
not an invention but a *retrieval*: *Selbstaffektion* is Heidegger's own term
for the innermost structure of finite subjectivity, and the claim that Heidegger
misassigns it to transcendence is a claim about GA 3 that can be argued from GA
3. Henry's position therefore has a genuine textual footing inside the tradition
it breaks with, and this is why the Kantbuch dependence is a strength rather than
an embarrassment. The weakest thing is that the borrowing is asymmetrical in a
way Henry does not acknowledge. He takes over the apparatus — imagination,
receptivity, pure affection, inner sense, finitude, the *Ent-stand*/*Gegenstand*
pair — while rejecting the framework that gives those terms their sense. In GA
3, *Selbstaffektion* is intelligible because time is ek-static: the self affects
itself by forming and receiving a horizon, and the "self" in question is finite
precisely in needing that detour. Detach the concept from the detour and it is
no longer clear what work "affection" is doing, since there is now nothing that
affects and nothing affected — only, as Henry says, an essence that never leaves
itself. Henry's answer, that this is exactly the point and that the detour was
always the problem, is coherent. But it means the term *auto-affection* is
carrying, into Henry's system, a suggestion of structure that his system has
removed, and a reader is entitled to suspect that some of the concept's
plausibility travels with the word rather than with the argument.

The honest summary is this. The Kantbuch is not one source among Henry's many.
It is the text against which radical immanence was thought out, the source of
the vocabulary in which it is stated, and — in the *Achtung* analysis — the
nearest anticipation of the doctrine it is used to establish. Henry's reading of
it is accurate and his critique of its circle is real. His inference from that
circle to immanence is not compelled by the critique, and the concept he carries
away keeps a name whose original sense his own thesis dissolves. That is not a
refutation of Henry. It is a statement of what a defence of him would have to
do: show that *affection* still means something when nothing stands over
against.

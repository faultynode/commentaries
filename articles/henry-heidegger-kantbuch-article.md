# The Borrowed Abyss

## Heidegger's *Kantbuch* in Michel Henry's *The Essence of Manifestation*

*A note on citation.* The evidence base for this article is the
paragraph-by-paragraph commentary on *L'essence de la manifestation* held
in this repository.[^1] I have no access to the printed French edition of
Henry's book, nor to a printed edition of Heidegger's *Kant und das
Problem der Metaphysik* — the book Henry's text calls simply the
Kant-book (*Kantbuch*), and which I refer to below as KPM. I therefore
cite Henry by section number (§ 7, § 23, …), which the commentary
preserves from his text, and, where a claim depends on the exact wording,
by the commentary's own line pointers in the form `:1467`. These pointers
are not pagination and will drift with any edit to the file; they are
given so that every claim below can be checked against the same text I
checked it against. KPM I cite by section, from knowledge of that text
rather than from a file in the corpus; where the argument turns on a
German formulation I give it, and where I am reporting the commentary's
English rendering rather than Heidegger's German I say so. Two of Henry's
own references to KPM survive in the commentary as bare page numbers —
"Kant-book, 244" and "Kant-book, 87" — with no edition named. For a
French book of 1963 the natural candidate is the de Waelhens–Biemel
translation, but I have not verified this and treat it as an inference,
not a datum.

A second caution. Throughout, I distinguish what Henry *says* from what
the commentary says he says. The commentary is a close paraphrase that
frequently reports Heidegger's formulations in indirect speech, and in
several places I rely on it for the fact that a formulation is
Heidegger's. Where that reliance is load-bearing I flag it.

## I. A citation count that misleads

Heidegger's *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* is named five times in
the whole of *The Essence of Manifestation* as the commentary records
it,[^2] and is cited twice more by bare page number.[^3] Against this,
*Sein und Zeit* is invoked ninety-seven times, and *Was ist Metaphysik?*
and the *Holzwege* essays nine times each — nearly twice as often as the
Kantbuch, and from a fraction of the argumentative work. (These are
counts of title-strings in the commentary file, not of Henry's footnotes,
which the commentary does not reproduce in full.) On a naive tally of
sources, KPM is a minor authority.

The tally is worthless. The five named citations, and the far larger body
of unnamed borrowing that surrounds them, are concentrated at exactly
three places: the demonstration that transcendence is essentially
receptive (§ 13, § 15); the demonstration that receptivity cannot ground
itself (§§ 22–25); and the derivation of auto-affection from — and
against — Heidegger's self-affection (§§ 24, 31). Those three places are,
respectively, the diagnosis that closes Section I, the aporia that closes
Section II, and the breakthrough that opens Section III. They are the
book's three structural joints. Everything Henry says about immanence
after § 31 is said in a vocabulary he takes from KPM, and the argument
that entitles him to say it is an argument conducted, from beginning to
end, on KPM's terrain.

This article makes three claims. First, that KPM functions in *The
Essence of Manifestation* not as a source among sources but as the
*proof-text* of the book's central negative thesis — the insufficiency of
what Henry calls ontological monism. Second, that Henry's positive
concept, the auto-affection of life, is lexically and argumentatively a
splitting of Heidegger's *Selbstaffektion* [self-affection], performed
inside Heidegger's own analysis, and that Henry's terminal formula in
§ 57 is Kant's formula for time with affectivity substituted for time.
Third, that the resulting argument is phenomenologically legitimate in
its form and lands at least two blows that Heidegger has no obvious
answer to, but that it terminates in a datum whose evidential status
Henry has made unfalsifiable by construction — which is a cost, and one
he does not price.

## II. Why Henry needs *this* book

The polemical target of *The Essence of Manifestation* is what Henry
calls ontological monism: the thesis, which he takes to govern
essentially the whole of Western philosophy, that there is one and only
one structure of manifestation, namely appearing-in-a-horizon, so that to
appear at all is to appear at a distance, over against, in the open.[^4]
Against this Henry sets a second, heterogeneous mode: immanent
self-revelation, which appears without distance, without horizon, without
standing over against anything — the self-affection of life.

The difficulty with such a thesis is evidential. It is easy to *assert*
that there is a second mode of phenomenality and very hard to *show* it,
because any showing seems to require exactly the horizonal structure
whose monopoly is in dispute. Henry's solution is not to exhibit
immanence directly but to drive monism into an aporia: to show that the
horizonal essence, pressed to account for its own internal possibility,
requires something it cannot supply. The immanent essence is then
introduced as what the aporia demands.

This strategy imposes a requirement on the choice of adversary. Henry
needs a monism that has already tried, with maximum rigour, to ground
itself — that has posed the question of its own internal possibility and
given an answer. A philosophy that merely *asserts* the horizon is no
use; there is nothing to break. *Sein und Zeit* is not quite what he
needs, because its account of the ekstatic unity of temporality is
offered more as a description than as a grounding. KPM is exactly what he
needs, because KPM is the text in which Heidegger asks, in so many words,
what makes transcendence internally possible, and answers: the
transcendental imagination, schematism, and time as pure self-affection.
KPM is the monist essence's own attempt at self-foundation. Henry's whole
book is the demonstration that the attempt fails, and the demonstration
can only be conducted on the text where the attempt is made.

This is why the KPM material is not distributed evenly across the book
but bunched at the joints. Henry does not consult it; he takes it over
whole, at the point where he needs monism to be at its strongest.

## III. The first imports: finitude and receptivity (§§ 13, 15, 22)

The earliest sustained use is § 13. Henry has just argued, in his own
voice, that Heideggerian transcendence — the going-beyond beings that
opens the horizon — is not free-floating but structurally *for* the
beings it transcends: the horizon is opened so that beings may appear in
it, and this "in view of" is receptivity. He states the thesis in a
formula that will be load-bearing for four hundred pages: "*Transcendence
is the need of the existent*," and "*Every transcendence is as such
essentially receptive.*"[^5]

Only then does he produce his authority, and it is KPM: "*Kant*," says
Heidegger, "*had to look for finitude in the rational being itself.*"[^6]
The commentary identifies the source and reads Henry's use of it exactly:
Kant's thesis, on Heidegger's reading, is that human cognition is finite
because it is essentially receptive (*hinnehmende Anschauung* — taking,
receptive intuition), and Heidegger's generalisation is that receptivity
is not an accident added to a rationality that would otherwise be
infinite but the structure of finite transcendence as such. The
commentary's verdict on Henry's attitude at this stage is unambiguous:
"Henry endorses Heidegger's reading and uses it to characterize the
structure of monism."[^7]

What Henry adds is a change of scope. Heidegger's claim is about the
finitude of *Dasein*'s transcendence; Henry's is about the essence of
manifestation as such, in any monist conception whatever. The KPM
doctrine is lifted out of its Kantian setting and made a thesis about
phenomenality in general.

The second import, at § 15, is the technical pair *Ent-stand* /
*Gegenstand* — Heidegger's coinage in KPM § 5, where he observes that for
an infinite, creative intuition (*intuitus originarius*) the being would
not stand *over against* as an object (*Gegen-stand*) but would, as it
were, *arise* in the intuiting (*Ent-stand*). Henry uses the pair to
prise finitude off the being and relocate it in the horizon: "Considered
in itself, the existent is as well the noumenon, the *Enstand* of
infinite intuition, as the *Gegenstand* of receptive knowledge."[^8] The
conclusion is his own and goes beyond Heidegger: "*Finitude is an eidetic
structure of the essence of phenomenality.*"[^9]

By § 22 the same pair is turned against its source. Henry now argues that
the contrast is incoherent, because every intuition — including any
"creative" one — must receive what it intuits as the content of its
intuition. "*Thus appears the inessential character of the Kantian
distinction between Entstand and Gegenstand.* In truth, this distinction
is not only inessential; it reveals itself fundamentally obscure."[^10]
And he names a specific error: "This confusion Heidegger commits when he
writes: '*to finite knowledge alone can be given a reality of the type
"object" (Gegenstand). It alone must expose itself to the existent that
already is.*'"[^11] The charge is that Heidegger has run together an
*ontic* character (the being pre-exists the knowing) with an
*ontological* one (the being is ob-ject), and given ultimate weight to a
distinction that only the first supports.

Three sections, three stages: adopt, generalise, demolish. The pattern
will repeat at a higher level.

## IV. The engine room: schematism and the circle (§§ 23–25)

Section II ends, at § 22, on a question rather than a thesis: "what is it
then that renders ontologically possible the receptivity of being — that
is, the manifestation of the pure horizon that the originary essence of
objectivation opposes to itself?"[^12] Sections 23 to 25 are Henry's
answer that monism has no answer, and they are conducted almost entirely
inside KPM.

The reconstruction is scrupulous. Henry works through Heidegger's chain
in order. What is to be received is the pure horizon, which contains
nothing ontic and is therefore equally the horizon of nothingness; the
power that posits something other than a being is imagination, and
transcendental imagination, since it belongs constitutively to
objectivation. Then the formula: "Heidegger's formula, that imagination
is the original and uncompounded unity of receptivity and spontaneity,
does not answer the question but restates it."[^13] The German behind
"original and uncompounded unity" is Heidegger's characterisation of pure
imagination as the *ursprüngliche* [original], *ungegliederte*
[unarticulated] unity of the two stems — the doctrine of KPM § 26 that
imagination is the common root Kant glimpsed and recoiled from.

Henry's objection to the formula is precise and worth stating in full,
because it is the hinge of the book. Imagination is said to be receptive
toward the horizon *because* it forms it. Henry inverts the dependency:
"That the pure ontological content is formed by the essence explains
nothing about its being received by the essence. The dependence runs the
other way. Forming the horizon has phenomenological sense only insofar as
what is formed shows itself — that is, is received." And then the
sentence that the rest of the book cashes: "the essence of spontaneity
lies in receptivity."[^14]

Heidegger's actual answer, Henry notes, runs the other way: not that
intuition is imagination but that imagination is intuition — "Heidegger
calls imagination a faculty of intuition (*Vermögen der Anschauung*) and
cites Kant's remark that it is constantly sensible."[^15] And this is
where Henry springs the circle. Intuition is possible only through the
opening of the transcendental field; but the horizon can render beings
accessible only by manifesting itself, and it is intuition that is
charged with manifesting the horizon. "Intuition thus finds its condition
in a horizon that becomes real only insofar as it is intuited … The
thought that asks intuition to realize what it conceives as intuition's
own condition is moving in a circle."[^16]

The obvious defence — that the intuition conditioned by the horizon is
*empirical*, while the intuition of the horizon is *pure* — Henry blocks
by pressing what "condition" means: pure intuition conditions empirical
intuition by constituting its essence, so the topic throughout is the
ontological power of intuition as such, and "the conditioned presents
itself as the condition of its own condition."[^17]

Schematism is then examined as the proposed way out, and found to rest on
the same foundation. Henry reports Heidegger's assignment accurately —
"the accomplishment of transcendence must at bottom be schematism, and
Kant necessarily comes upon transcendental schematism as soon as he seeks
the ground of transcendence's intrinsic possibility, so that
transcendental schematism is what explicates original, transcendental
truth in its essence"[^18] — and then asks how schematism manifests the
horizon. "By sensibilizing it" (*Versinnlichung*, sensible transposition)
— that is, by rendering it intuitable. "The perceptibility credited to
schematism is therefore grounded in intuition after all."[^19] The pure
image-schema (*Bild-schema*) is an image only through its intuitive
character; so schematism's last word is intuition, and intuition was the
term whose possibility was in question.

Henry's summary judgement on the whole construction is the sharpest thing
in Section II: intuition is "merely a name" for the receptivity that
secures the manifestation of the horizon, "or, when it is more than a
name, an unclarified and mysterious power borrowed outright from a
tradition that Henry has shown stands in need of grounding." And he
convicts Heidegger out of Heidegger's own mouth, noting that *Sein und
Zeit* itself observes that the idea of intuition has governed every
interpretation of knowledge from the beginning of Greek ontology
onward.[^20] The philosophy of being has not surpassed intuitionism; "it
has raised intuition's conditions to the absolute, and then resolved the
ultimate questions about those conditions by falling back on
intuition."[^21]

§ 25 closes the pincer by refusing Heidegger the escape route of the
groundless ground. Henry cites KPM's own declaration that laying the
ground of metaphysics "leads not to the absolute and clear evidence of a
first thesis or principle but deliberately directs us toward the
unknown," and its image of thought standing, with the transcendental
imagination, "before the abyss of metaphysics" (*Abgrund*).[^22] His
counter is not a counter-thesis but the phenomenological principle
itself: a ground defined solely by the privation of the phenomenality it
is supposed to produce explains nothing. "Introducing a ground defined
solely by privation of what it must produce makes the production less
intelligible, not more."[^23] Truth and the untruth grounding it become
"two juxtaposed essences," and the division of reality "between what
shows itself and what simply does not only appears to satisfy
phenomenology's demands."[^24]

That is the whole case against KPM, and it is worth noticing what kind of
case it is. Henry does not accuse Heidegger of getting Kant wrong. He
accepts the reading, reconstructs it faithfully — the commentary says so
twice, and once with emphasis: "Henry has reconstructed the Kant-book
faithfully in order to make its final term stand out as a term"[^25] —
and then argues that the reconstruction terminates in a term that cannot
bear the weight assigned to it. The critique is internal.

## V. The splitting of *Selbstaffektion* (§§ 24, 31)

§ 24 is where the borrowing becomes an appropriation. Its title —
"Reaffirming the central place of the problem of receptivity, and the
ontological interpretation of time as auto-affection" — announces KPM
§ 34 as its subject, and the section is a running engagement with it.

The chain Henry reconstructs is Heidegger's: time renders the horizon
perceptible; "time lends the horizon of transcendence, from the outset,
the character of a perceptible offering, and renders the 'opposedness' of
objectivity perceptible to a finite being."[^26] The horizon's
perceptibility rests on the essence's capacity to receive what it has
formed, that is, to affect itself. Hence time as self-affection.
Heidegger's formulations are then, in the commentary's own verb,
"conscripted": "time is by nature pure affection of itself; pure inner
sense is pure affection of itself, that is, original time; and pure
affection of itself determines the deepest essence of
transcendence."[^27] These render KPM § 34's *Die Zeit ist ihrem Wesen
nach reine Affektion ihrer selbst* and its companion theses. Henry also
takes over the supporting claim that inner sense "receives nothing from
outside,"[^28] and the corollary about space: "space is in a certain
sense always identical with time when time is understood as what is
formed in pure intuition, as pure object — but it is not in that form
that time is the original ground of transcendence, rather as pure
affection of itself."[^29]

And then, in the middle of the reconstruction, he inserts a distinction
that is not in Heidegger:

> Auto-affection has a double sense. It means, first, affection *by*
> itself: what affects time is time and nothing else … But auto-affection
> is not exhausted by this. More fundamentally it is affection *of*
> itself. Time is not only what affects; as what affects, it presupposes
> what is affected by it, and what is affected by time is time. Affection
> by itself is grounded in affection of itself.[^30]

The French behind this is *affection par soi* against *affection de soi*,
and the distinction is a wedge driven into a single German word.
Heidegger's *Selbstaffektion* does not distinguish them, because for him
the question of the *provenance* of what affects (it comes from time
itself, not from beings) settles the question of self-affection. Henry's
wedge separates provenance from givenness: that the affecting content
*originates* in the affected is one thing; that the affecting content
*is* the affected is another, and only the second is self-affection in
the strict sense.

Henry then makes this a methodological demand rather than a speculative
preference, and this is the most important sentence in the section for
assessing his phenomenological credentials: "Questions of that form stay
speculative so long as the answer is asked of speculation. The demand is
phenomenological: what is the link between the time that affects and the
time that is affected, and what does the claim that affection by itself
is grounded in affection of itself mean *phenomenologically*?"[^31]

The answer is a regress. The horizon of pure time cannot make anything
present unless it first manifests itself; its manifestation belongs not
to the time that *forms* it but to the time that *receives* it; therefore
affection by itself is possible only insofar as the horizon it opens is
received, that is, affects the original time. Affection by itself is
grounded in affection of itself.

§ 24 then delivers the conclusion that guts KPM from inside. If time
grounds transcendence by securing reception, and secures reception by
intuiting, then "what matters in the time that ultimately grounds the
essence of manifestation is not its temporal character but its intuitive
character."[^32] Time is doing its work in KPM under a false description.
It is not as *time* that original time is the ground of transcendence; it
is as *intuition* — and intuition, as § 23 established, is a name for an
unclarified receptivity. Heidegger's answer to "what makes transcendence
possible?" turns out to be a synonym for the question.

The verdict comes at § 31, and it is stated as a flat contradiction of
the text Henry has been living inside for eight sections:

> It is therefore false to say that pure affection of itself determines
> the deep essence of transcendence, if by pure affection of itself one
> means, as Heidegger does, auto-affection. And for the same reason
> auto-affection cannot be interpreted, as Heidegger also has it, as
> constituting the essence of time insofar as time is implicated in the
> intrinsic possibility of objectivation — that is, as
> transcendence.[^33]

The reason is the one prepared since § 22: in receiving the horizon it
projects, the essence does *not* receive itself, because the horizon is
the product of its imagination and does not coincide with its reality.
"Auto-affection lies neither in transcendence nor in
representation."[^34] Monism, Henry concludes, "has constantly confused
affection with auto-affection," and the ambiguity in Heidegger's concept
"does not mean there are two ways for the essence to affect itself; it
means nothing at all."[^35]

Henry is explicit that his own concept is not a coinage but a
rectification of Heidegger's. § 31 diagnoses *why* Heidegger reached for
transcendence to fill out auto-affection: "for that, transcendence is the
only essence a thought moving within monism has."[^36] The claim is not
that Heidegger used the wrong word but that he had no other structure
available. Henry's contribution is to supply the structure — immanence —
and to keep the word.

## VI. What the vocabulary carries into Section IV (§§ 52–57)

If KPM were merely a target, it would drop away once Section III begins.
It does not. It reappears at precisely the three places in Section IV
where Henry has to state his own positive doctrine.

At § 52 the Kantbuch is cited by page — Heidegger's formula "that all
affection is a manifestation in which an already given being announces
itself" — and the commentary notes exactly how Henry is using it: "the
formula concedes exactly what Henry needs, since if the manifestation of
a being requires the manifestation of a horizon, then every ontic
affection rests on an ontological one."[^37] Immediately after, Henry
"compresses Heidegger's Kant-book into a single chain in order to expose
its terminus," running opposition → object → imagination's reception →
time as intuitive reception → sensibility → inner sense, and stopping
there: "Henry has reconstructed the Kant-book faithfully in order to make
its final term stand out as a term — inner sense, not an absolute
beginning."[^38] The whole architecture of KPM is rebuilt inside § 52 for
the sole purpose of putting one more step behind its last one.

The commentary then registers, in its own voice, the interpretive stake:
"Readers who assimilate Henry's auto-affection to Heidegger's time as
pure self-affection collapse exactly the distinction this unit exists to
draw."[^39] The warning is necessary because the two doctrines are, on
their face, the same doctrine. They share a name, a structural position,
and a source.

At § 54 the borrowing is again by page: Henry glosses "Heidegger's claim
that the essence of sensibility lies in the finitude of intuition
(Kant-book, 87)" and extends it, arguing that pure sensibility, in which
empirical sensibility is founded, must also receive its content
intuitively — from which he draws the thesis that "the essence of
sensibility lies in affectivity."[^40]

And at § 57 the takeover is total. The section is titled "Affectivity as
the universal form of all possible experience in general and as the form
of that form," and its central proposition reads: "Affectivity is
precisely that: the universal form of all possible experience in general,
the ontological and transcendental dimension founding the reality of
everything that is."[^41] That formula is Kant's for time — the a priori
formal condition of all appearances whatever — and it is the formula KPM
makes the centre of its reading. Henry has not paraphrased it. He has
kept the sentence and changed the subject term. Where Kant and Heidegger
write *time*, Henry writes *affectivity*; and the second half of the
title, "the form of that form," is the added storey that his regress
requires: "if form designates the structure of affection, form must
itself be possible, and its own possibility … is the form in which form
is given to itself as form: auto-affection. Affectivity is the form of
the form, the essence of the essence."[^42]

This is the clearest evidence of the relation between the two books.
Henry's terminal doctrine occupies the exact syntactic and systematic
position that time occupies in KPM, expressed in the same words, reached
by one further application of the same regress. The Kantbuch is not a
source he consulted; it is the chair his own thesis sits in.

Two entries deserve comment.

The *Ent-stand* / *Gegenstand* pair is spelled inconsistently in the
commentary: "Enstand" at § 15, "Entstand" throughout § 22.[^43] I record
this as an observation about the commentary and do not know whether it
reflects Henry's printed text, the commentary's transcription, or the
French translation Henry used. Heidegger's term is *Ent-stand*, formed on
*entstehen* [to arise], and the pun on *Gegen-stand* is the whole point;
a text that prints "Enstand" has lost it.

The § 57 substitution is the only major takeover the commentary does not
flag as Heideggerian. It flags Heidegger elsewhere in the same section —
"the jab lands on Heidegger and on existential phenomenology
together"[^44] — but does not note that the formula Henry is filling with
affectivity is the one KPM fills with time. That is a genuine gap in the
commentary rather than a subtlety of Henry's, and I mark it as such.

## VIII. How central? A counterfactual test

The best way to measure the dependence is to ask what survives if KPM is
removed.

Henry's *motive* for immanence survives untouched. It does not come from
Heidegger and never did. The commentary's synoptic remarks on the
Introduction name the tradition Henry is recovering — Descartes at his
most radical, Maine de Biran, Fichte, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Husserl at
his most radical — and set it against "the reigning tradition
(Plato-Aristotle-Kant-Hegel-Heidegger, a tradition of horizonal
transcendence)."[^45] Sections III's long treatments of Eckhart and
Fichte owe nothing to KPM. Henry would still have wanted to say that the
ego's being is not horizonal.

What does not survive is his *entitlement* to say it. Remove KPM and the
following go with it:

1. **The demonstration that transcendence is receptive** (§ 13) loses its
   authority and reverts to assertion.
2. **The aporia that closes Section II** (§ 22) cannot be stated, because
   the problem of receptivity in its ontological form is KPM's problem.
3. **§§ 23–25 vanish entirely.** They are a commentary on KPM's §§ 22–35.
   With them goes the demonstration that monism's own best attempt at
   self-grounding is circular.
4. **Auto-affection loses its derivation.** Henry's concept is introduced
   in § 24 as a correction *internal to* Heidegger's, and § 31's central
   result is stated as the refutation of a Heideggerian thesis. Without
   the thesis there is nothing to refute, and auto-affection becomes a
   posit.
5. **§ 57's terminal formula loses its point.** "The universal form of
   all possible experience" is a polemical substitution; without the
   term it displaces, it is merely a grand phrase.

What remains, in other words, is Henry's conclusion without Henry's
argument. That is the measure of centrality. KPM is not where Henry got
his idea; it is what makes his idea a result rather than a conviction.

One qualification is owed. Henry's engagement is with *Heidegger's* Kant,
not with Kant. He says so: § 7 announces a separate work devoted to a
destruction of the Paralogisms, and a footnote concedes he has not been
able to include it.[^46] Almost every appearance of Kant in the argument
proper — finitude, receptivity, the *Ent-stand*, schematism, self-affection,
the pure image — arrives through KPM. The exceptions are genuine but
peripheral: the refutation of Mendelssohn at § 51, the Refutation of
Idealism at § 12 (itself read through *Sein und Zeit* § 43), the
architectonic at § 1. When Henry writes "Kant" in the load-bearing
sections, he means the Kant of 1929.

## IX. Does the argument work?

The question the borrowing raises is not whether Henry was fair to
Heidegger — on the evidence he was scrupulous, and reconstructed the
Kantbuch's chain more carefully than most of its admirers — but whether
the regress he runs on it is a phenomenological argument or a
metaphysical one wearing phenomenological clothes. There is a case each
way, and the honest answer is that he wins two rounds and loses one.

### The first blow lands: provenance is not givenness

Henry's wedge between *affection par soi* and *affection de soi* exposes
something real. Heidegger's argument in KPM § 34 that time is
self-affection turns substantially on the claim that what affects is not
a being but time's own product — the horizon of pure succession that time
itself forms. That is a claim about the *origin* of the affecting
content. Henry's objection is that origin is not a phenomenological
datum: "the origin of the content of an affection cannot substitute for
that affection's phenomenological reality."[^47]

This is a correct application of the phenomenological principle, and it
is applied to a thinker who accepts the principle. If reality is mode of
givenness, then a self-affection certified by the pedigree of what
affects, rather than by how it gives itself, is certified by the wrong
thing. Henry presses the point with a dilemma that is hard to answer:
either the posited time floats free, "collapsing into the fictional
entities of speculation," or it must be received by yet another time, in
which the ultimate possibility of self-affection would then reside.[^48]
Heidegger has no obvious third option; his recourse is to deny that the
question is well posed, which is a change of subject rather than a reply.

### The second blow lands: the unity of the ekstases is asserted

§ 35 makes an objection that is less famous than it deserves to be.
Heidegger's "unity of spontaneity and receptivity," Henry argues,
"designates only that the act which forms the horizon is essentially
identical with the act which receives it. What is thought under that
concept is one and the same act, the act of transcendence — not that
act's internal possibility of remaining one in itself throughout its
accomplishment."[^49] It is the coherence of the *whole structure*
(transcendence plus horizon), not the internal coherence of the essence,
and it has been substituted for the latter.

This is a real gap in KPM and in the ekstatic analysis generally. The
ekstatic unity of temporality is offered as a *datum* of the analysis —
the three ekstases are shown to belong together — but what makes them a
unity rather than a manifold is not accounted for beyond the assertion
that they are equiprimordial. Henry's demand for the ground of that unity
is not obviously answerable in Heidegger's own terms, and it is a demand
Heidegger's own project licenses.

### The counter-blow: has Henry hit a reflection theory Heidegger does not hold?

The strongest defence of Heidegger is that Henry's regress is a
reflection-theory regress — the argument, familiar from Fichte and
revived by Henrich and Frank, that any model of self-knowledge as a
relation between a knower and a known already presupposes an
unthematised self-familiarity — and that ekstatic temporality is
specifically designed not to be such a model. The three ekstases are not
a subject relating to itself across a distance; they are a self-differing
that is a unity in and through the differing. If so, Henry's demand that
the differing "hold itself near itself" prior to differing imports the
very substantialist self-identity that ekstasis was constructed to
replace, and the regress does not start.

Henry's answer is in § 33: to relate to something is to bring oneself
alongside, which means "not separating from itself, staying near itself,
remaining in itself, so that this 'self', maintained and preserved
throughout the movement, is carried with it."[^50] But that is a
restatement of the premise Heidegger denies, not an argument for it. On
this round I think Heidegger has a defensible position and Henry does not
close it — though it is worth noting that the position Heidegger has to
occupy to survive (that a self-differing unity needs no further ground) is
exactly the position § 35 attacks. The two objections work as a pincer
even where each alone is answerable.

### The cost Henry does not price

The deeper problem is not with the regress but with its terminus.

Henry's positive datum is defined by a series of exclusions that is, on
his own account, exhaustive. Feeling "is never and can never be
felt."[^51] It cannot be perceived, since "every perception, even a pure
one … presupposes at least the schematism of the understanding."[^52] It
is not the content of any sense, nor an object of reflection, nor
available in any thematic grasp. Auto-affection is precisely the
structure that ekstasis presupposes and in which "no ekstasis occurs and
transcendence is absent."[^53]

Henry sees the objection coming and answers it at § 52. Does the
description of immanence "remain formal"? Is the necessity it uncovers
"merely a necessity that is understood," an eidetic analysis reducing to
"the demands of reflective thought"? His answer is a change of register:
"to read in auto-affection what it is rather than deducing it. The
simplest experience, the one instituted before ekstasis and within it,
the immediate experience of self, the original feeling essence has of
itself — that can be recognized."[^54] The commentary's gloss is exact
and, I think, correct: "The answer to the charge of formalism is not a
further argument but a change of register."[^55]

This is a real answer and not an evasion. Phenomenology is entitled to
appeal to what shows itself, and Henry is entitled to say that
self-feeling shows itself in a mode that thematising acts distort. But
the appeal has a price he does not pay. A datum that cannot be felt,
perceived, thematised, or made the object of any act is a datum against
which no competing description can be tested. If a reader reports that
they find no such non-horizonal self-feeling, Henry's framework yields
the reply that they have looked for it in the wrong register — and there
is no further move. The evidential asymmetry is total: the doctrine can
be confirmed by recognition but cannot be disconfirmed by its absence.

This matters for the assessment of the whole enterprise, because it is
exactly the charge Henry brings against Heidegger. His indictment of the
*Abgrund* at § 25 is that a ground defined solely by the privation of the
phenomenality it produces cannot explain that production, and that
dividing reality "between what shows itself and what simply does not only
appears to satisfy phenomenology's demands."[^56] Henry's immanence is
not defined by privation — he insists at length that its negativity is
only apparent and that self-feeling is positively given — but it *is*
defined by its unavailability to every mode of access the tradition can
name. Whether that is a different thing or the same thing in a warmer
vocabulary is the question the book leaves open, and it is the question
on which the value of the borrowed abyss finally turns.

### The verdict

Henry's regress behind KPM is a legitimate phenomenological argument. It
uses phenomenology's own criterion against a phenomenologist, it lands at
least two blows for which there is no easy reply, and it is conducted with
unusual fidelity to the text it attacks. What it establishes, however, is
narrower than what Henry takes it to establish. It shows that Heidegger's
self-affection is not self-affection in the strict sense and that KPM's
grounding of transcendence terminates in an unclarified receptivity. It
does not show that the strict sense is *instantiated* — that there is in
fact a non-horizonal self-feeling — and Henry's transition from the
first to the second, at § 52, is made by appeal to a recognition that his
own framework has placed beyond challenge.

## X. Conclusion

Named five times and cited twice by page, *Kant und das Problem der
Metaphysik* is the most efficiently used text in *The Essence of
Manifestation*. It supplies the doctrine that makes Henry's diagnosis of
monism demonstrable rather than assertive (transcendence is essentially
receptive); the problem whose insolubility drives the book from Section
II to Section III (the internal possibility of receptivity); the analysis
whose circularity is Henry's central negative result (schematism and
time-as-intuition); the concept he splits in two to produce his own
(*Selbstaffektion*); and the sentence he empties and refills to state his
conclusion (the universal form of all possible experience). Where *Sein
und Zeit* is the pervasive interlocutor and the standing target, KPM is
the load-bearing wall.

The relation is best described not as influence and not as polemic but as
completion under protest. Heidegger, in KPM, pushed the horizonal essence
to the point where it had to account for its own possibility, gave an
account, and, at the limit, named the ground an abyss and the root
unknown. Henry took the same chain, ran it one link further, and claimed
that the link Heidegger declined to add is the one on which everything
depends. His radical immanence is, in its argumentative structure, the
refusal of KPM's last word — and it borrows KPM's own vocabulary to
refuse it, down to the name of the doctrine and the shape of the
concluding sentence.

Whether that final link holds is a question the argument cannot settle by
itself, because Henry has defined its terminus as something no argument
could reach. What can be settled is the historical and structural claim,
and it is this: without Heidegger's Kant-book, the central thesis of *The
Essence of Manifestation* would still have been believed by its author,
and could not have been proved.

# The Appearing of Appearing

## Phenomenality in Michel Henry's *Phénoménologie de la vie*

*A note on citation.* Henry is cited from the three volumes of *Phénoménologie de la vie* held as files in this repository's source folder, by a short name for the essay and the line number of the file, for example [PL, l. 493]. The short names are listed at the end. Line numbers are not pagination; they are given so that each claim can be checked against the text I checked it against. All translations from Henry's French are mine. Husserl's lectures on time are quoted from the English of the *Collected Works* edition held in the same folder, and *Sein und Zeit* is translated by me from the German file, each with its line number.

---

Michel Henry is commonly read as the philosopher of life: of auto-affection, of the invisible, of a flesh that is not a body, and, in the late work, of a Christianity taken as philosophy. It is also commonly said, and Henry himself reports the charge, that this thought belongs to a "theological turn of French phenomenology" [PL, l. 539]. On this view life is the doctrine and phenomenology the idiom in which it is delivered. The essays collected after his death under the title *Phénoménologie de la vie* do not support that order. They begin, again and again, from a definition of phenomenology by its object, and that object is not life. It is what Henry calls phenomenality (*phénoménalité*): that which "allows a phenomenon to be a phenomenon" [PL, l. 485]. Life enters these essays second, as the answer to a question about appearing which Henry holds that Husserl and Heidegger asked and could not answer.

In what follows I wish to show that the centre of Henry's investigation of phenomenality is a single requirement, which he draws from Husserl's own definition of the object of phenomenology: that appearing must itself appear, and that the way in which it does so has a describable "matter". I wish to show, second, that this requirement, and not the opposition of life and world for which Henry is known, carries his critique of Husserl and of Heidegger and contains what is original in his position. And I wish to show, third, that the same requirement produces in these essays two accounts of how the two modes of appearing stand to one another, a duality and a foundation, and that Henry's own standard of phenomenological determinacy is not met by the second.

The article rests on twelve essays from the three volumes, written between 1977 and 2000, in which the concept is worked out by name. From the first volume: the lecture "What is it that we call life?" of 1977, "Four principles of phenomenology" of 1991, "Non-intentional phenomenology" of 1992, "Phenomenology of birth" of 1994, and the Munich lecture "Phenomenology of life" of 2000. From the second: "The *cogito* and the idea of phenomenology" of 1983, "Philosophy and subjectivity" of 1989, and the essay on Descartes's *cogito* first given in 1991. From the third: "Material phenomenology and language", an essay on life and the republic, and two pieces on art. These were chosen by counting where the word *phénoménalité* is concentrated and were read in whole or in their relevant stretches; the essays on Marx, Schopenhauer and Nietzsche were not read. I leave aside the fourth volume, on Christianity, which is not in the folder, and Henry's books, including *The Essence of Manifestation*, to which the essays refer. A claim below about what Henry "does not" say is therefore a claim about these essays only. Where Henry cites Husserl or Heidegger I have checked the passage in their own text when the folder holds it, and I say so. One file is damaged: several paragraphs of "Non-intentional phenomenology" have been scattered into its notes by the scan, and I do not quote from them.

## I. The object of phenomenology and its indeterminacy

Henry's essays on phenomenology open in the same way. The other sciences study particular phenomena, physical, historical or economic. Phenomenology asks what makes any of them a phenomenon. The distinction that follows is "between what shows itself, what appears, and the fact of appearing" [PL, l. 491]. Henry gives the second term many names in one sentence: pure manifestation, showing, unveiling, discovery, appearing, revelation, and "truth" in a traditional sense [PL, l. 485]. For most of the period he treats these as equivalents. I shall use "phenomenality" for the concept and "appearing" (*apparaître*) for what Henry most often calls it, and note below where he later separates the names.

Henry does not present this definition as his own. He attributes it to Husserl, who in the lectures on time designates the objects of phenomenology as "objects in the How" (*Gegenstände im Wie*), and he glosses the phrase at once: the How of the givenness of a phenomenon is "not what appears but the mode of its appearing" [NI, l. 916]. The attribution can be checked. The English of Husserl's lectures reads "objects in their ways of appearing", with the German in brackets, and continues: "The form of the way of appearing is the orientation: the now, the just past, the future" [Husserl, *Time*, l. 13327–13331]. Henry also finds the distinction in Heidegger, who in § 44 of *Sein und Zeit* separates what is true, the unveiled, from unveiling as "the most original phenomenon of truth" [PL, l. 491; *SZ*, l. 3516]. The starting point is therefore common ground. Both predecessors, on Henry's account, already know that phenomenology has to do with the How.

Two consequences are drawn from the definition, and each is a thesis about the standing of phenomenology. The first is that phenomenology is defined by its object and not by its method. As method it is a process of elucidation, a bringing to evidence, and such a process "is possible only on the ground of appearing" [NI, l. 910]. Henry therefore proposes "to set aside as non-original the definition of phenomenology as method" [NI, l. 910]. The late essay on language repeats the point as its first numbered thesis and calls its result paradoxical: the question of method is absorbed into the question of the object, because phenomenality is itself the opening of the way that leads to the phenomenon [ML, l. 2726–2728].

The second consequence concerns being. Henry takes over, with a small alteration that he announces, a formula that he says Husserl borrowed from the Marburg school: "as much appearing, so much being" [PL, l. 493; FP, l. 629]. He reads it as a relation of foundation and not of proportion. "Appearing is everything, being is nothing", or rather being is only because appearing appears [FP, l. 651]. Hence: "Higher than ontology, then, stands phenomenology" [FP, l. 655]. The ancestor claimed for this subordination is Descartes, for whom, as Henry reads the *Meditations*, being "resides in appearing and is exhausted in it" [FP, l. 660]. The same thesis decides how Henry will meet Heidegger. Phenomenology, he writes, exists "from the moment ontology is considered a secondary discipline" [NI, l. 950].

So far Henry has stated what phenomenology already holds. The turn comes with the observation that its founding principles "remain in fact fundamentally indeterminate" [FP, l. 643]. The essay of 1991 lists four: the formula just quoted; Husserl's principle of principles, which makes every originally giving intuition a source of right for knowledge; the call "to the things themselves"; and the principle that Jean-Luc Marion had recently formulated, "so much reduction, so much givenness" [FP, l. 629–641]. Each speaks of appearing, intuition, access or givenness, and none says in what appearing consists. What does the first principle mean, Henry asks, if one does not know what "appearing" means, and what does the third mean if one does not know *how* things appear [FP, l. 699–701]? The principles are formal.

Is this to say that phenomenology has worked without any conception of phenomenality? Henry's answer is no, and this is the more serious charge. Under cover of the indeterminacy a particular conception has slipped in, the one that presents itself first to ordinary thought and is for that reason "the oldest and least critical prejudice of traditional philosophy": a conception of phenomenality "borrowed from the perception of the objects of the world" [PL, l. 493]. It has been taken not for "a particular form of experience", which it is, but for the structure of appearing in general, and Henry calls this a "catastrophic confusion of the appearing of the world with the universal essence of appearing" [FP, l. 707]. The same paragraph, almost word for word, stands in the essay on language five years later [ML, l. 2747]. The diagnosis is thus fixed early and does not change.

It is at this point that the requirement I take to be central is stated. Henry says that he radicalises the priority of phenomenology over ontology by adding that "it is only if appearing appears in itself and as such that something, whatever it may be, can appear in its turn" [PL, l. 493]. In the essay of 1992 the same thought is given as an argument. A thing has no capacity to bring itself into appearing. If, then, appearing did not itself appear as appearing, nothing would appear and there would be nothing at all [NI, l. 922]. Henry's name for what is required is self-appearing (*auto-apparaître*), and its classical form is the thesis that nothing would be conscious if consciousness were not itself conscious, which, he adds, classical thought never managed to establish [NI, l. 942]. That this requirement organises everything else is my ordering of the essays and is not stated by Henry in those words; what he does say is that the question of the appearing of appearing is the question of phenomenology, its "thing itself", in a passage the scan has broken [NI, l. 922 and notes]. The requirement has two parts, which the next sections follow separately. Appearing must appear; and one must be able to say in what "pure phenomenological matter" it does so, the matter "in which phenomenality as such phenomenalises itself" [PL, l. 493].

The themes of phenomenology that this touches can now be named. They are the concept of the phenomenon and its doubling into what appears and its appearing; givenness and the reduction that leads to it; the relation of phenomenology to ontology; the pre-reflective self-awareness of consciousness and the regress that threatens it; the constitution of time, where Husserl meets that regress; and affectivity, the body and language, which Henry will treat as fields whose description depends on which appearing is presupposed. What Henry adds to each depends on how he reads the two authors who, he says, left phenomenality undetermined.

## II. Husserl: making-seen and the appearing of intentionality

Henry expects resistance to the claim that the founder of phenomenology understood appearing as the appearing of the world, since Husserl refers phenomenality to consciousness and so, it seems, to an interior. His reply is that consciousness is defined as intentionality, and that intentional consciousness "is nothing other than the movement by which it throws itself outside" [PL, l. 497]. To reveal in such a movement is to make seen. Henry's term for this is making-seen (*faire-voir*), and he ties it to distance: the possibility of vision lies in the setting at a distance of what is placed before the seeing [PL, l. 497]. The principle of principles is read accordingly. It claims universality, since "originally giving intuition" names the condition of every phenomenon, and it names a particular mode, since intuition in Husserl is fulfilled intentionality. What makes the phenomenon a phenomenon is then "the transcendence of this object, its setting at a distance" [FP, l. 714].

Henry does not belittle what was done on this basis. The systematic elucidation of the correlations of act and object is an "immense work" that it is "out of the question to minimise" [NI, l. 944]; the difference between kinds of intentionality is Husserl's "discovery of genius" [NI, l. 997]; the intentional conception of language remains his "great discovery" [ML, l. 2752]. Nor does he place the whole of Husserl on one side. He credits the lectures of 1905 with the decisive step from the given to the mode of its givenness, so that the path from reduction to givenness "is travelled from 1905" [FP, l. 738]. He finds in "a few very rare texts" of the same lectures the thought that the sensuous impression is not a mere component of consciousness but what gives consciousness to itself before it gives anything else [NI, l. 979]. He quotes Husserl's sentence that the consciousness which judges a mathematical state of affairs "is an impression" [PL, l. 559]. And he takes from a late manuscript the sentence "I am not only for myself, but I am I" as evidence that Husserl saw a self prior to self-representation [PL, l. 545]. The parallels are real, and Henry states them himself.

The difference lies in one question. If intentionality makes everything seen, how is intentionality itself revealed? The essay of 1992 sets it in italics: the intentionality that gives beings, "how do we have access to it? Is it again by an intentionality?" [NI, l. 946]. If so, a second intentionality must stand behind the first, and phenomenology repeats "the bitter fate" of the classical philosophy of consciousness, a regress without end [PL, l. 497]. If not, there is a mode of revelation other than making-seen. Henry stresses that the question does not come from outside. It is the question of phenomenology, the question of givenness, put to the principle that phenomenology has made the source of givenness; when intentional phenomenology asks it of intentionality, it "destroys itself" as intentional phenomenology [NI, l. 946–948]. Henry distinguishes the question expressly from Heidegger's: it concerns "not the being" of intentionality "but its appearing" [NI, l. 950].

Henry supports the charge with a symptom in the vocabulary. Husserl's word for self-givenness, and Fink's, ought to name the givenness of givenness. In an article by Fink that Henry dates to 1935 it names the self-showing of beings: phenomenality is "the being-true of beings", and phenomenology the attempt to found knowledge "by a return to the self-givenness of beings" [NI, l. 936–938]. Henry calls this transfer "as paradoxical as it is absurd" [NI, l. 942], since a being is by itself foreign to givenness and cannot give givenness to itself. The consequence he draws is that under intentionality it is no longer appearing that appears but "what it makes appear in it: beings" [NI, l. 922]. Seeing eclipses itself in the being-seen of what is seen. "Seeing is never seen" [NI, l. 952].

The place where Henry holds that Husserl met the difficulty in full light is § 39 of the lectures on time, where the self-revelation of the absolute flow of consciousness is in question. "To try to resolve it Husserl appeals to intentionality" [NI, l. 960]: the flow constitutes itself, and this self-constitution is taken to be the structure of the flow. Henry's judgement is severe. By "a philosophical decision that is concealed but of extraordinary violence", the structure of conscious life is bent to the conditions of a self-revelation entrusted to intentionality "as to the only appearing one knows" [NI, l. 960]. The cost appears at once. The constituting phases cannot be identical with the constituted ones, the categories of the constituted do not apply to the constituting, and in the end "we know nothing of these" [NI, l. 960]. The essay of 1989 states the result as "the paradox of phenomenology": that which makes seen is never seen, "the principle of all phenomenality never phenomenalises itself", and this is what is "discreetly" called the anonymity of transcendental life [PS, l. 293].

How does this reading stand when set beside Husserl's text? On the main point it is accurate. Husserl writes in § 39 that the flow is so fashioned "that a self-appearance of the flow necessarily exists in it", that this self-appearance "does not require a second flow", and that "the constituting and the constituted coincide, and yet naturally they cannot coincide in every respect" [Husserl, *Time*, l. 10567–10577]. The non-coincidence that Henry cites is thus Husserl's own admission, and so is the lack of categories: of the constituting flow Husserl says elsewhere in the lectures, "For all of this, we lack names" [Husserl, *Time*, l. 9885]. Two qualifications are needed, and both are mine. First, Henry's Munich lecture says of the question how intentionality reveals itself that "there is no answer to this question in phenomenology" [PL, l. 497]. The essays of 1989 and 1992 are more exact: there is an answer, and Henry rejects its kind. Second, the word on which Henry builds, self-appearing, has its nearest ancestor in this very paragraph of Husserl, whose German word is *Selbsterscheinung*. Henry knows this. The essay of 1989 quotes the sentences just cited with the German word in brackets, and concludes that if the flow appears to itself only in so far as it constitutes itself, then the constituting phases are never given as constituting, which means that "what makes seen is never seen" [PS, l. 282–291]. The essays of the 1990s and the Munich lecture, in which *auto-apparaître* becomes Henry's own term, discuss § 39 without the German word; a search finds it only in the essay of 1989 and once in an essay of 1998. Henry's requirement that appearing appear is therefore closer to a thesis of Husserl's than his later presentations suggest. What separates them is the second half of the requirement: Husserl holds that the flow appears to itself and has no names for how; Henry holds that the how can be named, and that it is not an intentionality.

## III. Heidegger: the appearing of the world at its highest elaboration

Henry's treatment of Heidegger begins with credit. "It is in Heidegger that the appearing of the world is brought to its highest degree of elaboration" [PL, l. 502]. Heidegger is the one who "first thought the concept of the world in its original phenomenological meaning as pure appearing" [PL, l. 518]. Henry rests this on two passages, and both are as he reports them. In § 7 of *Sein und Zeit* the phenomenon is understood from the Greek word and its root, which means light, brightness, "that is, that within which something can become manifest, visible in itself" [*SZ*, l. 700]. In the second division temporality, with which Henry says the world is there identified, is called "the original 'outside-itself' in and for itself" (*das ursprüngliche »Außer-sich«*) [*SZ*, l. 5239]. Henry's standing term for the world's appearing, the outside-itself (*hors de soi*), is this phrase of Heidegger's. He adds a judgement about the composition of the book: in its second part the "ontic considerations on *Dasein*" are at last set aside and phenomenality is analysed for itself, as ecstatic temporality [ML, l. 2755].

From this Henry derives three traits of the world's appearing, which he presents in the same order in 1996 and in 2000. First, since this appearing consists in the coming outside of an Outside, everything that shows itself in it shows itself "as *exterior*, as *other*, as *different*" [PL, l. 506]. Second, an appearing that differs from what it unveils is indifferent to it. It lights up victims and executioners, a goat and a seaplane, "in a terrifying neutrality" [PL, l. 516]. Third, and this is the trait that matters for the argument, the indifference hides an impotence: the appearing of the world "is incapable of conferring existence" on what it unveils [PL, l. 518]. Henry ascribes the admission to Heidegger: unveiling opens, "but does not create (*macht nicht, öffnet*)" [PL, l. 518; ML, l. 2775]. He gives no reference for the German phrase in either essay, and I have not located it. He finds the same result in Kant, for whom the forms of intuition and the categories are ways of making-seen and are for ever unable to posit the reality of what they show, a reality that "Kant had to ask of *sensation*" [PL, l. 522].

The third trait is turned against the first principle. According to that principle it is phenomenality that delivers being. Yet here it is conceded that what appears does not owe its existence to the power of appearing, which only uncovers a being that precedes it. Henry calls this "a paradox that strikes at the heart of the very principle of phenomenology" [ML, l. 2777]. The move has the form of an objection turned: Henry does not bring a foreign measure to Heidegger but shows that, on Heidegger's own description, the world's appearing cannot do what phenomenology says appearing does.

It follows for Henry that Heidegger's critique of Husserl leaves the decisive point untouched. The critique is "inoperative" because what it opposes to intuition and intentionality under the titles of *Dasein*, transcendence and ecstatic truth "only states the condition of this intuition or this intentionality" [FP, l. 722]. No phenomenality other in its matter than the ecstasis in which every intuition unfolds is conceived. Henry is here more generous to Husserl than to Heidegger. Against the "too often accepted" criticism of the pupil, he recalls that all Husserl's fields of objects are subordinated to an instance which, however improperly named a transcendental ego, signifies pure phenomenality as such [FP, l. 776]. And he holds that Heidegger's thought remains bound to an "ontic prior": *Dasein*, however distinguished, is a being. A note recalls that he had made this criticism in 1963 [FP, l. 886].

The same reasoning yields Henry's position toward those who came after Heidegger. If being, in Heidegger, has its phenomenological basis in ecstatic truth, then it is "only a regional instance" [FP, l. 831], and it is legitimate to except oneself from it, as Levinas and Marion do. But the exception is possible only phenomenologically, that is, only if something still appears when ecstatic phenomenality is suspended. Henry compresses this into a sentence: "'Otherwise than being' means 'appearing otherwise'" [FP, l. 835]. The essay on birth applies the point to *Dasein* itself. As the outside-itself, *Dasein* gives access to a living being exactly as it gives access to a stone. That the being is alive is nothing the unveiling explains, for "the unveiling accomplished in such an appearing in no way explains what is unveiled by it" [PB, l. 1128]. To see a living thing is "in no way to become living oneself" [PB, l. 1126]. To be born, then, "does not mean to come into the world" [PL, l. 545].

Here a reader of Heidegger will object. Is this to say that Heidegger knows no disclosure other than a seeing at a distance? *Sein und Zeit* holds that attunement discloses before all cognition and volition. Its § 29 says that mood "has in each case already disclosed being-in-the-world as a whole and first makes possible a directing oneself toward" something, and that being attuned is no inner state that then colours things [*SZ*, l. 2315]. That is an affective disclosure which is not an act directed at an object, and it is the nearest thing in Heidegger to what Henry will call the revelation proper to life. In a certain way, then, one must grant the objection: Henry's three traits describe unveiling as Heidegger analyses it in § 7 and § 44, and they pass over the analysis in which Heidegger himself roots disclosure in affect. The silence is measurable. The words *Befindlichkeit* and *Stimmung*, and the usual French renderings I searched for, do not occur in the three volumes; Heidegger's anxiety is mentioned only in passing, for instance as the mood "in which everything becomes indifferent" [PL, l. 518]. However, it does not follow that Henry's criterion has nothing to say here. What attunement discloses, in the sentence just quoted, is being-in-the-world as a whole. On Henry's terms the question is not whether a disclosure is affective but whether the affect is itself revealed by what it opens upon or by itself, and an affect whose work is to open the world would still have its phenomenality in the outside. This reply is my construction from Henry's principles. The essays read here do not make it, and whether the book of 1963 does lies outside the texts I have used. The confrontation with the strongest Heideggerian candidate is therefore missing from these volumes, and Henry's verdict that "the simple question" of an affective phenomenological matter "at no moment comes to mind" in historical phenomenology [FP, l. 722–727] is, as a report on Heidegger, not established by them.

## IV. The matter of phenomenality: self-appearing as pathos

Husserl and Heidegger have now been read as two forms of one presupposition. Henry's own position begins with a reduction. The reduction that phenomenology practises is, in his terms, a "pure" reduction: it sets beings aside in order to thematise their appearing. But the appearing it isolates is the horizon of visibility. Henry therefore calls for a further step: "the radical reduction reduces appearing itself"; it sets aside "this stretch of light that we call the world" in order to find that without which the horizon would never become visible [FP, l. 748]. The essay on Descartes describes the same operation with a single image. The reduction strikes appearing and splits it in two "like a block of crystal broken by hammer blows" [CD, l. 747]. On one side is a seeing that is doubtful in the very light of its vision. On the other is the appearing in which seeing is given to itself without seeing itself, and which no one has ever seen.

Descartes is Henry's witness that such a reduction can be carried out, and the texts are the same from 1983 to 1992. When everything seen is suspended as possibly dreamt, there remains "it seems to me that I see", which Henry reads with a sentence from a letter: "we feel that we see" (*sentimus nos videre*) [CI, l. 413]. And there remains what article 26 of the *Passions of the Soul* says of the dreamer: one may imagine things that are not there, but one cannot feel sad or moved by any passion without its being very true that the soul has that passion [CD, l. 758]. Henry's conclusion is that seeing does not owe its certainty to its seeing. A seeing is possible only as a seeing that feels itself, and in that respect it is "a non-seeing" [CD, l. 760]. The essay of 1991 generalises: "all seeing bears in it a non-seeing without which it would see nothing" [FP, l. 758].

The question is then where such a self-appearing is to be found. Henry's answer in 1992 is abrupt: "Nowhere" [NI, l. 966]. Either it does not exist, or it is by its nature withdrawn from the seeing that looks for it. That the second is not an evasion is shown by the case already established: intentionality itself is never its own object. A self-appearing withdrawn from every horizon is, Henry says, at once the most mysterious thing and the most common, "what everyone knows": it is life [NI, l. 971]. Life is not phenomenological in the sense that it too shows itself, one phenomenon among others. It is phenomenological in the sense that it "creates phenomenality" [NI, l. 973]. The Munich lecture says that it is "phenomenological through and through", neither a being nor a mode of being of a being, and not the life of which biology speaks [PL, l. 526].

Henry calls this mode self-revelation, and he says exactly what the prefix means. First, it is life that does the revealing. Second, what it reveals is itself, so that the opposition between what appears and appearing, which phenomenology brought to the fore, "disappears in the case of life" [PL, l. 526]. His example is a pain, and he keeps it through the whole lecture. Practise on it the reduction that retains only its painful character. This pure suffering "reveals itself to itself": only suffering lets us know what suffering is, and what is revealed is suffering. No gap separates it from itself; driven back upon itself, it cannot take the least distance from what it is. For that reason no look can be directed at it. "No one has ever seen their suffering, their anguish or their joy" [PL, l. 528]. Henry's verb for this relation is *s'éprouver soi-même*, to experience or undergo oneself, and the French keeps the sense of a trial borne that the English loses.

The example yields the three traits in which the revelation of life is opposed "trait for trait" to the appearing of the world [ML, l. 2800]. Life reveals only itself, where the world's appearing shows only what is other. In life revelation and reality "are one", where the world's appearing strips what it shows of reality: I can represent my suffering, but then I have before me "a noematic irreality, the meaning 'suffering'" [PL, l. 533]. And life remains in what it reveals, where the world's appearing is indifferent to it. Henry insists that invisible here does not mean unreal or illusory. Already in 1977 he writes that if life has no face it is not nothing, "not the mere privation of phenomenality" [WL, l. 401], and in 1994 that the invisible "is not the concept antithetical to phenomenality" [PB, l. 1277].

The second half of the requirement is now met by a correction which Henry makes in the open. We said, he writes, that it is suffering that reveals suffering; "but this proposition must be corrected" [PL, l. 557]. The self-revelation cannot be the work of suffering in its particular content, since it is accomplished just as well in joy, boredom or effort. It is the work of the affectivity of each. The matter in which phenomenality originally phenomenalises itself is affectivity, which the later essays call *pathos*; his adjective *pathétique* means "of the nature of pathos" and has nothing of the English "pathetic". The essay of 1992 calls this "the pure non-intentional" [NI, l. 977]. The lecture of 2000 gives its two original tonalities, "a pure suffering and a pure enjoying" [PL, l. 569], and its character: a radical passivity of life with regard to itself, a bearing of oneself that is "stronger than any freedom" [PL, l. 567].

What is original in this conception can be set out in four points. The ordering is mine.

(1) *Phenomenality has a matter.* Husserl asked for the How of givenness and Heidegger for the meaning of the phenomenon. Henry turns the question of the How upon appearing itself and holds that the answer is not a structure, a form or a condition of possibility, but a phenomenological stuff. He gave this programme its name in 1983: "I call material phenomenology that which takes into account the phenomenological materiality of pure phenomenality" [CI, l. 443]. The claim excludes any univocal concept of appearing. Appearing "differs as such, in the pure phenomenological matter of which it is made" [FP, l. 705]. The sharpest use of the claim is not against Husserl or Heidegger but against Marion. Henry accepts the fourth principle and praises the reading that leads to it. But where Marion ends by subordinating the call of being to a "pure form of the call", Henry refuses: "the call is always determined", its determination is phenomenological, and the pair of call and response borrows its structure from the appearing in which opposition is constitutive, that of the world [FP, l. 839]. A pure givenness without a determinate how would have "only a verbal relation to phenomenology" [FP, l. 866]. That Henry applies the criterion to the author nearest to him shows that it is the working criterion of these essays.

(2) *Intentionality is founded and not rejected.* The non-intentional phenomenology is announced as critical, and also as the task "of founding intentionality itself" [NI, l. 908]. Seeing, left to itself, would be an unconscious vision and would see nothing; it can see only under the condition of its self-givenness in a revelation foreign to all seeing. Hence the formula: "There is no intentionality but only an intentional life" [NI, l. 993]. The claim reaches into the description of intentional acts themselves, since a seeing given to itself in pathos is always a seeing "in desire, in boredom, in passion" [FP, l. 758], and into aesthetics, where Henry describes vision as "a seeing that feels itself seeing" [KS, l. 1753].

(3) *The relation of evidence and reality is inverted.* The classical privilege of evidence is, for Henry, the reign of the visible. On his account what is seen is given with horizons, in adumbrations, always referring to what is not seen, whereas in life "everything is there in its entirety at each instant", with "neither adumbrations nor aspects, nor horizons of fulfilment" [FP, l. 750]. Reality lies where nothing can be looked at. This is why Henry can write, in a sentence that is not typical of his tone toward Husserl, that the principle of principles "is a murder" [FP, l. 720]: by requiring being to offer itself in intuition, it denies that a being other than an intuited one is possible.

(4) *There is no anonymous appearing.* Against the anonymity in which Husserl left the constituting ego, Henry holds that every self-revelation generates the ipseity in which it is undergone. There is no suffering "that could be the suffering of no one" [PL, l. 553], and all flesh "is the flesh of someone" [PL, l. 585]. The self is not what appearing is given to, as a pole or a dative; it belongs to the how of the original appearing.

From these points Henry derives consequences for fields that phenomenology had described on the other presupposition. Language is the clearest case. If the nature of language depends on the appearing it presupposes, then beside the language of the world, made of meanings foreign to the reality of what they mean, there must be another, for "the way life reveals is the way it speaks" [ML, l. 2816]. The cry of suffering is not the proposition "I am in pain"; the proposition is "a noematic irreality foreign to the reality of the suffering it signifies" [ML, l. 2857], while the cry belongs to suffering as one of its modalities. The body is treated in the same manner, as a flesh that is not seen before it is a body in the world, and so is birth, and so is the political, where Henry describes the city as "the world on a human scale" [VR, l. 1376], the space of what is visible to all.

One objection Henry raises himself. The Munich lecture moves from the finite life that each of us undergoes to an absolute Life that brings itself into life, and reads this with the Prologue of John. Has the analysis not left phenomenology for dogma, and yielded to the theological turn? Henry's reply is a question: "are we not, we too, living?" [PL, l. 543]. The step it supports is that I am given to myself, yet "it is not I who have given myself to myself" [PL, l. 545]. One may grant that the passivity of self-experience is described and not merely asserted. Whether the passage from this passivity to a first living Self is itself a description, the essays I read assert more than they show, and the question belongs to the fourth volume, which I have not read. It does not touch the four points above, none of which depends on it.

## V. Duality or foundation: two accounts of one relation

We have so far spoken, with Henry, of two modes of appearing. How do they stand to one another? One receives the impression that the essays give two answers, and that Henry holds both.

The first answer is a duality. The essay of 1991 speaks of "an absolute difference" between two heterogeneous modes of manifestation [FP, l. 705] and gives it a name that recurs to the end: the duplicity of appearing (*duplicité de l'apparaître*), where the French word means doubleness and carries no suggestion of deceit. The opposition of two calls "can only mean that of two phenomenalities and thus of two phenomenologies" [FP, l. 829]. On this account the world's appearing is a mode in its own right, with traits that can be listed and laws that can be studied. The duality explains ordinary facts. There is one body, which I see from outside and live from inside, and this "supposes a double appearing" [AP, l. 2497]. The cry of suffering is also heard as a sound in the world "solely because of the duality of appearing" [ML, l. 2859]. The political becomes intelligible through "a dichotomy of phenomenality itself" [VR, l. 1365].

The second answer is a foundation, and it takes back part of the first. The same essay of 1991 says that the appearing isolated by the pure reduction "does not subsist by itself". It is the chief illusion of contemporary phenomenology to believe "that transcendence is phenomenologically and ontologically an autonomous essence" [FP, l. 766]. Because this appearing has not accomplished its own self-givenness, "in fact it gives nothing, and itself least of all"; it "does not appear" [FP, l. 766]. It is "an abstract appearing" [FP, l. 768]. Correspondingly, "the world has being only in life" [FP, l. 756], and the content of the world is "only the objectivation of life" [NI, l. 987]. On this account there are not two modes side by side. There is one original appearing, and what the first answer called a second mode is a dependent moment that taken alone is nothing.

Henry does not leave the two answers unconnected. The essay on Descartes joins them in three theses: that there is a duplicity of appearing; that the second appearing founds the first; and that the second alone is "autonomous, absolute" [CD, l. 751]. The sentence of 1991 already says that the opposition "implies the duplicity of appearing at the same time as it indicates a hierarchy" [FP, l. 829]. A duality with an order of foundation is a coherent thought, and the analysis of seeing shows how it is meant: one and the same seeing is ecstatic as the seeing of something and pathos-laden as felt.

Two strains remain, and the diagnosis of them is mine. The first concerns the opposition "trait for trait". If the world's appearing does not appear unless it is self-affected in life, then exteriority, indifference and impotence are traits of an abstraction. The world as it in fact appears is, on Henry's own telling, a seeing in desire and boredom, a "living cosmos" [FP, l. 756]; it is not indifferent. The three traits then describe not the world's appearing but what Husserl and Heidegger took it to be, and the opposition holds between life and a theory. Henry's language does not decide between these readings.

The second strain concerns the foundation itself. Life is defined as the appearing that "makes nothing else appear", that does not turn away from itself and institutes no difference [NI, l. 975]. How, then, does it found the appearing of what is other? Henry's formulas state that it does: ecstatic affection "auto-affects itself in Life" [FP, l. 756]; seeing bears a non-seeing in it. They state a condition, and a strong one, namely that no outside appears unless the appearing of it is felt. They do not say how an appearing without distance gives rise to distance, or why a life that reveals only itself has a world at all. The essay of 1992 treats the matter in its last pages and says that it will establish it "rapidly" [NI, l. 985]. The essay on birth speaks of "the secret intertwining of the modes of appearing" [PB, l. 1093]. By the measure Henry applies to the four principles and to Marion, a relation whose how is called secret is still formally stated. I do not claim that Henry lacks the means to answer; the books left aside here may supply them. I claim that in these essays the foundation of the world's appearing in life is asserted with the same indeterminacy that the essays criticise in others.

The history of Henry's vocabulary bears on this. In the lecture of 1977 the word phenomenality occurs eight times, and in most of them it names the world's side: exteriority is "the pure phenomenality of what phenomenalises itself", and the invisible of life is set against "the declining modes of the phenomenality of the world" [WL, l. 361, l. 397]. Life is defended there as not being a privation of phenomenality, which is the defence one makes of something placed outside it. By 1983 the word names a genus with "two primitive modes" in which it "materialises" [CI, l. 446]. In 1991 the genus has the name duplicity. In 1996 and 2000 Henry announces a restriction: life is "the original mode of pure phenomenality, for which we shall henceforth reserve, for clarity of exposition, the name of revelation" [PL, l. 526; similarly ML, l. 2800], and "appearing of the world" becomes the fixed name of the other. The change of terms is an indication that the generic concept was becoming harder to hold: the late Henry no longer says that one appearing has two modes without also giving them two names. This reading of the sequence is an inference from the essays in which I counted the word, and the books might qualify it.

Henry's investigation of phenomenality, then, is not the vehicle of a philosophy of life. It is the reverse: life is the determination that these essays give to a concept which phenomenology had, on Henry's showing, placed at its foundation and left formal. What is new in it is the demand that the How of appearing be given a matter, and the description of that matter as an affectivity in which appearing and what appears are one. The demand is taken from Husserl. Its first half, that appearing must appear without a second appearing behind it, is stated in § 39 of the lectures on time in a word that Henry quotes in 1989 and that his own term renders, and Henry's reading of that paragraph is accurate where it reports Husserl's embarrassment. The second half is Henry's, and it holds against Heidegger to the extent that the world's unveiling does not posit what it unveils; it has not been tested, in these volumes, against Heidegger's own account of attunement. What stays open is the relation that the requirement itself made necessary. An appearing that reveals only itself has been described. That it founds the appearing of everything else has been said, and by the standard of these essays the saying is not yet a phenomenology of how. The theological vocabulary of the late texts does not decide that question, and it would be decided, for or against Henry, only by the kind of description he asks of others.

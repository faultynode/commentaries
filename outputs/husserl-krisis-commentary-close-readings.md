# Husserl, *Die Krisis der europäischen Wissenschaften und die transzendentale Phänomenologie*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserl-krisis-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md) works through the main text of the *Krisis* paragraph by paragraph. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries instead: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the book and, where the corpus allows, in his other works.

The text is the German of Husserliana VI, edited by Walter Biemel, from the converted file in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-6-die-krisis-der-europaischen-wissenschaften-und-die-transzendentale-phanomenologie.md`). The main commentary cites only by §. These readings add the printed page, "Hua VI n", taken from the nearest running head in the file. The file joins print lines into long paragraph lines, so a head can fall a little after the start of the page it marks, and a page number can be off by one. Where two heads stand together with no text between them, the citation gives both pages. Unit references such as "§2.3" name the third paragraph of the main commentary under § 2. All English renderings of Husserl and of other authors are my working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows. No published English translation of the *Krisis* is in the corpus, so the readings compare none.

The file is on the list of sources that are unsafe to quote without checking, and four kinds of damage bear on more than one reading. First, pages are lost at seams spliced in mid-sentence: Hua VI 15 (the opening of § 7), 46–47 (§ 9g), 115 (§ 29), 143 (§ 36) and about 269–274 (the end of § 72 and the start of the closing ⟨§ 73⟩). The readings never quote across a seam, and say so where a passage stands next to one. Second, sixteen § headings are missing from the body, and many others are plain text. Section titles are therefore given from the table of contents where the body lacks them. Third, the file renders Husserl's spaced type in three ways: as bold with the word spaces collapsed, as letters separated by spaces, or merged into italic. Emphasis can be argued from, but whether a word was italic or spaced in print cannot. Fourth, there are OCR errors. An obvious misspelling is noted where it falls in a quotation and is not repaired in the German. In quotations, line-end hyphens are joined, the print's margin line numbers and footnote markers are dropped, and word spaces that the file collapses inside emphasis are restored. Everything else is quoted as the file has it.

## §2, Hua VI 4–5: "mere" sciences of fact

Reads [§2.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-positivistic-reduction-of-the-idea-of-science-to-mere-factual-science-the-crisis-of-science-as-the-loss-of-its-significance-for-life), the third paragraph under § 2.

The crisis of § 2 is carried by one adjective. The positive sciences are not false and not unscientific; they are "mere" (*bloß*). "Mere" names a part that stands where a whole should stand: sciences that determine only what the world "factually is", with the questions of reason and sense cut away. The famous sentence says that this restriction does not stay in the sciences. It passes to the human beings whom the sciences form. And "fact" in this passage is not opposed to essence, as it was in Husserl's earlier vocabulary, but to reason.

> We take our start from a reversal in the general valuation of the sciences that set in at the turn of the last century. It concerns not their scientificity, but what they, what science as such, had meant and can mean for human existence. The exclusiveness with which, in the second half of the 19th century, the whole world-view of modern human beings let itself be determined by the positive sciences and be blinded by the "prosperity" owed to them meant an indifferent turning-away from the questions that are the decisive ones for a genuine humanity. Mere fact-sciences make mere fact-people.

German:

> Unseren Ausgang nehmen wir von einer an der Wende des letzten Jahrhunderts hinsichtlich der Wissenschaften eingetretenen Umwendung der allgemeinen Bewertung. Sie betrifft nicht ihre Wissenschaftlichkeit, sondern das, was sie, was Wissenschaft überhaupt dem menschlichen Dasein bedeutet hatte und bedeuten kann. Die Ausschließlichkeit, in welcher sich in der zweiten Hälfte des 19. Jahrhunderts die ganze Weltanschauung der modernen Menschen von den positiven Wissenschaften bestimmen und von der ihr verdankten „prosperity“ blenden ließ, bedeutete ein gleichgültiges Sichabkehren von den Fragen, die für ein echtes Menschentum die entscheidenden sind. Bloße Tatsachenwissenschaften machen bloße Tatsachenmenschen.

The sentence is built as a chiasmus without the crossing: two compounds with the same determinant, *Tatsachen-* [fact-], and the same adjective, with only the head noun changed from *-wissenschaften* [sciences] to *-menschen* [people]. Everything rests on the verb "make" (*machen*). It states a causal claim: the sciences form the people. The sentence before it says something else. There, the agent is the world-view of modern human beings, which "let itself be determined" and "be blinded" (*sich … bestimmen und … blenden ließ*). The reflexive *sich lassen* puts the fault on those who let it happen, and "exclusiveness" (*Ausschließlichkeit*) names their attitude, not a property of the sciences. The slogan then turns this attitude into the sciences' effect. Here the wording claims more than the paragraph shows. What the paragraph shows is that a culture which took the positive sciences as its whole world-view turned away from the decisive questions. That the sciences themselves "make" such people is the compressed form of a thesis the book has still to argue. The single English word in quotation marks, "prosperity", stands outside the German as a foreign word for a foreign value. Husserl will not let the benefit of the sciences speak German.

What "fact" means here is settled by the sentences that follow. The human sciences, "one says" (*sagt man*), demand that the researcher exclude "all questions about reason and unreason"; "scientific, objective truth is exclusively the determination of what the world … factually is" (*tatsächlich ist*). The questions excluded are "about sense or senselessness" (*nach Sinn oder Sinnlosigkeit*) of human existence. The contrary of fact is therefore reason and sense. The positive sciences that § 1 admired include "pure mathematics" alongside exact physics (Hua VI 1–3), and the heading of § 2 calls the whole group a reduction "to mere fact-science" (*auf bloße Tatsachenwissenschaft*). In 1913 Husserl had used the same compound for one side of a classification. *Ideen I* defines pure phenomenology "not as a fact-science, but as an essence-science (as an 'eidetic' science)" (*nicht als Tatsachenwissenschaft, sondern als Wesenswissenschaft*, Hua III/1 6), and there pure mathematics is the model of an eidetic science. Read with that vocabulary, "mathematics is a mere fact-science" would be a contradiction. The *Krisis* is not using that vocabulary. "Fact" now names whatever is determined without regard to reason, and a theorem of pure mathematics falls under it as much as a measurement. The old pair fact–essence has given way to the pair fact–reason.

The word that introduces this is "reduction". The heading of § 2 speaks of "the positivistic reduction of the idea of science" (*Die positivistische Reduktion der Idee der Wissenschaft*). Part III reserves the word for Husserl's own method. Here it names the opposite operation: what is left when something is taken away. "Mere" is the adjective of that kind of reduction, and § 3 gives it a noun, the "residual concept" (*Restbegriff*).

The paragraph reports more than it asserts. The young generation's verdict comes as hearsay, "so we hear" (*so hören wir*): "in our vital need this science has nothing to say to us". The human sciences' value-freedom comes with "one says". Husserl stages the complaint and asks his questions in its voice. What he endorses is the narrower point in the paragraph's first two sentences: the reversal concerns "not their scientificity" but what science has meant for "human existence" (*dem menschlichen Dasein*). This keeps the concession of § 1 intact. The heading's word for what is lost, the sciences' "significance for life" (*Lebensbedeutsamkeit*), occurs nowhere else in the file. The text says it without the noun.

The rest of Part I shows that "mere" was never a new defect of the fact-sciences. § 3 sets "the world as universe of mere facts" (*Universum der bloßen Tatsachen*) beneath the questions of reason, which "claim a higher dignity" than "the questions of fact, which lie beneath them in the order of questions" (Hua VI 7). Under the Renaissance ideal, too, the fact-sciences were "merely fact-scientific" undertakings "of the lower level" (*die bloß tatsachenwissenschaftlichen der Unterstufe*, Hua VI 8). § 5 says that they had their "sense as truths for mere regions of what is" (*ihren Sinn als Wahrheiten für bloße Gebiete des Seienden*), and had it only "in the inseparable unity of philosophy" (Hua VI 10). So the sciences were always "mere", as the lower storey of a building is mere without the rest of the building. What is new in the crisis is that the lower storey stands alone and is taken for the building, and that the people, too, have become mere. The slogan of § 2 is a claim about that change of place. The sciences are unchanged. Their meaning for life is lost because the whole to which they belonged has been dropped. That is the sense in which § 2 can grant their scientificity and still speak of their crisis.

## §3, Hua VI 7: the residual concept, and the beheading

Reads [§3.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-grounding-of-the-autonomy-of-european-humanity-through-the-new-conception-of-the-idea-of-philosophy-in-the-renaissance), the fourth paragraph under § 3.

Positivism, for Husserl, has no principle of its own. It is defined by subtraction, as what is left of the idea of philosophy once the questions of reason are dropped. The passage says what those questions have in common, and the answer is reason. It then gives the subtraction a shape: the questions dropped were the highest, so their loss is a beheading. Remainder and beheading are two images of one claim. The unity of the sciences was hierarchical, and reason was at its top.

> The positivistic concept of science in our time is therefore — historically considered — a *residual concept*. It has let fall all the questions that had been included in the now narrower, now wider concepts of metaphysics, among them all the unclearly so-called "highest and ultimate questions". Looked at exactly, these, and all the excluded questions altogether, have their inseparable unity in this: that, whether expressly or implied in their sense, they contain the problems of reason — of reason in all its particular shapes. […] All these "metaphysical" questions, taken widely, the specifically philosophical ones in the usual way of speaking, go beyond the world as universe of mere facts. They go beyond it precisely as questions that have the idea reason in view. And they all claim a higher dignity than the questions of fact, which lie beneath them in the order of questions too. Positivism beheads philosophy, so to speak.

German:

> Der positivistische Begriff der Wissenschaft in unserer Zeit ist also — historisch betrachtet — ein *Restbegriff*. Er hat alle die Fragen fallen gelassen, die man in die bald engeren, bald weiteren Begriffe von Metaphysik einbezogen hatte, darunter alle die unklar so genannten „höchsten und letzten Fragen“. Genau besehen haben sie, und alle zum Ausschluß gekommenen überhaupt, ihre unabtrennbare Einheit darin, daß sie, sei es ausdrücklich, sei es in ihrem Sinn impliziert, die Probleme der Vernunft — der Vernunft in allen ihren Sondergestalten — enthalten. […] Alle diese ,,metaphysischen" Fragen, weit gefaßt, die spezifisch philosophischen in der üblichen Rede, übersteigen die Welt als Universum der bloßen Tatsachen. Sie übersteigen sie eben als Fragen, welche die Idee Vernunft im Sinne haben. Und sie alle beanspruchen eine höhere Dignität gegenüber den Tatsachenfragen, die auch in der Frageordnung unter ihnen liegen. Der Positivismus enthauptet sozusagen die Philosophie.

The omitted middle lists the disciplines in which reason is the explicit theme (knowledge, valuation, ethical action) and the metaphysical questions (the human being as rational being, reason in history, God, immortality, freedom).

"Residual concept" carries a qualification that is easy to read past: "historically considered" (*historisch betrachtet*), set off by dashes. The claim is not that positivism's concept of science is incoherent, or that it is defined negatively by its own lights. It is that, measured against the history the paragraph before has told, the concept is what is left over. The "therefore" (*also*) draws on that history: the Renaissance ideal of one "all-embracing science" (*allbefassenden Wissenschaft*, italic) whose branches were the sciences in the plural, and which was to answer "problems of fact and problems of reason" (*Tatsachenprobleme und Vernunftprobleme*) in one system (Hua VI 6–7). Take the second half of that pair away and the remainder is positivism. The verb is passive in the right way: positivism "has let fall" (*hat … fallen gelassen*) the questions. It dropped them, and did not refute them.

The paragraph's central claim is that the dropped questions have an "inseparable unity", and that the unity is reason. Husserl does not put all of the old vocabulary in his own voice. The predicates of reason come in quotation marks: reason is "a title for 'absolute', 'eternal', 'supratemporal', 'unconditionally' valid ideas and ideals" (*ein Titel für ,,absolute", ,,ewige", ,,überzeitliche", ,,unbedingt" gültige Ideen*). So do "metaphysical", "sense" and "absolute" reason in the sentences on history and God. The quotation marks report the tradition's words without adopting its doctrine. Husserl commits himself to the formal point that these are all questions of reason, and to the hierarchy. He does not commit himself to the eternity of ideas. The "highest and ultimate questions" are likewise "unclearly so called" (*unklar so genannten*). What survives the distancing is a structure: the questions of reason "go beyond" (*übersteigen*) the world of facts, and the questions of fact "lie beneath them". The adjective "mere" from § 2 returns in the "universe of mere facts".

The beheading follows from that vertical order. A head is what is highest and what gives the body its direction, and the next sentences supply both. Metaphysics was "the queen of the sciences" (*die Würde der Königin der Wissenschaften*), "whose spirit first allotted the ultimate sense to all cognitions" (*erst den letzten Sinn zumaß*). Cut off the head, and the body goes on working; it has lost only its sense. This is the image the first two §§ needed. It explains how the positive sciences can flourish while being in crisis: a beheaded philosophy leaves its lower members intact, and it is the sense they had from the head that is gone. The "so to speak" (*sozusagen*) marks the image as an image, but nothing in the paragraph argues the hierarchy it presupposes. The questions of reason "claim" (*beanspruchen*) a higher dignity. Whether the claim is good is the question the book has to answer.

§ 5 shows that the remainder cannot stay as it is. The problem of a possible metaphysics "eo ipso" included "the possibility of the fact-sciences" as well, since they had their "sense of relation" (*Beziehungssinn*) in the unity of philosophy (Hua VI 10). Husserl then asks, with the whole question emphasized in the file, "Are reason and what is to be separated, where cognizing reason determines what is?" (*Ist Vernunft und Seiendes zu trennen, wo erkennende Vernunft bestimmt, was Seiendes ist?*). That is the reason why beheading is fatal and not merely a loss. Positivism supposes it can keep the facts and drop reason. If reason determines what a fact is, the remainder does not survive the subtraction. The rest of Part I turns this into history (the "inner dissolution" of the ideal), and Part II traces it back to Galileo.

The book names positivism's father later. In § 23 Hume, by settling into "the comfortable and very impressive role of academic skepticism", became "the father of a still effective feeble positivism" (*zum Vater eines noch immer wirksamen schwächlichen Positivismus*, Hua VI c. 92). The genealogy thus matches the definition. Positivism descends from a skeptic who would not "take up the fight with the countersense", that is, from a refusal of the questions of reason, not from an answer to them. A residual concept has a history but no argument, and that is the charge of the passage.

*Note on the text.* The sentence on Hume in § 23 runs into a seam a few words later, a seam the file's records do not list. The relative clause "which … the philosophical abysses" (*der den philosophischen Abgründen ausle*…) breaks off mid-word and resumes inside another sentence ("…ley sche und Hume sche 'Idealismus'", that is, the Berkeleyan and Humean "idealism"). The file has running heads 90, 92 and 93 but none for 91, so text is lost around Hua VI 91–93, and how much cannot be told from the file. Only the words before the seam are quoted.

## §5, Hua VI 10: primal institution

Reads [§5.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-ideal-of-universal-philosophy-and-the-process-of-its-inner-dissolution), the first paragraph under § 5.

A primal institution (*Urstiftung*) is a beginning that sets a task. What is instituted is not a doctrine or a set of results but an ideal, here the ideal of a universal philosophy with its method, and the ideal outlives the people who first held it because later generations carry its sense in themselves. The passage claims two things for the modern primal institution. First, the ideal dissolves from within, and its dissolution, not its success, drives the history that follows. Second, the institution of the new philosophy is the institution of modern European humanity itself. The second claim is what lets a crisis of a "cultural form" become a crisis of human existence.

> A *determinate ideal* of a *universal* philosophy and of a method belonging to it makes the beginning, as the *primal institution*, so to speak, of the philosophical modern age and of all its lines of development. But instead of this ideal being able to take effect in fact, it undergoes an inner dissolution. This motivates, against the attempts to carry it on and to renew and consolidate it, revolutionary new formations, some more and some less radical. So it is now really *the problem of the genuine ideal* of a universal philosophy and its genuine method that becomes the innermost driving force of all historical philosophical movements. But that means that in the end all the modern sciences, in the sense in which they were grounded as branches of philosophy and which they afterwards carried in themselves permanently, fell into a peculiar crisis, felt more and more as a riddle. It is a crisis that does not attack the specialist-scientific in its theoretical and practical successes, and yet shakes their whole sense of truth through and through. This is not a matter of a special cultural form, "science" or "philosophy", as one among others in European humanity. For the primal institution of the new philosophy is, according to what was set out earlier, the primal institution of modern European humanity, and indeed as a humanity that, against the previous one, the medieval and ancient, wants to renew itself radically through its new philosophy, and only through it. Accordingly the crisis of philosophy means the crisis of all modern sciences as members of the philosophical universality, a crisis of European humanity itself, latent at first but then coming more and more to light, in the whole meaningfulness of its cultural life, in its whole "existence".

German:

> Ein *bestimmtes Ideal* einer *universalen* Philosophie und einer dazugehörigen Methode macht den Anfang, sozusagen als *Urstiftung* der philosophischen Neuzeit und aller ihrer Entwicklungsreihen. Aber anstatt daß sich dieses Ideal in der Tat auswirken konnte, erfährt es eine innere Auflösung. Diese motiviert gegenüber den Versuchen seiner Fortführung und erneuern den Festigung revolutionäre Neugestaltungen, und dabei mehr oder minder radikale. So wird nun eigentlich *das Problem des echten Ideals* einer universalen Philosophie und ihrer echten Methode zur innersten Triebkraft aller historischen philosophischen Bewegungen. Das sagt aber, daß schließlich alle neuzeitlichen Wissenschaften nach dem Sinn, in dem sie als Zweige der Philosophie begründet wurden und den sie danach dauernd in sich trugen, in eine eigenartige, immer mehr als rätselhaft empfundene Krisis hineingerieten. Es ist eine Krisis, welche das Fachwissenschaftliche in seinen theoretischen und praktischen Erfolgen nicht angreift und doch ihren ganzen Wahrheitssinn durch und durch erschüttert. Es handelt sich hierbei nicht um Angelegenheiten einer speziellen Kulturform, ,,Wissenschaft" bzw. ,,Philosophie", als einer unter anderen in der europäischen Menschheit. Denn die Urstiftung der neuen Philosophie ist nach dem früher Ausgeführten die Urstiftung der neuzeitlichen europäischen Menschentums, und zwar als eines Menschentums, das gegenüber dem bisherigen, dem mittelalterlichen und antiken, sich radikal erneuern will durch seine neue Philosophie, und nur durch sie. Demnach bedeutet die Krisis der Philosophie die Krisis aller neuzeitlichen Wissenschaften als Glieder der philosophischen Universalität, eine zunächst latente, dann aber immer mehr zutage tretende Krisis des europäischen Menschentums selbst in der gesamten Sinnhaftigkeit seines kulturellen Lebens, in seiner gesamten ,,Existenz".

*Note on the text.* The file has *erneuern den Festigung*, where the grammar requires *erneuernden Festigung* [renewing consolidation], and *der neuzeitlichen europäischen Menschentums*, where it requires *des*. The English follows the evident sense, and nothing below rests on either word. The file's emphasis markup runs across word boundaries here ("*bestimmtes Idealei*-ner"); the German above gives the emphasized words as the markup's span suggests.

The grammatical subject of the first sentence is an ideal. An ideal "makes the beginning" (*macht den Anfang*), "undergoes" its dissolution (*erfährt*), and "motivates" new formations. No person acts in the sentence. This is the force of "primal institution" as against "origin" or "discovery". A *Stiftung* is a founding in the sense in which one founds an order or an endowment: an act that sets up something meant to last beyond the founder and to bind those who come after. The prefix *Ur-* [primal] makes the founding the first of a series. The passage names the series at once, "all its lines of development" (*Entwicklungsreihen*). Husserl hedges the term on its first appearance, "so to speak" (*sozusagen*), as he hedged "beheads" in § 3. The hedge falls away at once: four sentences later the noun is used twice without it, and the hedge does not return.

The history that a primal institution opens is not the history of its success. The sentence that names the shape of the process refuses three models in a row: not "a smooth development", not "a continuous growth of lasting acquisitions", not a "transformation… to be explained from the accidental historical situations" (Hua VI 10). What it puts in their place is an ideal that dissolves "from within" (*innere Auflösung*). The ideal that drives history is then no longer the ideal itself but "the problem of the genuine ideal" (*das Problem des echten Ideals*, emphasized). The doubled "genuine" (*echten Ideals … echten Methode*) is the key. The primal institution set up an ideal whose genuine form it did not yet know, and every later movement is an attempt to find out what was instituted. That is why the crisis "does not attack" the specialist successes and yet "shakes their whole sense of truth". The successes belong to the sciences. Their sense of truth belongs to the institution, and the institution has become a question.

The step from philosophy to humanity rests on an identity sentence: "the primal institution of the new philosophy *is* … the primal institution of modern European humanity". Its warrant is "according to what was set out earlier", that is, § 3's account of the Renaissance, in which a humanity "wants to shape itself anew in freedom" and takes the "'philosophical' form of existence" of the ancients as its model (Hua VI 5). The restriction that follows is the strongest word in the passage: this humanity wants to renew itself through its new philosophy, "and only through it" (*und nur durch sie*). Only that restriction makes the identity hold. If modern humanity had other sources of renewal, a crisis of philosophy would be a crisis of one cultural form "among others", which is exactly what the preceding sentence denies. The inference is valid, but its premise is a thesis about what modern humanity "wants" (*will*). The quotation marks on "existence" (*,,Existenz"*) at the end mark the word as borrowed from the vocabulary of the day. Husserl lets the existential register of the crisis speak only in quotation marks.

The passages that follow qualify the "primal". Two pages later, in § 5's account of reason, philosophy rises above everyday truth "in the first original primal institution, ancient philosophy" (*in der ersten originalen Urstiftung die antike Philosophie*, Hua VI 11). So there are two primal institutions, and the modern one is the second. § 15 says how they relate: we are what we are "out of a primal institution which is at the same time a re-institution and modification of the Greek primal institution" (*aus einer Urstiftung, die aber zugleich Nachstiftung und Abwandlung der griechischen Urstiftung ist*, Hua VI 72). The prefix *Ur-* thus does not mark an absolute first. It marks the beginning of a series of sense, and a beginning can itself repeat an earlier one. § 15 then adds the counterpart that completes the concept, the final institution (*Endstiftung*), which belongs to every primal institution as its task (row 11).

The rest of the book turns the term into a method and a verdict. In § 9 the scientist can keep his science meaningful only if he can "inquire back" (*zurückzufragen*, italic) to "the *historical sense of primal institution*" (*historischen Urstiftungssinn*) of his formations of sense (Hua VI 57). The primal institution is now what historical reflection asks about. § 16 names its bearers. Galileo "had accomplished the primal institution of the new natural science", and Descartes is the "*primally instituting* genius" (*urstiftenden Genius*) of all modern philosophy (Hua VI 75). The heading of § 16 calls Descartes the "primal founder" (*Urstifter*) of both "objectivistic rationalism" and "the transcendental motive that explodes it". One founder institutes both the ideal and the motive that will dissolve it, and that is § 5's "inner dissolution" given a name and a date. The last return closes the arc. At the start of Part III B, Husserl says Part III A tried to show that only a philosophy that inquires back to "the transcendental ego" can fulfil "the sense that is innate to philosophy from its primal institution" (*den Sinn …, der der Philosophie von ihrer Urstiftung her eingeboren ist*, Hua VI 195). The ideal instituted in the Renaissance, and before it in Greece, is redeemed, on Husserl's account, by transcendental phenomenology. The word "innate" ties this to the "telos inborn in European humanity" of § 6 (row 5). The primal institution of § 5 is a task whose genuine sense is fixed at the beginning and recognized only at the end. That is how the *Krisis* can be a history and a proof of phenomenology at once.

## §5, Hua VI 10–11: making oneself true

Reads [§5.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-ideal-of-universal-philosophy-and-the-process-of-its-inner-dissolution), the third paragraph under § 5.

Here truth passes from things and propositions to the person. The paragraph begins with truth in the ancient sense, the norm to which everything that seems to be is referred, and ends with a human being whose own "true being" is not given but has to be won, "in the form of the struggle for his truth, for making himself true". The understanding the passage carries is that a human being's true being is a task of the same kind as the true being of the world, and that losing faith in reason is therefore losing faith in oneself.

> The skepticism regarding the possibility of a metaphysics, the collapse of the faith in a universal philosophy as the guide of the new human being, means precisely the collapse of the faith in "reason", understood as the ancients set *episteme* against *doxa*. It is reason that ultimately gives sense to everything supposed to be, to all things, values, ends, namely their normative relatedness to what, since the beginnings of philosophy, the word truth — truth in itself — and correlatively the word being — *ontōs on* — designates. With this there falls, too, the faith in an "*absolute*" reason from which the world has its sense, the faith in the sense of history, in the sense of humanity, in its freedom, namely as the human being's capacity to procure rationally for his individual and general human existence [—]. If the human being loses this faith, that means nothing other than: he loses faith "in himself", in the true being that is his own, which he does not always already have, not already with the evidence of the "I am", but has and can have only in the form of the struggle for his truth, for making himself true.

German:

> Die Skepsis hinsichtlich der Möglichkeit einer Metaphysik, der Zusammenbruch des Glaubens an eine universale Philosophie als Führerin des neuen Menschen, besagt eben den Zusammenbruch des Glaubens an die ,,Vernunft", so verstanden, wie die Alten die Episteme der Doxa gegenüber setzten. Sie ist es, die allem vermeintlich Seienden, allen Dingen, Werten, Zwecken letztlich Sinn gibt, nämlich ihre normative Bezogenheit auf das, was seit den Anfängen der Philosophie das Wort Wahrheit — Wahrheit an sich — und korrelativ das Wort Seiendes — *ὄντως ὄν* — bezeichnet. Damit fällt auch der Glaube an eine „*absolute*“ Vernunft, aus der die Welt ihren Sinn hat, der Glaube an den Sinn der Geschichte, den Sinn des Menschentums, an seine Freiheit, nämlich als Ermöglichkeit des Menschen, seinem individuellen und allgemeinen menschlichen Dasein vernünftig zu verschaffen. Verliert der Mensch diesen Glauben, so heißt das nichts anderes als: er verliert den Glauben „an sich selbst“, an das ihm eigene wahre Sein, das er nicht immer schon hat, nicht schon mit der Evidenz des „Ich bin“, sondern nur hat und haben kann in Form des Ringens um seine Wahrheit, darum, sich selbst wahr zu machen.

*Note on the text.* The clause on freedom lacks the object of "procure" (*verschaffen*): the file reads *seinem individuellen und allgemeinen menschlichen Dasein vernünftig zu verschaffen*, with nothing for the human being to procure. Words are lost there, and the English marks the gap. The main commentary supplies "to give rational meaning". That is a plausible guess, but the file does not settle it, and nothing below depends on the missing word. *Ermöglichkeit* [capacity, enabling possibility] is unusual but is quoted as the file has it.

The paragraph is a chain of identities, and its particles carry them. Skepticism about metaphysics "means precisely" (*besagt eben*) the collapse of faith in reason. With that, "there falls, too" (*Damit fällt auch*) the faith in absolute reason, history, humanity and freedom. And losing that faith "means nothing other than" (*so heißt das nichts anderes als*) losing faith in oneself. Each link is stated as the unfolding of a meaning, not as a consequence. The noun that holds the chain together is faith (*Glaube*), five times in four sentences: in a universal philosophy, in reason, in absolute reason, in the sense of history, in oneself. Husserl does not say that the moderns knew reason and then lost the knowledge. What collapsed was a faith, and a few lines later he names it: the philosophy that lives straightforwardly in its task is "philosophy in the naive faith in reason" (*im naiven Glauben an die Vernunft*, Hua VI 11). The whole book is meant to turn that faith into insight. The passage states the stakes of failing.

Truth enters as a word with a history. Husserl does not define it. He points to "what, since the beginnings of philosophy, the word truth … and correlatively the word being … designates", and sets each word's classical sense between dashes: "truth in itself" (*Wahrheit an sich*) and the Greek "what really is" (*ὄντως ὄν*). Truth and being are "correlative" from the start. The final sentence then applies the word to the human being: "the true being that is his own" (*das ihm eigene wahre Sein*), and "making himself true" (*sich selbst wahr zu machen*). The idiom is ordinary German, to make a promise or a prophecy true, to make it come true. Here its object is the self. The human being is true in the way a promise is true, by being made so. The phrase "in himself" (*an sich selbst*) is in quotation marks, as if it were someone else's. It is the everyday idiom for believing in oneself, and it sits four lines below "truth in itself" (*Wahrheit an sich*). Whether Husserl intends the echo the passage does not say. A supplementary essay in *Erste Philosophie* II uses the same pair deliberately: self-knowledge is a "re-creation of the self" (*Selbstumschöpfung*) that "transfers the self out of the in-itself into the in-and-for-itself" (*aus dem An-sich in das An-und-für-sich überführt*) and "re-creates it into its true self". "In this our self-creation", the essay goes on, "we also create the true world" (Hua VIII 283). The essay adds that building up the sense of "'true' self and 'true' world (genuine humanity etc.)" would need a treatment of its own. The *Krisis* passage is that sense in brief.

The negations say what true being is not. It is not something one "always already" has (*nicht immer schon hat*), and it is "not already" given "with the evidence of the 'I am'". The second clause turns against Descartes before Descartes appears. The certainty that I exist is not the possession of my true being. The sentence that follows generalizes: "Everywhere true being is an ideal goal, a task of episteme, of 'reason', set against the being that in doxa is unquestioningly 'self-evident', merely supposed" (*Überall ist wahres Sein ein ideales Ziel, eine Aufgabe der Episteme*, Hua VI 11). The human being's true being has the same form as the true being of things: a goal set against what is taken for granted. The weakness of the inference lies here. The paragraph moves from "reason gives things their sense by their relation to truth" to "the human being's true being is a task of the same kind". The bridge is the word "true" and the ideal of a self-shaping by reason that § 3 drew from the Renaissance. It is not an argument that persons and things have their truth in the same way. The quotation marks on "self-evident" (*„selbstverständlichen“*) mark the word that the book will make its target. Part III will call the life-world's taken-for-grantedness its "self-evidences" (*Selbstverständlichkeiten*) and set out to make them understood.

In its place in § 5, the passage gives the content of the crisis that the preceding paragraph only named. A crisis of European humanity "in its whole 'existence'" (§ 5.2) now has a definition: the loss of faith in a reason that would give sense to things, to history and to oneself. What follows turns the definition into a history. Philosophy rises above the everyday sense of truth "in the first original primal institution, ancient philosophy", and the history of philosophy then "takes on the character of a struggle for existence" (*eines Kampfes ums Dasein*) between the philosophy of naive faith and the skepticism that denies or devalues it (Hua VI 11). The phrase is Darwin's in its German form, and Husserl turns it on the history of ideas. The struggle for one's own truth becomes the struggle of philosophy for its survival. The skeptic's weapon is "the actually experienced world, that of real experience, in which nothing of reason and its ideas is to be found" (Hua VI 11). That is the first appearance of the experienced world as an argument against reason. Part III will turn the same world into reason's ground.

The understanding returns at the end of the book, in the last lines that survive in the file. The closing word (⟨§ 73⟩) speaks of "the ultimate self-understanding of the human being as responsible for his own human being", of a humanity "understanding itself as rational, understanding that it is rational in willing to be rational" (*verstehend, daß sie vernünftig ist im Vernünftigseinwollen*), and that this means "an infinity of life and striving toward reason" (Hua VI 276). The struggle of § 5 has become a definition. To be rational is to will to be rational, as to be true is to make oneself true. The Cartesian "I am" also returns, now with a different sense of evidence. The closing word sets the "apodictic" being-for-itself of the philosophizing ego, the "bearer of absolute reason coming to itself", against what is usually called apodictic knowledge. The "absolute" reason that § 5 kept in quotation marks and emphasis is now said without either. The task § 5 set at the level of faith is claimed at the end as phenomenology's result.

*Note on the text.* The closing word survives only in part (see the foreword), and the file's last line breaks off mid-sentence ("worauf der Mensch als Mensch in seinem Innersten" [toward which man as man in his innermost —]). The quotations above are from that surviving page.

## §6, Hua VI 13–14: the entelechy of humanity

Reads [§6.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-history-of-modern-philosophy-as-the-struggle-over-the-meaning-of-the-human-being), the second paragraph under § 6.

An entelechy (*Entelechie*), in the Aristotelian word Husserl takes up, is an end that a being has within itself and that governs its becoming. Husserl claims that reason is such an end for humanity as such: that what "broke through" in Greece is an entelechy that belongs to human beings essentially, so that philosophy is the history in which humanity's own reason becomes manifest to itself. He also adds what Aristotle's word lacks. This entelechy can come "to itself" and then "consciously guide" human becoming. The passage puts the claim as a question to be decided. The reading turns on how open the question really is.

> To bring latent reason to the self-understanding of its possibilities, and thereby to make insightful the possibility of a metaphysics as a true possibility — that is the only way to bring a metaphysics, or universal philosophy, into the laborious course of realization. With this alone is it decided whether the *telos* inborn in European humanity with the birth of Greek philosophy, to want to be a humanity out of philosophical reason and to be able to be only as such — in the infinite movement from latent to manifest reason and in the infinite striving of self-norming through this its human truth and genuineness — is a mere historical-factual delusion, a chance acquisition of a chance humanity amid quite other humanities and historicities; or whether, rather, what first came to break through in Greek humanity is what is essentially enclosed in humanity as such as *entelechy*. Humanity in general is essentially being human in generatively and socially connected humanities, and if the human being is a rational being (*animal rationale*), he is so only insofar as his whole humanity is a humanity of reason — latently directed toward reason, or openly directed toward the entelechy that has come to itself, become manifest for itself, and now in essential necessity *consciously guides* human becoming. Philosophy, science, would accordingly be the historical *movement of the revelation of the universal reason "inborn" in humanity as such*.

German:

> Die latente Vernunft zum Selbstverständnis ihrer Möglichkeiten und damit einsichtig zu machen die Möglichkeit einer Metaphysik als einer wahren Möglichkeit — das ist der einzige Weg, um eine Metaphysik bzw. universale Philosophie in den arbeitsvollen Gang der Verwirklichung zu bringen. Damit allein entscheidet sich, ob das dem europäischen Menschentum mit der Geburt der griechischen Philosophie eingeborene Telos, ein Menschentum aus philosophischer Vernunft sein zu wollen und nur als solches sein zu können — in der unendlichen Bewegung von latenter zu offenbarer Vernunft und im unendlichen Bestreben der Selbstnormierung durch diese seine menschliche Wahrheit und Echtheit, ein bloßer historisch-faktischer Wahn ist, ein zufälliger Erwerb einer zufälligen Menschheit, inmitten ganz anderer Menschheiten und Geschichtlichkeiten; oder ob nicht vielmehr im griechischen Menschentum erstmalig zum Durchbruch gekommen ist, was als *Entelechie* im Menschentum als solchen wesensmäßig beschlossen ist. Menschentum überhaupt ist wesensmäßig Menschsein in generativ und sozial verbundenen Menschheiten, und ist der Mensch Vernunftwesen (*animal rationale*), so ist er es nur, sofern seine ganze Menschheit Vernunftmenschheit ist — latent auf Vernunft ausgerichtet oder offen ausgerichtet auf die zu sich selbst gekommene, für sich selbst offenbar gewordene und nunmehr in Wesensnotwendigkeit das menschliche Werden *bewußtleitende* Entelechie. Philosophie, Wissenschaft wäre demnach die historische *Bewegung der Offenbarung der universalen, dem Menschentum als solchen „eingeborenen“* Vernunft.

*Note on the text.* The first *Entelechie* is set in the file partly in spaced letters and partly in italic ("E n-*telechie*"), which shows emphasis but not its print form. The emphasis on the closing phrase runs, in the file's broken markup, from *Bewegung* to *eingeborenen*. The opening dash of the parenthesis on the infinite movement has no closing dash in the file.

The question is built so that it leans. Its first branch is loaded with deflating words: a "mere historical-factual delusion" (*ein bloßer historisch-faktischer Wahn*), "a chance acquisition of a chance humanity" (*ein zufälliger Erwerb einer zufälligen Menschheit*), with "chance" said twice. The adjective "mere" (*bloß*) is the one § 2 attached to the fact-sciences, and "historical-factual" (*historisch-faktisch*) puts the first branch on the side of fact against reason. Its second branch is introduced by "or whether not rather" (*oder ob nicht vielmehr*), the form of a question that expects yes. The main commentary, at §6.3, reads the passage as Husserl "posing" European superiority "as a philosophical question" rather than asserting it. That is right about the grammar of the "whether … or" and wrong about its weight. The next sentence leaves the question behind and asserts in the indicative: "Humanity in general *is* essentially…", and "if the human being is a rational being, he is so only insofar as his whole humanity is a humanity of reason". Only the conclusion returns to the subjunctive: philosophy "would accordingly be" (*wäre demnach*) the movement of reason's revelation.

The subjunctive is explained in the sentence after the passage, and the explanation changes what kind of question this is. "So it would really be, if the movement … *had proved* itself to be the entelechy come into the course of pure effectuation in the genuine and right way" (*So wäre es wirklich, wenn die … Bewegung sich als die … Entelechie erwiesen hätte*, Hua VI 14). The pluperfect subjunctive is counterfactual. The movement has not proved itself, because philosophy has not yet become the universal, apodictically self-norming philosophy that would show it. The question is left open, then, because the proof is outstanding, not because Husserl doubts what it would show. "Only then would it be decided whether European humanity bears an absolute idea in itself and is not merely an empirical anthropological type like 'China' or 'India'" (Hua VI 14). The quotation marks on the two names reduce whole civilizations to labels for "type". The book is offered as the proof that is still lacking. The question is open only until phenomenology has done its work.

The word that carries the claim moves between two registers. "Telos" (*Telos*) is said of European humanity, and is "inborn" in it "with the birth of Greek philosophy". "Entelechy" (*Entelechie*), the emphasized word, is said of "humanity as such". The step from one to the other is the step from a fact of European history to an essential property of the species, and the passage makes it by changing nouns. "Inborn" (*eingeboren*) follows the same path. It is first used of the European telos without quotation marks, and then, in the closing sentence, of the reason inborn "in humanity as such", where the file sets it in quotation marks (*„eingeborenen“*). The distancing comes exactly where the word is generalized to all humanity. Husserl marks as figurative the word on which the universal claim rests.

What Husserl adds to Aristotle is consciousness. An Aristotelian entelechy is the form that governs a living thing's growth without its knowing it. Husserl's entelechy is first "latent" and then "come to itself, become manifest for itself" (*zu sich selbst gekommene, für sich selbst offenbar gewordene*), and only then "*consciously guides*" (*bewußtleitende*, italic) human becoming. The vocabulary of coming to itself and being for itself is Hegel's more than Aristotle's, and "revelation" (*Offenbarung*) in the last sentence has a religious ring. The emphasis on "consciously guiding" marks the difference that matters to the book's argument. A humanity whose end works in it blindly needs no philosophy. A humanity whose end must become conscious to guide it needs exactly the "self-understanding" of latent reason that the first sentence calls "the only way". That is the contribution of the passage between its neighbours. § 5 described the crisis as a loss of faith in reason. § 6 says what would be needed to make that faith knowledge, and why the stakes are as large as "the sense of the world" against "a historical nonsense" (Hua VI 14). The paragraph that follows calls the eighteenth century's rationalism a "naivety" and asks whether its "genuine sense" must be given up with it. It closes on the irrationalists' "'lazy' reason" (*„faulen“ Vernunft*), which evades the struggle.

Husserl had said the same thing more cautiously a few months earlier. In the lecture delivered in the Vienna Kulturbund in May 1935, printed in Hua VI among the treatises, he writes: "I mean, we feel it (and for all its unclarity this feeling probably has its right), that an entelechy is inborn in our European humanity" (*Ich meine, wir fühlen es (und bei aller Unklarheit hat dieses Gefühl wohl sein Recht), daß unserem europäischen Menschentum eine Entelechie eingeboren ist*, Hua VI 321). There the claim is a feeling with a probable right, and the entelechy is "our European" one. He at once denies that it is anything like the biological development of an organism from germ to maturity. The *Krisis* turns the European entelechy into a question about humanity as such, and makes the question depend on a philosophy still to be achieved. The Vienna lecture's illustration does not appear in § 6: other humanities have a motive "always to Europeanize themselves, while we, if we understand ourselves rightly, will never, for example, Indianize ourselves" (Hua VI 321). § 6 compresses this into "the spectacle of the Europeanization of all foreign humanities", and leaves it as a question whether that spectacle shows "an absolute sense".

The word returns in the book's last surviving pages. Directly after the seam that has cost the end of § 72 and the opening of the closing word, the text speaks of a humanity "called to raise [itself] in a new historicity of human life, whose entelechy this new idea is", together with the philosophical practice that belongs to it (*in einer neuen Historizität menschlichen Lebens, deren Entelechie diese neue Idee ist*, Hua VI c. 271–274). The entelechy is no longer reason in general but phenomenology's "new idea". This is what the conditional of § 6 was waiting for. The entelechy that had to prove itself is claimed, at the end, as the idea phenomenology has made conscious.

## §9a, Hua VI 23–24: limit-shapes and ideal praxis

Reads [§9a.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#a-pure-geometry), the third paragraph under § 9a.

A limit-shape (*Limesgestalt*) is the ideal pole toward which a series of ever more perfect empirical shapes runs without ever reaching it: the straight line that every straighter edge approaches. Husserl's claim in this passage is about where such shapes come from. They are not found in intuition, and phantasy cannot produce them. They "prescribe themselves" out of a practice, the practice of perfecting things, once that practice is carried in thought beyond anything that can be done. Geometry is then a practice too, but an "ideal praxis". The reading follows that word. Exactness, on this account, is something made, the product of a praxis, and this is the premise of everything Husserl goes on to say about Galileo.

> Without going deeper from here into the essential connections (which has never been done systematically and is by no means easy), we will already understand that, from the practice of perfecting, in freely penetrating into the horizons of *conceivable* perfecting in the "again and again", *limit-shapes everywhere* prescribe themselves, toward which, as invariant and never-to-be-reached poles, each series of perfecting runs. Interested in these ideal shapes and consistently occupied with determining them and constructing new ones out of those already determined, we are "geometers". And likewise for the wider sphere, which also comprises the dimension of time, we are mathematicians of the "pure" shapes, whose universal form is the space-time form, itself idealized along with them. In place of real praxis — whether acting praxis or the praxis that considers empirical possibilities, which deals with actual and real-empirical bodies — we now have an *ideal praxis* of a "pure thinking", which keeps itself exclusively in the *realm of pure limit-shapes*.

German:

> Ohne von hier aus tiefer in die Wesenszusammenhänge einzugehen (was systematisch nie geschehen und keineswegs leicht ist), werden wir schon verstehen, daß sich von der Vervollkommnungspraxis her, im freien Eindringen in die Horizonte **erdenklicher** Vervollkommnung im „Immer wieder“, **überall Limes-Gestalten** vorzeichnen, auf die hin, als invariante und nie zu erreichende Pole, die jeweilige Vervollkommnungsreihe hinläuft. Für diese idealen Gestalten interessiert und konsequent damit beschäftigt, sie zu bestimmen und aus den schon bestimmten neue zu konstruieren, sind wir „Geometer“. Und ebenso für die weitere Sphäre, die auch die Dimension der Zeit befaßt, sind wir Mathematiker der „reinen“ Gestalten, deren universale Form die selbst mitidealisierte Raumzeitform ist. Anstelle der realen Praxis — sei es also der handelnden oder die empirischen Möglichkeiten bedenkenden, die es mit wirklichen und real-empirischen Körpern zu tun hat — haben wir jetzt eine **ideale Praxis** eines „reinen Denkens“, das sich ausschließlich im **Reiche reiner Limesgestalten** hält.

*Note on the text.* The file sets *erdenklicher*, *überall Limes-Gestalten*, *ideale Praxis* and *Reiche reiner Limesgestalten* in bold, with the word spaces collapsed in the last ("Reichereiner"). The bold is the file's rendering of spaced type, so these were spaced in print, but the file cannot settle where each spaced stretch began and ended. The English marks them in italic.

The passage moves through three words for possibility, and the move from the second to the third is what produces the ideal. The paragraph before it speaks of what can be done. The craftsman can make the straight "still straighter, the flat still flatter", but "a limit of ability" (*eine Grenze des Könnens*) is set to his normal technical capacity. Technique progresses, so "the ideal of perfection is pushed further and further out", and "we have always already an open horizon of *conceivable* improvement to be driven ever further" (Hua VI 23). "Conceivable" (*erdenklich*) is spaced there, and it is spaced again in the passage, "the horizons of *conceivable* perfecting". The emphasis falls on the word that frees the series from ability. What can be done stops somewhere. What can be conceived as done goes on "again and again" (*im „Immer wieder“*), and the quotation marks set off the iteration as a phrase of its own, the "and so on" of the ideal series. The limit-shapes "prescribe themselves" (*sich … vorzeichnen*) in that free penetration of an open horizon. The reflexive verb gives the ideal no author. Nobody constructs the pole. It is foreshadowed by the series running toward it.

The claim is admitted to be unproven. The opening concessive clause says that the "essential connections" have "never been done systematically" and are "by no means easy". Then comes "we will already understand" (*werden wir schon verstehen*). The step from a practice of improvement to an invariant pole is exactly what the essential analysis would have to show, and the passage asks the reader to understand it without that analysis. Husserl calls the poles "invariant and never-to-be-reached" (*invariante und nie zu erreichende*). Never reaching follows from the open horizon. Invariance, the one same straight line for every series of straightening, does not follow from it, and the passage does not argue it.

"Praxis" is the word that holds the passage together. The first sentence derives the limit-shapes from "the practice of perfecting" (*Vervollkommnungspraxis*). The last sets "ideal praxis" (*ideale Praxis*, spaced) in place of "real praxis" (*realen Praxis*), which it divides into acting and considering empirical possibilities. Geometry is not contemplation of the ideal, then. It is a second practice that replaces the first, with "pure thinking" (*„reinen Denkens“*, in quotation marks) as its activity. The quotation marks around "pure" (*„reinen“*) throughout §9a do the same work. The "pure" bodies, lines and planes are pure in the geometers' sense, and the paragraph two above lists them all with quotation marks. Husserl uses the word as the practitioners do while showing that their purity is an achievement. The geometers, too, are "geometers" in quotation marks. They are named after their interest in ideal shapes, not after a faculty of seeing them.

This is the premise § 9 needs. If exact shapes are the products of an ideal praxis, they can be handed on and used without the praxis being redone, and the sentences after the passage say so at once. The limit-shapes have become, through "the method of idealization and construction" developed long ago and practised "in intersubjective communalization", "habitually available acquisitions" (*habituell-verfügbaren Erwerben*). Like "all cultural acquisitions arising from human work", they "remain objectively cognizable and available, even without their sense-formation having to be explicitly renewed" (Hua VI 24). An acquisition that can be used without renewal can be used by someone who no longer knows it is an acquisition. That is the possibility of the forgetting that § 9h names. A practice became exact, and its products were then taken for the world. § 9b begins from this result: for Galileo geometry was "already given traditionally" as the guide for thinking that relates the empirical "to the mathematical limit-ideas" (*Limesideen*, Hua VI 26), and the craft of measuring perfects itself as "'approximation'" to the limit-shapes. In § 9c the concept meets its own limit. The sensible qualities have no limit-shapes in the same sense. Their gradations "are not idealizable in an analogous sense" (Hua VI 33), and that is why their mathematization must be indirect.

*Ideen I* had the limit concept without the praxis. In § 74 the ideation that yields exact essences yields them "as ideal 'limits', which are in principle not to be found in any sensory intuition, to which morphological essences 'approximate' more or less, without ever reaching them" (*als ideale „Grenzen”, die prinzipiell in keiner sinnlichen Anschauung vorfindlich sind, denen sich jeweils morphologische Wesen mehr oder minder „annähern”, ohne sie je zu erreichen*, Hua III/1 156, first edition 139). The structure of the pole is the same, and so is the never-reaching. But in 1913 the limit is the correlate of an act of ideation, a way of grasping essences, and geometrical concepts express "something that one cannot 'see'", whose "'origin'" is left in quotation marks (Hua III/1 155). The *Krisis* takes the quotation marks off the origin. It does so by putting a practice in place of the act: perfecting, its open horizon, and a long tradition of method passed on in communities. The limit-shape of 1936 has a history, and the book's argument depends on that history.

## §9h, Hua VI 49: the substitution of the substructed world

Reads [§9h.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#h-the-life-world-as-the-forgotten-meaning-fundament-of-natural-science), the first paragraph under § 9h.

§ 9h makes the life-world (*Lebenswelt*) a theme through a charge of substitution (*Unterschiebung*). The world of mathematical idealities has been put in the place of the world actually perceived, and has been taken for it. The substitution is not a false judgement about the life-world. It is the replacement of one world by another, and it is handed down from Galileo without anyone performing it again. The life-world enters the book as what has been displaced, and the passage defines it only by the series of appositions that say what was displaced.

> But now there is to be noted, as most important, a substitution, accomplishing itself already in Galileo, of the mathematically substructed world of idealities for the only actual world, the world actually given in perception, the world ever experienced and experienceable — our everyday life-world. This substitution was soon passed down by inheritance to his successors, to the physicists of all the following centuries.

German:

> Aber nun ist als höchst wichtig zu beachten eine schon bei Galilei sich vollziehende Unterschiebung der mathematisch substruierten Welt der Idealitäten für die einzig wirkliche, die wirklich wahrnehmungsmäßig gegebene, die je erfahrene und erfahrbare Welt — unsere alltägliche Lebenswelt. Diese Unterschiebung hat sich alsbald auf die Nachfolger, auf die Physiker der ganzen nachfolgenden Jahrhunderte vererbt.

*Unterschiebung* has the literal sense of pushing something under or into a place, and its ordinary use is pejorative: one "foists" a forged document or a changeling child on someone, or imputes to someone a view they do not hold. "Substitution" keeps the replacement and loses the suggestion of a false article passed off as genuine. The German word has both, and the sentence needs both. The world of idealities is put into the place of the given world, and it passes there as the real one. The participle that describes the false article belongs to the same family. The world of idealities is "substructed" (*substruiert*), built underneath, as a foundation is. The Latin prefix *sub-* and the German *unter-* say the same thing. The ideal world is built beneath the given world as its supposed ground, and then pushed into the given world's place. The heading of § 9h names the true ground: the life-world is "the forgotten sense-fundament" (*vergessenes Sinnesfundament*) of natural science. The passage is a dispute about what lies underneath. The substructed world claims to be the foundation, and the life-world is the foundation that has been forgotten.

The life-world is introduced by apposition, not by definition. It is "the only actual world" (*die einzig wirkliche*), "the world actually given in perception" (*die wirklich wahrnehmungsmäßig gegebene*), "the world ever experienced and experienceable" (*die je erfahrene und erfahrbare*), and only then, after a dash, "our everyday life-world". Each phrase adds a mode. The first is actuality, the second givenness in perception, the third experience, actual and possible. The pair "experienced and experienceable" makes the life-world a horizon of possible experience and not just the sum of what has been perceived. That matters for what follows, where it is "in its open infinite horizons of unknownness" that the life-world is the only world science's questions can concern (Hua VI 50). The first apposition carries the polemic, and here the wording claims more than the passage shows. That the perceived world is "the only actual" world is the thesis Part III must make good. Here it is the premise from which the charge of substitution follows. If the idealized world were also actual, putting it in place of the perceived world would be a change of description, not a foisting.

The grammar keeps Galileo from being the culprit. The substitution is "accomplishing itself" (*sich vollziehende*) "already in Galileo", a reflexive with no agent. It then "passed itself down by inheritance" (*hat sich … vererbt*), again reflexive, to the physicists. The next sentence makes Galileo an heir himself: "Galileo was himself an heir with regard to pure geometry" (*Galilei war hinsichtlich der reinen Geometrie selbst Erbe*), and the geometry he inherited was "already emptied of sense" (*sinnentleert*) in its "intuitiveness". The vocabulary of inheritance continues § 9a's account of idealities as "acquisitions" that remain available without their sense-formation being renewed. A substitution that is inherited needs no one to make it. It is in force as soon as the inherited method is used as if it gave the world itself. Husserl's charge against Galileo is accordingly one of omission. It was "a fateful omission" (*ein verhängnisvolles Versäumnis*) that he "did not inquire back" (*nicht … zurückfragte*) to the originally sense-giving accomplishment of idealization, practised on "the primal ground of all theoretical and practical life — the immediately intuitive world" (Hua VI 49). The paragraph ends by restating the thesis with a narrower object: "So with Galileo the substitution of idealized nature for pre-scientifically intuitive nature begins" (*Gleich mit Galilei beginnt also die Unterschiebung der idealisierten Natur für die vorwissenschaftlich anschauliche Natur*, Hua VI 50). The pair "world"/"life-world" has become the pair "idealized nature"/"intuitive nature". § 9h needs the narrower pair because its next step is the garb of ideas, which is fitted onto nature (row 8).

This is not the first occurrence of the word, and the main commentary's "introduces the concept" needs that qualification. The adjective first appears in § 9b: mathematics made an objective world "out of the indeterminately general life-worldly form space and time" (*aus der unbestimmt allgemeinen lebensweltlichen Form Raum und Zeit*, Hua VI 30). The noun appears twice in § 9f, as "the pre-scientific life-world" and "the practical life-world" (Hua VI 43–44). What § 9h adds is the heading and the role. The life-world becomes thematic at the moment it is shown to have been displaced. It is first a ground, known as the place where something else now stands.

The word "substitution" returns at the two later points where the book diagnoses the modern failure, and each time the structure is the same: a construct in the place of what was given. In § 18 Descartes's discovery of the ego "is devalued by a countersensical substitution" (*durch eine widersinnige Unterschiebung entwertet*): the pure soul is put in the place of the ego of the epoché, where a soul can only be a "phenomenon" in brackets (Hua VI 82; row 12). In § 23 the word is applied to Hume's own diagnosis. Necessary connection, "the *propter hoc*", is for Hume "a fictive substitution" (*eine fiktive Unterschiebung*) for the *post hoc* that immanent experience shows (Hua VI 90). Hume finds a substitution in causality, and does not unmask, Husserl says, "the supposed self-evidences on which this sensualism … rests" (Hua VI c. 92), the sensualism that puts data in the place of the experienced world. In Part III the life-world itself is made a theme, and the book has to show that it can be the "only actual world" without being a substitution of its own.

## §9h, Hua VI 51–52: the garb of ideas

Reads [§9h.3–4](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#h-the-life-world-as-the-forgotten-meaning-fundament-of-natural-science), the third and fourth paragraphs under § 9h.

The garb of ideas (*Ideenkleid*) is Husserl's image for what mathematical natural science is: a garment measured to fit the life-world, made of formulas whose work is foresight. The argument of the two paragraphs is carried by what happens to the garment between them. In the first it is "well-fitting", tailored to the world. In the second it "disguises" the world (*verkleidet*), so that we "take for true being what is a method". The understanding the passage carries is that the disguise comes from the fit. Because the garment follows the shape of the life-world into every fold of possible experience, it can pass for the body it covers.

> In geometrical and natural-scientific mathematization, then, we measure on the life-world — the world constantly given to us as actual in our concrete world-life — in the open infinity of possible experiences, a well-fitting *garb of ideas*, that of the so-called objective-scientific truths. That is, we construct, in a method that (as we hope) can actually be carried out in every detail and constantly proves itself, first of all determinate numerical inductions for the actual and possible sensory fillings of the concrete-intuitive shapes of the life-world, and precisely thereby we gain possibilities of a foresight of concrete world-events, not yet or no longer given as actual, namely life-worldly-intuitive ones; a foresight that infinitely surpasses the accomplishments of everyday foresight.
>
> The garb of ideas "mathematics and mathematical natural science", or, instead, the garb of symbols, of symbolic-mathematical theories, covers everything that, for the scientists as for the educated, stands in for the life-world as the "objectively actual and true" nature, disguises it. The garb of ideas makes us take for true being what is a method — a method that is there to improve, through "scientific" foresights *in progressus in infinitum*, the crude foresights that are originally alone possible within what is actually experienced and experienceable in the life-world: the disguise of ideas makes the proper sense of the method, of the formulas, of the "theories" remain unintelligible, and never understood in the naive genesis of the method.

German:

> In der geometrischen und naturwissenschaftlichen Mathematisierung messen wir so der Lebenswelt — der in unserem konkreten Weltleben uns ständig als wirklich gegebenen Welt — in der offenen Unendlichkeit möglicher Erfahrungen ein wohlpassendes *Ideenkleid* an, das der sogenannten objektivwissenschaftlichen Wahrheiten, d. i. wir konstruieren in einer (wie wir hoffen) wirklich und bis ins Einzelne durchzuführenden und sich ständig bewährenden Methode zunächst bestimmte Zahlen-Induzierungen für die wirklichen und möglichen sinnlichen Füllen der konkret-anschaulichen Gestalten der Lebenswelt, und eben damit gewinnen wir Möglichkeiten einer Voraussicht der konkreten, noch nicht oder nicht mehr als wirklich gegebenen, und zwar der lebensweltlich-anschaulichen Weltgeschehnisse; einer Voraussicht, welche die Leistungen der alltäglichen Voraussicht unendlich übersteigt.
>
> Das Ideenkleid „Mathematik und mathematische Naturwissenschaft“, oder dafür das Kleid der Symbole, der symbolisch-mathematischen Theorien, befaßt alles, was wie den Wissenschaftlern so den Gebildeten als die „objektiv wirkliche und wahre“ Natur die Lebenswelt vertritt, sie verkleidet. Das Ideenkleid macht es, daß wir für wahres Sein nehmen, was eine Methode ist — dazu da, um die innerhalb des lebensweltlich wirklich Erfahrenen und Erfahrbaren ursprünglich allein möglichen rohen Voraussichten durch „wissenschaftliche“ im Progressus in infinitum zu verbessern: die Ideenverkleidung macht es, daß der eigentliche Sinn der Methode, der Formeln, der „Theorien“ unverständlich blieb und bei der naiven Entstehung der Methode niemals verstanden wurde.

The verb of the first sentence is a tailor's verb. *Jemandem etwas anmessen* is to measure a garment on someone, to make it to their measure, and the object is "a well-fitting garb of ideas" (*ein wohlpassendes Ideenkleid*). The verb also contains the word on which the whole of § 9 has turned, measuring (*messen*). In § 9a the art of measuring (*Meßkunst*) was the practice from which geometry's idealities came. Here the measuring is turned on the life-world as a whole, and what it produces is clothing. The pun fits the argument. Mathematization is measurement, and measurement makes something to the measure of the thing measured, something that follows its shape but is not the thing. The "so-called" (*sogenannten*) in front of "objective-scientific truths" says in advance what the garment is woven from.

The "that is" (*d. i.*) translates the image back into method, and the translation says what the fit consists in. "We construct … numerical inductions" for the actual and possible sensory fillings of the life-world's shapes, and so "gain possibilities of a foresight" (*Voraussicht*) of events "not yet or no longer given as actual". The garment fits because it predicts. The paragraph before has prepared the word. "All life rests on foresight, we may also say on induction", and even simple perception is "a self-having in one with a fore-having, a fore-meaning" (*ein Selbsthaben in eins mit Vor-haben, Vor-meinen*, Hua VI 51). The hyphens split the ordinary words for "plan" and "presume" into their parts, so that every perception is shown to reach ahead of itself. The scientific foresight is the same reaching, raised to infinity. The garment is cut to "the open infinity of possible experiences", and so it fits the life-world where the life-world has not yet been experienced. The parenthesis "(as we hope)" (*(wie wir hoffen)*) qualifies only whether the method can be carried out in every detail. The fit is otherwise granted, and the method "constantly proves itself" (*sich ständig bewährenden*). Husserl does not dispute the science. He disputes what it is taken for.

The second paragraph changes the verb, and the disguise follows from the first paragraph's terms. The garb "stands in for the life-world" (*die Lebenswelt vertritt*) and "disguises it" (*sie verkleidet*). *Vertreten* is to represent as a deputy, to act in someone's place. *Verkleiden* is to dress up so as not to be recognized. The two verbs stand side by side with no connective, as if the second only said the first again. That is the point of the image. A garment that stands in for the body it fits is already a disguise. The prefix *ver-* turns *kleiden* [to clothe] into concealment, and the noun follows at the end of the paragraph: "the disguise of ideas" (*die Ideenverkleidung*). Between them stands the sentence that names the error, "The garb of ideas makes us take for true being what is a method" (*Das Ideenkleid macht es, daß wir für wahres Sein nehmen, was eine Methode ist*). The garment has a second name in the paragraph, "the garb of symbols" (*das Kleid der Symbole*), and it brings the formulas of § 9f into the image. What is worn are symbols, and a page later Husserl calls the nature of physics "given in formulas, to be interpreted only out of the formulas" (*die in Formeln gegebene, aus den Formeln erst heraus zu interpretierende*, Hua VI 53). The quotation marks distance the wearers' words ("objectively actual and true" nature, "scientific" foresights, "theories"). The words belong to the scientists and the educated, who see the garment.

The inference is sound as far as it goes, and its limit is instructive. Fit explains why the substitution of row 7 is possible: a method that predicts everything that can be experienced gives no occasion to notice that it is a method. It does not explain why the fit should be taken for identity. That needs the forgetting that the heading of § 9h names, and the paragraph after the passage turns it into a question. How could a method run usefully "through the centuries" without anyone understanding its sense? Science resembles "a machine" that anyone can learn to handle without understanding it, and Husserl asks whether this is a problem "in line with the problem of instincts", the problem of "the hidden reason that first knows itself as reason once it has become manifest" (Hua VI 52–53). The garment metaphor thus leads back to the latent reason of § 6 (row 5). The disguise belongs to a reason that has not yet come to itself. The next paragraph names Galileo "at once a discovering and a concealing genius" (row 9), the person in whose hands the fitting became the disguise.

*Erfahrung und Urteil* quotes this sentence and changes the image. There the "universe of determinations in themselves" of exact science is "nothing but a garb of ideas, thrown over the world of immediate intuition and experience, over the life-world" (*nichts ist als ein Kleid von Ideen, geworfen über die Welt unmittelbarer Anschauung und Erfahrung, über die Lebenswelt*), and it goes on to cite "This garb of ideas makes us take for true being what is a method" from the *Krisis* (EU 42). A garment "thrown over" (*geworfen über*) something does not fit it and is not made to its measure. It covers, and three pages later the text speaks again of "this garb of ideas thrown over the world" (*dieses über die Welt geworfenen Ideenkleides*, EU 45–46). The image keeps the covering and loses the tailoring, and with it the explanation that the *Krisis* gives of why the disguise succeeds. In the *Krisis* the life-world is hidden by a garment cut to its measure, not by a cloth thrown over it.

## §9h, Hua VI 53: discovering and concealing genius

Reads [§9h.6](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#h-the-life-world-as-the-forgotten-meaning-fundament-of-natural-science), the sixth paragraph under § 9h.

Husserl's verdict on Galileo is a single formula: he is "at once a discovering and a concealing genius". The formula claims that discovery and concealment are one act, not a merit and a fault that can be weighed against each other. What Galileo uncovered, mathematical nature as the "true" world, is the cover laid over the world he started from. The passage ends by saying that nothing in later physics, not even the critique of the causal law, has lifted that cover, because what conceals is not determinism but the idea of a nature mathematical "in itself".

> Galileo, the discoverer — or, to do justice to his forerunners, the completing discoverer — of physics, or of physical nature, is at once a *discovering and concealing* genius. He discovers mathematical nature, the methodical idea, he breaks the path for the infinity of physical discoverers and discoveries. Against the universal *causality of the intuitive world* (as its invariant form) he discovers what has since been called, without further ado, the law of causality, the "a priori form" of the "true" (idealized and mathematized) world, the "law of exact lawfulness", according to which every event of "nature" — the idealized one — must stand under exact laws. All this is discovery-concealment, and we take it to this day as plain truth. Nothing changes in principle, after all, through the supposedly philosophically revolutionary critique of "the classical law of causality" on the part of the new atomic physics. For with all that is new there remains, as it seems to me, what is essential in principle: nature as in itself mathematical, given in formulas, to be interpreted only out of the formulas.

German:

> *Galilei*, der Entdecker — oder, um seinen Vorarbeitern Gerechtigkeit angedeihen zu lassen: der vollendende Entdecker — der Physik bzw. der physikalischen Natur ist zugleich *entdeckender und verdeckender* Genius. Er entdeckt die mathematische Natur, die methodische Idee, er bricht der Unendlichkeit physikalischer Entdecker und Entdeckungen die Bahn. Er entdeckt gegenüber der universalen *Kausalität der anschaulichen Welt* (als ihrer invarianten Form) das, was seither ohne weiteres das Kausalgesetz, die „apriorische Form“ der „wahren“ (idealisierten und mathematisierten) Welt, das „Gesetz der exakten Gesetzlichkeit“, wonach jedes Geschehen der „Natur“ — der idealisierten — unter exakten Gesetzen stehen muß. Das alles ist Entdeckung-Verdeckung, und wir nehmen das bis heute als schlichte Wahrheit. Es ändert sich ja im Prinzipiellen nichts durch die angeblich philosophisch umstürzende Kritik „des klassischen Kausalgesetzes“ vonseiten der neuen Atomphysik. Denn bei allem Neuen verbleibt doch, wie mir scheint, das prinzipiell Wesentliche: die an sich mathematische Natur, die in Formeln gegebene, aus den Formeln erst heraus zu interpretierende.

*Note on the text.* The third sentence has no main verb for its relative clause ("what has since been called, without further ado, the law of causality…" ends without *heißt* or a like verb). The English supplies "been called". The words are quoted as the file has them.

The formula rests on a single stem. *Decken* is to cover. *Ent-decken*, to discover, is literally to take the cover off, and *ver-decken*, to conceal, is to cover over. The two prefixes pull in opposite directions on the same root. Husserl joins the participles with "and" (*entdeckender und verdeckender*, italic), and the summary sentence then joins the nouns with a hyphen: "All this is discovery-concealment" (*Entdeckung-Verdeckung*). The hyphen makes one word of two and denies that there are two events. English loses the stem: "discover" keeps the image of a cover, but "conceal" does not, and the pun cannot be rendered. The sense is not lost with it. Taking the cover off one thing is laying a cover over another, and the second paragraph above gave the cover its shape: the garb of ideas that "disguises" the life-world (row 8). What is uncovered is "mathematical nature, the methodical idea". What is covered over is the world in which the method arose.

The third sentence shows how a single discovery does both. Galileo discovers the law of causality "against the universal *causality of the intuitive world*" (*gegenüber der universalen Kausalität der anschaulichen Welt*, italic), which Husserl calls that world's "invariant form". The intuitive world already has a universal causality. In § 9c it is the "invariant general style of being" of the intuitive world in its unceasing change (Hua VI 34). What is new in Galileo is a different law, the law of the idealized world, and the sentence crowds it with quotation marks: "a priori form", "true" world, "law of exact lawfulness", "nature". Each phrase is the physicists' own, and each is held at a distance. The parentheses do the work of the critique inside the sentence: the "true" world is glossed as "(idealized and mathematized)", and "nature" as "the idealized one". So the sentence says twice what the garb of ideas hides, by naming the idealization each time the scientists' word leaves it out. The concealment is the moment when the qualifier drops away. The law of the idealized world is then taken "without further ado" (*ohne weiteres*) as the law of causality as such, and the causality of the intuitive world, from which it was idealized, falls out of view. "We take it to this day as plain truth" (*schlichte Wahrheit*). The garment is now worn by everyone.

The last two sentences, which the main commentary's block does not take up, turn the verdict into a claim about the present. "The new atomic physics" has criticized "the classical law of causality" (in quotation marks), and the critique is "supposedly" (*angeblich*) philosophically revolutionary. Husserl answers that "nothing changes in principle". The hedge "as it seems to me" (*wie mir scheint*) is one of the few places in Part II where he speaks in the first person singular. It marks a judgement about physics, a field in which he does not claim competence. The judgement is nonetheless decisive for the argument. If the concealment lay in strict determinism, indeterministic physics would have lifted it. Husserl places it elsewhere, in "nature as in itself mathematical" (*die an sich mathematische Natur*). That nature is "given in formulas" and "to be interpreted only out of the formulas". Whether the laws are deterministic or statistical, nature is still what the formulas say. So the concealment survives every change of physical theory, because it belongs to the relation between theory and the world, not to any theory's content.

The paragraph that follows protects the verdict against a misreading. "I name Galileo, of course quite in earnest, at the head of the greatest discoverers of the modern age", and "admire quite in earnest" the great physicists. Their achievement "is not at all diminished" by the critique, which shows that the proper sense of their theories "remained hidden from the physicists, even the great and greatest, *and had to remain hidden*" (*verborgen blieb, und verborgen bleiben mußte*, Hua VI 53). "Had to" completes the formula. Concealment is not a fault of Galileo's that a better thinker would have avoided. It is the necessary other side of the discovery of a method. That is also what makes it "genius" and not error. In § 16 Descartes is the "*primally instituting* genius" (Hua VI 75). Husserl's geniuses are the founders of traditions whose sense outruns them.

The pair of words gives Husserl his account of history as well as his account of Galileo. In § 9l the reflection that is trying to understand Galileo's beginning runs at once into "the shifts of sense and concealments (*Verdeckungen*) of the later and latest times", in whose "spell" (*Bann*, italic) "we who perform the reflections stand ourselves" (Hua VI 59). The historian stands under the cover he is trying to lift. § 16 repeats the method for Descartes. It separates what became conscious to him from what "certain, admittedly very natural *self-evidences* concealed from him or substituted into his thoughts" (*verdeckt bzw. seinen Gedanken unterschoben haben*, Hua VI 77). Concealment and substitution (row 7) are now named together, as two sides of one operation. The book's answer comes at its end, and it uses the third verb the family needs. In the last pages of § 72 Husserl writes: "What was concealed to me in humanity, I uncover (*enthülle*) in transcendental research" (*Was in der Menschlichkeit mir verdeckt war, enthülle ich in transzendentaler Forschung*, Hua VI c. 268–271). Galileo's discovery laid a cover. Transcendental research is to take it off. Husserl does not call this a discovery. He calls it an unveiling, a word that has no concealment folded into it.

*Note on the text.* The sentence on Descartes in § 16 comes just after another seam that the file's records do not list. The same line runs "So wirken die uns hier intereseiner sorgsamen Auslegung Raum gebe" [So the … that interest(…) us here — give room to a careful interpretation], joined mid-word, and the file has running heads 75 and 77 but none for 76. The words quoted above come after the seam and form a complete clause.

## §14, Hua VI 70: transcendentalism and what is "first in itself"

Reads [§14.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#anticipatory-characterization-of-objectivism-and-transcendentalism-the-struggle-between-these-two-ideas-as-the-meaning-of-modern-intellectual-history), the second paragraph under § 14.

Transcendentalism, as § 14 defines it, is the claim that the sense of the world's being is a formation (*Gebilde*) produced by the life that experiences it, and that subjectivity, not the world, is therefore "first in itself". The definition is built out of objectivism's own vocabulary. Objectivism asks what the world is "in itself" and seeks what is "ultimately" existing. Transcendentalism keeps both words, "in itself" and "ultimately", and gives them to subjectivity. The reading follows that transfer. The book's verdicts on Descartes, Hume and Kant are all measured by it.

> What is characteristic of *objectivism* is that it moves on the ground of the world pre-given as self-evident through experience, and asks after its "objective truth", after what is unconditionally valid for it, for every rational being, after what it is in itself. To accomplish this universally is the affair of episteme, of ratio, or of philosophy. Thereby, it holds, what ultimately is would be reached, behind which it would make no rational sense to inquire further.
>
> Transcendentalism, by contrast, says: the sense of being of the pre-given life-world is a *subjective formation*, is the accomplishment of experiencing, pre-scientific life. In it the sense and the validity of being of the world builds itself up, and in each case of the world that actually holds for the one experiencing it. As for the "objectively true" world, that of science, it is a formation of a higher level, on the basis of pre-scientific experiencing and thinking, or of its accomplishments of validity. Only a radical inquiry back into subjectivity, namely into the subjectivity that *ultimately* brings about all world-validity with its content, in all pre-scientific and scientific ways, and into the what and how of the accomplishments of reason, can make objective truth intelligible and reach the ultimate sense of being of the world. So it is not the being of the world in its unquestioned self-evidence that is the first in itself, and the mere question is not to be asked of what belongs to it objectively; rather, the first in itself is subjectivity, and indeed as the subjectivity that naively pre-gives the being of the world and then rationalizes it, or, what counts as the same: objectivates it.

German:

> Das Charakteristische des **Objektivismus** ist, daß er sich auf dem Boden der durch Erfahrung selbstverständlich vorgegebenen Welt bewegt und nach ihrer „objektiven Wahrheit“ fragt, nach dem für sie unbedingt, für jeden Vernünftigen Gültigen, nach dem, was sie an sich ist. Das universal zu leisten, ist Sache der Episteme, der Ratio, bzw. der Philosophie. Damit werde das letztlich Seiende erreicht, hinter das zurückzufragen keinen vernünftigen Sinn mehr hätte.
>
> Der Transzendentalismus dagegen sagt: der Seinssinn der vorgegebenen Lebenswelt ist *subjektives Gebilde*, ist Leistung des erfahrenden, des vorwissenschaftlichen Lebens. In ihm baut sich der Sinn und die Seinsgeltung der Welt auf, und jeweils der Welt, welche dem jeweilig Erfahrenden wirklich gilt. Was die „objektiv wahre“ Welt anlangt, die der Wissenschaft, so ist sie Gebilde höherer Stufe, aufgrund des vorwissenschaftlichen Erfahrens und Denkens bzw. seiner Geltungsleistungen. Nur ein radikales Zurückfragen auf die Subjektivität, und zwar auf die *letztlich* alle Weltgeltung mit ihrem Inhalt, und in allen vorwissenschaftlichen und wissenschaftlichen Weisen, zustandebringende Subjektivität, sowie auf das Was und Wie der Vernunftleistungen kann die objektive Wahrheit verständlich machen und den letzten Seinssinn der Welt erreichen. Also nicht das Sein der Welt in seiner fraglosen Selbstverständlichkeit ist das an sich Erste, und nicht die bloße Frage ist zu stellen, was ihr objektiv zugehört; sondern das an sich Erste ist die Subjektivität, und zwar als die das Sein der Welt naiv vorgebende und dann rationalisierende oder, was gleich gilt: objektivierende.

The two paragraphs speak in different moods. Objectivism's claim is reported in the subjunctive of indirect speech: "Thereby what ultimately is would be reached" (*Damit werde das letztlich Seiende erreicht*), "behind which it would make no rational sense to inquire further" (*hätte*). The subjunctive keeps the claim in objectivism's mouth. Transcendentalism is introduced by "says" and a colon, and what it says is given in the indicative, without quotation and without reported speech. In grammar, Husserl presents both as positions, and he adopts only the second. The first paragraph also places its key words in quotation marks: objectivism asks after "objective truth". The second puts the same phrase on the world of science, "the 'objectively true' world". The quotation marks are the only sign, in a definition that pretends to be neutral, that the first position is being described from the standpoint of the second.

Two words pass from one paragraph to the other. Objectivism asks what the world "is in itself" (*was sie an sich ist*). Transcendentalism answers that "the first in itself" (*das an sich Erste*) is not the world's being but subjectivity. The phrase "in itself" has moved from the world's being to the order of priority. The objectivist wants the world as it is in itself. The transcendentalist says that what is first in itself, the first thing in the order of grounds, is the subjectivity for which the world is. The second word is "ultimately" (*letztlich*). Objectivism hopes to reach "what ultimately is" (*das letztlich Seiende*), the being behind which no further question makes sense. Transcendentalism directs the question to "the subjectivity that *ultimately* (*letztlich*, italic) brings about all world-validity", and claims that only this can reach "the ultimate sense of being" (*den letzten Seinssinn*) of the world. The italic marks where the ultimacy now lies. Objectivism stops its questions at the world. Transcendentalism makes the world the thing to be questioned back from, "inquiry back" (*Zurückfragen*), the verb already used of objectivism's refusal ("behind which it would make no rational sense to inquire further"). The question objectivism declares senseless is the transcendentalist's method.

The positive vocabulary of the definition belongs to production. The sense of being is "a *subjective formation*" (*subjektives Gebilde*, italic), an "accomplishment" (*Leistung*) of experiencing life, and in that life the world's sense "builds itself up" (*baut sich … auf*). Science's world is "a formation of a higher level" (*Gebilde höherer Stufe*), built on pre-scientific "accomplishments of validity" (*Geltungsleistungen*). The vocabulary does not say that the world is made by subjects, as an artefact is made. It says that the world's *sense* and *validity of being* are formed, and the definition is careful to keep that object every time. The weak point is in the last clause, where subjectivity "naively pre-gives the being of the world" (*das Sein der Welt naiv vorgebende*). Here the object is "the being of the world", not its sense, and the line between giving the world's sense and giving the world is crossed in a participle. The clause then equates rationalizing with objectivating: "or, what counts as the same" (*oder, was gleich gilt*). That equation is the root of the whole book's critique. Reason, in the modern era, has been carried out as objectivation, and the second word claims to say only what the first means.

The paragraph after the passage at once names the danger the definition invites. "Here the countersense threatens in advance, since it first seems self-evident that this subjectivity is the human being, therefore psychological subjectivity" (Hua VI 71). A "matured transcendentalism" protests against psychological idealism and claims "a *completely new kind* of scientificity" (*eine völlig neuartige Wissenschaftlichkeit*). The definition is "anticipatory" (*vordeutend*), as the heading says. It states the thesis that the history to come will test, and it postpones the question of whose subjectivity is "first in itself". That question governs the rest of the book. Descartes will discover the ego and turn it into a soul (§ 18, row 12). Hume will discover that the world is "being out of subjective accomplishment" and conclude skepticism (§ 24, row 13). In Part III the paradox of human subjectivity (§ 53–54, row 22) returns to the threat named here. § 55 corrects the first approach by reducing it to "the absolutely unique, ultimately functioning ego" (*letztlich fungierende ego*, row 23). The "ultimately" of § 14 ends in that title.

The phrase "first in itself" was Husserl's own term in the *Cartesian Meditations*. There it names an order of evidence. The beginning of philosophy asks after "the cognitions that are first in themselves" (*die an sich ersten Erkenntnissen*), the ones that can carry the whole edifice of universal knowledge (Hua I 55), and the transcendental "I am" is "the ground of cognition first in itself" (*des an sich ersten Erkenntnisbodens*), whose actuality "stands absolutely firm" (Hua I 63, § 9). In § 14 the phrase has changed its domain. What is first in itself is subjectivity "as" the subjectivity that pre-gives and objectivates the world, first because it accomplishes the world's validity, not because its evidence comes first. The *Krisis* will make the same shift openly. Its § 43 contrasts its own way to the reduction with "the Cartesian way", which leads "as in one leap" (*wie in einem Sprunge*) to the transcendental ego but brings it into view "in an apparent emptiness of content" (*in einer scheinbaren Inhaltsleere*, Hua VI 158). The priority of accomplishment that § 14 states is what the new way is for.

## §15, Hua VI 73–74: final institution

Reads [§15.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#reflection-on-the-method-of-our-historical-mode-of-observation), the second paragraph under § 15.

A final institution (*Endstiftung*) is the point at which the task set by a primal institution (row 3) comes to "complete clarity", and with it to an apodictic method that can go on without failing. § 15 claims that every primal institution has one "essentially", as the task it sets for history. It then makes the final institution the condition of understanding history at all. Only from the end can one see what "it" was aiming at in all the philosophers. The reading follows this second claim, because it is what makes the historical part of the *Krisis* a "self-reflection" and not a history of ideas, and because it is the passage's least secure step.

> But essentially there belongs to every primal institution a final institution set as a task for the historical process. It is accomplished when the task has come to complete clarity, and with that to an apodictic method which in every step of attainment is the constant passage to new steps that have the character of absolutely succeeding ones, that is, of apodictic ones. Philosophy as an infinite task would thereby have come to its apodictic beginning, to its horizon of apodictic continuation. (It would of course be fundamentally wrong to [—] the usual sense of the apodictic, taken from traditional mathematics, for the most principled sense of the apodictic that announces itself here.) […] But however precisely we are informed by historical research about such "self-interpretations" (even about those of a whole chain of philosophers), we learn nothing from them yet about what "it" ultimately "was aiming at" in all these philosophers, in the hidden unity of intentional inwardness that alone makes up the unity of history. Only in the final institution does this reveal itself, only from it can the unified directedness of all philosophies and philosophers open up, and from it an illumination can be won in which one understands the past thinkers as they could not have understood themselves.

German:

> Wesensmäßig aber gehört zu jeder Urstiftung eine dem historischen Prozeß aufgegebene Endstiftung. Sie ist vollzogen, wenn die Aufgabe zur vollendeten Klarheit gekommen ist, und damit zu einer apodiktischen Methode, die in jedem Schritte der Erzielung der ständige Durchgang ist für neue Schritte, die den Charakter von absolut gelingenden haben, d. h. von apodiktischen. Die Philosophie als unendliche Aufgabe wäre damit zu ihrem apodiktischen Anfang gekommen, zu ihrem Horizont apodiktischer Fortführung. (Es wäre natürlich grundverkehrt, dem hier sich anzeigenden prinzipiellsten Sinn des Apodiktischen den üblichen, von der traditionellen Mathematik abgenommenen Sinn zu unterschieden.) […] Aber wenn wir durch historische Forschung noch so genau über solche „Selbstinterpretatione“ (und sei es auch über die einer ganzen Kette von Philosophen) unterrichtet werden, so erfahren wir daraus noch nichts über das, worauf „es“ letztlich in der verborgenen Einheit intentionaler Innerlichkeit, welche allein Einheit der Geschichte ausmacht, in all diesen Philosophen „hinauswollte“. Nur in der Endstiftung offenbart sich das, nur von ihr aus kann sich die einheitliche Ausgerichtetheit aller Philosophien und Philosophen eröffnen, und von ihr aus kann eine Erhellung gewonnen werden, in welcher man die vergangenen Denker versteht, wie sie sich selbst nicht hätten verstehen können.

*Note on the text.* The parenthesis ends in *zu unterschieden*, which is not a German verb form. The dative construction ("to the most principled sense … the usual sense") calls for a verb such as *unterzuschieben* [to substitute, foist], which would give the sentence a clear sense: it would be wrong to foist the mathematical sense of "apodictic" onto the principled one. That is a conjecture, and the English leaves the verb open. The reading uses only what is certain, that the parenthesis separates two senses of "apodictic". *Selbstinterpretatione* is a misprint for *Selbstinterpretationen* [self-interpretations]. The omission between the two paragraphs is a warning that every historical philosopher carries out his own self-reflections and forms a self-understanding of what he was "aiming at" (Hua VI 74).

The first sentence is an essential law of history, and its first word says so: "essentially" (*Wesensmäßig*). That every primal institution has a final institution is not found by surveying institutions. It is asserted as belonging to the sense of instituting. The participle that links the two terms is "set as a task" (*aufgegebene*). The *Endstiftung* is not given to the historical process as an outcome. It is given to it as an assignment (*Aufgabe*), the noun that the next sentence picks up: "when the task has come to complete clarity". A few lines earlier the same participle, in emphasis, named our own situation: the task stands before us "not only as objectively necessary but as set as a task for us, today's philosophers" (*als uns, den heutigen Philosophen, aufgegebene*, Hua VI 72). The final institution is the end of a task that is ours. That is why the paragraph before can call historical reflection "the genuine self-reflection of the philosopher on what he really aims at" (Hua VI 73).

The final institution is defined by method, not by doctrine. It is accomplished when the task reaches clarity "and with that" (*und damit*) an apodictic method, one in which each step is "the constant passage to new steps" that "succeed absolutely". The end of philosophy is a beginning: "Philosophy as an infinite task would thereby have come to its apodictic beginning", to "its horizon of apodictic continuation". The final institution does not close the infinite task. It makes it securely infinite. The verb is subjunctive (*wäre … gekommen*), as the conclusion of § 6 was (row 5). The final institution has not happened, and the conditional states what would be the case if it had. The parenthesis then warns against reading "apodictic" in the sense "taken from traditional mathematics". The apodicticity of a method that goes on is not the certainty of a deduced theorem. Husserl announces a new sense of the word and leaves it unexplained here. The closing word fulfils the announcement in its last surviving lines: "the discovery of the radical contrast between what is usually called apodictic cognition and what in a transcendental understanding designates the primal ground and the primal method of all philosophy" (Hua VI 276). The parenthesis of § 15 is redeemed in the book's last pages.

The second claim, about understanding, carries most of the weight. The philosophers' "self-interpretations" (in quotation marks) tell us nothing about "what 'it' ultimately 'was aiming at'" (*worauf „es“ letztlich … „hinauswollte“*). The quotation marks around "it" (*„es“*) mark an impersonal subject. It is not this or that philosopher who aims, but something in all of them, located "in the hidden unity of intentional inwardness, which alone makes up the unity of history". The verb *hinauswollen* (to be driving at, to aim at) is in quotation marks too, because it is said of an "it" that has no will of its own. Then comes the double "only" (*Nur … nur*): "Only in the final institution does this reveal itself, only from it" can the unified directedness of all philosophers open up. The result is the formula that past thinkers can be understood "as they could not have understood themselves" (*wie sie sich selbst nicht hätten verstehen können*). Here the passage is weakest. The final institution has not happened, as the subjunctive of the first paragraph said. Yet the understanding of history that it alone makes possible is the understanding the *Krisis* has been offering since § 1. The book reads Galileo, Descartes and Hume from an end that it does not yet have. § 14 has said what that end is: a directedness "toward a final form of transcendental philosophy — as *phenomenology*" (*eine Ausgerichtetheit auf eine Endform der Transzendentalphilosophie*, Hua VI 71). Husserl reads history from the final institution that he expects his own philosophy to be. The § 15 reflection makes the circle visible, but it does not break it. Its answer is that the circle is the structure of any self-reflection: one understands one's beginnings by what one is now trying to do, and corrects both as one goes. The method of § 9l, going forward and back "in a zigzag" (Hua VI 59), is the practice of this circle.

The pair of terms does not return under these names. "Final institution" occurs only in § 15, twice. What returns is its content. In the transition to Part III B Husserl says that only a philosophy that inquires back to the transcendental ego "can fulfil the sense that is innate to philosophy from its primal institution" (Hua VI 195), which is the final institution described without the noun. The closing word ends on a humanity that "understands itself as rational" in "willing to be rational", in "an infinity of life and striving toward reason" (Hua VI 276; see row 4). That is the apodictic beginning of an infinite task, as § 15 promised. Whether the *Krisis* has brought it about, or only described it, is the question § 15 leaves open by its own subjunctive.

## §18, Hua VI 81: the ego is no residuum

Reads [§18.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#descartes-self-misinterpretation-the-psychologistic-falsification-of-the-pure-ego-won-through-the-epoch), the first paragraph under § 18.

A residuum is what is left over when part of something is taken away. Husserl's correction of Descartes turns on denying the word to the ego and giving it to the soul. The soul is what remains of a human being when the natural scientist abstracts the body away, a part left over from a whole. The ego of the epoché is not left over from anything. It is a "positing" that only the bracketing of the world's validity as a whole makes possible. The difference is the difference between abstraction and epoché, and Descartes's error, on this reading, is to have taken the one for the other.

> The *ego* is not a residuum of the world, but the absolutely apodictic positing that is made possible only through the epoché, only through the *"bracketing"* of the *entire* world-validity, and is made possible by it as the only thing it makes possible. The *soul*, however, is the *residuum of a prior abstraction* of the pure body, and after this abstraction, at least apparently, a complementary piece of this body. But (as must not be left out of account) this abstraction does not happen in the epoché, but in the way of looking of the natural scientist or psychologist on the natural ground of the pre-given world, the world that self-evidently is.

German:

> Das *ego* ist nicht ein Residuum der Welt, sondern die absolut apodiktische Setzung, die nur durch die Epoché, nur durch die *„Einklammerung“* der **gesamten** Weltgeltung ermöglicht, und als einzige ermöglicht wird. Die **Seele** aber ist das **Residuum einer vorgängigen Abstraktion** des puren Körpers, und nach dieser Abstraktion, mindestens scheinbar, ein Ergänzungsstück dieses Körpers. Aber (wie nicht außer Acht zu lassen ist) diese Abstraktion geschieht nicht in der Epoché, sondern in der Betrachtungsweise des Naturforschers oder Psychologen auf dem natürlichen Boden der vorgegebenen, der selbstverständlich seienden Welt.

*Note on the text.* The file sets *gesamten*, *Seele* and *Residuum einer vorgängigen Abstraktion* in bold (spaced type in print), the last with its word spaces collapsed. The page before this one is missing. The file has running heads 79 and 81 but none for 80. In the paragraph before the passage, the sentence on the sphere of being reached by the epoché breaks off ("…die prinzipiell allem erdenklichen für mich Seienden und seinen Seinssphären" [which in principle … to everything conceivable that is for me and its spheres of being]) and resumes in another ("…gemeinen Lebens sei. Nun schaltet er den Leib aus" [… of common life. Now he excludes the body]). The heading of § 18 and the opening of the section, with the "hidden ambiguity" of the Cartesian thoughts that the main commentary's § 18.1 summarizes, are not in the file. The passage itself is complete.

The two sentences are built in parallel. Each has a subject, "the ego" and "the soul", and each predicates "residuum" of it, the first negatively and the second positively. The emphasis falls where the parallel breaks. The soul is "the *residuum of a prior abstraction*" (spaced), and the ego is set against a residuum "of the world". A residuum of the world would be what is left once the world has been subtracted, and that is how the ego looks if the epoché is read as an operation on the world's contents. Take away everything worldly and what remains is the thinking thing. Husserl denies that reading by changing the noun. The ego is a "positing" (*Setzung*), the act of setting something as existing, and the operation that makes it possible is named twice: "only through the epoché, only through the 'bracketing' of the *entire* world-validity". The repeated "only" (*nur … nur*) and the closing clause, "and is made possible by it as the only thing it makes possible" (*und als einzige ermöglicht wird*), tie the ego to the epoché in both directions. The epoché alone yields the ego, and the ego is all that the epoché yields as a positing. Everything else becomes phenomenon.

The emphasized "entire" (*gesamten*) carries the distinction. Abstraction takes a part from a whole and leaves the rest standing. The natural scientist abstracts the "pure body", and the soul is what is left. The leftover is then "a complementary piece" (*Ergänzungsstück*) of the body, a second piece of the same world. The epoché does not take away a part. It suspends the validity of the whole, and so it leaves nothing over in the world-sense of leaving. The quotation marks around "bracketing" (*„Einklammerung“*, italic) mark the word as a figure. A bracketed expression stays on the page with its content intact, but it is withheld from the calculation. The parenthesis "at least apparently" (*mindestens scheinbar*) reserves a later point. Whether the abstracted soul really is a complementary piece of the body is the question of Part III B, where § 62 denies that souls and bodies can be ranked equally as realities.

The third sentence places the error. The abstraction "does not happen in the epoché" but "on the natural ground of the pre-given world, the world that self-evidently is". Descartes performed the epoché and then, in determining what it had yielded, used a result won on the ground he had bracketed. The paragraph before the passage asked whether Descartes was not "dominated in advance by the Galilean certainty of a universal and absolutely pure world of bodies" (Hua VI 81). If so, the abstraction of the pure body had already taken place in his mind before he began to doubt, and the soul was waiting to be found. The paragraph ends: "The epoché must be seriously carried out and remain carried out". The next sentence draws the consequence. The identification of the ego with the pure soul is "a countersensical substitution" (*eine widersinnige Unterschiebung*, Hua VI 82), the word with which § 9h named Galileo's replacement of the life-world (row 7). In the epoché a soul can only be a "soul" in "brackets", "a mere 'phenomenon', just as the body is". That is the "*new* concept of 'phenomenon'" that, Husserl says, "first arises with the Cartesian epoché".

The word "residuum" has a history in Husserl's own work, and the passage corrects it. In *Ideen I* the pure consciousness left by the phenomenological exclusion of the world was precisely "the sought-for 'phenomenological residuum'" (*das gesuchte ,,phänomenologische Residuum"*), which "remains over" (*übrig bleibt*) "although we have 'excluded' the whole world with all things, living beings, human beings, ourselves included" (Hua III/1 107, § 50). The 1913 text put the word in quotation marks too, and it at once added: "We have really lost nothing". The *Krisis* keeps the second claim and drops the first. In § 41 the philosopher, placed "*above*" his natural being and the natural world by the epoché, "loses nothing of their being and their objective truths" (Hua VI 154–155). In § 18 the word that described the ego in 1913 has become the word for Descartes's error. Read against *Ideen I*, the correction of Descartes is also a correction of the vocabulary of 1913. Whether Husserl meant it as such, the passage does not say. It names only Descartes.

The distinction of § 18 is the one on which the book's later account of the epoché depends. § 40 warns against the "temptation" to misunderstand the epoché "as an abstention, to be achieved step by step, from all individual validities" (*als eine schrittweise zu leistende Enthaltung von allen einzelnen Geltungen*, heading, Hua VI 152). A step-by-step abstention would be abstraction again, a subtraction of parts with something left over at each step. The "total" epoché of § 39 is the "entire" bracketing of § 18. And when § 53 asks how the subject for the world can also be an object in it, the answer cannot be that the ego is a residue found inside the world. It has to be that the ego is the ego of a positing that has no place in the world (row 22).

## §25 (commentary §24.1), Hua VI 99–100: Hume's problem, the world-riddle

Reads [§24.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-genuine-philosophical-motive-of-the-shattering-of-objectivism-hidden-in-the-countersense-of-humean-skepticism), the first paragraph under § 24. In the source the passage belongs to § 25 (see the Corrections in the key-passage list).

The world-riddle (*Welträtsel*), in Husserl's "deepest and ultimate sense", is the riddle of a world whose being is "being out of subjective accomplishment", together with the evidence that no other world is conceivable. Husserl claims that this, "and nothing else", is Hume's problem, and that Kant never reached it. The claim reverses the usual places. Hume, the skeptic, becomes the discoverer of the transcendental problem, and Kant, the transcendental philosopher, becomes a rationalist who stopped short of it. The reading turns on how Husserl gets from Hume's theory to Hume's "problem", because the reversal rests entirely on that step.

> But how is this *most radical subjectivism*, which subjectivizes the world itself, to be grasped? The world-riddle in the deepest and ultimate sense, the riddle of a world whose *being is being out of subjective accomplishment*, and that in the evidence that another cannot be conceivable at all — that and nothing else is *Hume's problem*.
>
> *Kant*, however, who — as is easy to see — has so many *presuppositions* in "self-evident" validity, which in the Humean sense are included in this world-riddle, never pressed through to the riddle itself. His problematic stands entirely on the ground of the rationalism running from *Descartes* through *Leibniz* to *Wolff*.

German:

> Aber wie ist dieser *radikalste Subjektivismus*, der die Welt selbst subjektiviert, faßbar? Das Welträtsel im tiefsten und letzten Sinne, das Rätsel einer Welt, deren *Sein Sein aus subjektiver Leistung* ist, und das in der Evidenz, daß eine andere überhaupt nicht denkbar sein kann — das und nichts anderes ist *Humes Problem*.
>
> *Kant* aber, der — wie leicht zu sehen ist — so viele *Voraussetzungen* in „selbstverständlicher” Geltung hat, die im Humeschen Sinne in diesem Welträtsel einbegriffen sind, ist zu diesem selbst nie vorgestoßen. Seine Problematik steht eben ganz auf dem Boden des von *Descartes* über *Leibniz* zu *Wolff* verlaufenden Rationalismus.

*Note on the text.* The file italicizes *Sein*, *Sein* and *aus subjektiver Leistung* as three separate spans, so the whole phrase was emphasized, probably spaced in print. The § 24 heading is missing from the file, but § 25's heading stands at :1479, well before this passage, so the passage is in § 25. The main commentary places it in § 24.

The phrase that defines the riddle repeats its noun: "a world whose *being* is *being* out of subjective accomplishment" (*deren Sein Sein aus subjektiver Leistung ist*). The doubling is not stylistic. The first "being" is the world's being as such, the second is a kind of being, being-from. The sentence says that the world's being is not a being that then also has a relation to subjects; it *is* the being of something accomplished. The preposition "out of" (*aus*) names origin, not location or dependence. The same construction has already defined transcendentalism: the world's sense of being is a "subjective formation" and an "accomplishment" (§ 14, row 10). Here the "sense" has dropped out, and the riddle is put in terms of being itself. The second clause makes it a riddle rather than a thesis: "and that in the evidence that another cannot be conceivable at all". If the world's being were from subjective accomplishment only as a matter of fact, there would be a theory to test. Because no other world is conceivable, the world as we live in it becomes unintelligible as soon as we ask how its being can come from us. The "radical subjectivism" of the first sentence is radical because it "subjectivizes the world itself" (*die Welt selbst subjektiviert*), not just our ideas of it.

The attribution to Hume carries a strong particle: "that and nothing else" (*das und nichts anderes*). The paragraph before the passage shows what it rests on, and it is not Hume's text. "Hume, as Kant understands him, is not the real Hume". The real problem is found "if we transform Hume's skeptical theory, his total assertion, back into his *problem*" (*zurückverwandeln in sein Problem*), "extending it into the consequences that do not find quite complete expression in the theory" (Hua VI 99). The concession follows at once: "though it is hard to assume that a genius of Hume's kind did not see the consequences" not explicitly drawn. The "real Hume" is therefore a reconstruction, Hume's theory turned back into a question and extended beyond what he wrote, with a hedge on whether he saw it. The confidence of "that and nothing else" outruns that procedure. The passage says what Hume's problem must be if his skepticism is read as a symptom of a true problem. That is the reading § 24 announced in its title, the "genuine philosophical motive … hidden in the countersense of Humean skepticism", and Husserl's method of reading history from its final institution (row 11) licenses it. It does not show that Hume posed the problem.

The same paragraph formulates the universal problem in a phrase that gives the whole *Krisis* its programme: "How is the *naive self-evidence* of the world-certainty in which we live … to be brought to an *intelligibility*?" (*Wie ist die naive Selbstverständlichkeit der Weltgewißheit … zu einer Verständlichkeit zu bringen?*, Hua VI 99). The two nouns share a stem. *Selbstverständlichkeit* (self-evidence, what is understood of itself) is to become *Verständlichkeit* (intelligibility, what is understood). Taking away the prefix "self-" is the task. What is understood "of itself" is not yet understood. The world-riddle of the passage is the moment at which that self-evidence breaks down, because the world turns out to be "a validity sprung from my subjectivity" (Hua VI 99) and is then no longer self-evident.

Kant is judged by the same word. He has "so many *presuppositions*" in "'self-evident' validity" (*in „selbstverständlicher” Geltung*). The quotation marks set off the word the paragraph before had made the target. His presuppositions are things that, "in the Humean sense", are "included in this world-riddle". What Kant takes for granted is exactly what Hume's problem calls into question. Hence "never" (*nie*), the strongest word of the verdict, and its explanation: Kant's problematic stands "entirely" (*ganz*) on the ground of the rationalism of Descartes, Leibniz and Wolff. The reversal of the usual order is complete. Kant, not Hume, is the one held by tradition. The verdict is qualified a page later, in the sentence that closes § 25: in reaction against Hume's "data-positivism", "*for the first time since Descartes*" a great systematic scientific philosophy appears that is to be called "*transcendental subjectivism*" (Hua VI 100). Kant is the first transcendental subjectivist even though he never reached the transcendental problem. § 27 will try to say what in his philosophy is nonetheless "transcendental" in Husserl's own sense, and § 30 turns the charge into a charge about his language, his "mythical" concepts (row 15).

The world-riddle is not new in § 25. It was promised at the start. § 2 announced "a kind of world-riddles foreign to earlier times" (*Welträtseln, die den früheren Zeiten fremd waren*, Hua VI 3), which "all lead back to the riddle of subjectivity". § 13 announced that "world-riddles of a never-suspected style" now appear and call for a "completely new kind of philosophizing", the "greatest of all revolutions", the "*turn of scientific objectivism*" (Hua VI 69). In those places the plural "world-riddles" named a family of difficulties. In § 25 the singular names their ground, "the world-riddle in the deepest and ultimate sense". It is attributed to Hume because § 25 is a history, and in a history the problem must have a first bearer. The verdict on Hume returns in § 56 with its limit stated. The transcendental philosophy of "the English and Kant", in its "first immature shapes", was not a detour, although "even Hume withdrew into a feeble academic skepticism" (Hua VI 195). Hume found the riddle and refused to work on it.

## §26, Hua VI 100–101: "transcendental" as a motive

Reads [§26.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#preliminary-discussion-of-our-guiding-concept-of-the-transcendental), the first paragraph under § 26.

Husserl's own concept of the transcendental, as § 26 states it, names a motive, not a doctrine and not a school: the motive of inquiring back to the ultimate source of all formations of cognition, which is the cognizing "I-myself". The passage adds something the main commentary's block leaves out. The concept cannot be documented from the systems of the philosophers who are said to have had it. It is won by immersion in the history of modern philosophy as a whole, as the concept of a task striving "from vague *dynamis* to its *energeia*". The Aristotelian pair carries the claim. The transcendental is the potency of modern philosophy, and phenomenology is to be its actuality.

> I want to remark here at once: the word "*transcendental philosophy*" has become current since Kant, and also as a general title for universal philosophies whose concepts are then oriented by the type of the Kantian. I myself use the word "transcendental" in *a widest sense* for the original motive, discussed in detail above, which through Descartes is the sense-giving one in all modern philosophies and which in all of them wants, so to speak, to come to itself, to gain the genuine and pure shape of its task and its systematic working-out. It is the motive of inquiring back to the ultimate source of all formations of cognition, of the cognizer's reflecting on himself and his cognizing life, in which all the scientific formations valid for him happen purposively, are stored as acquisitions and have become and become freely available. […] Of course this most general concept of the "transcendental" is not one that can be documented; it is not to be won by the immanent interpretation of the individual systems and their comparison. Rather it is a concept acquired by immersion in the unified historicity of the whole of modern philosophy: the concept of its task, demonstrable only in this way, lying in it as the driving force of development, striving from vague dynamis to its *energeia*.

German:

> Ich möchte hier gleich bemerken: das Wort „*Transzendentalphilosophie*” ist seit Kant gebräuchlich geworden, und dies auch als allgemeiner Titel für universale Philosophien, deren Begriffe man dann am Typus der Kantischen orientiert. Ich selbst gebrauche das Wort „transzendental” in *einem weitesten Sinne* für das — von uns oben ausführlich erörterte — originale Motiv, das durch *Descartes* in allen neuzeitlichen Philosophien das sinngestende ist und in ihnen allen sozusagen zu sich selbst kommen, die echte und reine Aufgabengestalt und systematische Auswirkung gewinnen will. Es ist das Motiv des Rückfragens nach der letzten Quelle aller Erkenntnisbildungen, des Sichbesinnens des Erkennenden auf sich selbst und sein erkennendes Leben, in welchem alle ihm geltenden wissenschaftlichen Gebilde zwecktätig geschehen, als Erwerbe aufbewahrt und frei verfügbar geworden sind und werden. […] Natürlich ist dieser allgemeinste Begriff des „Transzendentalen“ kein dokumentarisch zu belegender; er ist nicht zu gewinnen durch die immanente Auslegung der einzelnen Systeme und deren Vergleichung. Vielmehr ist er ein durch Vertiefung in die einheitliche Geschichtlichkeit der gesamten philosophischen Neuzeit erworbener Begriff: der Begriff von ihrer nur so nachweisbaren, in ihr als Entwicklungstriebkraft liegenden, von vager Dynamis zu ihrer *Energeia* hinstrebenden Aufgabe.

*Note on the text.* *sinngestende* is an OCR error for *sinngebende* [sense-giving]. The emphasis on *einem weitesten Sinne* is split in the file into three italic words, probably spaced type in print. The omitted sentences say that, carried out radically, the motive is that of a "*universal philosophy grounded ultimately*" from this source, that the source "has the title *I-myself*", and that the whole transcendental problematic turns on the relation of this I to "my soul" and to the world (Hua VI 101).

The paragraph begins by separating a word from its owner. "The word 'transcendental philosophy'" has been "current since Kant", and the general title is used for philosophies oriented "by the type of the Kantian". Then: "I myself use the word 'transcendental' in *a widest sense*". The first person singular and the word "word" (*Wort*) mark a stipulation. Husserl does not argue that Kant's usage is wrong. He takes the word into his own usage, and gives it a wider extension than Kant's while keeping Kant inside it. § 27 makes use of that at once: Kant's system "is very well to be called 'transcendental-philosophical' also in the defined general sense", although it is far from a radical grounding, and it is "on the way" to it (Hua VI 101–102). The width of the definition is what lets Kant in and also what measures how far short he falls.

What the word names is a "motive" (*Motiv*), and the grammar of the sentence gives the motive a will. It is the motive that "in all of them wants, so to speak, to come to itself" (*in ihnen allen sozusagen zu sich selbst kommen … will*). The hedge "so to speak" is the one Husserl used for "primal institution" and "beheads" (rows 2, 3). The phrase "come to itself" is the one § 6 used of the entelechy of humanity (row 5). The transcendental is not first a position someone takes. It is a tendency working in the philosophies, which becomes a position only when it reaches its "genuine and pure shape of task". Its content is an act of reflection, "inquiring back" (*Rückfragen*) to "the ultimate source", "the cognizer's reflecting on himself". The verb is the one § 14 gave to transcendentalism against objectivism (row 10). The source is then named in the omitted sentences: "I-myself" (*Ich-selbst*, italic), and the problematic is the relation of "this my I — the 'ego' — to what is at first self-evidently put in its place: my soul" (*zu dem, was zunächst selbstverständlich dafür gesetzt wird: meiner Seele*, Hua VI 101). "Put in its place" (*dafür gesetzt*) is the substitution that § 18 found in Descartes (row 12), now part of the definition. The transcendental motive is the motive of finding the ego, and its typical failure is finding a soul instead.

The second paragraph admits the price of the definition, and the commentary block does not record it. The concept is "not one that can be documented" (*kein dokumentarisch zu belegender*). It cannot be won by "the immanent interpretation of the individual systems and their comparison". No reading of Descartes, Locke, Hume and Kant one after the other will turn it up. It is "acquired by immersion in the unified historicity of the whole of modern philosophy". It is the concept of a task "demonstrable only in this way" (*nur so nachweisbaren*). The "only" admits that the evidence for the concept and the method that finds it cannot be separated. That is the circle of § 15 again (row 11). A history read from its final institution yields a concept that only that reading can show. The paragraph that follows the passage calls its view of history "teleological" in quotation marks (*„teleologischen“ Geschichtsbetrachtung*), and says that only "the further presentations" are to prove its right (Hua VI 101). Husserl does not pretend that § 26 has proved anything. It is, in his word, an "anticipation" (*Vordeutung*).

The Aristotelian pair gives the anticipation its form. The task "strives from vague dynamis to its *energeia*" (*von vager Dynamis zu ihrer Energeia hinstrebenden*). *Dynamis* is capacity or potency, *energeia* actuality or being-at-work. The adjective "vague" falls on the potency, and the emphasis falls on "*energeia*" and its possessive "its". The task has its own actuality, toward which it strives. The pair puts into one phrase the structure the book has been building since § 5: a primal institution whose sense is fixed at the beginning and becomes clear only at the end, a telos "inborn" that must come to itself. Phenomenology, on this account, is the transcendental motive in act. It claims to be the *energeia* of which modern philosophy was the *dynamis*. That is a strong claim, and the passage keeps it in its place. It is one of the few in Part II that Husserl admits cannot yet be shown. He invites the reader's skepticism, "very welcome" if it is not a resolve to reject but "a free withholding of every judgement" (*eine freie Zurückhaltung jedes Urteils*, Hua VI 101). The skepticism he welcomes is an epoché.

## §30, Hua VI 116: Kant's mythical discourse

Reads [§30.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-lack-of-an-intuitively-exhibiting-method-as-the-ground-for-kants-mythical-constructions), the first paragraph under § 30.

"Mythical", in Husserl's charge against Kant, is a property of words. A discourse is mythical when its words point to something subjective that can in principle not be made intuitable, by an example or by a genuine analogy. Such words cannot be redeemed in intuition, and so they can only be believed. The passage shows why Kant's transcendental concepts fall under the charge. If one tries to redeem them in intuition, one lands in psychology, and Kant's own doctrine of inner sense forbids that landing. The charge depends on Husserl's demand that every philosophical concept be redeemable in intuition, and the passage presupposes that demand without arguing it.

> In fact Kant falls into a peculiar kind of mythical discourse, whose word-sense does point to something subjective, but to a mode of the subjective that we can in principle not make intuitable to ourselves, neither in factual examples nor through genuine analogy. If we try it with the intuitively redeemable sense to which the words point, we stand after all in the humanly personal, the psychic, psychological sphere. But then we remember the Kantian doctrine of inner sense, according to which everything that can be exhibited in the evidence of inner experience is already formed by a transcendental function, [that] of temporalization.

German:

> In der Tat gerät Kant in eine eigene Art mythischer Reden, deren Wortsinn zwar auf Subjektives verweist, aber eine Weise des Subjektiyen, die wir uns prinzipiell nicht anschaulich machen können, weder an faktischen Exempeln noch durch echte Analogie. Versuchen wir es mit dem anschaulich einlösbaren Sinn, auf den die Worte verweisen, so stehen wir doch in der menschlich personalen, der seelischen, psychologischen Sphäre. Aber da erinnern wir uns an die Kantische Lehre vom inneren Sinn, wonach alles in der Evidenz der inneren Erfahrung Aufweisbare schon durch eine transzendentale Funktion, der die Zeitigung, geformt sei.

*Note on the text.* The file carries the print's line numbers ("25", "30") inside these lines; they are dropped here. *Subjektiyen* is an OCR error for *Subjektiven* [subjective]. In *der die Zeitigung* the article is garbled; the sense, "that of temporalization", is clear enough, and the reading does not rest on the words. More seriously, two pages are lost a little later. The file has running heads 117 and 120 but none for 118–119, and the sentence "His transcendental concepts therefore have a quite peculiar" (*Seine transzendentalen Begriffe haben daher eine ganz eigentümliche*) breaks off and resumes in another ("So konnte selbstverständlich Kant…" [So Kant could of course not…]). The main commentary's § 30.1 continues that sentence ("a peculiar unclarity that for principled reasons can never be converted into clarity…"). The continuation is not in the file, and the heading of § 31 is lost with it.

The sentence locates the myth in the relation between words and their sense. Kant's discourse has a "word-sense" (*Wortsinn*) that "does point to something subjective" (*zwar auf Subjektives verweist*). The concession "does" (*zwar*) grants that the words are not empty. They refer, and they refer in the right direction. The "but" (*aber*) places the fault in what they refer to: "a mode of the subjective that we can in principle not make intuitable". The words point at something no one can be shown. The paragraph opens with the complaints of Kant's readers, about the obscurity of his "regressive method" and his transcendental-subjective "'capacities', 'functions', 'formings'" in quotation marks. The quotation marks show the words in question. They are psychological words ("capacity", "function") used of something that is not supposed to be psychological.

The second sentence turns on a commercial verb. "The intuitively redeemable sense" (*dem anschaulich einlösbaren Sinn*) is a sense that can be cashed, as a cheque or a promise is redeemed (*eingelöst*). A word's sense is a claim on intuition, and the claim is good if intuition can pay it out. Husserl tries to cash Kant's words and finds that the only intuition they can be paid in is "the humanly personal, the psychic, psychological sphere". The third sentence closes that way out. Kant's own doctrine of inner sense says that everything exhibitable in inner experience is "already formed" by a transcendental function. What inner experience shows is therefore the product of the transcendental and not the transcendental itself. The dilemma is complete. Redeemed in intuition, the transcendental becomes psychological. Kept apart from psychology, it cannot be redeemed at all. A discourse whose words cannot be redeemed but must still be accepted is what Husserl calls "mythical".

The inference rests on a premise the passage does not state, that a concept is legitimate only if its sense can be redeemed in intuition. That is Husserl's principle, not Kant's. Kant's method, as the first sentence of § 30 itself calls it, is "regressive" (Hua VI 116). It argues back from the fact of experience to the conditions that make it possible, and does not claim to exhibit those conditions in intuition. Against a regressive method, the charge that its results cannot be made intuitable is not a refutation. It is a statement that Husserl requires a method of another kind. The sentences after the passage say what kind: Kant's concepts could gain a clear sense only if "inner perception" can be given "another than the psychological sense", an "actually apodictic" one, "as of the Cartesian *ego cogito*", which would be "the source of all ultimate concepts of cognition" (Hua VI 117). The myth-charge is the negative of this programme. Kant is "mythical" because he lacked the apodictic, non-psychological intuition that Husserl claims phenomenology supplies, the "intuitively exhibiting method" whose absence the heading of § 30 names.

The passage gives Part III A its turn from history to method. § 29 asked whether the life-world could be disclosed as a realm of "anonymous" subjective phenomena. § 30 explains why Kant, who looked for the subjective accomplishments behind the world, could not disclose them: he had words for them but no intuition. In the next pages that survive, after the lost leaves, the dilemma is restated. Kant constructs a transcendental subjectivity whose hidden functions form the world of experience. He thereby falls into the difficulty that a special feature of the human soul, itself part of the world, is supposed to perform the forming of the whole world. "As soon as we distinguish this transcendental subjectivity from the soul, we fall into something unintelligibly mythical" (*geraten wir in ein unverständlich Mythisches*, Hua VI 120). The charge returns at the start of Part III B as a verdict on the whole tradition. "All of Kant's transcendental concepts, those of the I of transcendental apperception, of the various transcendental capacities, of the 'thing in itself' … are constructive concepts that in principle resist an ultimate clarification" (Hua VI 204). Two pages later the successors are said to "lose themselves" in dark metaphysics or "'mythics'" (*„Mythik“*, in quotation marks, Hua VI 206). By then the word has become a term of Husserl's vocabulary for a concept that is constructed and never redeemed.

## §32, Hua VI 121–122: surface life and the depth dimension

Reads [§32.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-possibility-of-a-hidden-truth-in-kants-transcendental-philosophy-the-problem-of-a-new-dimension-the-antagonism-between-surface-life-and-depth-life), the first paragraph under § 32.

§ 32 introduces the transcendental as a "new dimension" of life, hidden beneath the surface on which all natural life, practical and scientific, goes on. The image is borrowed from Helmholtz: beings living on a surface, who have no inkling of the depth of which their world is a projection. The understanding that the passage carries is that the surface includes reflection on oneself. The depth is not the inner life as against the outer world. Everything that natural reflection can find, the psychic life included, lies on the surface. The image also fits Husserl's problem better than his solution, and the passage shows where it stops fitting.

> This schema of a possible clarification of the problem of objective science reminds us of the well-known Helmholtzian image of the surface-beings who have no inkling of the depth dimension in which their surface world is a mere projection. Everything that can become conscious to human beings, to scientists as to all others, in their natural world-life, experiencing, cognizing, practically planning, acting, as a field of objects of the outer world, as the ends related to them, as means, as processes of action, as final results, as also, on the other hand, in self-reflection, as the mental life functioning in all this — all this remains on the "surface", which is yet only, though unnoticeably, the surface of an infinitely richer depth dimension.

German:

> Dieses Schema einer möglichen Aufklärung des Problems der objektiven Wissenschaft erinnert uns an das bekannte Helmholtz'sche Bild von den Flächenwesen, die von der Tiefendimension, in der ihre Flächenwelt eine bloße Projektion ist, keine Ahnung haben. Alles, was den Menschen, den Wissenschaftlern wie allen sonst, in ihrem natürlichen Weltleben bewußt werden kann, erfahrend, erkennend, praktisch vorhabend, handelnd, als ein Feld außenweltlicher Gegenstände, als die auf sie bezogenen Zwecke, als Mittel, als Prozesse der Handlung, als Endergebnisse, wie andererseits auch, in der Selbstbesinnung, als das dabei fungierende geistige Leben — all das verbleibt in der ,,Fläche", die doch nur, obschon unmerklich, Fläche einer unendlich reichen-ren Tiefendimension ist.

*Note on the text.* *reichen-ren* is a conversion error for *reicheren* [richer]. The corpus has no text by Helmholtz, so the image is discussed only as Husserl gives it.

The image is introduced as a reminder, not as an argument. The "schema of a possible clarification" (*Schema einer möglichen Aufklärung*) "reminds us of" (*erinnert uns an*) Helmholtz's image. Both nouns are modest: a schema, of a possible clarification. The paragraphs before the passage state the schema as a conditional. "Should a truth nevertheless dwell in the Kantian theory, as is in fact the case, this would only be possible" if the transcendental functions belonged to "a dimension of living spirituality" that "had to remain hidden" from humanity and from the scientists of millennia, but can be made accessible by "a method of disclosure appropriate to it" (Hua VI 121). The conditional carries an assertion inside it, "as is in fact the case" (*wie es in der Tat der Fall ist*). The truth of Kant's theory is asserted in a parenthesis, and the hypothesis of the depth dimension is what that assertion requires. After § 30 has called Kant's concepts mythical (row 15), § 32 says what would have to be true if something in them is not. The image gives the requirement a shape.

The long second sentence lists what lies on the surface, and the list is built to leave nothing out. It names the modes of consciousness (experiencing, cognizing, planning, acting), then their objects (the field of outer objects, ends, means, processes, results), and then, "on the other hand, in self-reflection, the mental life functioning in all this" (*in der Selbstbesinnung, als das dabei fungierende geistige Leben*). The last item decides how the image is to be read. If the depth were the inner life, self-reflection would reach it. Husserl puts self-reflection, and the functioning life it finds, on the surface. The dash and "all this remains" (*all das verbleibt*) gather the whole list into one surface. The surface is not the outer world. It is everything natural world-life can become conscious of, its own psychic life included. "Surface" is in quotation marks, as a term of the image, and the clause that ends the sentence gives it the sense of the image: "the surface of an infinitely richer depth dimension", unnoticeably (*obschon unmerklich*). Surface-beings do not notice that they live on a surface, because a surface is all their world is.

The paragraph after the passage draws the consequence and names what would be the obvious misunderstanding. "It is just not the case that we are dealing with a mere turn of the gaze into a sphere hitherto merely unnoticed, but accessible without further ado to theoretical experience." Everything experienceable in that way "is object and field of possible positive knowledge, it lies in the 'surface', in the world of actual and possible experience, experience in the natural sense of the word" (Hua VI 122). Psychology, however introspective, is a surface science. The heading's pair, "surface life" and "depth life" (*„Flächenleben“* and *„Tiefenleben“*, both in quotation marks), becomes at the end of the paragraph the antagonism between the "patent" surface life and the "latent" depth life (*dem „patenten“ Flächenleben und dem „latenten“ Tiefenleben*). The pair "latent"/"patent" recalls the "latent" and "manifest" reason of § 6 (row 5). The depth is the latent life that has to become manifest.

The image fits the problem better than the solution, and this is the passage's weak point. As Husserl describes them, the surface-beings "have no inkling" (*keine Ahnung*) of the depth, and their world is "a mere projection" of it. Nothing in the image, as given, lets them reach the depth by experience. A being bound to the surface could at most infer the depth from oddities in its world. That is the position of Kant's regressive method, which argues back from experience to conditions it cannot exhibit. Husserl claims more than the image offers: the depth can be made accessible "as a realm of experiencing and theoretical evidence" (*als ein Reich erfahrender und theoretischer Evidenz*, Hua VI 121). The image explains why the dimension stayed hidden. It cannot explain how it could be entered. For that the book needs the epoché, which § 35 introduces with the image of a "religious conversion" (row 17). The image of a geometric dimension gives way, when entry is at issue, to the image of a change in the person.

The image returns once, with a stronger verb. At the end of § 33 Husserl asks whether the problem of the life-world, which first looks like a special topic in the theory of science, does not open "that 'third dimension'" (*jene „dritte Dimension”*), "called in advance to devour (*verschlingen*) the *whole* theme of objective science (like all other themes in the 'surface')" (Hua VI 126). The depth dimension has become the third dimension of the image, and its relation to the surface is no longer projection but devouring. § 53 will use the same verb for the paradox of subjectivity: the subject-part of the world "devours, so to speak, the whole world and thereby itself" (row 22). The surface-and-depth image of § 32 thus leads directly to the book's central paradox. A depth of which everything in the world is the surface will absorb the subject who lives on that surface too.

## §35, Hua VI 140: from vocation to conversion

Reads [§35.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#analytic-of-the-transcendental-epoch-the-first-step-the-epoch-from-objective-science), the third paragraph under § 35.

The epoché gets two descriptions in this paragraph, and Husserl keeps them apart. In how it is performed, it is like a vocation. It has its working hours, it alternates with other interests, and it leaves the person's standing interests as they were. In what it may do to the person who performs it, it is like a conversion: a complete transformation of the person. The first description is asserted. The second is put forward as something that "perhaps" will "show itself". What joins them is one German word family. The vocation (*Beruf*) of the first half becomes, in the second, what the epoché "is called" (*berufen ist*) to bring about. The apparatus shows that this joining word, and most of the hedging around it, belong to a revision of the text.

> There are good reasons why I have so sharply stressed the vocation-like character of the attitude of the "phenomenologist" too. It is a first point in describing the epoché in question here that it is a habitual epoché of performance, which has its times in which it takes effect in work, while other times are devoted to some other interests of work or play; and above all, that the exclusion in performance changes nothing in the interest that goes on in the personal subjectivity and goes on being valid — as its habitual directedness to the aims that remain to it as its validities — and that for that very reason it can be actualized again and again, in this identical sense, at another time. In further consequence, however, this by no means says that the life-world epoché — to which still further significant moments belong, as we shall show — means no more for human existence, practically-"existentially", than the vocational epoché of the shoemaker, and that it comes basically to the same whether one is a shoemaker or a phenomenologist, but also whether one is a phenomenologist or a positive scientist. Perhaps it will even show itself that the total phenomenological attitude and the epoché belonging to it is at first essentially called to effect a complete personal transformation, which would be comparable at first with a religious conversion, but which beyond that harbours the significance of the greatest existential transformation that is set as a task for humanity as humanity.

German:

> Es hat gute Gründe, warum ich das Berufsartige auch der Einstellung des „Phänomenologen“ so scharf hervorgehoben habe. Es ist ein Erstes der Beschreibung der hier fraglichen Epoché, daß sie eine habituelle Vollzugsepoché ist, die ihre Zeiten hat, in denen sie sich in Arbeit auswirkt, während andere Zeiten irgendwelchen anderen Arbeits- oder Spielinteressen gewidmet sind; und vor allem, daß die Vollzugsaussch Ausschaltung an dem in der personalen Subjektivität fortwerdenden und fortgeltenden Interesse — als ihrem habituellen Ausgerichtetsein auf die ihr als ihre Geltungen verbleibenden Ziele — nichts ändert, und eben darum in diesem identischen Sinne in anderer Zeit immer wieder aktualisiert werden kann. In weiterer Folge besagt das aber keineswegs, daß die lebensweltliche Epoché — zu welcher noch weitere bedeutsame Momente gehören, wie wir zeigen werden — für das menschliche Dasein praktisch-,„existenziell“ nicht mehr bedeutet wie die Berufsepoché des Schusters und daß es im Grunde gleichkommt, ob man Schuster oder Phänomenologe ist, aber auch, ob man Phänomenologe oder positiver Wissenschaftler ist. Vielleicht wird es sich sogar zeigen, daß die totale phänomenologische Einstellung und die ihr zugehörige Epoché zunächst wesenmäßig eine völlige personale Wandlung zu erwirken berufen ist, die zu vergleichen wäre zunächst mit einer religiösen Umkehrung, die aber darüber hinaus die Bedeutung der größten existenziellen Wandlung in sich birgt, die der Menschheit als Menschheit aufgegeben ist.

*Note on the text.* The file carries the print's line numbers ("10" to "30") inside the paragraph; they are dropped here. *Vollzugsaussch Ausschaltung* is a doubled, broken word for "exclusion in performance" (*Vollzugsausschaltung*). *fortwerdenden* is probably an OCR error for *fortwährenden* [enduring], *wesenmäßig* is a misspelling of *wesensmäßig* [essentially], and the punctuation of *praktisch-,„existenziell“* is garbled (the apparatus quotes the earlier text as *praktisch „existenziell“*). The reading rests on none of these words. More seriously, a page is partly lost just before the passage. At src L1988 the § 35 opening breaks off after "the whole of the objective theoretical interests" and resumes inside another sentence ("der die wiederum selbst ihren einzigen reellen Wert in Nützlichkeiten des Lebens haben" [which in turn themselves have their only real value in usefulness for life]). The apparatus places the vocational comparison itself ("Im speziellen Sinne … Berufszeiten" [In the special sense … vocational times]) at Hua VI 139, lines 18–27, and no "vocational time" survives in § 35. The running head "140" in the file therefore stands too early: the § 35 opening is on p. 139, and the passage is p. 140, lines 9–33, as its margin numbers and the apparatus agree.

The word family carries the argument from one half of the paragraph to the other. The first sentence names "the vocation-like" (*das Berufsartige*) of the phenomenologist's attitude, and the third names the "vocational epoché of the shoemaker" (*Berufsepoché des Schusters*). In the fourth, the attitude "is called" (*berufen ist*) to effect a transformation. *Beruf* means an occupation and also a calling, and the paragraph moves from the first sense to the second without changing the stem. In the first half, vocation is a matter of hours. The epoché "has its times" (*ihre Zeiten*), and other times belong to work or play. In the second half, the same word names a destination. The joining sentence is a negation with a narrow scope. "This by no means says" (*besagt das aber keineswegs*) denies only an inference: that because the epoché is performed like a job, it means no more than a job. The denial clears room for the conversion but does not assert it.

The assertion is hedged four times. "Perhaps" (*vielleicht*), "it will even show itself" (*wird es sich sogar zeigen*), "would be comparable" (*zu vergleichen wäre*, subjunctive), and "at first" (*zunächst*), which occurs twice. The doubled "at first" matters most. The transformation is "at first" personal, and comparable "at first" with a religious conversion, "but beyond that" (*aber darüber hinaus*) it is the greatest existential transformation "set as a task" (*aufgegeben*) for humanity. Religion supplies the first analogue, and the clause then passes beyond it. The conversion of one person is a step toward a task of humanity, the vocabulary of task that § 15 gave to final institution (row 11). The noun itself, "conversion" or literally "turning around" (*Umkehrung*), is not religious elsewhere in the main text. § 9b uses it for "a reversal of the direction of the gaze" (*eine Umkehrung der Blickrichtung*, Hua VI 27) toward the origin of knowledge, and § 57 for "the complete reversal of the natural posture of life, into an 'unnatural' one" (*Die völlige Umkehrung der natürlichen Lebenshaltung*, Hua VI 204). Only here is the turning qualified as religious, and only as a comparison. § 39, where the method is laid out, uses other words: "total change" (*totale Änderung*, in its heading) and "total re-setting" (*totale Umstellung*, Hua VI 151), the language of attitudes. The key-passage list's gloss, that § 39 calls for a "total reversal", gives § 39's *Umstellung* the sense of § 57's *Umkehrung*. Husserl borrows the religious image once and goes on in the vocabulary of method. The word "existential" undergoes a small change inside the paragraph. Its first use, "practically-'existentially'", stands in distancing quotation marks, as a word of the day. By the final clause, "the greatest existential transformation", it has lost them, and Husserl speaks it in his own voice.

The apparatus shows how much of this belongs to a revision. It gives the earlier text of lines 20–33: "Perhaps it will show itself that the total phenomenological epoché is at first an existential transformation, analogous to a religious conversion, but beyond that harbours the significance of the greatest existential transformation that is set as a task for humanity as humanity" (*Vielleicht wird es sich zeigen, daß die totale phänomenologische Epoché zunächst eine existenzielle Wandlung ist, analog einer religiösen Umkehrung, aber darüber hinaus …*). The earlier text said the epoché *is* a transformation. The printed text says the attitude is "called to effect" one. Identity becomes task, and the verb that makes it a task is *berufen*, which the earlier text lacks. "Analogous" becomes the subjunctive "would be comparable", and "even" and "essentially" are added. The earlier text called the individual's change "existential" as well as humanity's. The printed text calls the individual's change "personal" and keeps "existential" for humanity's task, so that the two levels of the sentence now have two words. The shoemaker comparison is extended to the positive scientist, and the vocational lines on p. 139 are marked as an insertion. The vocational frame and the conversion that exceeds it were both sharpened in revision. The printed wording is marked "E. u. V. von F.", insertion and improvement "by F.". The key to the main text's sigla survives only as a displaced footnote in the apparatus, and it reads "F." as Eugen Fink ("Stammen die Anmerkungen von Eugen Fink, so wird das durch ein F. vermerkt" [If the notes come from Eugen Fink, this is marked by an F.]). The revision is therefore Fink's, made in the typescript of §§ 28–71, which is his transcription. It cannot say whose hand made it.

The passage is a defence before it is a promise. The lines just before it, after the seam, turn on "careless readers and hearers, who in the end hear only what they want to hear" (*flüchtiger Leser und Hörer, die schließlich nur hören, was sie hören wollen*), and on "the few for whom one speaks" (*Die Wenigen, für die man spricht*), who will wait to see where the way leads. The suspicion they are warned against is that the epoché treats the sciences by their use for life. "There are good reasons" answers it. The vocational comparison was meant to show that the epoché suspends without destroying: the interests stay valid and the work can resume "in this identical sense". The conversion clause stops the comparison from being taken as the whole. § 36 then asks how the life-world can become the theme of a science, so the conversion remains a prospect while the method takes its first step.

When the comparison returns, the religion has gone. The end of § 40 recalls "what we said of the earlier epoché, comparing it with vocational attitudes": that "in the 'vocational time'" (*in der „Berufszeit“*, in quotation marks) it sets all other interests "out of play" but does not give up their mode of being as ours. It also recalls "what was said as a protest against a devaluing equation with other vocations, and of the possibility of the radical change of the whole of humanity through this epoché, which reaches into its philosophical depths" (*von der Möglichkeit der radikalen Änderung des gesamten Menschentums*, Hua VI 154; the sentence opens with a garble, *daß sie wir*, before the quoted words). The conversion of a person has become a "possibility" for humanity, and the change is now *Änderung*, the word of the § 39 heading. The 1898 footnote of § 46 speaks the same way of "a total re-setting of the whole natural manner of life" (*einer totalen Umstellung der ganzen natürlichen Weise des Lebens*, Hua VI 170 n.).

The Vienna lecture of 1935, printed in the same volume, shows what the religious comparison reverses. There, too, the theoretical attitude is "again a vocational attitude" (*obzwar wiederum eine Berufseinstellung*), with periodic "vocational temporalities (the office hours of the civil servant, etc.)" (*Berufszeitlichkeiten (den Dienststunden des Beamten etc.)*), and it rests on "a voluntary epoché from all natural … praxis" (Hua VI 329). But the religious-mythical attitude is the universal attitude that is still *practical*, and the "theoretical" attitude of wonder "stands out sharply" from it (Hua VI 331). In Vienna, religion is what theory turns away from. In § 35 it is the nearest image for what the transcendental epoché does to the theorist. The vocational half of the paragraph comes from the Vienna lecture. The conversion half claims that the phenomenological epoché is a vocation of a different order from the Greek one: a transformation of the person, and then of humanity. The *Krisis* asserts only the first half and offers the second as a hope.

## §36, Hua VI 143–144: the a priori that is "in itself earlier"

Reads [§36.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#how-can-the-life-world-after-the-epoch-from-the-objective-sciences-become-the-theme-of-a-science-principled-separation-between-the-objective-logical-a-priori-and-the-a-priori-of-the-life-world), the third paragraph under § 36.

The a priori, in § 36, has a ground. Husserl's earlier vocabulary set the a priori against the empirical, as what holds before and independently of experience. Here the a priori of logic and mathematics, "the a priori in the usual sense", is a level. It is "grounded in" another a priori, one that is "in itself earlier": the invariant structure of the pre-scientific life-world. The order of the in-itself is turned round. Objectivism called the world of exact science the world "in itself", and the life-world its subjective appearance. § 36 says that what is earlier in itself is the a priori of the life-world, and that logic, which takes itself to be first, is late. A logic that does not know this is not false. It is "naive".

> These bring us, moreover, the fundamental insight that the universal a priori of the objective-logical level — that of the mathematical sciences and of all other sciences a priori in the usual sense — is grounded in a universal a priori that is in itself earlier, precisely that of the pure life-world. Only by recourse to this a priori, to be unfolded in an a priori science of its own, can our a priori sciences, the objective-logical ones, gain a really radical, a seriously scientific grounding, which in this state of affairs they unconditionally demand.
>
> We can also put it this way: the supposedly completely self-standing logic which the modern logicians — even under the title of a truly scientific philosophy — believe they can develop, namely as the universal a priori fundamental science for all objective sciences, is nothing other than a naivety. Its evidence lacks the scientific grounding from the universal life-worldly a priori, which it constantly, always, presupposes in the form of self-evidences never scientifically formulated in universal terms, never brought to the generality of an essential science. Only when this radical fundamental science once exists can that logic itself become a science. Before that it floats groundless in the air and is, as it has been until now, so very naive that it has not even become aware of the task that adheres to every objective logic, every a priori science in the usual sense: namely to investigate how it itself is to be grounded, thus no longer "logically", but by leading back to the universal pre-logical a priori, from which everything logical, the whole edifice of an objective theory in all its methodological forms, shows its legitimate sense, and by which, therefore, all logic itself must first be normed.

German:

> Dieselben bringen uns zudem die fundamentale Einsicht, daß das universale Apriori der objektiv-logischen Stufe — das der mathematischen und aller sonstigen im gewöhnlichen Sinne apriorischen Wissenschaften — in einem an sich früheren universalen Apriori, eben dem der reinen Lebenswelt, gründet. Nur durch Rekurs auf dieses, in einer eigenen apriorischen Wissenschaft entfaltende Apriori können unsere apriorischen Wissenschaften, die objektiv-logischen, eine wirklich radikale, eine ernstlich wissenschaftliche Begründung gewinnen, die sie bei dieser Sachlage unbedingt fordern.
>
> Wir können dafür auch sagen: die vermeintlich völlig eigenständige Logik, welche die modernen Logiker — sogar unter dem Titel einer wahrhaft wissenschaftlichen Philosophie — glauben ausbilden zu können, nämlich als die universale apriorische Fundamentalwissenschaft für alle objektiven Wissenschaften, ist nichts anderes als eine Naivität. Ihre Evidenz entbehrt der wissenschaftlichen Begründung aus dem universalen lebensweltlichen Apriori, das sie beständig, in Form wissenschaftlich nie universal formulierter, nie auf wesenswissenschaftliche Allgemeinheit gebrachter Selbstverständlichkeiten, immerzu voraussetzt. Erst wenn einmal diese radikale Grundwissenschaft da ist, kann jene Logik selbst zur Wissenschaft werden. Vorher schwebt sie grundlos in der Luft und ist, wie bisher, so sehr naiv, daß sie nicht einmal der Aufgabe innegeworden ist, welche jeder objektiven Logik, jeder apriorischen Wissenschaft gewöhnlichen Sinnes anhaftet: nämlich zu erforschen, wie sie selbst zu begründen sei, also nicht mehr ,,logisch", sondern durch Rückleitung auf das universale vor-logische Apriori, aus dem alles Logische, der Gesamtbau einer objektiven Theorie, nach allen ihren methodologischen Formen, seinen rechtmäßigen Sinn ausweist, durch welchen also alle Logik selbst erst zu normieren ist.

*Note on the text.* The passage begins just after a seam. The sentence before it in the file reads "Das Katoriale der Lebenswelt allen objektiven Wissenschaften, wenn wir sie auch als die von allen objektiv-apriorischen Wissenschaften verstehen…" [The cat(eg)orial of the life-world — all objective sciences, if we also understand them as that of all objective-a-priori sciences…]: it breaks off after "life-world" and resumes inside another sentence. "These" (*Dieselben*) therefore refers to "considerations just carried out" that are partly lost. The main commentary's sentence "There would thus be needed a systematic separation of universal structures: universal life-worldly a priori and universal 'objective' a priori" is not in the file; only the heading's "principled separation" (*Prinzipielle Scheidung*) survives. A second loss stands at the opening of § 36 (src L2000–2004), where the file runs from "so müs-" [so (we) must] into "aber das Ziel" [but the goal] and the apparatus records text of Hua VI 141 that the file lacks. *entfaltende* is probably short for *zu entfaltende* [to be unfolded], as rendered. The running heads around the passage are unreliable, so the page is given as 143–144.

The first sentence moves the phrase "in itself". In the paragraph before the seam, "in itself" belongs to the objectivist substruction and stands in quotation marks each time: a world "'in itself'", determined in "'truths in themselves'", the world that is "'objective in itself'" (*„an sich“*, *„Wahrheiten an sich“*, *„an sich objektiv“*, Hua VI 142). In the passage the phrase returns without quotation marks, in Husserl's own voice, and attached to the order of grounding: an a priori "in itself earlier" (*an sich früheren*). The in-itself is withdrawn from the world of exact science and given to the life-world. § 14 had put the same reversal at the transcendental level: "not the being of the world in its unquestioned self-evidence is what is first in itself … what is first in itself is subjectivity" (*das an sich Erste ist die Subjektivität*, Hua VI 70; row 10). § 36 carries out one stage of it. The life-world a priori is earlier than logic, and subjectivity, on § 14's account, will prove earlier still.

The wording makes the a priori a thing with levels and a ground. "The objective-logical level" (*Stufe*) is the language of founding, and the verb "is grounded" (*gründet*) is applied to what was supposed to need no ground. The two qualifications of the logical a priori, "in the usual sense" (*im gewöhnlichen Sinne*, twice in the passage), concede the old usage and mark it as one usage among two. The grounding is, in the second paragraph, "no longer 'logical'" (*nicht mehr ,,logisch"*). The quotation marks keep the word at a distance, because a logical grounding of logic would assume what it is meant to ground. Its place is taken by "leading back" (*Rückleitung*) to the "pre-logical" (*vor-logische*) a priori. The hyphen marks the prefix as temporal and ordinal. The pre-logical is not illogical. It is what logic comes after. § 34 used the unhyphenated adjective for the same relation: the life-world "constantly functions as subsoil", and its "manifold pre-logical validities" are "grounding for the logical, the theoretical truths" (*wie ihre mannigfachen vorlogischen Geltungen begründende sind für die logischen*, Hua VI 128).

The charge against logic rests on a distinction between two words for what is evident. Logic has "evidence" (*Evidenz*). What it lacks is grounding, because its evidence presupposes "self-evidences" (*Selbstverständlichkeiten*), things that go without saying, "never scientifically formulated in universal terms". *Selbstverständlichkeit* is the book's word for what is accepted without being examined, the word § 14 attached to the being of the world. Logic is "naive" in that exact sense. It takes for granted the life-world structures from which its concepts draw their sense. The image of floating "groundless in the air" (*schwebt grundlos in der Luft*) puts the point in one picture: an edifice (*Gesamtbau*) with no foundation under it. The target is unnamed. "The modern logicians" (*die modernen Logiker*) work "even under the title of a truly scientific philosophy", a title that the "even" (*sogar*) treats as an aggravation. The commentary's "logisticians" sharpens the source, which says only "logicians".

The final clause claims more than the paragraph shows. Leading logic back to the pre-logical a priori lets "everything logical" show "its legitimate sense" (*seinen rechtmäßigen Sinn ausweist*). That is a claim about sense, about the origin of logical concepts in the structures of the experienced world. The clause then concludes "therefore" (*also*) that all logic must first "be normed" (*zu normieren*) by that a priori. Norming is a claim about validity. Husserl moves from "logic's concepts have their sense from the life-world" to "logic's laws are subject to life-world norms" in one relative clause. The first is what § 9 showed for geometry, whose idealizations arise from the practice of measuring (rows 6–7). The second would need an argument that the pre-logical a priori can correct logic, and none is given here.

The passage gives the life-world science of § 34 its object. After the first epoché, the § 36 opening says, we are "in embarrassment" (*in Verlegenheit*): the goal of a truth "for everyone" would lead back to objective science, which the epoché has forbidden. The embarrassment "vanishes" once the life-world, "in all its relativities", is seen to have a "general structure" that "is not itself relative" (*allgemeine Struktur*, italic, Hua VI 142). The passage then states the rank of that structure: it is the ground of the logical a priori. § 37 begins on the "formally most general structures" of the life-world, thing and world, and row 19 reads its opening. § 51 adds a qualification that changes how the passage reads. The life-world has a "fixed typology" graspable as "pure a priori", and an "ontology of the life-world" could have been worked out "without any transcendental interest, thus in the 'natural attitude'" (Hua VI 176). The grounding of logic in § 36 is therefore not yet transcendental. It is a relation between two ontologies, the life-world's and logic's, and it can be stated before the epoché reaches subjectivity.

*Experience and Judgement* makes the same claim about logic in a milder voice. Its § 10 says that the realm of doxa, of pre-predicative experience, is "not one of evidences of lesser rank than that of episteme" but "the realm of ultimate originality" to which exact knowledge refers back. "In this there is to lie no devaluation of exact knowledge, and just as little of the apodictic evidences of the logician himself" (*Darin soll keinerlei Abwertung der exakten Erkenntnis liegen und ebensowenig eine solche der apodiktischen Evidenzen des Logikers selbst*). It means only an illumination of the way to the higher evidences "and of the hidden presuppositions on which they rest" (EU 43–44). The structure is that of § 36: an evidence on top, an unexamined presupposition beneath. But *Experience and Judgement* protects the logician's evidence, and the *Krisis* calls the logician's self-understanding "nothing other than a naivety". The difference lies in what the logician claims. Logic as a body of evident laws is protected in both texts. Logic that claims to be the self-standing "fundamental science" of all science is the naivety, because it has mistaken a late level for the first.

## §37, Hua VI 146: the world's uniqueness

Reads [§37.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-formally-most-general-structures-of-the-life-world-thing-and-world-on-the-one-side-thing-consciousness-on-the-other), the third paragraph under § 37.

The world, in § 37, is unique in a sense that has nothing to do with number. That there is exactly one world would be a count, and a count presupposes things to count. Husserl's claim is that counting does not apply to the world at all: things are one, or many, only as things "in" a horizon that is itself neither. The world is therefore no object, not even the largest, and the consciousness of it is no thing-consciousness widened to the whole. It is a different kind of consciousness, horizon-consciousness, which never occurs without the consciousness of some object and never becomes one. The paragraph states this as a grammatical fact, the senselessness of a plural, and draws the difference of consciousness from it.

> There is, however, a fundamental difference in the manner of world-consciousness and of thing-consciousness, object-consciousness (in a broadest but purely life-worldly sense), while on the other hand *the one and the other form an inseparable unity.* Things, objects (always understood purely life-worldly), are "given" as valid for us in each case (in some modes or other of certainty of being), but in principle only in such a way that they are conscious as things, as *objects in the world-horizon*. Each is something, "something from" the world, the world constantly conscious to us as horizon. This horizon, on the other hand, is conscious only as horizon for existing objects, and cannot be actual without objects that are conscious in particular. Each has its possible modes of variation of validity, of the modalization of certainty of being. On the other hand, world does not exist as an existent does, as an object does, but exists in a *uniqueness* for which the plural is senseless. Every plural, and every singular lifted out of it, presupposes the world-horizon. This difference between the manner of being of an object in the world and that of the world itself obviously prescribes to the two the fundamentally different correlative manners of consciousness.

German:

> Es besteht aber ein grundsätzlicher Unterschied in der Weise des Weltbewußtseins und des Dingbewußtseins, des Objektbewußtseins (in einem weitesten, aber rein lebensweltlichen Sinne), während andererseits *eines und das andere eine untrennbare Einheit bilden.* Dinge, Objekte (immer rein lebensweltlich verstanden) sind ,,gegeben“ als für uns jeweils (in irgendwelchen Modis der Seinsgewißheit) geltende, aber prinzipiell nur so, daß sie bewußt sind als Dinge, als *Objekte im Welthorizont*. Jedes ist etwas, ,,etwas aus“ der Welt, der uns ständig als Horizont bewußten. Dieser Horizont ist andererseits nur als Horizont für seiende Objekte bewußt und kann ohne sonderbewußte Objekte nicht aktuell sein. Jedes hat seine möglichen Abwandlungsmodi des Geltens, der Modalisierung der Seinsgewißheit. Ackererseits ist Welt nicht seiend wie ein Seiendes, wie ein Objekt, sondern seiend in einer *Einzigkeit*, für die der Plural sinnlos ist. Jeder Plural und aus ihm herausgehobene Singular setzt den Welthorizont voraus. Diese Differenz der Seinsweise eines Objektes in der Welt und der Welt selbst schreibt offenbar beiden die grundverschiedenen korrelativen Bewußtseinsweisen vor.

*Note on the text.* "Ackererseits" is an OCR error for *Andererseits* [on the other hand], rendered so. The passage survives, but what led up to it does not. The file runs from the last paragraph of § 36 ("…um nun die große Aufgabe einer reinen Wesenslehre von der Lebenswelt" […in order now (to take up?) the great task of a pure essential doctrine of the life-world]) straight into "in der Art, daß jeweils Einzeldinge gegeben sind" [in such a way that individual things are given in each case], which ends a sentence on the next page. The § 37 heading and its opening, which the table of contents puts on Hua VI 145, are lost with that page. The apparatus confirms that the opening spoke of the world given to "the waking, the always somehow practically interested subjects" "as universal field of all actual and possible praxis", with "as horizon" noted in the margin (145,28–31), and of an "essential doctrine of these *onta*" (145,9f.). The main commentary's §37.1–2 therefore cannot be checked beyond those fragments, and nothing before "There is, however" is quoted here. The apparatus also records that "object-consciousness (in a broadest but purely life-worldly sense)" stands where an earlier wording had the bare "object-consciousness" (146,3f.).

The key sentence works through the article. The world does not exist "as an existent does, as an object does" (*wie ein Seiendes, wie ein Objekt*): what is denied is the indefinite article, the grammatical mark of something that is one of a kind and could be one of several. The participle is kept: the world is "existing" (*seiend*), so the sentence does not deny the world being. It denies it the form of being that admits a count. The next sentence, which the commentary reports without drawing its consequence, makes the denial stronger. "Every plural, and every singular lifted out of it, presupposes the world-horizon." The singular, too, is derived. An object is "one" only as lifted out of a possible many, and that many is a many within the horizon. The world's uniqueness is therefore not the singular. It is the condition on which there is a singular at all, and the word for it, *Einzigkeit*, is set in italic to keep it apart from oneness. In these pages Husserl often writes "world" without an article (*ist Welt nicht seiend*; "Welt ist das Universalfeld" in § 38), so the bare noun is habit and cannot be weighed, though it suits the claim.

The paragraph keeps two words apart that the English "one" would merge. The world-consciousness and the thing-consciousness form "an inseparable unity" (*eine untrennbare Einheit*, italic); the world exists in a "uniqueness" (*Einzigkeit*). Unity names the bond between two kinds of consciousness, neither of which is actual without the other. Uniqueness names the world's manner of being. The preposition carries the relation between object and world: each object is "'something from' the world" (*„etwas aus“ der Welt*), in quotation marks. *Aus* is the preposition of drawing out, the same movement as the singular "lifted out" (*herausgehobene*) of the plural. The world is the stock from which objects are taken, and it is never itself one of the things taken.

The sentence the commentary drops gives the contrast its content. "Each has its possible modes of variation of validity, of the modalization of certainty of being" (*Modalisierung der Seinsgewißheit*). Objects can become doubtful, merely possible, null; "on the other hand" the world is not as an object is. The paragraph sets uniqueness against modalization but does not say that world-certainty cannot be modalized. A supplementary text in the same volume says it outright, against Descartes. Doubting whether one is really sitting by the fire is no doubt about "the being of the world as such": it is "the peculiarity of world-certainty that it remains unbroken in its manner, even apodictically, as world-certainty, despite all possible and actual doubts about situations", although the individual realities are open to doubt "and often enough undergo the change of validity from being into illusion" (*die Geltungswandlung von Sein in Schein*, Beilage VI, Hua VI 407). § 37 supplies the reason the supplement only asserts. Modalization is something that happens to one object among others, against the background of the rest. The world is not among others, so the operation has nowhere to take hold.

The qualifiers keep the claim on the near side of the epoché. Objects are "always understood purely life-worldly", and object-consciousness is meant in a "purely life-worldly" sense. These are additions to the earlier wording, and they mark the distinction as a description of the life-world, not a thesis of formal ontology or of transcendental philosophy. The title of § 37 calls it one of the "formally most general structures of the life-world" (table of contents). This is the "general structure" that § 36 found "not itself relative" (row 18), and it can be stated before any reduction. Its last sentence points beyond it. The difference in manner of being "prescribes" a difference in "correlative manners of consciousness", and correlation is the next stage of the book. § 38 calls everything said so far "only the formal frame" (*erst der formale Rahmen*, Hua VI 147) for two ways of being awake to the world. The first lives "straight on" towards objects, and so cannot meet the world, which is never an object. The second turns to how "the unitary, universal validity world" comes about (*die einheitliche, universale Geltung Welt*). § 41 names what the turn discovers: the correlation "of world itself and world-consciousness" (Hua VI 154). The uniqueness of § 37 is what makes world-consciousness a theme of its own and not a sum of thing-perceptions.

The noun *Einzigkeit* returns, and changes its subject. In § 54 it is the ego's. The ego reached in the epoché is "not *an* I" (*nicht e i n Ich*, spaced) that still has its You and We, and the error of the first approach was to skip "the primal I, the ego of my epoché, which can never lose its uniqueness and personal undeclinability" (*seine Einzigkeit und persönliche Undeklinierbarkeit*, Hua VI 188). The grammar is the same as in § 37: no indefinite article, no plural, and a form that grammar would decline is refused declension. But § 54 adds a clause that § 37 does not have. The ego "only apparently" contradicts its uniqueness when, "by a constitutive accomplishment of its own", it "makes itself transcendentally declinable" and constitutes the intersubjectivity in which it counts itself "a merely privileged member" (Hua VI 188). The ego's uniqueness admits a plural that it produces. The world's admits none: of the world the plural is "senseless", not yet to be constituted. A manuscript of 1932 marks the same difference with one adverb. The primordial monad is the "'monad' before the world of monads, a monad that has as yet no plural" (*Monade, die noch keinen Plural hat*, Hua XV 591, Beilage XLII). "As yet" (*noch*) is what § 37 withholds from the world. § 62 gives the word a third subject. The I, unlike a body, has no individuality from causality; as an *ens per se* "it has in advance in itself its uniqueness", and space and time are no principles of individuation for it (*Als das hat es vorweg in sich seine Einzigkeit*, Hua VI 222). § 71 ends the line in the "absolutely unique *ego*" of the psychologist's reduction (*absolut einzige e g o*, emphasized, Hua VI 260).

The passage thus fixes the form of uniqueness before the book has a subject to give it to. In § 37 it belongs to the world as horizon, the one thing that cannot be counted because every count takes place inside it. By § 54 the same word belongs to the ego for which the world is a phenomenon, and there it no longer excludes the plural absolutely. It precedes the plural and produces it. The paradox of § 53, that the subject for the world is also an object in it, is the question how something "from" the world can be what the world is from. § 37 does not raise it, but its grammar is the grammar in which § 54 answers.

## §48 note (commentary §46.2), Hua VI 169–170: the correlation a priori and its two breakthroughs

Reads [§46.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-universal-correlation-a-priori), the second paragraph under § 46. The remark it reads stands in a footnote to § 48, not in § 46.

The correlation a priori, in Husserl's footnote, is the essential law that ties every object of experience to the manifold of its modes of givenness: no object without its system of appearances, no appearance that is not the appearance "of" an object. The footnote dates its discovery to 1898, inside the work on the *Logical Investigations*, and dates the phenomenological reduction separately, "some years" after 1900/01. The understanding the note carries is that the two are different discoveries. The correlation was found first and without the reduction. The reduction to "absolute transcendental subjectivity" came later, because the correlation, once human subjectivity was drawn into it, could not keep the sense it had. The note is autobiography, and it is also a summary of the argument the rest of Part III A will run.

> The first breakthrough of this universal correlation a priori of object of experience and modes of givenness (during the working-out of my "Logical Investigations", approximately in the year 1898) shook me so deeply that since then my entire life's work has been governed by this task of a systematic working-out of this correlation a priori. The further course of the reflections of the text will make it intelligible how the inclusion of human subjectivity in the problematic of correlation necessarily had to force a radical change of sense of this whole problematic and finally had to lead to the phenomenological reduction to absolute transcendental subjectivity.
>
> The first breakthrough of the phenomenological reduction, still very much in need of clarification, came some years after the appearance of the "Logical Investigations" (1900/01); the first attempt at a systematic introduction to the new philosophy in the transcendental reduction appeared in 1913 as a fragment (*Ideas for a Pure Phenomenology and Phenomenological Philosophy*, vol. I).
>
> The contemporary philosophy of the decades since, that of the so-called phenomenological schools included, preferred to persist in the old philosophical naivety. Admittedly, the first breakthroughs of so radical a turn, a total change of attitude of the whole natural manner of life, were hard to bring to a well-motivated presentation, especially since particular reasons here, intelligible in the further course of the text, constantly invite misinterpretations through relapses into the natural attitude.

*Note on the text.* The remark is a footnote, and it belongs to § 48 ("All beings of every sense and every region as index of a subjective system of correlation", table of contents, Hua VI 168), not to § 46, which runs from Hua VI 161 to 163 and contains no such note. The main commentary puts it in § 46 and reads it as running text. The file has damaged the note's surroundings in three ways. First, its opening is split from the rest: the first sentences stand under the § 48 text at the top of Hua VI 170 (src L2379–2385), and the file then places the § 49 heading and its first lines, and only after them the rest of the note, from "dieser ganzen Problematik" on (L2391–2393). The § 49 text resumes after that ("kon-" … "kret", L2389, L2397), which shows that the interposed lines are not § 49's. Second, the file numbers both notes on the page 15 and shows one marker, after "verwirk-licht" in the sentence on the "total manifold" of modes of givenness; a second note, referring to Fink's supplement on the "unconscious", carries the same number. Which sentence the 1898 note hangs on cannot be told. Third, Hua VI 168 is lost: the file runs from the end of § 47 on p. 167 ("…tretender Menschen", L2336) into p. 169 mid-word ("forschbaren Tiefen", L2340). The § 48 heading and opening are gone with it, including the sentence the main commentary reports in §48.1 on the "first breakthrough of 'transcendental phenomenology' in the *Logical Investigations*". The apparatus keeps a fragment of that page (168,7–10: "that no conceivable human being could have had a world given in possible experience otherwise…"), but not that sentence. The "this" (*dieses*) of the note's first line therefore points back to § 48's thesis, of which only the part on p. 169 survives: "everything stands in such correlation to its own and by no means merely sensory modes of givenness" (*Alles steht in solcher Korrelation zu seinen ihm zugehörigen und keineswegs bloß sinnlichen Gegebenheitsweisen*, Hua VI 169).

The note names the terms of the correlation, and the commentary drops them. It is the correlation a priori "of object of experience and modes of givenness" (*von Erfahrungsgegenstand und Gegebenheitsweisen*). Neither term is a subject. The pairing is the one of § 46, where reflection finds that "I am immediately conscious of the existing thing" while the experience "presentation of" changes from moment to moment, "with its remarkable 'of'" (Hua VI 162). That is a correlation within experience between the thing and its appearances. No ego and no world as a whole is among its terms. The note's second sentence says what happened when one was added. "The inclusion of human subjectivity" (*die Einbeziehung der menschlichen Subjektivität*) in the problem "necessarily had to force a radical change of sense" (*notwendig … erzwingen … mußte*). The subject enters the correlation late, and when it enters it is "human" subjectivity, which then cannot stay what it is: the end of the road is "absolute transcendental subjectivity". The note thus states in a sentence the sequence of §§ 46–55. The correlation is laid out in §§ 46–51. § 53 meets the paradox of the human subject as subject for the world and object in it, and § 55 corrects the first approach "by reduction to the absolutely unique, ultimately functioning ego" (Hua VI 190–191; rows 22–23). The note's "will make it intelligible" is a promissory note on exactly those sections.

The connectives claim more than the note shows, and Husserl says as much by deferring. "Necessarily" (*notwendig*), "force" (*erzwingen*) and "had to" (*mußte*) present a stretch of biography as a chain of entailments. Whether the inclusion of the human subject "forces" the reduction, or only motivates it, is what the coming sections must argue. The note claims the outcome in the language of necessity and hands the proof to "the further course of the text". The same structure, a necessity asserted and its demonstration deferred, returns at the end of the note: the "particular reasons" for the misinterpretations are "intelligible in the further course of the text".

The noun the note turns on is "breakthrough" (*Durchbruch*), used three times, the third time in the plural. Each breakthrough is "first", and the second is "still very much in need of clarification" (*noch sehr klärungsbedürftige*). A breakthrough is a beginning that is not yet a possession, and the word lets Husserl date the origin of his work while denying that the origin was understood. It also lets him count. There were "breakthroughs" (*Durchbrüche*), not one, and the note keeps them apart by date and by object. The verb for the first is the strongest in the note: the discovery "shook me" (*erschütterte mich*). § 18 uses the noun for Descartes. "The shock (*Erschütterung*) which Descartes himself experienced at the discovery of this *ego*" announced "something truly great", the "Archimedean point" that had to come to light "through all errors and aberrations" (Hua VI 82–83; row 12). Descartes was shaken by the ego and lost it to the soul. Husserl was shaken by the correlation, which in its first form had no ego in it. In both cases the first shock is genuine and its object is not yet the right one.

That separation is what the *Krisis* adds to Husserl's earlier accounts of his beginning. The Introduction to *Ideas I* (1913) speaks of a single event: pure phenomenology is "the same that came to a first breakthrough in the 'Logical Investigations', and whose sense has opened itself to me ever more deeply and richly in the continued work of the last decade" (*dieselbe, die in den „Logischen Untersuchungen“ zu einem ersten Durchbruch kam*, Hua III/1 4–5, first edition 2; checked against the English of the Collected Works edition). A text of 1922/23 dates "'transcendental phenomenology'" itself to "a first breakthrough already around 1900" (*einer neuen, schon um 1900 zum ersten Durchbruch gekommenen philosophischen Grundwissenschaft und -methode*, Hua XXVII 95, Beilage I to the first *Kaizo* article). The *Krisis* note divides what those texts join. The *Logical Investigations* keep the correlation; the reduction moves to "some years" after them. Two later passages hold to the division. § 68 places the novelty of the *Logical Investigations* in their "subjectively directed investigations (chiefly the Fifth and Sixth…)", in which "for the first time the *cogitata qua cogitata*" come into their own (Hua VI 237). § 70 says, of the one who was drawn into the epoché "in a certain naivety", that "only four years after the completion of the 'Logical Investigations'" did he come to an "explicit, and even then still imperfect, self-consciousness of his method" (Hua VI 247). The commentary's §48.1, by its report of the lost page, would have the *Krisis* call the *Logical Investigations* the first breakthrough of "transcendental phenomenology". If the source says so, the quotation marks may be the hedge. The lost page cannot settle it.

The note ends on a charge, and the charge depends on the separation. The "so-called phenomenological schools" (*der sogenannten phänomenologischen Schulen*) "preferred to persist in the old philosophical naivety". On the note's own chronology this is precise. They took up the first breakthrough, the correlation and the descriptive work of the *Logical Investigations*, and refused the second. Husserl partly excuses them. The breakthroughs of "a total change of attitude" (*einer totalen Umstellung*) "were hard to bring to a well-motivated presentation". That is the confession the *Krisis* exists to make good: a book that gives the reduction its motivation, by a way through the history of the sciences and the life-world. The 1898 date, which the commentary takes as the anchor of the whole project, anchors only its first half. The life's work began with the correlation, and the book is written to show why the correlation could not stay where it began.

## §49, Hua VI 170–171: constitution as the only real explaining

Reads [§49.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#preliminary-concept-of-transcendental-constitution-as-original-formation-of-meaning-the-exemplary-narrowness-of-the-analyses-carried-out-indication-of-further-horizons), the first paragraph under § 49.

Constitution, in § 49, is a kind of explanation, and the only kind that deserves the name. To explain something is not to derive it from causes or laws but to lead it back to the intentional accomplishments in which it acquires its sense and its validity. Husserl calls this "original formation of meaning" (*ursprüngliche Sinnbildung*). The world explained in this way is "made intelligible" as a meaning-formation, and intelligibility is the measure of explanation. The claim takes the word "explaining" from natural science and gives it to intentional analysis, and it does so in a paragraph that names the accomplishing subject as the communalized intersubjectivity, not the single subject. Both moves are marked provisional by the heading ("preliminary concept"), and § 54 will withdraw the second.

> How far all this is to be understood (whereby the concepts "being", "modes of givenness", "syntheses" and so on are relativized again and again) can be seen from the fact that what is at issue is a many-levelled intentional total accomplishment of the subjectivity in each case, but not of the single one, rather of the whole of the intersubjectivity communalized in the accomplishing. Again and again it turns out that, beginning from what is superficially visible, the modes of appearance of the unity-forming manifolds [are] themselves in turn unities of deeper-lying manifolds, which constitute them through appearances, so that we are led back into a dark horizon, though into one that can always be uncovered by methodical inquiry back. All the levels and strata through which the syntheses that reach intentionally from subject to subject are interwoven form a universal unity of synthesis; through it the objective universe, the world, comes about as the world that is concretely and livingly given, and as it is given (and as the world pregiven for all possible praxis). In this respect we speak of the "intersubjective constitution" of the world, comprising in it the total system of the modes of givenness, however hidden, but also of the I-like modes of validity; through them, if we uncover them systematically, the world that exists for us is made intelligible, intelligible as a meaning-formation out of the elementary intentionalities. Their own being is nothing other than meaning-formation functioning together with meaning-formation, "constituting" new meaning in synthesis. And meaning is never anything other than meaning in modes of validity, thus related to I-subjects as intending and as performing validity. Intentionality is the title for the only real and genuine explaining, making-intelligible. To lead back to the intentional origins and unities of meaning-formation: that yields an intelligibility which (admittedly an ideal case), once attained, would leave no meaningful question over. But even every serious and genuine going back from a "finished being" to its intentional origins yields, with respect to the strata already uncovered and to the clarification of what is accomplished in them, an understanding that is indeed only relative, but, as far as it reaches, a real one.

German:

> Wie weit dies alles zu verstehen ist (wobei die Begriffe „Seiendes“, „Gegebenheitsweisen“, „Synthesen“ usw. sich immer wieder relativieren), ist daraus zu ersehen, daß es sich doch um eine vielstufige intentionale Gesamtleistung der jeweiligen Subjektivität handelt, aber nicht der einzelten, sondern um das Ganze der im Leisten vergemeinschafteten Intersubjektivität. Stets von neuem zeigt sich, daß, angefangen von dem oberflächlichen Sichtlichen, die Erscheinungsweisen der einheitbildenden Mannigfaltigkeiten selbst wieder Einheiten tiefer liegender Mannigfaltigkeiten, die sie durch Erscheinungen konstituieren, so daß wir in einen dunklen Horizont zurückgeleitet werden, allerdings auf einen stets durch methodische Rückfrage aufzudeckenden. Alle Stufen und Schichten, durch welche die intentional von Subjekt zu Subjekt übergreifenden Synthesen verflochten sind, bilden eine universale Einheit der Synthesis, durch sie kommt das gegenständliche Universum, die Welt, als die und so wie sie konkret lebendig die gegebene ist (und die für alle mögliche Praxis vorgegebene), zustande. Wir sprechen in dieser Hinsicht von der ,,intersubjektiven Konstitution" der Welt, darin also befassend das Gesamtsystem der noch so verborgenen Gegebenheitsweisen, aber auch der ichlichen Geltungsmodi; durch sie wird, wenn wir sie systematisch enthüllen, die für uns seiende Welt verständlich gemacht, verständlich als ein Sinngebilde aus den elementaren Intentionalitäten. Deren eigenes Sein ist nichts anderes als Sinnbildung mit Sinnbildung zusammen fungierend, in der Synthese neuen Sinn ,,konstituierend". Und Sinn ist nie anderes als Sinn in Geltungsmodis, also bezogen auf Ichsubjekte als intendierende und Geltung vollziehende. Intentionalität ist der Titel für das allein wirkliche und echte Erklären, Verständlichmachen. Auf die intentionalen Ursprünge und Einheiten der Sinnbildung zurückführen — das ergibt eine Verständlichkeit, die (was freilich ein Idealfall ist), einmal erreicht, keine sinnvolle Frage übrig ließe. Aber schon jedes ernstliche und echte Zurückgehen von einem ,,Fertig-Seienden" auf seine intentionalen Ursprünge ergibt hinsichtlich der schon aufgedeckten Schichten und der Aufklärung des darin Geleisteten ein zwar nur relatives, aber, soweit es reicht, doch ein wirkliches Verständnis.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph is interrupted in the file. After "so wie sie kon-" (src L2389) the file sets the continuation of the footnote to § 48 (row 20), and the paragraph resumes with "kret" after the head for Hua VI 171 (L2397). The two parts are joined here. "einzelten" is an OCR error for *einzelnen* [single], rendered so. The second sentence has no main verb after "Einheiten tiefer liegender Mannigfaltigkeiten"; a *sind* [are] is supplied in brackets in the English and not in the German. The heading's quotation marks around "original formation of meaning" are the file's, and match the table of contents.

The quotation marks in the paragraph sort Husserl's words into his own and those still on loan. "Constituting" stands in quotation marks (*„konstituierend“*), and so do "intersubjective constitution" and the heading's "original formation of meaning". The concept is "preliminary" (*Vorläufiger Begriff*), and the marks say that its name is not yet earned. "Explaining" (*Erklären*) stands without them. That reverses the usage of Part II. In § 11 the physicalist expects that physics will finally "'explain'" all concretions "physically-rationally", and psychology is given the same ascent from description to "ultimate theoretical 'explanation'" as biophysics (*„erklären“*, *„Erklärung“*, Hua VI 64–65). There the word is held at a distance because natural science claims it. In § 49 the distance passes to Husserl's own term, and the borrowed word is claimed outright. The apposition "explaining, making-intelligible" (*Erklären, Verständlichmachen*) fixes the sense in which it is claimed: explaining is making intelligible, and nothing else.

The paragraph defines the being of what does the explaining, and its definition is circular by design. The "elementary intentionalities" have as "their own being" nothing other than "meaning-formation functioning together with meaning-formation" (*Sinnbildung mit Sinnbildung zusammen fungierend*). Meaning is formed out of meaning, and the next sentence ties meaning to validity: "meaning is never anything other than meaning in modes of validity, thus related to I-subjects as intending and performing validity". The "I-like modes of validity" (*ichlichen Geltungsmodi*) are therefore not an addition to the modes of givenness. Without them there would be appearances but no sense, since sense is sense only as held valid by someone. This is where the correlation a priori of row 20, a correlation of object and modes of givenness, acquires its third term. The ego enters § 49 as the bearer of validity, and the title of § 50 at once orders all problems "under the titles: ego — cogito — cogitatum".

The claim is stronger than its own qualifications. Intelligibility through return to the origins would leave "no meaningful question over", but this is "admittedly an ideal case" (*was freilich ein Idealfall ist*), and every real return yields an understanding that is "only relative". The ideal is unreachable on the paragraph's own account. The sentence before shows why: every mode of appearance proves to be the unity of a deeper manifold, so that "we are led back into a dark horizon". What saves the claim is the restriction "as far as it reaches" (*soweit es reicht*). The understanding is partial in extent and "real" in kind. The comparison with natural science is therefore not one of degree. Physics is not a weaker explanation than intentional analysis. On § 55's account it is no explanation at all.

§ 55 states the thesis again, and sharpens it. "No objective science, however exact, explains or can ever explain anything in earnest. Deducing is not explaining." Predicting, or knowing the objective structure of physical or chemical bodies and predicting by it, "explains nothing, but needs explanation". "The only real explaining is: making transcendentally intelligible" (*Das einzig wirkliche Erklären ist: transzendental verständlich machen*, Hua VI 193). Two words have changed. "Transcendentally" now qualifies "intelligible", and the colon turns § 49's title-claim into a definition. The intelligibility of § 49 was reached on the way through the life-world, and its subject was intersubjectivity. The intelligibility of § 55 is reached after the correction of that way. Between them stands the sentence of § 54 that undoes § 49's choice of subject: "what was wrong was the method, the jumping straight into transcendental intersubjectivity and the skipping of the primal I, the ego of my epoché" (*das sogleich Hineinspringen in die transzendentale Intersubjektivität*, Hua VI 188; row 22).

§ 49 is that jump. Its accomplishment is "not of the single one, rather of the whole of the intersubjectivity communalized in the accomplishing", and its syntheses reach "from subject to subject". The Paris Lectures of 1929 had put the same correlation in the first person singular: what truly is, real or ideal, "has meaning only as a particular correlate of *my own* intentionality", though "not of an isolated *cogito*" (*Bedeutung nur als ein besonderes Korrelat meiner eigenen Intentionalität*, Hua I 23). The *Krisis* reaches intersubjectivity first, from the life-world, where the world is always the world "for all". That is the advantage of its way, and § 54 names its cost. The provisional concept of § 49 is correct about what constitution does, the return from a "finished being" to its origins, and premature about who does it. "Explaining", claimed here for intentionality, keeps its sense through § 55. What changes is the subject to whom the intelligibility is finally owed: not "we" in communalized accomplishment but the ego of the epoché, which constitutes the "we" in its turn.

## §§53–54, Hua VI 183–188: the I that is "I" only by equivocation

Reads [§53.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-paradox-of-human-subjectivity-being-a-subject-for-the-world-and-at-the-same-time-being-an-object-in-the-world) and [§54.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-resolution-of-the-paradox), the second paragraph under § 53 and the third under § 54.

The paradox of § 53 is a paradox of counting: a part of the world, human subjectivity, is to constitute the whole of which it is a part. § 54 resolves it through grammar, not through a change of attitude. The ego reached in the epoché is not one of the subjects counted in the world, and not one of the persons the pronouns sort. It is "I" only by an equivocation, an equivocation that is "essential" because reflection has no other word for it. Husserl calls it the primal I (*Ur-Ich*): unique, and "personally undeclinable", it produces by its own accomplishment the declension of I, You and We, and then counts itself as one member of the plural it has constituted. The human being is that ego's self-objectivation. The identity of the transcendental and the human I is real, and § 72 states it outright, but it holds across constitution, in one direction. It is not one subject seen in two attitudes.

> But just here lies the difficulty. The universal intersubjectivity into which all objectivity, everything that is at all, dissolves, can obviously be none other than humanity, which is undeniably itself a partial stock of the world. How is a partial stock of the world, its human subjectivity, to constitute the whole world, namely to constitute it as its intentional formation? — the world, a formation, always already become and still becoming, of the universal connection of intentionally accomplishing subjectivity — while they, the subjects accomplishing in community, are themselves to be only partial formations of the total accomplishment?
>
> The subject-stock of the world devours, so to speak, the whole world and with it itself. What a countersense. Or is it after all a meaningfully resolvable, even a necessary paradox, arising necessarily from the constant tension between the power of the self-evidence of the natural objective attitude (the power of *common sense*) and the attitude of the "disinterested onlooker" that sets itself against it?

From § 54 b:

> Nevertheless we cannot be satisfied, and remain stuck on the paradox. Our naive procedure was in fact not quite correct, namely through the self-forgetfulness of ourselves, the philosophizers; or, to speak more plainly: the epoché is performed by me, and even if there are several, and even if they practise the epoché in actual community with me, for me in my epoché all other human beings with their whole life of acts are included in the world-phenomenon, which in my epoché is exclusively mine. The epoché creates a unique philosophical solitude, which is the fundamental methodical requirement for a really radical philosophy. In this solitude I am not a single individual who, out of some wilfulness, however theoretically justified (or by chance, say as a castaway), separates himself from the community of humanity, to which he nevertheless knows himself to belong even then. I am not *an* I that still has its You and its We and its community of co-subjects in natural validity. The whole of humanity, and the whole division and order of the personal pronouns, has become a phenomenon in my epoché, together with the privilege of the I-human-being among other human beings. The I that I reach in the epoché, the same that in a critical reinterpretation and improvement of the Cartesian conception would be the "ego", is properly called "I" only by equivocation, although it is an essential equivocation, since when I name it reflectively I can say nothing other than: it is I, I who practise the epoché, I who question the world, the world now valid for me in its being and being-so, with all its human beings of whom I am so completely certain, as a phenomenon; thus I who stand above all natural existence that has sense for me and am the I-pole of the transcendental life in each case, in which the world at first has sense purely as world for me: I who, taken in full concreteness, encompass all this. This does not mean that our earlier evidences, already expressed as transcendental, were illusions, or that it could not be justified that we must nevertheless speak of a transcendental intersubjectivity constituting the world as "world for all", in which I appear again, but now as "a" transcendental I among the others, and "we all" as transcendentally functioning. But what was wrong was the method: the jumping straight into transcendental intersubjectivity and the skipping of the primal I, the ego of my epoché, which can never lose its uniqueness and personal undeclinability. It contradicts this only apparently that it — but through a particular constitutive accomplishment of its own — makes itself transcendentally declinable for itself; that it thus, from itself and in itself, constitutes the transcendental intersubjectivity to which it then reckons itself, as a merely privileged member, namely as I.

German, § 53:

> Aber hier gerade liegt die Schwierigkeit. Die universale Intersubjektivität, in die sich alle Objektivität, alles überhaupt Seiende auflöst, kann offenbar doch keine andere sein als die Menschheit, die unleugbar selbst ein Teilbestand der Welt ist. Wie soll ein Teilbestand der Welt, ihre menschliche Subjektivität, die ganze Welt konstituieren, nämlich konstituieren als ihr intentionales Gebilde? — Welt, ein immer schon gewordenes und fortwerdendes Gebilde des universalen Konnexes der intentional leitenden Subjektivität — wobei sie, die im Miteinander leistenden Subjekte, selbst nur Teilgebilde der totalen Leistung sein sollen?
>
> Der Subjektbestand der Welt verschlingt sozusagen die gesamte Welt und damit sich selbst. Welch ein Widersinn. Oder ist es doch eine sinnvoll auflösbare, sogar eine notwendige Paradoxie, notwendig entspringend aus der beständigen Spannung zwischen der Macht der Selbstverständlichkeit der natürlichen objektiven Einstellung (der Macht des *common sense*) und der sich ihr gegenübersetzenden Einstellung des ,,uninteressierten Betrachters"?

German, § 54 b:

> Gleichwohl können wir nicht befriedigt sein und bleiben noch an der Paradoxie haften. Unser naives Vorgehen war in der Tat nicht ganz korrekt, und zwar durch die Selbstvergessenheit unserer selbst, der Philosophierenden, oder deutlicher gesprochen: die Epoché vollziehe ich, und selbst wenn da mehrere sind, und sogar in aktueller Gemeinschaft mit mir die Epoché üben, so sind für mich in meiner Epoché alle anderen Menschen mit ihrem ganzen Aktleben in das Weltphänomen einbezogen, das in meiner Epoché ausschließlich das meine ist. Die Epoché schafft eine einzigartige philosophische Einsamkeit, die das methodische Grunderfordernis ist für eine wirklich radikale Philosophie. In dieser Einsamkeit bin ich nicht ein Einzelner, der aus irgendeinem, sei es auch theoretisch gerechtfertigten Eigensinn (oder aus Zufall, etwa als Schiffbrüchiger) sich aussondert aus der Gemeinschaft der Menschheit, der er sich aber auch dann noch zugehörig weiß. Ich bin nicht e i n Ich, das immer noch sein Du und sein Wir und seine Allgemeinheit von Mitsubjekten in natürlicher Geltung hat. Die ganze Menschheit und die ganze Scheidung und Ordnung der Personalpronomina ist in meiner Epoche zum Phänomen geworden, mitsamt dem Vorzug des Ich-Mensch unter anderen Menschen. Das Ich, das ich in der Epoche erreiche, dasselbe, das in der kritischen Umdeutung und Verbesserung der Descartes'schen Konzeption das „ego“ wäre, heißt eigentlich nur durch Äquivokation „Ich“, obschon es eine wesensmäßige Äquivokation ist, da, wenn ich es reflektierend benenne, ich nicht anders sagen kann als: ich bin es, ich der Epoché-Übende, ich, der die Welt, die mir jetzt nach Sein und Sosein geltende Welt, mit allen ihren Menschen, deren ich so völlig gewiß bin, als Phänomen befrage; also ich, der ich über allem natürlichen Dasein, das für mich Sinn hat, stehe und der Ichpol bin des jeweils transzendentalen Lebens, worin zunächst Welt rein als Welt für mich Sinn hat: Ich, der ich, in voller Konkretion genommen, all das umfasse. Das besagt nicht, daß unsere früheren schon als transzendentale ausgesprochenen Evidenzen Täuschungen waren und daß es nicht zu rechtfertigen sei, daß trotzdem von einer transzendentalen, die Welt als „Welt für alle“ konstituierenden Intersubjektivität gesprochen werden muß, in der ich wiederum auftrete, aber nun als „ein“ transzendentales Ich unter den Anderen, und dabei „wir alle“ als transzendental-fungierende. Aber verkehrt war das Methodische, war das sogleich Hineinspringen in die transzendentale Intersubjektivität und das Überspringen des Ur-Ich, des ego meiner Epoche, das seine Einzigkeit und persönliche Undeklinierbarkeit nie verlieren kann. Dem widerspricht nur scheinbar, daß es sich — aber durch eine besondere ihm eigene konstitutive Leistung — für sich selbst transzendental deklinierbar macht; daß es also von sich aus und in sich die transzendentale Intersubjektivität konstituiert, der es sich dann zurechnet, als bloß bevorzugtes Glied, nämlich als Ich.

The next page opens "der transzendentalen Andern" [of the transcendental others], so the full stop may be spurious and the clause may run "as I of the transcendental others". The print line numbers inside § 54 b are dropped.

The paradox is stated in the vocabulary of inventory. Humanity is a "partial stock" (*Teilbestand*) of the world, and the subjects are "the subject-stock of the world" (*Der Subjektbestand der Welt*). A stock is something counted among the contents of a whole. Row 19 read the grammar that forbids this: the world exists in a "uniqueness for which the plural is senseless", and every object is "'something from' the world" (Hua VI 146). If the constituting subjects are a stock of the world, they are counted inside the very horizon they are to constitute. The image draws the consequence. The part "devours, so to speak" (*verschlingt sozusagen*) the whole "and with it itself": what swallows the world swallows itself as part of the world. The "so to speak" marks the image as an image, and the judgement follows without a verb: "What a countersense" (*Welch ein Widersinn*). Then a question turns the countersense into a "necessary paradox" that "arises necessarily" from the tension between two attitudes. The question names the source of the paradox, not its resolution. The main commentary's §54.3 makes the attitude the resolution as well: subject for the world and object in it are "two aspects of the same subjectivity, distinguished only by the attitude in which they are grasped". § 53 already has that thought, and has it as the reason the paradox arises.

§ 54 b starts from a fault in the first approach, and names it in a reflexive pleonasm: "the self-forgetfulness of ourselves, the philosophizers" (*die Selbstvergessenheit unserer selbst*). The correction moves from "we" to "I", and the word order carries the move. "The epoché is performed by me" renders a German sentence that puts the object first and the pronoun last: *die Epoché vollziehe ich*. Even co-performers of the epoché, in actual community with me, fall within "the world-phenomenon, which in my epoché is exclusively mine". The solitude this creates is "unique" (*einzigartige*), and Husserl separates it from the solitude of a castaway, who is cut off from a community he still knows he belongs to. The castaway is one of many who happens to be alone. The ego of the epoché is alone because the many are a phenomenon for it.

The grammar of the pronouns does the rest. "I am not *an* I" (*nicht e i n Ich*, spaced) "that still has its You and its We". The indefinite article is the mark of membership in a kind, the article § 37 denied to the world ("not … as an existent does", row 19). Then "the whole division and order of the personal pronouns has become a phenomenon". The system of persons is something the epoché brackets, so the one who performs it cannot be one of its persons in the ordinary sense. Yet he can only name himself with one of them. Hence the ego "is properly called 'I' only by equivocation" (*nur durch Äquivokation „Ich“*), and the equivocation is "essential" (*wesensmäßige*), because "when I name it reflectively I can say nothing other than: it is I". The quotation marks round "I" in this sentence mark the word as mentioned, a name for the ego and not the ego's pronoun. When the article returns, later in the paragraph, it is in quotation marks too: in transcendental intersubjectivity I appear "as 'a' transcendental I among the others" (*als „ein“ transzendentales Ich*). The spaced *ein* denies membership, and the quoted *ein* restores it as constituted.

The resolution is stated as a grammar of case. The primal I "can never lose its uniqueness and personal undeclinability" (*seine Einzigkeit und persönliche Undeklinierbarkeit*). Grammar declines nouns and pronouns through case and number, and here Husserl stretches the word to person. The primal I has no forms of its own. It "only apparently" contradicts this when, "through a particular constitutive accomplishment of its own", it "makes itself transcendentally declinable for itself" (*für sich selbst transzendental deklinierbar macht*). The reflexive carries the claim: the ego is not declined by a grammar that precedes it; it gives itself its inflections, and constitutes the intersubjectivity in which it then counts itself "a merely privileged member". The error, therefore, was not to speak of transcendental intersubjectivity, whose evidences were no "illusions", but to start there. "What was wrong was the method" (*verkehrt war das Methodische*): "the jumping straight into transcendental intersubjectivity and the skipping of the primal I". That jump was § 49's (row 21), where the accomplishment was that of "the whole of the intersubjectivity communalized in the accomplishing".

The end of § 54 states the identity that the commentary takes for the resolution, and states its conditions. Only from the ego can transcendental intersubjectivity be shown, and only on that path can one understand that each transcendental I "must necessarily be constituted as a human being in the world", so that each human being "'carries a transcendental I in itself'", "not as a real part or a layer of his soul (which would be a countersense), but insofar as he is the self-objectivation of the transcendental I in question, which phenomenological self-reflection can exhibit" (*Selbstobjektivation des betreffenden transzendentalen Ich*, Hua VI 190). The first epoché had attributed to humanity "in one leap and groundlessly, thus illegitimately" the transformation into functioning subjectivity that "I had performed in myself alone", and yet "despite the methodical illegitimacy there was a truth in it" (*lag darin doch eine Wahrheit*). § 72 states that truth in the first person, with the particle of the self-evident: "For as transcendental I, I am the same that in worldliness is human I" (*Ich bin ja als transzendentales Ich dasselbe, das in der Weltlichkeit menschliches Ich ist*, Hua VI c. 269). It draws a consequence that rules out a mere difference of attitude: "What was concealed from me in humanity I uncover in transcendental research", and every new transcendental cognition becomes "an enrichment of the content of the human soul". If the two I's differed only by the attitude in which one regards them, nothing found in one attitude would change what is there in the other. On § 72's account it does change it.

Husserl had put the ego's place before the pronouns in Part II, as something Descartes could not see. The ego discovered in the epoché "is not yet 'an' I at all, that could have others or many co-I's outside it", and "all such distinctions as I and You, inside and outside, first 'constitute' themselves in the absolute *ego*" (*noch gar nicht „ein“ Ich*, Hua VI 84). § 54 carries out what § 19 asserted against Descartes. The paradox of § 53 arose because the constituting subjects were counted as a stock of the world. It is resolved because the ego that constitutes is not counted at all: it is unique as the world is unique (row 19), and unlike the world it gives itself a plural. The human being and the transcendental I are the same I, but the sameness runs one way. The human is what the primal I makes of itself in the world, and the word "I" names both only by an equivocation the language cannot avoid. The commentary's "same subjectivity seen in two attitudes" keeps the identity and loses the direction, and with it the route of § 55 (row 23).

## §55, Hua VI 190–191: the second epoché and the mute ego

Reads [§55.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-principled-correction-of-our-first-approach-through-the-way-over-the-life-world), the first paragraph under § 55.

The correction of § 55 concerns the subject of constitution, not the life-world. The first epoché, carried out on the way from the life-world, gave constitution to "we", the communalized intersubjectivity of humankind, and so ran into the paradox of § 53. The correction reduces the epoché "to the absolute ego as the ultimately unique functional centre of all constitution". The way from the life-world is not withdrawn. It becomes the direction of method: the ego is apodictically given, but as a "mute concretion", and it learns what it is only by inquiring back from its "concrete world-phenomenon". § 55 thus avoids two leaps at once. One is the Cartesian leap to an ego that looks empty, which § 43 had refused. The other is the leap to intersubjectivity that § 54 has just condemned. The ego comes first in order of grounding, the world-phenomenon first in order of access.

> § 55. The principled correction of our first approach to the epoché by reducing it to the absolutely unique, ultimately functioning ego.
>
> Accordingly, as against the first approach to the epoché, a second is needed, or rather a conscious reshaping of the first, by reduction to the absolute ego as the ultimately unique functional centre of all constitution. This determines henceforth the whole method of transcendental phenomenology. In advance there is the world, pregiven constantly in certainty of being and self-confirmation, and indubitable. Even if I have not "presupposed" it as ground, it is valid for me, the I in the cogito, out of constant self-confirmation, with everything that it is for me, in particulars now objectively legitimate, now not, and also with all sciences and arts, with all social and personal formations and institutions, insofar as it is precisely the world that is for me the actual one. There can therefore be no stronger realism, if this word means no more than: "I am certain of being a human being who lives in this world, etc., and I do not doubt it in the least". But the great problem is precisely to understand this "self-evidence". The method now requires that the ego inquire back systematically from its concrete world-phenomenon and thereby come to know itself, the transcendental ego, in its concreteness, in the system of its constitutive strata and its unspeakably intertwined foundings of validity. The ego is apodictically given at the outset of the epoché, but as a "mute concretion". This must be brought to explication, to expression, and indeed in a systematic intentional "analysis" that inquires back from the world-phenomenon. In this systematic procedure one first obtains the correlation of the world and of the transcendental subjectivity objectivated in humankind.

German:

> § 55. Die prinzipielle Korrektur unseres ersten Ansatzes der Epoché durch Reduktion derselben auf das absolut einzige letztlich fungierende ego.
>
> Demnach bedarf es gegenüber dem ersten Ansatz der Epoché eines zweiten, bzw. einer bewußten Umgestaltung derselben durch Reduktion auf das absolute ego als das letztlich einzige Funktionszentrum aller Konstitution. Das bestimmt hinfort die ganze Methode der transzendentalen Phänomenologie. Vorweg ist die Welt, die immerfort in Seinsgewißheit und Selbstbewährung vorgegebene und unzweifelhafte. Habe ich sie auch nicht als Boden ,,vorausgesetzt", so ist sie für mich, das Ich im cogito, doch aus ständiger Selbstbewährung in Geltung, mit allem, was sie für mich ist, im einzelnen bald objektiv rechtmäßig, bald nicht, auch mit allen Wissenschaften, Künsten, mit allen sozialen, personalen Gestalten und Institutionen, soweit es eben die Welt ist, die mir die wirkliche ist. Einen stärkeren Realismus kann es also nicht geben, wenn dieses Wort nicht mehr besagt als: „ich bin dessen gewiß, ein Mensch zu sein, der in dieser Welt lebt usw., und ich zweifle daran nicht im mindesten“. Aber es ist eben das große Problem, diese „Selbstverständlichkeit“ zu verstehen. Die Methode erfordert nun, daß das ego von seinem konkreten Weltphänomen aus systematisch zurückfragt und dabei sich selbst, das transzendentale ego, in seiner Konkretion, in der Systematik seiner konstitutiven Schichten und seiner unsagbar verschlungenen Geltungsfundierungen kennen lernt. Das ego ist im Einsatz der Epoché apodiktisch gegeben, aber als „stumme Konkretion“. Sie muß zur Auslegung, zur Aussprache gebracht werden, und zwar in systematischer, vom Weltphänomen aus zurückfragender intentionaler „Analyse“. In diesem systematischen Vorgehen gewinnt man zunächst die Korrelation der Welt und der transzendentalen, in der Menschheit objektivierten Subjektivität.

*Note on the text.* The heading is joined from four print lines (src L2716–2719). The main commentary's heading for § 55, "through the way over the 'life-world'", is not the source's title, which the file has in the body and in the table of contents alike. The apparatus has an entry for the very phrase that carries the correction, "by reduction to the absolute *ego* as the ultimately unique functional centre of all constitution" (190,25f., siglum "E."), so the formula has a textual history. The key to the sigla, which survives only as a displaced footnote in the apparatus, reads "E." as insertion (*Einfügung*): the phrase was added in revision. The passage itself is continuous across the head for Hua VI 191.

"Accordingly" (*Demnach*) makes the correction a consequence of § 54, and the next words hesitate over what kind of correction it is. A "second" epoché is needed, "or rather" (*bzw.*) "a conscious reshaping of the first" (*einer bewußten Umgestaltung derselben*). A second epoché would be a new operation, and the first could be dropped. A reshaping keeps the first and changes what it was not conscious of. The "or rather" corrects itself toward the second reading, and § 54 had prepared it: the earlier evidences "were not illusions", and "there was a truth" in the illegitimate leap (Hua VI 188, 190; row 22). The word "conscious" (*bewußten*) answers the "self-forgetfulness" (*Selbstvergessenheit*) of § 54 b. The first epoché was performed by an ego that forgot itself. The second is the same epoché performed by an ego that knows it is performing it.

The heading and the sentence say "unique" twice, and the word is § 54's. The ego is "absolutely unique" (*absolut einzige*) and "ultimately unique functional centre" (*letztlich einzige Funktionszentrum*). Row 22 read the primal I's "uniqueness and personal undeclinability". "Functional centre" adds a spatial image to the grammatical one: the ego is the point from which all constitution radiates, as the I-pole was the centre of its acts. "Ultimately" (*letztlich*) concedes that there are other functioning subjects, the transcendental others of § 54 b, and puts them in second place. They function too, but not ultimately: their functioning is constituted from the centre.

The paragraph then does something the commentary does not record: it reaffirms realism. The world is "in advance" (*Vorweg*), "pregiven constantly in certainty of being and self-confirmation, and indubitable", with its sciences, arts and institutions. "There can therefore be no stronger realism", provided realism means only the certainty "I am a human being who lives in this world". The quotation marks around "presupposed" (*„vorausgesetzt“*) mark the one thing the epoché refuses: to use the world as a premise. They do not deny its validity. The epoché leaves the world as it is and makes its self-evidence the problem: "the great problem is precisely to understand this 'self-evidence'" (*diese „Selbstverständlichkeit“*). This is § 53's word once more ("the only universe of pregiven self-evidences", Hua VI 183), and the paradox is restated as a task.

The method follows from that order, and its key word is "inquire back" (*zurückfragt*, and "inquiring back", *zurückfragender*). The ego must "inquire back systematically from its concrete world-phenomenon", and so "come to know itself". It is "apodictically given at the outset of the epoché, but as a 'mute concretion'" (*als „stumme Konkretion“*). Apodicticity and muteness go together: that the ego is, is beyond doubt; what it is, in "the system of its constitutive strata", it cannot say. Its concretion "must be brought to explication, to expression" (*zur Auslegung, zur Aussprache*). The *Cartesian Meditations* had used the same image with the opposite order. There "the beginning is the pure and, so to speak, still mute experience, which must first be brought to the pure expression of its own sense", and "the truly first expression is the Cartesian one of the *ego cogito*" (*Die wirklich erste Aussprache ist aber die Cartesianische des ego cogito*, Hua I 77, § 16). In 1929 the ego was the first word spoken. In § 55 the ego is what is mute, and the first word spoken is the world-phenomenon, from which the ego's concretion is recovered by inquiring back.

That reversal is the point of the "way" of Part III A. § 43 had faulted the Cartesian way because it leads "as in a leap" (*wie in einem Sprunge*) to the transcendental ego, but shows it "in an apparent emptiness of content", so that one is "at a loss" what has been gained (Hua VI 158). The way from the life-world was to give the ego content in advance. § 54 then found a second leap on this way: the first epoché attributed transcendental functioning to all humankind "in one leap and groundlessly" (*in einem Sprunge und unbegründet*, Hua VI 190). § 55 keeps the content and drops the leap. The ego is not empty but mute, its content is the whole world-phenomenon, and the order of exposition runs from that phenomenon back to the ego. Even the vocabulary of § 53 changes register here, though the text does not exploit it: the subject-stock that "devours" (*verschlingt*) the world becomes an ego with "unspeakably intertwined foundings of validity" (*unsagbar verschlungenen Geltungsfundierungen*). The German verb has both senses, swallowing and interlacing.

The last sentence states what the method yields "first" (*zunächst*): "the correlation of the world and of the transcendental subjectivity objectivated in humankind" (*der transzendentalen, in der Menschheit objektivierten Subjektivität*). The human community is now the objectivation of transcendental subjectivity, which is § 54's "self-objectivation" generalized. It is what the first epoché had taken for the constituting subject itself. The rest of § 55 shows why the order matters. Once humankind is the objectivation, new questions press: whether the insane, children and animals are objectivations of the world-constituting subjects, to whom "their manner of transcendentality" must be assigned "as 'analogies' of us", and then generativity, birth and death, the sexes and the "unconscious" (Hua VI 192). Those questions cannot be asked of a "we" that is simply humankind. They are questions about the reach of the objectivation, asked from the centre. § 55 closes by fixing the centre's evidence. "Arrived at the ego, one becomes aware that one stands in a sphere of evidence behind which it would be nonsense to want to inquire back", and the ego cogito is no "premise" from which to deduce: "the task is not to secure objectivity but to understand it" (*Es gilt nicht, Objektivität zu sichern, sondern sie zu verstehen*, Hua VI 192–193). The inquiry back that begins at the world-phenomenon ends at the one point where inquiring back stops. The commentary's reading, on which the correction finds the life-world to be a constitutive formation, states a premise of the whole of Part III A. The correction of § 55 is narrower and comes later: it settles who constitutes, and in what order the analysis may say so.

## §58, Hua VI 207–210: twinning

Reads [§58.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#twinning-and-difference-of-psychology-and-transcendental-philosophy-psychology-as-the-field-of-decisions), the only paragraph under § 58.

Psychology and transcendental philosophy, in § 58, are twins because they have one parent: a single accomplishment, the life in which I "have" a world. Transcendentally I question it as the accomplishment whose "ultimate I-pole" I am as ego. Psychologically I know it as the life of the "mundanized I", the soul, a human being among things and animals. The twinning is "inseparable" because whatever one finds the other must find again, under another apperception. It is also a difference, and the heading sets the two words side by side: the twins are never one. Husserl took the word from § 9, where the sensory fillings of bodies are "twinned" with their shapes "in a regulated way". There the twinning licensed treating one side as a function of the other, and that licence was the root of the mathematization of nature. In § 58 the same word names a correspondence that forbids the substitution. The two sides correspond everywhere, and neither can be computed from the other.

> § 58. *Twinning and difference of* psychology and transcendental philosophy. Psychology as the field of decisions.
>
> This total accomplishment, whose ultimate I-pole I myself am as "ego", I can question transcendentally, and follow its intentional build-up of sense and validity. By contrast, as psychologist I set myself the task of knowing myself, the already worldly I, objectivated with its real sense in each case, the so to speak mundanized I (concretely, the soul), precisely in the manner of objective, naturally mundane (in the widest sense) knowledge: myself as a human being among the things, the other human beings, the animals and so on. We understand, then, that there is in fact an inseparable inner twinning between psychology and transcendental philosophy. But from there it is also to be foreseen that a way to a transcendental philosophy must be possible through a concretely executed psychology. One can tell oneself in advance: if I myself perform the transcendental attitude as a way of raising myself above all world-apperceptions and my human self-apperceptions, and purely with the intention of studying the transcendental accomplishment out of and in which I "have" the world, then I must surely find this accomplishment again afterwards in a psychological inner analysis, though then as having entered again into an apperception, thus apperceived as something really psychic, really related to the real body.

German:

> § 58. *Verschwisterung und Verschiedenheit von* Psychologie und Transzendentalphilosophie. Die Psychologie als das Feld der Entscheidungen.
>
> Diese Gesamtleistung, deren letzter Ichpol ich selbst als „ego“ bin, kann ich transzendental befragen und ihrem intentionalen Sinn- und Geltungsaufbau nachgehen. Hingegen als Psychologe stelle ich mir die Aufgabe, mich, das schon weltliche, mit dem jeweiligen realen Sinn objektivierte, sozusagen mundanisierte Ich — konkret die Seele — zu erkennen, eben in der Weise objektiver, natürlich mundaner (im weitesten Sinn) Erkenntnis, mich als Menschen unter den Dingen, den anderen Menschen, den Tieren usw. Wir verstehen also, daß in der Tat eine unlösliche innere Verschwisterung zwischen Psychologie und Transzendentalphilosophie gegeben ist. Nun ist aber von da aus auch vorauszusehen, daß ein Weg zu einer Transzendentalphilosophie über eine konkret ausgeführte Psychologie sich müsse führen lassen. Im voraus kann man sich doch sagen: vollziehe ich selbst die transzendentale Einstellung als eine Weise, mich über alle Weltapperzeptionen und meine menschlichen Selbstapperzeptionen zu erheben, und rein in der Absicht, die transzendentale Leistung, aus und in der ich Welt „habe“, zu studieren, so muß ich doch auch diese Leistung hinterher in einer psychologischen Innenanalyse wiederfinden, obschon dann in eine Apperzeption wieder eingegangen, also apperzipiert als Realseelisches, real bezogen auf den realen Leib.

The footnote to the last sentence:

> If I learn to clarify, to understand from myself as ego, how other human beings are human beings only for themselves and have the world in validity as constantly existing for them, as that in which they live with others and with me, how they too are ultimately transcendental subjects, in accomplishments of world- and self-objectivation, then one will again surely say to oneself: what my transcendental clarification yields with regard to the transcendental self-objectivations of the others, I must surely attribute to their human, their psychologically assessable being.

German:

> Lerne ich aufklären, von mir als ego aus verstehen, wie andere Menschen für sich selbst Menschen nur sind und Welt als ständig für sie seiende in Geltung haben, als in welcher sie mit Anderen und mit mir leben, wie auch sie letztlich transzendentale Subjekte sind, in Leistungen der Welt- und Selbstobjektivierung, so wird man abermals sich doch sagen: Was meine transzendentale Aufklärung hinsichtlich der transzendentalen Selbstobjektivierungen der Anderen ergibt, muß ich doch ihrem menschlichen, ihrem psychologisch einzuschätzenden Sein zurechnen.

*Note on the text.* The heading's emphasis covers "Verschwisterung und Verschiedenheit von" with the word spaces collapsed, which is how the file renders spaced type; the spaces are restored here. Two pages of § 58 are lost. Hua VI 209 is missing between the end of p. 208 ("…ein widersinniger Zirkel", src L2820) and the head for p. 210, after which the file resumes mid-sentence ("bzw. dazu immer wieder werdend" [or rather ever again becoming so], L2824). The quotation starts after that fragment, so the antecedent of "this total accomplishment" is on the lost page. Most of pp. 211–212 are lost too: the sentence after the quotation breaks off at "worin jedermann seine Weltvorstellungen" [in which everyone (has) his world-representations] and resumes after the head for p. 213 with "Apperzeptionen herstammt" [stems from apperceptions] (L2847–2868; the apparatus records text at 211,3–212,30). The § 59 heading, which the table of contents puts on p. 212, falls in that gap. The footnote quoted after the passage is attached to its last sentence; the file sets it after the next page head. Print line numbers are dropped.

"Twinning" (*Verschwisterung*) is a word of kinship: to make siblings of two things. The paragraph gives the kinship a ground in the first sentences. There is one "total accomplishment" (*Gesamtleistung*), and two ways of knowing it. Transcendentally, I "question" it and "follow its intentional build-up of sense and validity", with myself as its "ultimate I-pole" (*letzter Ichpol*) "as 'ego'". Psychologically, I know "myself, the already worldly I", the "so to speak mundanized I (concretely, the soul)" (*sozusagen mundanisierte Ich — konkret die Seele*). "So to speak" is the hedge of a coinage: *mundanisiert* turns the Latin *mundus* into a verb of process, so that the soul is the ego worlded. The two descriptions are of the same I, and the relation between them is directional, as row 22 found for the human and the transcendental I. The soul is the ego "objectivated with its real sense". The heading's second word, "difference" (*Verschiedenheit*), keeps the direction from collapsing into identity. The siblings have the same parent and are still two.

The twinning works in both directions, and the sentence that states the first direction carries the weight of the section. If I perform the transcendental attitude and study "the transcendental accomplishment out of and in which I 'have' the world", then "I must surely find this accomplishment again afterwards in a psychological inner analysis" (*so muß ich doch auch diese Leistung hinterher in einer psychologischen Innenanalyse wiederfinden*). The "must surely" (*muß … doch*) appeals to what anyone would grant. The qualification follows: found again, but "as having entered again into an apperception", "apperceived as something really psychic, really related to the real body". What transcendental reflection finds as accomplishment, psychology finds as a real event in a real soul tied to a body. The footnote extends this to others: whatever my transcendental clarification yields about "the transcendental self-objectivations of the others", "I must surely attribute to their human, their psychologically assessable being" (*muß ich doch ihrem menschlichen, ihrem psychologisch einzuschätzenden Sein zurechnen*). The next sentence, cut off by the lost pages, runs "conversely": a radical psychological unfolding of my apperceiving life "would have to gain transcendental meaning at once in the transition to the transcendental attitude".

The inference is weaker than the "must surely" lets it sound. That the same accomplishment can be described twice does not show that every transcendental finding has a psychological counterpart, still less that the psychologist can reach it. The paragraph itself says that the origin of the apperceptions "can be questioned back only in the break of naivety, in the method of the transcendental reduction" (*nur im Bruch der Naivität*, Hua VI 213). So the psychologist "finds again" what he could not have found first. The twinning guarantees correspondence, not access. That is why it also grounds a "way" (*Weg*): "a way to a transcendental philosophy must be possible through a concretely executed psychology". Psychology can lead to the transcendental because its findings are the mundanized form of transcendental ones, and it needs the break to see them as such. The end of § 57 states the same thing as a necessity of development: the consistent and pure carrying-out of psychology's own task "had to lead of itself and with necessity to a science of transcendental subjectivity" (Hua VI 207).

§ 9 used the same verb, and its use there shows what § 58 avoids. The "indirect" mathematization of the sensory side of the world is thinkable only in the sense that the specifically sensory qualities, the "fillings" (*„Füllen“*), "are twinned in a quite particular way, in a regulated way (*geregelt verschwistert*), with the shapes essentially belonging to them" (Hua VI 34). In § 9 the twinning is a rule of co-variation between a mathematizable side and one that is not, and it is what allows the second to be treated through the first. The colours and sounds are then taken as indices of mathematical events, and the life-world is overlaid with the garb of ideas (rows 7–8). Twinning in § 9 is the warrant for a substitution. In § 58 the twinning is complete, "inseparable" (*unlösliche*), and it holds between two descriptions of one accomplishment, not between two sides of a thing. No side is privileged as the computable one. The section that follows makes the point against the substitution that naturalism performs: "to want to treat such problems on naive-objective ground and in the method of the objective sciences would be a countersense-laden circle" (*ein widersinniger Zirkel*, Hua VI 208). Whether Husserl meant the echo cannot be told from the text. The word is the same, and its structure is reversed. In § 9 it made one side a function of the other; in § 58 it names a correspondence in which neither can be derived from the other, and the passage from one to the other is a change of attitude, not a calculation.

The correspondence then has a direction of flow. The table of contents gives § 59 the subtitle "the problem of 'inflowing'" (*Das Problem des „Einströmens“*, Hua VI 212), and the heading of that section is on the lost pages. § 72 states the result in the passage row 22 read: "every new transcendental cognition turns, by essential necessity, into an enrichment of the content of the human soul", because "as transcendental I, I am the same that in worldliness is human I" (Hua VI c. 269). Transcendental findings flow into psychology, not the reverse. The main commentary's last sentence, that reformed psychology "reveals itself, at its deepest level, to be transcendental phenomenology under another name", keeps the twinning and drops the difference that the heading names. A psychology that receives every transcendental finding remains a science of the mundanized I, of the soul as real and bodily. It becomes transcendental philosophy only through the break that changes its subject, which § 72 calls "the break-in into the transcendental" (*der Einbruch ins Transzendentale*, Hua VI 268–271; row 26). Twinning makes psychology the "field of decisions": the place where the two can be confused and where they must be told apart.

## §68, Hua VI 235–237: intentionality as a character and as a problem

Reads [§68.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-task-of-an-explication-of-consciousness-as-intentional-accomplishment), the only paragraph under § 68.

For Brentano, in Husserl's account, intentionality is a character: a mark that sets the psychic apart from the physical and so marks off psychology's region. For Husserl it is a problem: the universal problem of how a world is there for us at all. § 68's heading names the second ("the universal problematic of intentionality") and puts Brentano's reform in brackets after it. The paragraph on Brentano says why the first is not a halfway stage on the road to the second. Brentano exchanged one kind of datum for another. Sensory data became intentional data. The region stayed a region, parallel to nature and causally bound to it. A psychology of intentionality was posed "only formally". It had no point of attack, because the one thing that would open it, an abstention from the validity of the world, was missing.

> § 68. The task of a *pure* explication of consciousness as such: the universal problematic of intentionality. (Brentano's attempt to reform psychology.)
>
> […]
>
> Here is the place to recall the extraordinary merit that *Brentano* earned by beginning his attempt to reform psychology with an investigation of the peculiar characters of the psychic (in contrast with the physical), and by exhibiting intentionality as one of these characters; the science of "psychic phenomena", then, has everywhere to do with experiences of consciousness. Unfortunately he remained in essentials caught in the prejudices of the naturalistic tradition. These are not yet overcome if the psychic data are conceived, instead of as sensory (be it of the outer or the inner "sense"), as data of the remarkable kind of intentionality: if, in other words, the dualism, psychophysical causality, remains in force. Here belongs also his idea of a descriptive psychology as a parallel to descriptive natural science, as the parallel procedure shows, with the task of classifying and descriptively analysing the psychic phenomena set wholly in the sense of the traditional interpretation of the relation between descriptive and explanatory natural sciences. None of this would have been possible if Brentano had pressed through to the true sense of the task of investigating conscious life as intentional, and that at first, since the grounding of psychology as an objective science was in question, on the ground of the pregiven world. So he posed a psychology of intentionality as a task only formally, but had no point of attack at all for it.

The file also prints "B ewußtseins" with a stray space, restored without emphasis. The table of contents gives the title without "pure" (*Die Aufgabe einer Auslegung des Bewußtseins als solchen*); the body heading has the word. The omitted paragraph between heading and passage (Hua VI 236) is the one discussed below. The running head "237" stands before the Brentano paragraph, but the apparatus places the original wording of its third sentence (marked, in the file's OCR, *urvpr.* Text) at 236,38–237,5, so the paragraph begins near the foot of p. 236. The apparatus line numbers for pp. 236–237 run on without a gap, so no page is lost here.

The paragraph's praise and its charge use the same noun. Brentano began with "the peculiar characters of the psychic" (*Charaktere des Psychischen*) and exhibited intentionality "as one of the characters" (*als einen der Charaktere*). A character, in this sense, is a distinguishing mark, and "one of" counts it among several. The *Logical Investigations* had already said so: Brentano drew his class of "psychic phenomena" by a "mutual separation" of the two main classes, guided by a "classificatory interest", and gained "six determinations" (*sechs Bestimmungen*, Hua XIX/1 379). A mark that sorts phenomena into two classes presupposes the two classes. The first sentence's "then" (*also*) draws the consequence: the science of "psychic phenomena" has everywhere to do with experiences of consciousness. The quotation marks around "psychic phenomena" hold Brentano's term at a distance. The *Logical Investigations* had refused it outright (Hua XIX/1 384). What Brentano gained is a province, and the praise is for having found the right province.

The charge then turns on a conditional that the main commentary does not record. The prejudices of naturalism "are not yet overcome if" (*noch nicht überwunden sind, wenn*) the psychic data are taken as data of an intentional kind instead of sensory ones. The scope of the negation is the point. Husserl does not say that Brentano kept the sensory data. He says that replacing them changes nothing so long as they remain data. "The remarkable kind" (*merkwürdigen Art*) is the irony of a naturalist's description: intentionality becomes a curious property that some data have. "In other words" (*m. a. W.*) then equates two claims, that the psychic consists of data and that "the dualism, psychophysical causality, remains in force". The equation is quicker than the argument. Someone can conceive experiences as items without assigning them causes in the body. Husserl can claim the equation because § 67 has just argued it historically: in the empiricist "first shape of naturalism" the soul is detached as "a real sphere of psychic data", and the naive equation of these data with the data of bodily experience "leads to a reification of them" and "to the parallelizing of the tasks on both sides" (*zur Parallelisierung der Aufgaben beiderseits*, Hua VI 235). On that account a datum is already half a thing, and its place beside the body is already a causal place.

"Parallel" carries the charge from there. Brentano's descriptive psychology is "a parallel to descriptive natural science, as the parallel procedure shows". The word closes a series that § 67 opened: the "parallel abstraction" and the "inner perception" parallel to outer perception (Hua VI 234), Berkeley's and Hume's immanent idealism, which "consumes the one member of the 'parallel'", and Wundt's "monistic naturalism with two parallel faces", "a variant of Spinozist parallelism" (Hua VI 235). For Husserl Brentano is the last and best member of that series, not its exception. His psychology describes and classifies as the descriptive natural sciences do, and it awaits an explanatory science as they do. § 71 confirms the reading: a pure descriptive psychology "at any rate demands an 'explanatory' psychology alongside it", "as was still Brentano's and Dilthey's standpoint at the end of the last century" (Hua VI 249). The paragraph that § 72 opens on "parallelism" (row 26) takes the word over for the relation between psychology and transcendental philosophy, and only there does it stop meaning a parallel to nature.

The counterfactual says what the true task would have been, and it is not yet transcendental. Brentano would have had to "press through" to the task of "investigating conscious life *as* intentional" (*Bewußtseinsleben als intentionales zu erforschen*), "and that at first … on the ground of the pregiven world". "As intentional" turns the adjective from a predicate of some data into the respect under which the whole of conscious life is studied. "At first on the ground of the pregiven world" keeps the task inside psychology. Husserl does not blame Brentano for missing the transcendental reduction. He blames him for missing what a psychologist could have seen. The paragraph before the passage gives that psychological finding. Taken "quite without prejudice" in immediate reflective self-experience, conscious life shows "nothing less than" colour-data, sound-data and the rest, but "the *cogito*, *intentionality*", in shapes familiar from speech: "I see a tree, which is green", "I remember my schooldays", "nothing other than 'consciousness of …'" (Hua VI 236). Its first sentence calls the error to be overcome a "naivety" (*Naivität*) that makes conscious life, "in which and through which the world is for us what it is", "a real property of the human being". The heading's "universal problematic" is this: consciousness is not one more real thing in the world but the life in which the world is there.

"Point of attack" (*Angriffspunkt*) is the word § 69 redeems. Brentano "had no point of attack at all" for the task he set. In § 69 the reformed descriptive psychology "has its first points of attack in the real intentionalities that stand out in the natural attitude, the behaviours of human beings in doing and leaving undone. And so it grasps first in them, by abstaining from co-validity, an 'inner'" (*durch Enthaltung von der Mitgeltung ein „Innerliches“*, Hua VI 242). The point of attack is the epoché of validity. Without it, as § 69 says, the intentionalities of a person "have the sense of *real relations* between the person and other realities" (Hua VI 239). Brentano described the relation, but as a real one, between a psychic thing and a physical one. The epoché turns it into the person's own "immanent 'relating-itself'" (Hua VI 241), and this is the material a pure psychology can analyse. "Only formally" therefore means more than "incompletely". Brentano named the theme in the form of a definition and had no method that could make it a field of work.

The edition shows how Husserl weighed his debt. Against the sentence after the passage, where Brentano's school refuses "the decisive novelty of my *Logical Investigations* (although his demand for a psychology of intentional phenomena had its effect in them)", the apparatus records for 237,14–16 the wording "the problem of intentionality was taken over from him" (*das Problem der Intentionalität von ihm übernommen war*), marked *V. von*. The key to the sigla survives only as a displaced footnote in the apparatus, and it reads "V." as correction (*Verbesserung*): the printed wording corrects this one. Husserl first credited Brentano with the problem and then only with a demand. The Vienna lecture of 1935 already speaks only of a demand: "only when *Brentano* demanded (*forderte*) psychology as a science of intentional experiences was an impulse given that could lead further", although he "did not yet overcome objectivism and psychological naturalism" (Hua VI 346). The same paragraph of § 68 says what the novelty was: not the "merely ontological investigations", which "worked one-sidedly against the innermost sense of the work", but the subjectively directed Fifth and Sixth Investigations, where "the *cogitata qua cogitata*" first came to their right as essential moments of every experience (Hua VI 237). This is the Husserl of 1936 reading the Husserl of 1901. In 1901 the Fifth Investigation "holds fast to Brentano's essential determination" and rejects only his terminology (*Während wir Brentanos wesentliche Bestimmung festhalten*, Hua XIX/1 384). § 68 instead finds the naturalism in the classifying itself. The 1901 book rejected Brentano's term. The 1936 book reads that rejection as the start of a break, and claims the break as the decisive novelty of 1901. Brentano ends § 68 as the man who demanded intentionality and the one whose method kept it a property of data.

## §72, Hua VI 265–266: the subjectivity that objectivates itself in the human

Reads [§72.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-krisis-commentary.md#the-relation-of-transcendental-psychology-to-transcendental-philosophy-the-parallelism-and-the-transition), the second paragraph under § 72.

The last surviving paragraph of § 72 ends on a formula for what the whole method exists to find: "the absolutely functioning subjectivity, not as the human but as the one that objectivates itself in the human, or at first in the human". The human is not what this subjectivity is. It is the place where it makes itself an object, and makes itself so continually. Row 22 read the identity of the transcendental and the human I, which § 72 states a few pages later ("as transcendental I, I am the same that in worldliness is human I"), and found that the identity runs one way. The formula here gives that direction its grammar, and draws from it a consequence for the "parallel" that § 72 builds and then breaks. Pure psychology relates to transcendental philosophy as natural science relates to pure mathematics, except at one point. The a priori of nature precedes nature and leaves it untouched, while the a priori of the soul is discovered by the subject that objectivates itself in the soul, so that discovering it changes what the soul contains.

> The world is not a hypothesis in the sense in which alone hypotheses have sense for positive science, say as hypotheses about the structure of the Milky Way systems; all hypotheses of positivity are hypotheses on the ground of the "hypothesis" world, for which to seek a grounding in the same positive-scientific sense would be a countersense. In fact, only from transcendental psychology or philosophy can we see and understand what is missing here, as a questioning of the "hypothesis" world: what that is, and what it demands to put it in question. Wholly outside the theme, in a certain way forgotten, out of validity together with the content of each occasion that gains sense and gives sense in us, are we all as the functioning subjects in and from whose functioning world is for us. One may not say that functioning subjectivity was discovered long ago, in the form of empiricist epistemology since Locke. For either this was psychology of positivity and spoke of human beings as the functioning subjects; then it presupposed the ground of the world and moved in a circle. Or it really put this ground in question, like Hume, who was in this as radical as Kant; then it plunged us into a paradoxical solipsism and skepticism, and in any case into a horrifying unintelligibility of the being of the world. The reason has become evident to us. The problem of the ground-validity of the world as world, which is what it is out of actual and possible cognition, out of actual and possible functioning subjectivity, had announced itself at all. But enormous difficulties had to be overcome in order not only to begin the method of epoché and reduction but to bring it to its full self-understanding, and thereby for the first time to discover the absolutely functioning subjectivity, not as the human but as the one that objectivates itself in the human, or at first in the human.

The sentence that ends the paragraph rests on a single noun that it never repeats. "Subjectivity" (*Subjektivität*) stands once, after "absolutely functioning", and the two phrases that follow are its attributes, each with the noun understood: not "the human" (*die menschliche*) but "the one that objectivates itself in the human" (*die in der menschlichen … sich selbst objektivierende*). Inside the second phrase the noun is elided again, after "in the human". So there is one subjectivity named twice, once as what it is and once as where it acts. "Human" is first refused as its attribute and then admitted as its location, in a dative governed by "in". The verb is reflexive and present: subjectivity objectivates itself, and is still doing so. Nothing outside it makes it an object, and the objectivation is not over. The construction repeats the one row 22 read in the "same I" sentence, "the same that in worldliness is human I", where "in worldliness" did the work that "in the human" does here. The participle adds a process to that sentence. The text after it says what the process yields: "every new transcendental cognition turns, by essential necessity, into an enrichment of the content of the human soul".

The paragraph reaches this formula through a dilemma, and the first horn explains the "not as the human". Empiricist epistemology since Locke either was "psychology of positivity" and "spoke of human beings as the functioning subjects", in which case it "presupposed the ground of the world and moved in a circle", or it questioned that ground as Hume did and ended in "solipsism and skepticism". The first horn is the reading the formula rules out: functioning subjectivity identified with human beings, who are already items on the ground of the world. It is the circle § 58 named in nearly the same words, to treat these problems "on naive-objective ground" "would be a countersense-laden circle" (Hua VI 208; row 24). "Not as the human" refuses that identification. "As the one that objectivates itself in the human" keeps the human as the form in which this subjectivity appears in the world, which the second horn could not keep. In Hume the ground was questioned and the world became unintelligible.

The scare quotes on "hypothesis" carry the same point at the level of the world. All the hypotheses of positive science stand "on the ground of the 'hypothesis' world". The quotation marks say that the world is called a hypothesis only by analogy, and the analogy fails: to seek a positive grounding for it "would be a countersense". The world is presupposed by every hypothesis and cannot be one. What is "missing" from the positive sciences is the subjects whose functioning makes the world be for us, and they are missing in the way the paragraph describes: "wholly outside the theme, in a certain way forgotten, … out of validity" (*Völlig außerthematisch, gewissermaßen vergessen*). The subject of that sentence is "we all as the functioning subjects". The forgotten subjectivity is ours. That is the condition under which it can be discovered "for the first time" (*allererst*) and still have been functioning all along.

The connectives claim a history. The problem "had announced itself at all" (*überhaupt hatte sich gemeldet*), which credits the tradition with the problem and not with its solution. The enormous difficulties were those of bringing the method "not only to begin" but "to its full self-understanding", and only "thereby" (*damit*) is absolutely functioning subjectivity discovered. Beginning the epoché is what Descartes, Hume and Kant did in the history of Part II. Its full self-understanding is the correction that the psychological path repeated at the end of § 71: "the radical and complete reduction to the *absolutely unique* ego" (*auf das absolut einzige ego*, emphasized in the file, Hua VI 260), the words of § 55's title (*das absolut einzige letztlich fungierende ego*, Hua VI 190; row 23). The "absolutely functioning subjectivity" of the formula is that ego, reached this time from psychology.

"Or at first in the human" (*oder zunächst in der menschlichen*) corrects the formula as it is made. "At first" admits that the human is the nearest form of the self-objectivation and leaves room for others. The surviving text of § 72 does not say which. Two neighbouring texts suggest the range. A question just after the p. 267 seam asks whether psychophysical dualism does not keep its right after all, and with it "a psychology of human beings and animals after the analogy of natural science" (Hua VI 268–271). The closing ⟨§ 73⟩ speaks of "absolute intersubjectivity (objectivated in the world as all-humanity)" (*objektiviert in der Welt als Allmenschheit*, Hua VI 276). Animal subjectivity lies below the single human being, and humanity as a whole above it, and the hedge leaves the form of each open. The *Cartesian Meditations* had already spoken of stages. There the pure soul is "a self-objectivation of the monad carried out in the monad, whose various stages are essential necessities if there are to be others for the monad at all" (*eine in der Monade sich vollziehende Selbstobjektivierung derselben*, Hua I 159).

The same section of the *Meditations* names the relation that the main commentary takes as the theme of § 72. Its title is "the parallelism (*Parallelität*) of inner-psychic and egological-transcendental explication", and the parallelism is "not hard to clarify": the pure soul is the monad's self-objectivation, so that "every transcendental-phenomenological analysis … can also be carried out on natural ground, by giving up the transcendental attitude", and "to a *pure* psychology corresponds a transcendental phenomenology, and conversely" (Hua I 159). In 1931 the self-objectivation grounds a correspondence. The surviving pages of § 72 do not use the word "parallelism" for the relation between psychology and transcendental philosophy. They state something stronger and something weaker. The stronger claim is an identity. Pure psychology "is nothing and can be nothing other than the same as what was sought in advance, with philosophical intent, as absolutely grounded philosophy", and "I as pure psychologist or transcendental philosopher" names one person with an "or" (Hua VI 265). The weaker claim is the parallel. When I "return to the natural attitude, with a change of vocation" (*unter Berufswechsel*), transcendental philosophy plays for psychology on the world-ground "the role of the a priori science", as pure mathematics does for natural science, and the text calls this "the genuine parallel (*echten Parallele*) between psychology and natural science" (Hua VI 265). Until here in Part III B the word was a charge, or was allowed only as "a formal parallel" between description and explanation in every science (Hua VI 227). In §§ 67–68 it named the error of Wundt and Brentano, a psychology built as a parallel to natural science (row 25). Here it names the true relation, and in the same sentence "an enormous difference shows itself" (*ein ungeheurer Unterschied*). The seam follows at once.

The difference survives in the pages after the formula, and it follows from the formula. Since every transcendental cognition enriches the soul, the progress of phenomenology shows "the abysmal difference between mathematics, between every a priori science of the world, and phenomenology as a priori psychology". "The a priori of nature 'precedes the being of the world'", but no progress in mathematics "could influence the being of nature itself"; nature is in itself mathematical, "however much or little we know of mathematics". "For the world as world, which also contains spiritual beings, the being-ahead (*Vorwegsein*) is a countersense; a Laplacean spirit is unthinkable there", and an ontology by which every possible world would be knowable *more geometrico*, "this idea that still seduced Leibniz", is "a nonsense" (Hua VI 268–271). The parallel breaks where the prepositions of the formula said it would. Mathematics is not the self-objectivation of nature. Transcendental subjectivity is what objectivates itself in the soul, and so its a priori cannot stand ahead of its object, finished, while the soul waits to be measured against it. The self-objectivation is still going on, and knowing it is part of it. The reformed psychologist, returning to the world with a change of vocation, finds the human being richer than before, because the subjectivity that objectivates itself in the human has come to know itself.

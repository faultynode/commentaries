# Michel Henry, *L'essence de la manifestation*, read against Heidegger's "Hegels Begriff der Erfahrung" (GA 5)

Henry is cited by section and paragraph (R §11 ¶9), counting the typographical paragraphs of each section, since the edition used carries no page numbers; his notes are cited by number (n. 46). The companion commentary on Henry's book sometimes runs two of his paragraphs together (in §8 it counts thirteen units where the text has sixteen); this commentary follows the text. Heidegger's essay on Hegel's concept of experience Henry cites from the German *Holzwege* of 1950 ("H" and a page) and translates himself. This commentary reads it in GA 5 (115–208), whose margins carry the 1950 pagination, and gives each locus once as "H n / GA 5 p", then in that form. Every rendering of the essay in Henry's book is his own, so every departure from the German is his move; Wolfgang Brokmeier's French translation appeared in 1962, a year before the book, and nothing shows whether Henry consulted it. Hegel's *Phenomenology* Henry quotes in Jean Hyppolite's translation ("PhE" with volume and page). The Introduction to the *Phenomenology* is printed whole at the head of the essay, and Henry's quotations from it are checked against Hegel's German there, cited by the essay's numbering of Hegel's sixteen sections and the GA 5 page. Hyppolite's French is not available to this commentary, so where Henry's French departs from Hegel's German the departure is laid to Henry only when his argument rests on it. The commentary on the essay is cited as Commentary P, by its units (headed by GA page), and its section headings are its own, not Heidegger's. The marginal notes GA 5 prints from Heidegger's copy of the 1950 edition, and the Hegel lectures and treatises published after 1963, are retrospective and marked so. All English translations are working renderings made for this commentary. Each unit opens by restating Henry's move in his own terms; everything after that is assigned explicitly to Henry, to Heidegger, to Hegel or to this commentary.

## Section I. Elucidation of the concept of phenomenon: ontological monism

The pair has its own traps. The essay speaks in three voices: Hegel's Introduction, printed at its head; Heidegger's paraphrase of its sixteen sections in Hegel's terms (consciousness, appearing, natural and real knowledge, the absolute); and Heidegger's own frame, in which Hegel's absolute is the subjectity of the subject, its Parousia is presence "in the manner of representation" (in der Weise der Repräsentation, H 134 / GA 5 146), and modern metaphysics is one epoch in a history of being. Henry quotes all three as "Heidegger says", so a quotation has to be assigned before his use of it can be judged. Where the essay expounds what consciousness and the subject mean from Descartes to Hegel, Henry's Section I reads an eidetic of consciousness that he holds to be Heidegger's own. That reading may be right, since Heidegger's thinking of appearing may stay inside the frame he expounds, but it is Henry's thesis and has to be argued at each locus. Heidegger coins subjectity (*Subjektität*) for the being of the subject as reflected subject-object relation; Henry renders it "subjectivité" (subjectivity), which erases the mark of the epoch. The essay's *Dasein* is Hegel's existence or determinate presence, which Heidegger glosses as presencing; it is not the being-there of *Being and Time*. Heidegger applies the word "being" to what Hegel calls actuality, appearing and absoluteness, and says so (GA 5 154–155), while for Hegel being (*Sein*) names the still-untrue immediacy of the object; when Henry writes "l'être" (being) against a quotation, the word's owner has to be fixed. The essay maps natural consciousness onto "ontic consciousness" and its unthought representing of objectness onto the ontological (H 161–163); Henry quotes the mapping approvingly in Section I and contests it in Section II, and the later verdict must not be read back into the earlier use. The reversal of consciousness (*Umkehrung*) is Hegel's phrase before it is Heidegger's gloss. As to chronology, the essay comes from seminars of 1942–43 and was first printed in 1950. Henry could not know the marginalia (1977), the Hegel lecture course of 1930–31 (GA 32, 1980) or the Hegel treatises collected in GA 68 (1993). He reads the essay with the Kantbuch of 1929 and *Being and Time* as one "philosophy of being", so where the turn between them matters, above all on presence, history and forgetting, it has to be marked. Section I cites the essay nine times. Far more of it is present without citation, and §8 shows the pattern: Henry's account of the essence takes over the essay's vocabulary of the Parousia, the means, real knowledge, shining and the formal before it names the essay once.

### § 8. The elucidation of the essence of the phenomenon, central task of phenomenology

**¶1.** Phenomenology is the description, prior to all theory and free of presupposition, of whatever offers itself as existing; it rejects hypotheses and explanatory principles and treats scientific theories, their explanatory value bracketed, as data in their turn, so protecting science from becoming a new metaphysics. No GA 5 locus is engaged, and the definition is the one the essay sets aside. Heidegger says that in Hegel's title "phenomenology" names neither a discipline nor "a particular kind of research concerned to describe the given" (eine besondere Art von Forschung, der daran liegt, das Gegebene zu beschreiben, H 185 / GA 5 201); it names the Dasein of spirit, "the Parousia of the absolute holding sway" (die Parusie des Absoluten in ihrem Walten, H 185 / GA 5 202). Henry begins from the Husserlian sense that the essay excludes, and by ¶9 he speaks of phenomenology as seeking "the Parousia of the absolute". The section passes from one sense to the other without marking the passage. Whether the Husserlian sense survives the passage is a question Henry's argument raises without stating it.

**¶2.** Phenomenology's positive sense must be fixed on the phenomenon: Husserl's principle that every originary giving intuition is a source of right, Scheler's promise that metaphysics, being and life are restored "as absolute givens". Absolute is the appearance "insofar as it is an appearance". No GA 5 locus is engaged. The unit fixes Henry's first use of "absolute": the Husserlian-Schelerian absolute of the indubitable given, what "escapes the reduction". By ¶6 the word will name appearing itself, and by ¶9 the Parousia, which is the essay's use. Henry's term changes owner within the section, and the change, not the Husserlian starting point, carries the argument toward GA 5.

**¶3.** Phenomenology replaces the problem of error with the more radical problem of illusion: the content of an appearance is always "true", illusion transports a state of affairs (*Sachverhalt*) into a layer of being not its own, and error is a special case of illusion (Scheler). The task is to let the appearance appear as it appears. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The unit prepares the use of H 129 in ¶7. Scheler and the essay both refuse to dismiss semblance as mere falsity. In the essay, semblance "dismissed only as the false" (nur als das Falsche abgewiesen) "has not yet even been heard in its shining" (ist noch nicht einmal in seinem Scheinen vernommen, H 129 / GA 5 141). The grounds differ. Scheler locates the truth of illusion in its content, misplaced; Heidegger locates it in the shining of the absolute, which even semblance needs. Henry's ¶7 passes from Scheler's ground to Heidegger's without remarking the change of ground.

**¶4.** The absolute sense of phenomenology rests on the presence of the thing, which a philosophy of consciousness turns into a dogmatism of intentionality. Yet every appearance is surrounded by a zone of shadow: its implications refer to what is not there, their explication costs the given its presence, and there is no intuitive totality. Does the finitude by which an appearance must *always* be elucidated point to a deeper finitude by which elucidation is *never* possible? No GA 5 locus is cited, but the structure of the paragraph is set out in the essay. Heidegger lists the ways in which something can appear: emerging side by side with other things; occurring and, in occurring, pointing to something "that does not yet come forth" (was noch nicht hervor-kommt); and being the advance glimpse "of something that has not itself appeared or never will appear" (was selbst nicht erschienen ist oder nie erscheinen wird, H 128–129 / GA 5 140). Henry's two finitudes are the second and third of these modes: the always-still-to-be-elucidated and the never-elucidable. Heidegger's verdict on them bears directly on Henry's question. These modes "remain inappropriate" (bleiben ungemäß) to the appearing of science, which "can never spread itself out as itself in them" (kann in ihnen sich niemals als sich selbst ausbreiten, H 129 / GA 5 140). Henry's question whether horizonal appearing can ever be total is the question the essay settles for Hegel by moving science's appearing out of these modes. Henry does not cite the page, and the coincidence of structure does not show that he drew on it.

**¶5.** The law that binds appearance to a process of explication is foreign to natural consciousness, for which appearances merely succeed one another, and to phenomenology as positivism. It is the law of the essence: what founds the presence of what appears is the act of appearing. Natural consciousness laments the temporal destiny of what befalls it; the content suffers that destiny as a foreign law, for one and the same law posits it in appearance and tears it from existence, "and the content does not contain it". No GA 5 locus is cited. The paragraph's figure comes from Hegel's eighth section, printed in the essay. There, what is confined to natural life "is driven beyond by another, and this being-torn-out is its death" (wird durch ein anderes darüber hinausgetrieben, und dies Hinausgerissenwerden ist sein Tod), whereas consciousness "is for itself its concept" and so "suffers this violence ... from itself" (leidet also diese Gewalt ... von ihm selbst, GA 5 121–122). Henry's content, torn from existence by a law it does not contain, has the place of Hegel's natural life, not of consciousness. Heidegger's gloss carries Hegel's point further: the tearing-out (*Hinausriß*) is the death of natural consciousness, but the violence "comes from consciousness itself" (kommt jedoch aus dem Bewußtsein selbst), and it is "the will of the absolute" (der Wille des Absoluten, H 147–148 / GA 5 160–161). Henry keeps Hegel's split between what bears the law and what suffers it, and moves the line: the law belongs to the essence, and nothing that appears contains it. His "natural consciousness ... for which appearances succeed one another" is the essay's natural consciousness, which "everywhere and always finds only beings, what appears" (überall und immer nur Seiendes, Erscheinendes findet, H 144 / GA 5 157); his lament answers loosely to Hegel's anxiety before the truth and Heidegger's "anxiety about its own subsistence" (Angst um sein eigenes Bestehen, H 149 / GA 5 162).

**¶6.** The dependence of what appears on the act of appearing makes the latter the theme. Phenomenology must pass beyond the existent to its being, the act of appearing; it is the science of phenomena "in their reality", the phenomenological reduction is one with the eidetic, and what it frees is the essence of every phenomenon. Being and truth are contemporaneous (n. 6: *SZ* 230), and this truth Henry also calls the Form, the formal, properly ontological element to which every phenomenon belongs. No GA 5 locus is cited. Three of Henry's terms are the essay's. "Phenomena in their reality" is Heidegger's reading of Hegel's real knowledge: "the thinghood of the thing, the reality of the real, is appearing itself" (die Sachheit der Sache, die Realität des Realen ist das Erscheinen selbst, H 157 / GA 5 171). The Form is what Heidegger says Hegel calls "the formal" (das Formelle): the look of what appears, its εἶδος, "the beingness of beings" (die Seiendheit des Seienden), which is never "for" natural consciousness (H 173–174 / GA 5 189). And the passage from the existent to its being as appearing restates Heidegger's sentence that the way consciousness presences "is appearing" (ist das Erscheinen, H 166 / GA 5 180). The departure lies in scope. In the essay, appearing is the being of *consciousness*, the way in which "being, which since antiquity means presencing" (Sein bedeutet von altersher: anwesen) is determined once beings have become subject and object. Heidegger dates the identification and limits it: beingness as presence is "only one, though decisive, way of being, which by no means necessarily appears only as the presence of what is present" (nur eine, wenngleich entscheidende Weise des Seins, das keineswegs notwendig nur als Anwesenheit des Anwesenden erscheint, H 142 / GA 5 155). Henry makes the identification of being with appearing the timeless content of the reduction. What Heidegger presents as the being of beings in the epoch of subjectity, Henry takes up as the eidetic of the phenomenon as such. Henry reads the essay's exposition of modern metaphysics as an eidetic here, before he has quoted it once.

**¶7.** The problematic of the essence is universal: error, illusion and truth have a common ground, for the essence is "the act of appearing that even appearance needs in order to be mere appearance" (n. 7). This answers Hegel's question how true knowledge can prove itself against untrue knowledge (n. 8). The untrue lacks only an understanding of the sense of its own affirmation: that it *is* makes it, in an absolute sense, true knowledge, since knowledge is manifestation. True and untrue knowledge are equal before absolute knowledge, and truth in the absolute sense, like the sun on the just and the unjust, promotes illusion and "reality" alike. The quotation is from the essay (R §8 ¶7, n. 7 / H 129 / GA 5 141). Heidegger writes: "The appearing of science has its necessity in that shining which even seeming needs in order to be a mere semblance" (Das Erscheinen der Wissenschaft hat seine Notwendigkeit in jenem Scheinen, dessen sogar der Anschein bedarf, um ein bloßer Schein zu sein). Henry renders shining (*Scheinen*) as "l'acte d'apparaître" (the act of appearing), his word for appearing (*Erscheinen*), and both seeming (*Anschein*) and semblance (*Schein*) as "apparence" (appearance). The paragraph Henry quotes from turns on the four words he collapses: science appears (*erscheint*), the shining of the ray (*Strahl*) lights even semblance, and the presentation must turn against mere seeming. Henry also cuts the subject. The sentence states why *science's* appearing is necessary, and with it why science must present appearing knowledge; Henry makes it a definition of the essence. The next sentences of the essay define the shining: "appearing out of this shining of the ray means presencing in the full radiance of self-presenting representation" (Anwesen im vollen Glanz der sich präsentierenden Repräsentation, H 130 / GA 5 141). The formula Henry adopts as the neutral essence of every phenomenon is glossed on the same page by the representation that his §11 will make the mark of monism. The Hegel quotation (n. 8: *PhE* I 68) is from Hegel's fourth section: by such an assurance science "would declare its being to be its strength; but untrue knowledge appeals just as well to the fact that it is" (erklärte sie ihr *Sein* für ihre Kraft; aber das unwahre Wissen beruft sich eben so darauf, daß *es ist*, GA 5 118–119). Hegel's subject is science (*sie*); Henry's question makes it "true knowledge", and in his French the pronoun follows. For Hegel the bare "it is" is no argument, since "one dry assurance counts just as much as another" (*ein* trockenes Versichern gilt aber gerade soviel als ein anderes); science has to present appearing knowledge. Henry inverts the objection into the answer: the bare "is" of untrue knowledge, read as its manifestation, makes it absolutely true. Heidegger comes halfway. Untrue knowledge appears "as the untrue, that is, not yet true, knowledge within the truth of absolute knowledge" (als das unwahre, d. h. noch nicht wahre Wissen innerhalb der Wahrheit des absoluten Wissens, H 129 / GA 5 141), and the presentation brings "the pure shining of the ray" to light "even in mere semblance" (sogar im bloßen Schein das reine Scheinen des Strahls, ibid.), which is the source Henry's sun most nearly recalls. But Heidegger's "not yet" carries the essay's whole movement, and he says expressly that natural consciousness and real knowledge "are indeed the same ... but for that very reason they are not equal" (sind in der Tat das Selbe ... Aber beide sind eben deshalb nicht das Gleiche, H 138 / GA 5 150). Henry's "equality of true and untrue knowledge" is the word Heidegger refuses. The refusal does not settle the matter against Henry. Heidegger's "not yet" belongs to the Darstellung, the path of experience that Henry's Section II will reject (R §20), and Henry's equality is already the thesis of that rejection: the essence is wholly present in untrue knowledge and has no path to travel.

**¶8.** Natural consciousness forgets the essence that gives it presence, and true or real knowledge is the knowledge that recognizes the essence's work: phenomenology, the science of the essence of the phenomenon, which Heidegger can call ontology because he understands being as the essence of the phenomenon. First phenomenology, whose problems are those of the essence, must be distinguished from second phenomenologies of regions; ego and time may belong to the internal structure of the essence. The ontology claim is referred to *Being and Time* (n. 9 in ¶9: *SZ* 38). The pairing of natural consciousness with "true or real knowledge" is the essay's. Hegel's sixth section says that natural consciousness will prove to be "only concept of knowledge, or not real knowledge" (nur Begriff des Wissens oder nicht reales Wissen), while it takes itself for real knowledge (GA 5 119). Heidegger reads "real" through reality as beingness: real knowledge is the knowledge that "represents beings in their beingness (reality), what appears in its appearing" (das Seiende in seiner Seiendheit (Realität), das Erscheinende in seinem Erscheinen, vorstellt, H 136 / GA 5 148). Henry adopts Heidegger's reading of Hegel's term, not Hegel's own use, in which "real knowledge" is first of all what natural consciousness wrongly takes itself to be. His "forgets" is stronger than the essay's verbs. In the essay natural consciousness "does not heed being, and must heed it all the same" (achtet ... nicht auf das Sein und muß es gleichwohl achten, H 136 / GA 5 148); it cannot help representing beingness, since without "the light of being" (das Licht des Seins) it could not even lose itself in beings. Heidegger's word is an inattention that presupposes being, not oblivion. The division between first and second phenomenology repeats the essay's account of first philosophy, beside which the other sciences are "science in the second rank" (die Wissenschaft in zweiter Linie, H 179 / GA 5 194), and of Hegel's two parts of science (H 179–184 / GA 5 195–200). Heidegger adds what Henry omits: first philosophy is onto-theology, and the ground of its unity "remains so hidden that it is not even asked about" (H 179 / GA 5 195). Henry's surmise that the ego belongs to the internal structure of absolute truth has a counterpart in the essay's conclusion that "our essence itself belongs to the Parousia of the absolute" (gehört unser Wesen selbst zur Parusie des Absoluten, H 176 / GA 5 191). There "our" essence is the "for us" of those who perform the reversal, not an ego; Henry leaves open which kind of belonging he means.

**¶9.** The object of first phenomenology is internal to it: phenomenology is the "how" of treating what is to be treated, and what is to be treated is itself a "how", the manner in which reality manifests itself. Heidegger's *Being and Time* joins ontology and phenomenology as object and mode of treatment (n. 9); here the object is the mode of treatment. Phenomenology offers itself as a means of bringing near "the essence of presence, the absolute insofar as it is the Parousia", but the means is the absolute itself, and "phenomenology seeks the Parousia of the absolute on the ground of the absolute understood as the Parousia." The cited text is *Being and Time* §7. The paragraph's conclusion is the essay's argument on Hegel's first section, uncited. Hegel rejects the representation of knowing as an instrument or medium, and says in a subordinate clause that the absolute "is and wants to be in and for itself already near us" (an und für sich schon bei uns wäre und sein wollte, GA 5 116). Heidegger names this being-near-us the Parousia and draws Henry's consequence: knowledge of the absolute "stands in the ray of light ... and is so in its essence the ray itself, no mere medium" (steht im Strahl des Lichtes ... und ist so in seinem Wesen der Strahl selbst, kein bloßes Medium, H 120 / GA 5 131), and the difficulty of science is that "within the Parousia and so out of it" (innerhalb der Parusie und so aus ihr) we must bring our relation to it forth (H 126–127 / GA 5 138). Henry's chiasmus, the Parousia sought on the ground of the Parousia, restates that sentence. Henry joins two different texts. *Being and Time* §7 makes phenomenology a methodological concept, the "how" of research; the essay denies that knowing is a means at all and makes Hegel's phenomenology the Parousia itself, not a method. In Henry's ¶9 the "how" of §7 becomes the means of Hegel's first section, which the essay has already dissolved into the absolute. What the essay attributes to Hegel's absolute, Henry assigns to phenomenology in the Husserlian sense with which he began. The fusion is productive, and it is Henry's: neither text says that the method of phenomenology is the Parousia.

**¶10.** Applied to the essence of the phenomenon, phenomenology moves in a circle, which is the sign of its absolute character. It has a presupposition, the ground itself; it is reflection on itself; "phenomenology is its own object." No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry's circle answers to two passages of the essay. Hegel's propositions that the absolute alone is true and the true alone is absolute "cannot be grounded", Heidegger says, because "they have posited what itself first grounds" (Sie haben das gesetzt, was selbst erst gründet, H 124 / GA 5 135); and at the end "the ring has closed" (Der Ring hat sich geschlossen, H 188 / GA 5 205). The divergence is in the last sentence. Heidegger insists that spirit "is the subject of phenomenology, not its object" (ist das Subjekt der Phänomenologie, nicht ihr Gegenstand, H 185 / GA 5 201), and reads the genitives of Hegel's title as subjective before they are objective (H 182 / GA 5 198). Henry's "its own object" keeps the form of an object. A phenomenology that reflects on itself and has itself for object has, in the essay's terms, the structure of subjectity, "the subject-object relation reflected into itself" (der in sich reflektierten Subjekt-Objektbeziehung, H 134 / GA 5 146). Henry states the self-relation of first phenomenology in the form the essay assigns to the metaphysics of the subject, and ¶12 will ask whether that form, representation, can hold.

**¶11.** The sense of phenomenology is decided by the nature of its ground: the "how" of its approach is subordinate to the "how" of the reality it approaches, which comes to meet us. Must not the manner of our reception be the same as the manner in which the absolute comes into us? "The eye by which the absolute looks at us is the same as that by which we look at the absolute." What, then, is vision? No GA 5 locus is cited, and the formula of the eye is Eckhart's (R §39). The essay asks Henry's questions and answers them for Hegel. Reception and coming are the same: the presentation "follows the appearing of appearing knowledge that comes to meet it" (folgt dem ihr entgegenkommenden Erscheinen, H 150 / GA 5 164), and our contribution is "willed" by the absolute (die Zutat will demgemäß den Willen des Absoluten, H 175 / GA 5 191). The single eye is the ray that knowing "gives back" (gibt ihn zurück, H 120 / GA 5 131). Vision is the having-seen, "vidi", in which seeing is thought "as having-before-oneself in representing" (als Vor-sich-haben im Vor-stellen, H 133 / GA 5 145). The essay's answer to "what is vision?" is representing. Henry leaves his question open, and his book will answer it against the essay, by an eye that does not see.

**¶12.** The question of the essence of the phenomenon governs all the problems of first phenomenology. The essence is not foreign to natural consciousness: insofar as natural consciousness is a consciousness, "an être-là" (a being-there), absolute knowledge is immanent to it. But does true knowledge of the essence consist in representing it? It could only if the essence itself consisted in representation. No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry's "être-là" is Hyppolite's rendering of Hegel's *Dasein*, and here the word carries Hegel's sense, the existence of consciousness. It matches the essay's "the immediate Dasein of consciousness or of knowledge is appearing" (Das unmittelbare Dasein des Bewußtseins oder des Wissens ist das Erscheinen, H 134 / GA 5 146), not the being-there of *Being and Time*. The immanence of absolute knowledge in natural consciousness is the essay's thesis: natural consciousness "cannot avoid co-representing the being of beings in general" (kann nicht umhin, das Sein des Seienden im allgemeinen mit vorzustellen, H 136 / GA 5 148), and is thus "in itself beyond itself and yet not outside itself" (an ihm selbst über sich hinaus und gleichwohl nicht außerhalb seiner, H 146 / GA 5 159). Henry's conditional is the point. The essay answers it affirmatively, for Hegel: real knowledge "represents" beings in their beingness (H 136), the presentation is a representing of knowledge "in its appearing" (in seinem Erscheinen vorstellen, H 134 / GA 5 146), and subjectity "*is* presence in the manner of representation" (H 134). Henry asks as an open question what the essay asserts as the meaning of consciousness in absolute metaphysics. §11 will quote the assertion as Heidegger's account of the essence.

**¶13.** Husserl's "in the psychic sphere there is no difference between being and appearance" remains vague until appearing has been defined. Likewise, if one says that something in the essence does not appear, the not-appearing remains as undetermined as manifestation; it may be not-appearing only relative to a one-sided concept of the essence, and a fuller determination might reveal a more fundamental "how" that gives presence, "though of another order", to what was thought not to appear. No GA 5 locus is cited. The second target is Heidegger's concealment, and the essay is one of its sites. There the being whose unconcealment withdraws in the epoch of subjectity (ob und inwiefern die Subjektität ein eigenes Wesensgeschick des Seins ist, darin sich die Unverborgenheit des Seins ... *entzieht*, H 176 / GA 5 192) and the "hidden way in which being itself reveals and conceals itself" (verborgenen Weise, in der das Sein selbst sich enthüllt und verbirgt, H 143 / GA 5 155) are what does not appear. Henry and Heidegger converge on a suspicion: both doubt that appearing as presence is the whole of manifestation. Heidegger traces the presence of the modern representation to "a still unthought essence of a hidden time" (aus einem noch ungedachten Wesen einer verborgenen Zeit, H 142 / GA 5 155). Henry suspects that what is called non-appearing is non-appearing only relative to a one-sided concept of appearing. They diverge on what lies beyond. For Heidegger it is withdrawal and a time not yet thought; for Henry it is another order of presence, which his book calls immanence and the invisible. Henry's question re-describes Heidegger's concealment as a symptom of a unilateral concept of phenomenality. The essay could answer that it already limits presence to one destiny of being. Henry could reply that Heidegger names what exceeds presence only by its withdrawal.

**¶14.** The determination of the essence must also settle its relation to the existent it grounds: whether the passage beyond positivism is without return, and whether the essence reached in that passage can close upon itself, abstract from ontic determination, and keep its absoluteness in that autonomy. No GA 5 locus is cited. The essay answers the question for Hegel, and answers it negatively. The ontological comparing "does not separate itself from natural consciousness" (trennt sich nicht vom natürlichen Bewußtsein ab, H 164 / GA 5 179); in the reversal, natural consciousness "should not leave its sojourn in the midst of beings" (soll ... den Aufenthalt inmitten des Seienden auch nicht verlassen, H 190 / GA 5 206). Henry will quote that sentence at §13 ¶6. Here he puts as an open alternative what the essay closes, and §16 will lean toward the autonomy the essay rules out.

**¶15.** Only the determination of the internal structure of the essence can delimit the field of first phenomenology and say whether, and in what sense, the phenomenology of the ego belongs to it. No GA 5 locus is cited. The essay delimits that field with the "for us". The "near us" of the first section is revealed at the end as "not without us" (nicht ohne uns), and "we" are those "who sceptically heed the being of beings" (die skeptisch das Sein des Seienden eigens beachten, H 188 / GA 5 204–205). The subject Heidegger admits into the Parousia is no ego: "the subject is not the subjective in the sense of the self-seeking egoistic" (Das Subjekt ist freilich nicht das Subjektive in der Bedeutung des auf sich erpichten Egoistischen, H 121 / GA 5 132). Henry's question whether the ego belongs to the first problems is put in terms the essay would refuse, and that refusal is the ground on which Henry will contest the philosophy of being.

**¶16.** The determination of the essence is the central task, all the more urgent because philosophy has always posed and solved its problems on the basis of an unexplicated concept of the phenomenon; when the essence finally became an explicit theme, the problematic only ratified, by carrying them to the absolute, the ontological presuppositions that had guided and misled philosophy almost without interruption since its origin. No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry's thesis has the form of the essay's history of metaphysics. In the essay "the difference holding sway in the ambiguity of ὄν remains unthought, in such a way that this remaining unthought constitutes the essence of metaphysics" (die in der Zweideutigkeit des ὂν waltende Differenz ungedacht bleibt, so zwar, daß dieses Ungedachtbleiben das Wesen der Metaphysik ausmacht, H 163 / GA 5 177). Hegel's absolute metaphysics is there the completion that takes the land of self-certainty "completely into possession" (vollständig in den Besitz, H 121 / GA 5 132) without seeing its ground. Henry keeps the form and changes both terms. The unthought is not the difference of being and beings but a concept of the phenomenon. The completion that ratifies it is not Hegel alone but every explicit ontology, Heidegger's own included. Henry turns Heidegger's pattern of reading upon Heidegger. One divergence will matter at R §21: for Heidegger the unthought has a history, while Henry's presupposition is said to have guided philosophy "since the origin", with no history in the essence of the presupposition itself.

### § 9. The unilateral determination of the essence of phenomenon and the concept of "phenomenological distance"

**¶1.** Gassendi objects to Descartes that nothing acts on itself: the eye sees itself only in a mirror, because between eye and mirror there is a space. A contemporary author (Malverne) says the same of modern ontology: to see, one needs a field, "immediate knowledge is in reality always mediate", and "being must be at a distance from itself". Henry reads in the two texts the persistence through history of a common ontological horizon, which every revolution of thought, the Cartesian included, has left intact because it took place inside it. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's historical claim has the shape of the essay's. There Descartes "enters the land of modern philosophy, the subiectum as ego cogito, but at bottom does not see the landscape at all" (zwar das Land der neuzeitlichen Philosophie, das subiectum als das ego cogito, betritt, aber die Landschaft im Grunde überhaupt nicht sieht, H 139 / GA 5 151), and beingness as presence prevails "from the beginning of Greek thought to Nietzsche's doctrine of eternal recurrence" (seit dem Beginn des griechischen Denkens bis zu Nietzsches Lehre, H 142 / GA 5 155). Gassendi's mirror also has its counterpart in the essay's exposition of knowing, which "reflects" the ray (strahlt ihn wider, H 120 / GA 5 131) and represents the object by setting it "toward itself and so back" (auf sich zu und so zurückstellt, H 134 / GA 5 145). The difference lies in what persists. Heidegger's persisting presence is a history whose epochs change the sense of being; Henry's horizon persists "intact" beneath variable philosophical material, a presupposition without epochs. The difference will become Henry's thesis at R §21.

**¶2.** The presupposition in these texts is vague, and Malverne compromises its ontological sense by measuring distance from the cornea, as though it were an ontic property. Yet distance enters as a universal condition with eidetic necessity, and what it conditions is not only knowledge but the possibility of a phenomenon in general, its essence. As identified with the essence, it deserves the name "phenomenological distance", the ultimate ontological presupposition beneath these texts. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The concept Henry names here has a precise counterpart in the essay's exposition of consciousness. Consciousness "sets something off from itself, representing it, yet sets what it has set off toward itself" (setzt, vorstellend, etwas von sich ab, jedoch das Abgesetzte auf sich zu); it is "a distinguishing that is none" (ein Unterscheiden, das keines ist), and this ambiguity "is the essence of representing" (ist das Wesen des Vorstellens, H 153 / GA 5 167). Henry never cites the page. His §10 will build the doubling of being from the same structure, and his §11 will quote from H 133–134 the representing it defines. In the essay that structure is the essence of *representing*, that is, of consciousness in modern metaphysics. Henry makes it the essence of phenomenality as such. Hegel's own text gives the spatial figure Henry wants to exclude: consciousness's beyond is posited with the limited, "even if only, as in spatial intuition, *beside* the limited" (wäre es auch nur, wie im räumlichen Anschauen, *neben* dem Beschränkten, Hegel's eighth section, GA 5 122).

**¶3.** Phenomenological distance is not spatial distance, measured or lived. Lived distance rests on an originary spatiality, the phenomenological milieu opened so that space can manifest itself, and this is the world, "the phenomenon of all phenomena, their visibility as such". Space rests in the world, not the world in space (Heidegger, *Being and Time*). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The world-as-visibility comes from *Being and Time*. Its counterpart in the essay is the site of appearing, which §11 will quote: "the site of appearing, as its stage, is formed *in* appearing and through it" (die Stätte des Erscheinens als dessen Schauplatz *im* Erscheinen und durch dieses selbst gebildet wird, H 134 / GA 5 146), and the reversal "opens the compass of the site in which appearing appears to itself" (öffnet den Umfang der Stätte, in der das Erscheinen sich erscheint, H 176 / GA 5 191). The two are not the same. The world of *Being and Time* is a structure of Dasein's being-in-the-world; the site of the essay is formed by consciousness's appearing to itself, and opened by "our contribution". Henry's identification of distance with world prepares his reading of the site as world (R §11), and joins the two texts before he has cited either.

**¶4.** Distance is a power, not something found: the originary work of transcendence that unfolds the horizon, "de-severance" (*Ent-fernung*) taken, as Heidegger wants, "in an active and transitive sense" (n. 13: *SZ* 105). Before it is being-distant, distance is what distances: the ontological event that makes the horizon arise within which approach and withdrawal occur. All presence is presence from the horizon. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's power is the transcendence of *Being and Time*, and the essay has a different subject for the act. In it the opening is done by the reversal of consciousness, "our contribution", which "overtakes everything that appears ... encompasses it and opens the compass of the site" (überholt ... alles Erscheinende ... umfängt es und öffnet den Umfang der Stätte, H 176 / GA 5 191). Heidegger subordinates that act to the absolute's will: the contribution "wills the will of the absolute" (H 175 / GA 5 191). The active sense of distancing that Henry finds in 1927 is in 1942–43 the absolute's coming, which our contribution only answers. Henry will read the two as one transcendence, across the turn between them.

**¶5.** Nearness and remoteness are modes within a more fundamental remoteness, the transcendental distancing that is the essence of the phenomenon. Nearness taken ontologically is one with it: "the far is the essence of the near". Heidegger: man is "a being of distance", and only through the original distances he forms in his transcendence does true nearness to things grow (n. 14: *Vom Wesen des Grundes*). Husserl's degrees of nearness and "absolute proximity" concern contents; ontologically, nearness is always absolute. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay supplies a counterweight Henry does not consider. Its Parousia is nearness: the absolute's "being-near-us" (Bei-uns-sein), and "according to its absoluteness the absolute is near us of itself" (Gemäß seiner Absolutheit ist das Absolute von sich aus bei uns, H 130 / GA 5 141). There nearness is not founded on a remoteness that man forms in transcending. It is the absolute's will, and the difficulty lies in bringing our relation to it forth "within the Parousia" (H 127 / GA 5 138). The 1929 essay that Henry quotes makes man the being of distance; the 1942–43 essay makes the absolute the one that comes near. Henry's thesis that the far is the essence of the near holds for *Vom Wesen des Grundes*; for the Hegel essay it would have to be argued that the Parousia presupposes a distance, which is what Henry's later readings of it will claim (R §11 ¶25).

**¶6.** Phenomenological distance differs also from "existential distance", the nearness of things according to our concern. Care lives in the surrounding world but cares only for what happens within it, never for the world as such; what is near is always a content, never nearness itself, which is farthest from us because it is never the object of care. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The thesis repeats *Being and Time*'s ontically nearest and ontologically farthest. The essay states its own version without care. The truth "behind" which natural consciousness never gets "is itself, in truth, the foreground of the light within which every kind of knowledge and consciousness already stands" (der Vordergrund des Lichtes, innerhalb dessen jede Art von Wissen und Bewußtsein als ein Gesehenhaben schon steht, H 164 / GA 5 178). In the essay what hides the nearest is opinion (*Meinen*), the natural consciousness that holds what it represents as its own; in Henry what hides it is care. Henry's terms are those of 1927. The essay's ground for the same structure is not care but representing.

**¶7.** Categorial determinations rest on existential structures; being-distant owes what it is to original distancing. The unelaborated concept of distance is how natural, pre-philosophical consciousness pictures the condition of the phenomenon, using elements that presuppose the essence: action at a distance, species, reflection and image are symbols of their own ground. "Heidegger thinks the same thing as Gassendi, but he thinks it in its ontological truth." No GA 5 locus is engaged. The claim that natural consciousness pictures the essence by means that presuppose it is the essay's thesis on natural consciousness, which represents beingness without knowing it (H 136 / GA 5 148), turned into a reading of the history of philosophy. Henry's last sentence applies to Heidegger the procedure the essay applies to Hegel. Heidegger justifies calling Hegel's appearing and absoluteness "being" by the claim that the language of thinking "calls what another thinking thought into the brightness of its own thinking, in order to free the other into its own essence" (ruft jedoch das Gedachte eines anderen Denkens in die Helle ihres Denkens, um das andere in sein eigenes Wesen frei zu geben, H 143 / GA 5 155), and he anticipates the charge that this "looks like arbitrariness" (wie eine Willkür aussehen, ibid.). Henry reads Gassendi into Heidegger's ontological truth, and he will read Heidegger into the truth of monism, in the same way and open to the same charge. Henry's resemblance between Gassendi and Heidegger is not an influence, and he does not claim one; he claims a common presupposition. Whether a presupposition shared by a seventeenth-century empiricist and a twentieth-century ontology is one presupposition, or two that look alike only in Henry's vocabulary, is what his Section I has to show.

### § 10. Phenomenological distance and the doubling of being: presence and alienation

**¶1.** Being is a phenomenon only at a distance from itself: phenomenological distance institutes the interval in which being can "appear to itself", and the manifestation of being is its existence, being outside its being. Fichte's wall: its "is" differs from the wall as something independent, and the "is" is immediately existence (nn. 16–17: Fichte, *Die Anweisung zum seligen Leben*). The ontological argument is the reading of being's phenomenal condition. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's "s'apparaître à lui-même" (appear to itself) is the word he will use in Section II to render the essay's self-appearing (*Sicherscheinen*, H 170, 173), and the essay gives it to consciousness: consciousness "accomplishes its self-appearing in such a way that in self-appearing it forms for itself the site of appearing" (vollbringt sein Sicherscheinen selbst und zwar so, daß es im Sicherscheinen für sich die Stätte des Erscheinens bildet, H 148 / GA 5 162). Fichte's natural consciousness, which has "no time to contemplate the 'is'", is the essay's natural consciousness, which "does not heed being" (H 136 / GA 5 148). Henry builds the structure from Fichte, not from Hegel or Heidegger. The essay is not his source for it, though it supplies him with the same structure stated of consciousness.

**¶2.** Existence is not stable being: it withdraws from being into absolute exteriority and, annihilating itself before it, posits it opposite as another absolute existence. Existence is thus the image of being, its concept, its form, what Fichte calls knowledge, "the manifestation and revelation of being in its only possible form" (n. 20). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay ties knowing to the image in its own exposition of consciousness. Representing "brings the look, the image, into presence" (bringt ... den Anblick, das Bild, in die Präsenz ein) and is "the bringing-in of the image: imagination" (das Einbringen des Bildes: die Einbildung, H 133 / GA 5 145); the history of consciousness brings consciousness "into the image of its essence" (in das Bild seines Wesens, H 140 / GA 5 153), and this is its formation (*Bildung*). Henry's Fichtean knowledge-as-image and the essay's representing-as-imagination name one structure in two idioms. Henry does not cite H 133 for the image, though he cites the page for representing at §11 ¶9. The GA 3 run has noted that the same page links the essay to the Kantbuch's imagination.

**¶3.** The dualism of being and its image is an eidetic condition of all phenomenality, binding even God: without it God would be the most obscure and most abstract *Ungrund*. More exactly, God is one with these conditions, the movement that actualizes his vocation to manifest himself, so that the essence of the divine is the essence of manifestation. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's thesis has an exact counterpart in the essay's conclusion. Without its "near us" the absolute "would be the solitary, which could not appear to itself in what appears" (wäre das Absolute das Einsame, das sich nicht erscheinen könnte im Erscheinenden), and Hegel's last sentence speaks of the throne "without which it would be the lifeless solitary" (ohne den er das leblose Einsame wäre, H 187–188 / GA 5 204). Heidegger draws from it that "the absolute needs the throne in its absoluteness" (bedarf in seiner Absolutheit des Thrones, H 188 / GA 5 204). Henry and the essay agree that an absolute that does not manifest itself is abstract and lifeless, and that manifestation belongs to the absolute's essence. Henry's Section I will make that agreement a charge. The Fichtean God, the Hegelian absolute and Heidegger's being all exist only by going outside themselves, so they share one concept of manifestation.

**¶4.** Fichte's commentary on the prologue of John repeats these presuppositions on the plane of the absolute: God as Word is God as existence, divided by the dualism of being and existence, posited in appearance only as his image arises opposite him. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay reads Hegel's two parts of science as two theologies. The *Phenomenology* is "the theology of the absolute with regard to its Parousia in the dialectical-speculative Good Friday" (die Theologie des Absoluten hinsichtlich seiner Parusie im dialektisch-spekulativen Karfreitag, H 186 / GA 5 202), and the *Logic* "the theology of the absoluteness of the absolute before creation" (die Theologie der Absolutheit des Absoluten vor der Schöpfung, H 187 / GA 5 203); Heidegger adds that both are ontology, and that Christian theology "is metaphysics" (H 187 / GA 5 203). Henry's treatment of Fichte's theology of the Word as a repetition of ontological presuppositions follows the same rule of reading: theology is read as ontology. He applies it without citing the essay, and to Fichte rather than Hegel.

**¶5.** Being and existence in God are equally primitive: the Father is not separable from the Son he begets. Yet their unity does not abolish difference but posits it: the unity of being and existence divides being and expels it from itself. "Alienation is real not as something outside the absolute but as constituting its very essence" (italics Henry's). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The word "alienation" brings Henry next to the essay at its most explicit. Heidegger glosses Hegel's externalization (*Entäußerung*), which Hyppolite renders "aliénation": "what appears externalizes itself into its appearing ... The externalization of the absolute is its recollection (*Er-innerung*) into the course of the appearing of its absoluteness. Externalization is so little estrangement into abstraction that through it appearing becomes at home in what appears" (Das Erscheinende entäußert sich in sein Erscheinen ... Die Entäußerung ist so wenig die Entfremdung in die Abstraktion, daß durch sie das Erscheinen gerade heimisch wird im Erscheinenden als einem solchen, H 176 / GA 5 192). Both make externalization constitutive of the absolute rather than a fall. They read its sense oppositely. For Henry it is separation, being outside its being; Heidegger reads it as a holding-to-itself, a becoming-at-home, and refuses estrangement (*Entfremdung*) by name. Henry's word carries the estrangement Heidegger excludes, and the word is Hyppolite's before it is Henry's. Henry's Appendix will take up Hegel's alienation directly (R §76), outside this run's range.

**¶6.** Set in its phenomenological frame, the passage from being-in-itself to being-for-itself is being's positing outside itself: its being-outside-itself is its for-itself, its existence; in it being becomes other, alienates itself, and so realizes the conditions of its manifestation. "Alienation is the essence of manifestation." No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay glosses Hegel's in-itself and for-itself in the opposite spatial sense. "In the will to be near us the absolute is presencing. Bringing itself near (*an-bringend*) in itself in this way, it is for itself" (Im Willen, bei uns zu sein, ist das Absolute an-wesend. An sich dergestalt sich an-bringend ist es für sich, H 130 / GA 5 141). Heidegger's for-itself is a bringing-itself-near; Henry's is a going-outside-itself. The same Hegelian transition is read by Heidegger as nearing and by Henry as distancing. That difference is the hinge on which Henry will convict the essay of distance: he reads its nearness (the Parousia) as obtained through what he here calls alienation (R §11 ¶25).

**¶7.** The being that manifests itself is present being, and "the essence of presence is alienation": presence to self is one with separation from self in becoming other. Sartre: "every 'presence to' implies duality, therefore separation"; presence "is an immediate degradation of coincidence" (nn. 25–26). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay gives the structure as the meaning of presence in the epoch of subjectity. "Representing presents the object by representing it to the subject, in which representation the subject itself presents itself as such" (Das Vorstellen präsentiert das Objekt, indem es dieses dem Subjekt repräsentiert, in welcher Repräsentation das Subjekt selbst sich als solches präsentiert, H 121–122 / GA 5 133). Presence doubled into re-presentation is there the being of the subject since Descartes and Leibniz. It is not presence as such: for the Greeks, the presencing of what is present rests on "the self-showing appearing of the unconcealed" (im sich zeigenden Erscheinen des Unverborgenen, H 162 / GA 5 176), with no subject to which it is re-presented. Henry states as the eidetic of all presence what the essay assigns to modern presence. Whether the Greek presencing escapes Henry's charge, because it shows itself without a doubling, or falls under it, because unconcealment is still a coming-forth into an open, is a question Henry does not put to the essay.

**¶8.** These conditions are universal and transcendental: phenomenological distance is the "insuperable and perpetually saved" distance from which a presence can be discerned (n. 27: Dufrenne), and it is identical with the pure concept of alienation. "Alienation is insurmountable"; its suppression is eidetically absurd, for a suppression could be a phenomenon only on the ground of alienation. It does not vanish "as the distance separating the walker from his goal vanishes when the goal is reached". Fichte's life is a second unity within a duality "that is not suppressed by it but subsists eternally" (n. 28). No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's thesis contradicts the goal of Hegel's Introduction as the essay expounds it. Hegel's goal is the point "where appearance becomes equal to essence" (wo die Erscheinung dem Wesen gleich wird, Hegel's sixteenth section, GA 5 128), and Heidegger: "At this point the pure appearing of the absolute coincides with its essence" (An diesem Punkt fällt das reine Erscheinen des Absoluten mit seinem Wesen zusammen, H 181 / GA 5 197). Henry agrees with the essay on one point. The essay too rejects the walker who reaches the goal at the end of the road: the presentation is no itinerary (H 131–132 / GA 5 143–144), and its movement comes "from the goal" (aus dem Ziel her, H 146 / GA 5 159). Heidegger's Hegel keeps the coincidence while refusing the road; Henry keeps neither. Henry's verdict here anticipates his critique of Hegelian experience in Section II (R §20): there is no coincidence to come, since what is supposed to be overcome is the condition of every phenomenon.

**¶9.** The suppression of alienation can only mean the unity of presence, which joins us to things, as our gaze carries us to the tree on the hillside; but the essence of that unity is alienation itself. "Difference is the essence of unity": presence is obtained through the mediation of phenomenological distance, a nearness in the far. Hölderlin: "We separate only to be more united." No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's formula restates what the essay says of consciousness, "a distinguishing that is none" (ein Unterscheiden, das keines ist, H 153 / GA 5 167). Heidegger goes further than Henry in one respect. He charges that Hegel "levels the differences into a general distinguishing" (die Unterschiede doch in ein allgemeines Unterscheiden nivelliert, H 161 / GA 5 175), and that the level is set by the distinction of *ratio*, which Hegel grasps as the negation of the negation. Heidegger finds in Hegel a single structure of difference that flattens the differences within consciousness. Henry finds a single structure of difference beneath Hegel, Fichte and Heidegger together. The essay's critique of levelling is an instrument Henry could have turned against his own monism thesis, and he does not take it up.

**¶10.** Present being is therefore at once given and withdrawn: being is there for itself as what it is not, separated by an insuperable distance, and so can be desired in vain. The essence, unable to surmount itself, closes in on itself and "gives itself to itself only as what it eternally lacks". No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's closed and lacking essence answers to the essay's absolute in its labour: "the toil of pain, of enduring the tornness" (die Mühsal des Schmerzes, die Zerrissenheit auszuhalten), in which "the infinite relation" fulfils itself (H 127 / GA 5 138), and the absolute that "needs the throne" (H 188 / GA 5 204). The difference is in the outcome. In the essay the toil is the absolute's self-comprehension at work, and its need is met by the Parousia. In Henry the lack is eternal, since what would meet it is the distance that produces it. Henry turns the essay's drama of the absolute into a law of the essence: the drama has no end.

**¶11.** Presence grounds knowledge and is the theme of transcendental knowledge (Kant). The a priori, which is the being of objects, gives them the characters that follow "from the willing of the essence": separate objects that knowledge reaches only through their separation, so that knowledge is "always knowledge of what we are not" (n. 30). Being is the desire of itself, its own nostalgia. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's phrase "the willing of the essence" (le vouloir de l'essence) recalls the essay's leading term. Heidegger reads Hegel's absolute as will: "the will of the absolute is to be in and for itself already near us" (Der Wille des Absoluten ist es, an und für sich schon bei uns zu sein, H 125 / GA 5 137), the presentation is "itself a willing" (selbst ein Wollen, H 130 / GA 5 141), and the essence of the will "conceals itself with the essence of experience in the essence of being" (H 175 / GA 5 190). Both read the absolute's relation to itself as a striving. For Heidegger it is a will that achieves its Parousia "near us"; for Henry, with Sartre, it is a desire that achieves only its own separation. Henry's knowledge of "what we are not" is the essay's natural consciousness, for which the true stands over against it as an other. In the essay that otherness belongs to natural consciousness and is overcome in experience. In Henry it is the essence and cannot be overcome. The essay's Kant, who measures knowledge as a means before using it (H 118 / GA 5 129), is the critique Hegel's first section dismisses; Henry's Kant is the transcendental thinker of presence, whom he does not set beside the essay's.

### § 11. Ontological monism and the problem of its overcoming: philosophy of consciousness and philosophy of being

**¶1.** The presuppositions just set out as the essence of the phenomenon are named "ontological monism". Since their Greek origin they have governed Western philosophy and prescribed the single direction in which anything can show itself. Every advance, the modern ones included, has taken place within their horizon, and contemporary ontology has only freed that horizon and brought it into the concept as "the horizon of being". No GA 5 locus is engaged. "The horizon of being" names *Being and Time* and the Kantbuch; the Hegel essay uses neither horizon nor transcendence for being. Henry's claim that monism has governed philosophy "since its origin in Greece" is the claim §8 ¶16 made, and it again has the shape of the essay's history: the difference unthought "since the beginning of metaphysics", which "coincides with the beginning of Western thought" (fällt der Beginn der Metaphysik mit dem Beginn des abendländischen Denkens zusammen, H 162 / GA 5 176). The divergence is the same as before. For Heidegger the beginning opens a history in which "the sense changes and is historically fixed each time" (inwiefern jeweils die Bedeutung sich wandelt und wie sie jeweils geschichtlich festliegt, H 161 / GA 5 175). For Henry contemporary ontology is the clarified form of a presupposition that has not changed.

**¶2.** Has not the philosophy of consciousness, since the dawn of modern philosophy, opened another dimension of existence and essence, another mode of reality in an ontological sense? No GA 5 locus is engaged. The question states the hope that Henry's section will refute, and the essay has already refused it. For Heidegger the modern turn to consciousness is the entry into "the land" (das Land) of self-certainty (H 118 / GA 5 129), and the subject is the latest determination of the ὑποκείμενον that "philosophy has had to know since antiquity as what is present" (das die Philosophie von altersher als das Anwesende zu erkennen hat, H 121 / GA 5 132). Consciousness is not another dimension of being but a new name for presence. Henry will reach the same verdict, with his own argument and to his own end.

**¶3.** The opposition of consciousness and being holds only at a pre-philosophical level, while one of its terms remains undetermined. Greek ontology, Heidegger notes, is naive: it takes beings as they offer themselves and their givenness for granted. Questioning that givenness lifts "the ambiguity" of the word "being" (ὄν), which means both something and its beingness and so is also "ontological"; for, in Heidegger's words, "the Greek ὄν, 'being', conceals within itself an essence of beingness of its own" (n. 32). To heed this essence is to divide the ontic from the ontological in beings, and the concept of being then refers exclusively to the ground. Henry's quotation (R §11 ¶3, n. 32 / H 161 / GA 5 175) is accurate as far as it goes, and it stops in mid-sentence. Heidegger writes that the Greek ὄν "conceals within itself an essence of beingness (οὐσία) of its own, *which in the course of its history by no means remains the same*" (birgt in sich ein eigenes Wesen von Seiendheit (οὐσία), das im Verlauf seiner Geschichte keineswegs das gleiche bleibt). The cut relative clause is what the sentence is for. Heidegger introduces "ontic consciousness" only "with due caution and the necessary reservations" (Bei gehöriger Vorsicht und mit den nötigen Vorbehalten), because using the words ὄν and "being" thoughtfully presupposes that we heed "how the meaning changes each time" (inwiefern jeweils die Bedeutung sich wandelt, H 161 / GA 5 175). Henry keeps the ambiguity of ὄν and drops its history. His construal of the ambiguity also reverses Heidegger's. For Henry, lifting the ambiguity is the achievement of ontological questioning, after which "being" refers "exclusively" to the ground. For Heidegger, ὄν "is already, by its ambiguity, gathered as a being upon beingness. It is ontological" (ist sogar schon gemäß seiner Zweideutigkeit als Seiendes auf Seiendheit versammelt. Es ist ontologisch, H 161–162 / GA 5 176). The Greeks are not naive about this, and the ambiguity is never lifted: it "names both at once and neither as such" (nennt beide zugleich und keines als solches, H 162 / GA 5 176). Its remaining unthought "constitutes the essence of metaphysics" (H 163 / GA 5 177). Henry's dissociation of ontic and ontological, the precondition of his whole Section I, is in the essay the unthought that metaphysics lives from, not a result it achieves. The "naive" Greek ontology Henry attributes to Heidegger comes from *Being and Time* (§6), not from this page, which credits Greek νοεῖν with perceiving presencing as such (H 162 / GA 5 176). Section I uses the passage approvingly, here and at §12 ¶2; Section II will contest the same pages, and that later verdict must not be read back into this use.

**¶4.** Once being is determined ontologically, the classical opposition of consciousness and thing falls under the same dialectic as that of being and beings: the thing requires an essence, thinghood, and consciousness is that thinghood. Consciousness presupposes beings, not being; it is itself essence and ground. Modern idealism is the first properly philosophical questioning of the condition of the thing, the completion of the ancient philosophy of being, and it is one with contemporary ontology. No GA 5 locus is engaged, and the thesis is the essay's. In Heidegger's exposition the subject is the ὑποκείμενον of old, now "what is certainly present" (das gewiß Vorliegende, H 121 / GA 5 132); the beingness of beings "is determined from the subjectity of consciousness" (aus der Subjektität des Bewußtseins bestimmt, H 161 / GA 5 176); and Hegel's absolute knowledge is a "completion" (*Vollendung*) of modern philosophy (H 121 / GA 5 132). Henry's "the philosophy of consciousness is an end and not a beginning" restates the essay's account of modern metaphysics. What Henry adds is the last clause: modern idealism "is one with contemporary ontology". That Heidegger's own ontology belongs to the history he expounds is the step the essay does not take, and Henry takes it.

**¶5.** The concept of consciousness is the means by which modern philosophy thinks the thing's possibility of manifesting itself, the essence of manifestation as such. If being is the form of manifestation, consciousness is that form; Fichte calls consciousness "the absolute existence or the manifestation and revelation of being in its only possible form" (n. 33) and names it with existence, form, representation, image and being, all titles of being's division from itself. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay states the same equivalence for Hegel: consciousness "is as such in itself what appears" (ist als solches in sich das Erscheinende), and its immediate Dasein "is appearing" (H 134 / GA 5 146). Henry establishes the equivalence through Fichte, not through Hegel. The difference is one of witness, not of thesis. When he cites H 134 in ¶17, the essay will confirm a structure Fichte has already supplied.

**¶6.** Consciousness designates the essence of manifestation interpreted by the presuppositions of monism. Because it is being's self-division, consciousness is always presented as separating from being, rising above it, opposing it. "Consciousness is nothing but the alienation of being, that is, being as such." Schelling's absolute, without the duality that is the condition of all consciousness, cannot reach consciousness; history is the never-completed manifestation of the absolute that "divides itself in consciousness, that is, only in order to appear" (nn. 36–37). No GA 5 locus is engaged. Schelling's history as the absolute's self-division to appear has its Hegelian counterpart in the essay's "history of the formation of consciousness" (Bildungsgeschichte), which is "the historicity of history" (die Geschichtlichkeit der Geschichte, H 141 / GA 5 153) as the absolute appears. The essay does not read this history as division. The coming of appearing and the going of what appears is "the movement that consciousness itself *is*" (die Bewegung, als welche das Bewußtsein selbst *ist*, H 140 / GA 5 152), and consciousness is "the original unity of both" (die ursprüngliche Einheit beider, H 140 / GA 5 153). Where Henry finds division as the condition of appearing, the essay's Hegel finds the unrest of a unity that distinguishes itself (H 145–146 / GA 5 158–159). Henry will say the unity is obtained through the distinction. The essay allows that reading, but does not make it.

**¶7.** In the *System of Transcendental Idealism* Schelling sets intelligence irreducibly against its action, because opposition is the essence of manifestation and of consciousness: intelligence must detach itself from production for consciousness to arise. No GA 5 locus is engaged, and nothing in the essay engages Schelling. The unit contributes to the run only negatively. The essay's Hegel does not oppose consciousness to its action; consciousness "*is*" its testing (Das Bewußtsein ist, sofern es Bewußtsein ist, das Prüfen, H 159 / GA 5 173). Henry's lineage of separation runs through Schelling and Boehme, and he will fold Hegel into it by a footnote (¶9, n. 41), not by the essay.

**¶8.** Boehme already made difference (*Schiedlichkeit*) the condition of consciousness and of vision, thinking consciousness with otherness, mirror and doubling; the late Schelling interprets mythology and creation from the necessity of a coming-to-consciousness by separation (*Scheidung*). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay names Boehme only once, as the figure Hegel leaves behind when, with Descartes, philosophy "first" reaches land (H 118 / GA 5 128–129). Henry's mirror and doubling recall §9 ¶1's Gassendi. The essay's nearest counterpart is again its exposition of consciousness as "a distinguishing that is none" (H 153 / GA 5 167), which Henry never cites.

**¶9.** The monist interpretation of consciousness is not confined to one current; beyond the post-Kantians "and notably Hegel" (n. 41, to the Appendix), it dominates the whole philosophy of consciousness and finds its general formula in the understanding of consciousness as "representation". To represent is to make present; representation is a presentation that implies a redoubling, founded on the opposing doubling by which being separates from itself to perceive itself. Fichte: the existence of being is "the consciousness or representation of being" (n. 45). Representation is therefore not one mode of conscious life beside sensible, conceptual or affective life; it is the eidetic structure of consciousness as such, which is why Heidegger can say that "representing (*Vor-stellen*) rules over all modes of consciousness" (n. 46). Representation so understood "presents in the mode of representation" (n. 47), and invites us to understand presence as the presence of something that "comes up before", in a milieu of exteriority whose opening is the common essence of consciousness and representation. Both quotations are from the essay. The first (R §11 ¶9, n. 46 / H 133 / GA 5 145) renders exactly "Das Vor-stellen waltet in allen Weisen des Bewußtseins" (Representing holds sway in all modes of consciousness), keeping Heidegger's hyphen; the emphasis is Henry's. The second (n. 47 / H 134 / GA 5 145) is "Das Vorstellen präsentiert in der Weise der Repräsentation" (Representing presents in the manner of representation), again with Henry's emphasis. Three points have to be fixed. First, voice. Heidegger's sentences belong to his reading of Hegel's word "consciousness": knowing as "vidi", representing as *perceptio* and as the *co-agitatio* behind Descartes's *cogitatio*, consciousness as *conscientia* (H 133 / GA 5 145). They state what consciousness means for modern metaphysics, with an etymology that ties it to Descartes and Leibniz. Henry's "this is why Heidegger can say" makes them Heidegger's witness to an eidetic truth. Henry's thesis is that the modern meaning *is* the eidetic of consciousness as understood by monism, and that thesis is compatible with the sentences only because Henry has already defined monism as the eidetic that all philosophy of consciousness shares. The sentences confirm it only on that prior definition: they are exposition, and Henry reads them as doctrine. Second, scope. Henry's denial that representation is one mode of life beside the affective agrees with the page he quotes: representing holds sway "whether what is presented is something sensibly perceived, or something non-sensibly thought, or willed, or felt" (gleichviel ob das Präsente ein sinnlich Wahrgenommenes ist oder ein nicht sinnlich Gedachtes oder Gewolltes oder Gefühltes, H 133 / GA 5 145). Heidegger's "felt" is the precise point at which Henry's later book will contest monism, since affectivity for Henry is a revelation without representation. Henry here grants, through the essay, that for monism even feeling is represented. Third, what Henry adds. His gloss, presence of something that "comes up before" in "a milieu of exteriority", keeps the "before" (*vor*) and drops the "back". Heidegger's representing sets what it represents "toward itself and so back" (auf sich zu und so zurückstellt, H 134 / GA 5 145), and "in this representation the subject itself presents itself as such" (in welcher Repräsentation das Subjekt selbst sich als solches präsentiert, H 121–122 / GA 5 133). In the essay the "re-" of re-presentation is a return in which the subject is present to itself. Henry's exteriority omits that return. He omits it because his thesis is that the essence of monism has no self-presence of its own; on the essay's text, it is an omission.

**¶10.** The philosophy of consciousness often specifies representation as certainty. Since Descartes the true being (*ens verum*) is the certain being (*ens certum*). This is not a new interpretation of the truth of beings but the moment at which that truth first becomes a problem: the truth of beings consists in their being represented, their emergence from the night into the light. No GA 5 locus is cited. The Cartesian formula is the essay's: "The true, the *ens verum*, is since Descartes the *ens certum*" (Das Wahre, das ens verum, ist seit Descartes das ens certum, H 136 / GA 5 147–148). Henry contradicts the essay's reading of it. Heidegger presents certainty as a transformation of truth: "truth used to count as the agreement of representing with beings ... As certainty, however, truth is now representing itself" (Die Wahrheit galt vordem als die Übereinstimmung des Vorstellens mit dem Seienden ... Als die Gewißheit aber ist die Wahrheit jetzt das Vorstellen selbst, H 124 / GA 5 135). Henry's "not a new interpretation" removes the "used to" and the "now". He reads the epoch Heidegger dates as a problem that was always there, first brought into view. This is the same de-historicizing move as the cut in ¶3, here without a quotation.

**¶11.** If certainty is the being-certain of the certain, the appearing of what appears, representation effects it: certainty is the certainty of representation, its phenomenological meaning, and so always certainty of something, the truth of beings. "With the concept of certainty it is not another form of truth, another truth than that of beings, but that very truth, in and for itself, that is thought." No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry's claim meets the essay at its sharpest point. Heidegger's Hegel defines absolute certainty as the release of self-certainty from the object relation. Knowing "detaches itself from the relation to objects" (löst sich aus der Relation zu den Gegenständen los); this detachment is "absolvence" (*Absolvenz*), and its completion is the absoluteness of the absolute (H 124–125 / GA 5 136). Read thus, the certainty of absolute knowledge is not certainty "of something". Heidegger adds what Henry needs: "The detachment lets this representing persist", and "all these moments of absoluteness have the character of representation. In them the Parousia of the absolute comes to presence" (Die Loslösung läßt dieses Vorstellen bestehen ... Alle diese Momente der Absolutheit haben den Charakter der Repräsentation. In ihnen west die Parusie des Absoluten, H 125 / GA 5 136). Henry's thesis holds against the essay's Hegel on its second half: the self-certainty that frees itself from the object remains representation. On its first half it does not hold, because the essay's absolute certainty is precisely not "always certainty of something". Henry has to argue that a representation freed from its object is still the truth of beings. The essay offers him the premise, since absolvence keeps the form of representation, but not the conclusion.

**¶12.** With certainty, subjectivity appears: representation stands to the represented as subjective to objective. The subject is the condition of the phenomenality of phenomena, the power that places the object "before"; the object is manifest as conscious, and its being-conscious lies in the subject. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's ob-ject, placed before by the subject, is the essay's object, which representing "presents ... by representing it to the subject" (präsentiert das Objekt, indem es dieses dem Subjekt repräsentiert, H 121–122 / GA 5 133). Henry's "subjectivity" here is still the classical word. The essay's term for the being of the subject is subjectity, which Henry will render as subjectivity at ¶17.

**¶13.** Consciousness is not the subject. Classical philosophy's opposition of subject and object is not an opposition between the conscious and the non-conscious, since the object is precisely what is conscious. Consciousness resides in the relation of subject and object, it *is* that relation; "the ego of consciousness is not pure subject, it is at once subject and object" (Schelling, n. 51). No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry reaches by his own route Heidegger's definition of subjectity: "the subject-being of the subject, that is, of the subject-object relation, is the subjectity of the subject" (Das Subjektsein des Subjekts, d. h. der Subjekt-Objekt-Beziehung, ist die Subjektität des Subjekts, H 122 / GA 5 133), and "the being of the subject as the subject-object relation reflected into itself" (H 134 / GA 5 146). The essay's definition has a term Henry's lacks. "The subject essentially unfolds in the representing relation to the object. But as this relation it is already the representing relation to itself" (Das Subjekt west in der vorstellenden Beziehung auf das Objekt. Aber als diese Beziehung ist es schon die vorstellende Beziehung auf sich, H 121 / GA 5 132). Henry's relation is the relation between the terms. The essay's is the relation "reflected into itself". The self-relation Henry will deny to the essence of monism (R §§16, 28–29) is, in the essay's exposition, built into subjectity. Henry's later argument will have to show that this reflection is itself a distance, which is what his §10 has prepared by making the "re-" a redoubling.

**¶14.** The relation is the concrete term and its terms are abstract: "the pure subjective as much as the pure objective is an abstraction" (Hegel, n. 52); subject and object are "two abstract moments of a single structure which is presence" (Merleau-Ponty, n. 53). Sartre, confusing consciousness with the abstract subject, nevertheless affirms that the relation is concrete. The relation is the fundamental ontological region, and consciousness is that region; "the philosophy of consciousness appears to itself as an expression of ontological monism." No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay's last word on the subject-object relation agrees with Henry's on concreteness and differs on the subject. The genitives of Hegel's title "name the subject-object relation of the absolute subject in its subjectity" (nennen die Subjekt-Objekt-Beziehung des absoluten Subjekts in ihrer Subjektität, H 182 / GA 5 198). For Heidegger the relation belongs to the absolute *subject*; for Henry the relation *is* the subject, and no subject stands behind it. For Henry, to call the relation an absolute subject would misname the "between"; Heidegger calls the relation's being subjectity because, in metaphysics, that being has the character of a subject.

**¶15.** Consciousness, not a term of a duality, is the being of the "between" (n. 58: a definition "accepted by Heidegger", *SZ* 132). The subject is the ontological event that makes beings accede to the condition of object, and nothing outside that event; "the subject is the being of the object". The dualism of subject and object is the dualism of essence and ontic determination, the pre-critical form of the distinction of being and beings; behind the split lies one essence, and the unity it names is "the uniqueness of the mode of manifestation". No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry's "the subject is the being of the object" states as an eidetic what the essay states as history: when beingness "has been lit up as objectness" (sich die Seiendheit als die Gegenständlichkeit gelichtet hat), this rests on the ontology by which the subiectum's being "has been determined from the subjectity of consciousness" (aus der Subjektität des Bewußtseins bestimmt worden ist, H 161 / GA 5 175–176). The essay's "between" (δια) is the space the reversal opens for the conversation between natural consciousness and absolute knowledge (H 177 / GA 5 192). Henry's "between" is *Being and Time*'s. Henry joins them under one word, as he joined world and site in §9 ¶3.

**¶16.** Monism also gives the mode of manifestation a definite structure. As relation, the subject establishes and maintains a distance, unfolds the horizon, opens the place where something can appear; "consciousness is itself the space deprived of determinability" (Hegel, n. 62: *PhE* I 331). As relation the subject is being-in, being-in-the-world; and the world is nothing but being-in, the self-separation of the essence that makes the interval arise. "The light that arises in this interval is at once that of the world and that of consciousness." No GA 5 locus is engaged. The Hegel quotation is from the chapter on reason, outside the essay. Henry's light, which is at once world's and consciousness's, has a counterpart in the essay's "light of being" (das Licht des Seins, H 136 / GA 5 148) and in the ray (*Strahl*) of the absolute. In the essay the light is not that of a world opened by the subject's distancing. It is that of the absolute, which "shines on us" (uns bescheint, H 130 / GA 5 141), and "the foreground of the light within which every kind of knowledge already stands" (H 164 / GA 5 178). Henry's identification of world and consciousness is a reading of *Being and Time*; he will extend it to the essay through H 134 in the next paragraph.

**¶17.** Husserl's consciousness that is "nothing of the world" means only that, as essence, it is the world in its pure worldhood (n. 64: *SZ* 209). World and being-in name a presence accomplished in pure spatiality, excluding any subjectivity not belonging to it; the world is "subjective" only in being "more objective than any object" (n. 65: *SZ* 366). Transcendence is the essence of manifestation; as being-in and relation, the subject is appearing as such. "The subjectivity of the subject, says Heidegger, is *Erscheinen* itself" (n. 66). "It is the appearing of what appears, the being of beings, that constitutes the subjectivity of the human subject. The light that passes through us is that of the world" (n. 67: Beaufret, Birault). Consciousness has the measure of truth within it, but the truth that constitutes our interiority "is only the absolute light of exteriority". "Human subjectivity is the transcendence of the world." The quotation (R §11 ¶17, n. 66 / H 134 / GA 5 146) renders a sentence that opens: "The actuality of this actual, the subjectity of the subject, is appearing itself" (Die Wirklichkeit dieses Wirklichen, die Subjektität des Subjekts, ist das Erscheinen selbst). Three departures, each small, together decisive. Henry drops the apposition "the actuality of this actual", which ties the sentence to Hegel's "real knowledge" and to consciousness as the actual. He renders subjectity as "subjectivity", erasing the coinage. And in his gloss he adds "human": "the subjectivity of the *human* subject". The essay's subject is the subject of modern metaphysics, culminating in the absolute subject, and Heidegger's next sentence places the claim at once: this being "enters representation, like all being of all beings in all metaphysics, only in such a way that beings present themselves as beings" (H 134 / GA 5 146). What Heidegger says of the subject of metaphysics, Henry says of the human subject, which lets him identify it with *Being and Time*'s Dasein and its transcendence. He keeps "Erscheinen" in German, marking the word, but not the coinage beside it. The intermediaries are named in n. 67: Jean Beaufret's "clearing of light" in the "nearest neighbourhood" and Henri Birault's light "of Being and of the being we are". Henry reads the identity of subjectivity and world through the French Heideggerians of 1947–51. Their formulas, not the essay, supply "the light that passes through us". One further uncited Hegelian sentence stands behind "consciousness has in it the measure of truth": Hegel's second proposition, "consciousness gives its standard in itself" (Das Bewußtsein gibt seinen Maßstab an ihm selbst, Hegel's twelfth section, GA 5 124). Heidegger insists on Hegel's "an ihm selbst" (on itself) against "an sich selbst" (in itself): the standard lies in consciousness's essence, but consciousness "gives and at the same time does not give" it (Es gibt und gibt zugleich doch nicht, H 156 / GA 5 170). Henry's "in it" (en elle) has none of that reserve.

**¶18.** The identification of consciousness with the pure exteriority of transcendence appears when the problem of consciousness grasps its ontological meaning; the progress in determining consciousness runs from a subject of knowledge, to the subject's being as relation, to intentionality, to the assertion that consciousness is nothing but transcendence (Sartre: "all consciousness is positional and exhausts itself in this position", n. 69). The essence is dialectic, exchange and passage, and it is the humanity of man; the most intimate being of man is originary spatiality. "Transcendence is universal existence. The existence of man is the existence of things. The subjectivity of the subject is only the objectivity of the object." No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry's formula is the essay's thesis on subjectity in his own vocabulary. "Within subjectity every being as such becomes object" (Innerhalb der Subjektität wird jedes Seiende als solches zum Gegenstand, H 177 / GA 5 192); "since Leibniz *every* ens qua ens is a res cogitans and in this sense subject" (H 121 / GA 5 132). And the essay supports Henry's use of "being" for objectivity, once the word's owner is fixed: Heidegger uses "being" "both for what Hegel, with Kant, calls objectness and objectivity" (sowohl für das, was Hegel mit Kant die Gegenständlichkeit und die Objektivität nennt, H 142 / GA 5 154) and for the actuality of spirit. When Henry writes that the subject's subjectivity is the object's objectivity, the essay's own use of words licenses the step for the epoch of subjectity. It does not license it as an eidetic, and it gives no warrant for "the humanity of man". Henry's "the essence is dialectic, exchange and passage" also echoes the essay's dialectic as the conversation (διαλέγεσθαι) in which consciousness gathers itself (H 169 / GA 5 183–184). Henry hears dialectic as passage outward; the essay hears it as gathering.

**¶19.** The identity long stayed hidden because the objectivity of the object had not been made a problem. In Kant the determination of the possibility of objects is realized in the analysis of the subjectivity of the subject: the a priori form of all possible experience is at once the being of subject and object, the ground of the "agreement" of thought and things. The formula must even be reversed: the subjectivity of the subject is determined progressively as the a priori structure of the object, to which it is reduced. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The unit reads Kant through the Kantbuch, and the GA 3 run treats that reading. The essay touches it twice. For Kant "experience" is "the name for the only possible theoretical knowledge of beings" (der Name, der die einzig mögliche theoretische Erkenntnis des Seienden bezeichnet, H 184 / GA 5 200), while for Hegel it has become the name of being; and Kant is the one who "measures" knowing "by its nature and limits" (H 118 / GA 5 129). The essay places Kant before Hegel's step from knowledge to being. Henry's Kant has already taken that step, since the a priori form of experience is the being of the object. Henry reads Kant as the essay reads Hegel.

**¶20.** For Lachelier, necessity, the structure of mind, constitutes the existence of things in an ontological sense: "truth or existence", "the existence of a thing as distinct from this thing" (n. 73), the a priori truth of all things and the subject of knowledge (n. 74). No GA 5 locus is engaged, and the essay offers no counterpart to Lachelier. Henry's "existence as distinct from the thing" restates the ontological difference in a French idealist idiom, as §10 restated it in Fichte's. The unit widens the lineage of monism; it has no bearing on the essay beyond confirming that Henry finds the essay's structure of subjectity everywhere, under every name.

**¶21.** Lagneau tries to free the subject from the necessity that is its own nature and that of things, by a retreat expressed in doubt. But the retreat reveals that nature rather than escaping it; it is the movement by which mind separates from itself to manifest itself. Necessity is the transcendental principle of the unity of representation, an eidetic structure of transcendence's ontological freedom, so that transcendence is at once the essence of subject and of things. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay passes a parallel verdict on doubt. The Cartesian way of doubt "puts many modes of representing in question, but only in order to remain in the initial position" (stellt zwar vielerlei Weisen des Vorstellens in Frage, aber nur, um in der Ausgangsstellung zu bleiben), and shows that doubting "has already brought itself into a security" (H 138–139 / GA 5 150–151). Henry's Lagneau, who doubts to escape and so manifests his nature, is the essay's Descartes. The difference is in what doubt fails to leave. For the essay it is the fundamentum absolutum of self-certainty; for Henry it is distance.

**¶22.** The ontological sense of transcendental philosophy can be lost in a moral psychologism that makes the subject a free, privileged being, but the privilege has its ground in the essence. The replacement of the classical subject by a "temporal subject" only affirms the interpretation of its being as transcendence: "temporality is the essence of transcendence", at once subjectivity of the subject and "horizon of being". No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's temporal subject is that of *Being and Time* and the Kantbuch. The essay withholds the step. It traces the presence of modern representation, like Greek οὐσία, to "a still unthought essence of a hidden time" (aus einem noch ungedachten Wesen einer verborgenen Zeit, H 142 / GA 5 155). In 1942–43 time is what presence comes from and what is not yet thought, not the horizon of a temporal subject. Henry's equation of temporality, transcendence and the horizon of being belongs to 1927–29. Applying it to the essay joins 1929 to 1942–43 across the turn.

**¶23.** The common being of the subjectivity of the subject and the objectivity of the object is the ontological ground of Hegel's demonstration of the identity of matter and spirit, and of the traditional identity of thought and being. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay states its own version of the traditional identity: with the δόξα that takes up what is present "belongs together" the νοεῖν that perceives presencing (zusammengehört, H 162 / GA 5 176). For Heidegger this is a belonging-together of perceiving and presencing in the ambiguity of ὄν, not an identity of subject and object. Henry's sentence takes the identity in the modern form the essay calls subjectity.

**¶24.** The identity of subject and object exposes the vanity of the quarrels between objectivists and subjectivists. Watson's behaviourism demands "show us consciousness", while appealing at every step to the power of showing that consciousness is. Sartre's objection that Heidegger skips the cogito has no content, since for Sartre consciousness exhausts itself in positing the object and is exactly what Heidegger calls the ontological understanding (*Verstehen*) of being. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's behaviourist is the essay's natural consciousness raised into a principle. Natural consciousness "everywhere and always finds only beings", and whatever is not of the kind it finds "falls under the decree that there is no such thing" (fällt dem Machtspruch anheim, daß es dergleichen nicht gibt, H 144 / GA 5 157). The essay adds a philosophical version, close to Henry's verdict on Sartre: a philosophy that grants objectness only as "the unobjective" (das Ungegenständliche) "sides with the usual opinion" (hält es ... mit dem gewöhnlichen Meinen, H 164 / GA 5 178). Henry and the essay agree that the critique of consciousness in the name of the object presupposes what it denies. Henry adds that consciousness and the understanding of being are the same.

**¶25.** "Subjectivity, declares Heidegger, commenting on classical philosophy, *is* presence in the mode of representation" (n. 80). Representation is founded on alienation, which opens the transcendental horizon of being, the transcendence of the world: "the world is my representation". The reversal in Heidegger's last works, which seeks the principle of phenomenality in the radical exteriority of being, has its premises in *Being and Time* and in traditional philosophy. Whether access to the transcendental goes through being or through consciousness matters little if the essence of manifestation is the same in both. "There is no difference between the philosophy of consciousness and the philosophy of being." The quotation (R §11 ¶25, n. 80 / H 134 / GA 5 146) renders "Sie *ist* die Präsenz in der Weise der Repräsentation" (It *is* presence in the manner of representation); "sie" is subjectity, which Henry again makes "subjectivity". The emphasis on "is" is Heidegger's, and Henry, rightly, does not claim it. Here, and only here in Section I, Henry marks the voice: Heidegger says this "commenting on classical philosophy". In form, then, Henry does not take the sentence as Heidegger's doctrine. He argues that the late Heidegger's being has the same structure as the subjectity Heidegger expounds, and so falls under his own description. The argument rests on two premises. The first, that representation is founded on alienation, is Henry's §10, built from Fichte and Sartre; the essay gives representation a return to self, not an alienation (¶9 above). The second, that the "radical exteriority of being" in the late works is the same essence, is asserted, with *Being and Time* as the bridge. The essay itself refuses the identification Henry needs. It distinguishes the presence that "happens in the representation of the skepsis of consciousness" from Greek οὐσία, and both from a being "which by no means necessarily appears only as the presence of what is present" (H 142 / GA 5 155). It asks whether subjectity is "a destiny of being of its own, in which the unconcealment of being ... withdraws" (H 176 / GA 5 192). Heidegger does not leave his own thinking inside subjectity; he places subjectity inside a history he claims to think from outside it. Henry's charge is that the outside has the same structure as what it describes. That charge is not refuted by the essay's self-placement, but the quotation does not establish it either. The sentence Henry quotes is evidence for his thesis about the philosophy of consciousness. For his thesis about the philosophy of being it is evidence only if the premise of §10 holds. Henry's Schopenhauerian "the world is my representation" turns the essay's formula against both philosophies at once: if subjectity is presence as representation and world is transcendence, then the world of *Being and Time* is representation. It is Henry's provocation, and it states his thesis exactly.

### § 12. The critique of the philosophy of consciousness

**¶1.** If the philosophy of being and the philosophy of consciousness do not differ, why does Heidegger criticize the latter so radically? Because he will not let the essence fall to the rank of the determination it grounds: his rejection of subject, subjectivity, consciousness, reason and person, as left unquestioned in their being, expresses the transcendence of being beyond whatever is posited as the principle of phenomenality. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's account of the motive fits *Being and Time*, where these concepts are rejected as left unquestioned in their being. The essay has a different relation to them. It does not reject "consciousness" and "subject"; it rethinks them as names of being in an epoch. "Consciousness" names a being, but its "-being" "must not remain an empty sound" (darf uns nicht ein leerer Wortschall bleiben, H 133 / GA 5 145), and the subject is not "the subjective in the sense of the self-seeking egoistic" (H 121 / GA 5 132). The coinage subjectity exists to keep the being of the subject apart from subjective determinations. That is the motive Henry names, enacted as reinterpretation rather than rejection. Henry's Section I will treat the two procedures, rejection in 1927 and reinterpretation in 1942–43, as one critique.

**¶2.** The essence cannot be kept in its original distance because its being is understood from what happens within it: being-in is "distorted" when Dasein understands it from the beings it lets appear (n. 81: *SZ* 58), and understands itself as a being related to another being, subject to object. Heidegger rejects this degradation. "Natural consciousness could be called an ontic consciousness insofar as it is directed straight at the object as a being *and at its knowledge of this object as something that is likewise a being*" (n. 82, emphasis Henry's). The philosophy of consciousness is rejected as a thesis of natural consciousness, guilty of an omission: "natural consciousness immediately places before itself what it represents *and its act of representing* as beings, without considering the being that it is thereby already representing" (n. 83, emphasis Henry's). Knowledge posited as a being must be questioned on its being; until then its claim to be a principle attests the forgetting of the ontological problem. The two quotations are from the essay. The first (R §12 ¶2, n. 82 / H 161 / GA 5 175) renders "Das natürliche Bewußtsein könnte, insofern es geradezu auf den Gegenstand als einen seienden, insgleichen auf sein Wissen von ihm als etwas Seiendes geht und stets dabei bleibt, das ontische Bewußtsein heißen" (Natural consciousness could, insofar as it goes straight at the object as a being, and likewise at its knowledge of it as something that is, and always stays with this, be called ontic consciousness). Henry drops "and always stays with this" (und stets dabei bleibt), which marks the persistence that makes the consciousness natural. He also drops the frame. The sentence before introduces the term "with due caution and the necessary reservations" as "a difference named earlier elsewhere" (eine Differenz ..., die früher anderwärts genannt wurde, H 161 / GA 5 175). Heidegger flags it as his own earlier distinction, applied to Hegel's with reservations. Henry quotes it as Heidegger's verdict. The second quotation (n. 83 / H 172–173 / GA 5 188) renders "Das natürliche Bewußtsein stellt sein Vorgestelltes und sein Vorstellen unmittelbar vor als Seiendes, ohne des Seins, das es dabei schon vorstellt, zu achten" (Natural consciousness represents what it represents and its representing immediately as beings, without heeding the being that it is thereby already representing). The rendering is close; "considérer" (consider) for "achten" (heed) softens nothing. The context is Hegel's fifteenth section: experience is made "on the old object", and the sentence explains why consciousness must go back to what is already in its representedness. The next sentence names the outcome: consciousness has "turned from what appears back to appearing and so turned round" (vom Erscheinenden zum Erscheinen zurück- und somit umgekehrt, H 173 / GA 5 188). Henry takes the diagnosis and leaves the reversal. The departure that matters is of target. In the essay natural consciousness is not the philosophy of consciousness. Hegel's absolute knowledge is the real knowledge that natural consciousness is not, and the critique of natural consciousness is Hegel's, performed in the Introduction and expounded by Heidegger. Henry turns it into Heidegger's critique *of* the philosophy of consciousness. The essay gives him a foothold: "natural knowledge lives in all shapes of spirit ... even and precisely that of absolute knowledge" (Das natürliche Wissen lebt in allen Gestalten des Geistes, lebt jede von ihnen in seiner Weise, auch und gerade diejenige des absoluten Wissens, H 137 / GA 5 149), and philosophy can side with opinion (H 164 / GA 5 178–179). So the philosophy of consciousness *can* harbour natural consciousness, but it is not defined as its thesis. This is Section I's approving use of the ontic/ontological mapping, which Section II will contest: Henry adopts it here, with his own emphasis on knowledge taken as a being. His "forgetting of the ontological problem" again replaces the essay's "not heeding" with oblivion. The forgetting of being is a term of the later Heidegger, but it is not the word of these pages.

**¶3.** The preservation of the truth of existence is the aim of Heidegger's critique of Cartesian ontology (*SZ* §§19–21): Descartes interprets the being of the world, and of Dasein, from the substantiality of the mathematical object, so that "res cogitans" and "res extensa" are two titles of one fall of one essence. "Cartesian dualism is an alteration of ontological monism." No GA 5 locus is engaged. The Descartes of the essay is not this one. In the essay Descartes inaugurates subjectity: the *ens certum*, the self-certainty of representing, the land that "the climate" makes critical of its own instrument (H 118 / GA 5 129), and an ego cogito certain "without the absolute", which needs "the back door of a proof of God" (durch die Hintertür eines Gottesbeweises, H 123 / GA 5 134). The res cogitans is the subiectum co-agitans, whose presentation is representation (H 170–171 / GA 5 186). Henry reads Descartes through the substance-critique of 1927, where the error is to understand Dasein from the present-at-hand. The essay reads him through certainty, where the event is the turn of the subject into the ground of beingness. The two critiques support Henry's thesis differently. *Being and Time* gives him a fall of the essence into the ontic; the essay gives him an essence, subjectity, that is presence as representation, with no fall. Henry's ¶6 will rely on the first; his §11 ¶25 relied on the second.

**¶4.** The critique of truth as the agreement of representation and object has the same sense: representation, the essence as opening and discovery of beings, falls to the rank of the proposition, an intramundane reality; agreement is the decline of the originary truth of existence (n. 88: *SZ* 224 ff.). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay states the relation of agreement and certainty as history, not as derivation. "Truth used to count as agreement ... As certainty, truth is now representing itself" (H 124 / GA 5 135). In *Being and Time* agreement is founded on disclosedness and derived from it. In the essay agreement is replaced by certainty in an epoch of truth. Henry uses the derivation, which suits his structure of fall from essence to ontic determination. The essay's succession of epochs would not sustain that structure.

**¶5.** The same decline appears in Kant's refutation of problematic idealism: the idea of proving the reality of the external world confuses the world with the totality of intramundane reality and makes the relation between inner and outer one more given relation (nn. 89–90: *SZ* §43). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay's Hegel makes a structurally similar refusal. Critical concern about knowing presupposes that "the absolute stands on one side and the knowers on the other" (Vollends getrennt voneinander stehen dergestalt das Absolute auf der einen Seite und die Erkennenden auf der anderen Seite), and "an absolute that stands on one side is in no case the absolute" (H 123 / GA 5 134). Heidegger's 1927 critique of the external-world problem and his 1942–43 exposition of Hegel's critique of critique reject the same presupposition of separated terms. Henry cites only the first.

**¶6.** Heidegger's critique of the philosophy of consciousness thus refuses the fall of the essence into ontic reality: the problem is not how the subject reaches an object but what makes it ontologically possible that beings occur within the world and are objectified, and the answer lies in "the ecstatically, horizonally founded transcendence of the world" (n. 90: *SZ* 366). Transcendence, not the subject, is the essence; but it is the essence the philosophy of consciousness aimed at under the title of subject. Heidegger's critique has ontological significance, and for that very reason none: it works at the boundary of the ontological, rejecting an improper understanding of "the one and only essence of manifestation", which "remains what it was". The philosophy of being thinks exteriority in its purity instead of lodging it in a subject, but "the essence of manifestation resides in every case in this exteriority as such". No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's verdict has a counterpart he does not test against the essay. On its own account, the essay does more than purify. It questions the structure Henry calls the one essence: beingness as presence is "only one, though decisive, way of being" (H 142 / GA 5 155), and the withdrawal of unconcealment in subjectity is "a completely different question" that the essay raises (H 176 / GA 5 192). Henry's reply is implicit in his formula. Heidegger questions the epochal forms of presence, not the essence of manifestation as exteriority or unconcealment, since unconcealment is still a coming-forth into an open. Whether a "coming forth into unconcealment" (Hervorkommen in eine Unverborgenheit, H 134 / GA 5 146) is exteriority in Henry's sense is the question on which his whole reading of the essay depends. He answers it by definition, not by a reading of the essay.

**¶7.** On the ground of this common essence the two philosophies exchange themes. The insertion of being's essential components into the subjective principle of consciousness would not have been possible had that principle not always been thought as the condition of objectivity. Sartre's identification of nothingness with consciousness is absurd, yet it attests the permanence of monism's presuppositions. Such an insertion is not a "subjectivation": that would require subjectivity to be an essence. It is a making-into-a-being (*étantisation*) of the essence, imputable to natural consciousness and promoting no new mode of manifestation. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay supports Henry's refusal to see a subjectivation in the philosophy of consciousness. Subjectity names the being of beings, not a subjective mode: "that this is so lies not in the view of this thinker but in the being of beings" (Daß dem so ist, liegt nicht an der Ansicht dieses Denkers, sondern am Sein des Seienden, H 121 / GA 5 132). Henry and the essay agree that what modern philosophy calls the subject is not subjective. They draw different consequences. For the essay, subjectity is a destiny of being with its own epoch. For Henry, it is the one essence of monism, which no subject has ever possessed. Henry's next step, that "subjectivity" could only mean something if it were another essence, is the hinge of his book, and the essay, which never asks for another essence, has nothing to say to it.

**¶8.** To keep the essence in radical transcendence over every being is no small thing, if the essence lies in that transcendence. But the more rigorously the essence is understood as the nihilating ground of being, the more incomprehensible becomes the constantly repeated assertion that "Dasein" is a being. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's target is *Being and Time*. The essay speaks otherwise of man: "the essence of man is itself the escort of being" (das Wesen des Menschen im Geleit des Seins dieses Geleit selbst *ist*, H 189 / GA 5 205), and "our essence belongs to the Parousia" (H 176 / GA 5 191). In 1942–43 Heidegger places man's essence in being's escort rather than calling Dasein a being among beings. Henry does not consider the shift. His §13 will take the essay's sentence on the reversal into an argument about Dasein, transcendence and finitude, and the shift will matter there.

### § 13. The ambiguity of "Dasein": essence and determination

**¶1.** Does the fundamental ambiguity of Heidegger's Dasein mean that the philosophy of being, like that of consciousness, could not keep to the rigour of its ontological aim? Or is the inevitability of the fall inscribed in the essence itself, an eidetic property "which historializes itself in it according to its own will"? No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's second alternative is the essay's own thesis about concealment, stated as a hypothesis about the essence. Heidegger traces Hegel's usage to "the hidden way in which being itself reveals and conceals itself" (H 143 / GA 5 155), and asks whether subjectity is "a destiny of being of its own" in which unconcealment withdraws (H 176 / GA 5 192). Henry's "will" of the essence again echoes the essay's will of the absolute (§10 ¶11). Henry's combination is his own: a fall that is eidetic and yet "historializes itself". The essay's withdrawal is a destiny with epochs. Henry's fall is a property of the essence, which history only unfolds.

**¶2.** Transcendence surpasses every being in such a way that beings find their being in it; but in surpassing, the essence also gains its proper sojourn, which is the sojourn of the existent. "Being is the being of beings." The transgression is not without return, because its beyond is the being of every existent as such. No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry here answers the question §8 ¶14 left open, and his answer is the one the essay gives for Hegel: the ontological "does not separate itself from natural consciousness" (H 164 / GA 5 179). The essay adds what Henry's formula leaves unthought: the relation that the reversal uses without thinking it is "the relation of being to beings as the relation of beings to being" (das Verhältnis des Seins zum Seienden als das Verhältnis des Seienden zum Sein, H 182 / GA 5 198). Henry's "being is the being of beings" states that relation as settled. The essay states it as the unthought of metaphysics.

**¶3.** The proposition states the essential unity of being and beings. To penetrate the content is to grasp being within beings; the opposition by which beings always arise as the opposed results from the immanence in them of being, that is, of nothingness. Beings are negated, held at a distance, and so appear; "the concrete life of determinateness is ... the operation of dissolving itself" (Hegel, n. 92: *PhE* I 48). Nothingness preserves the determination it negates; being is identical with nothingness, and negation is interior to the content. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The Hegel quotation is from the Preface, outside the essay. The essay subordinates the negativity Henry relies on. "Everything in any way thetic has its essence in consciousness, in which negativity too is grounded" (worin auch die Negativität gründet, H 168 / GA 5 183), and the interpretation of the dialectical as infinite negativity is "always correct but always only derivative" (H 169 / GA 5 184). For the essay, negativity rests on the dialectic of consciousness as experience, which is the being of beings in subjectity. Henry makes nothingness the being of beings as such, by way of *What is Metaphysics?* and Sartre. Heidegger's 1938–39 treatise on Hegel's negativity (in GA 68) shows that he pursued the question of the ground of negativity further; that text was published in 1993 and its use here is retrospective.

**¶4.** Bound to beings in the act by which it gives them being, transcendence is essentially finite. Its "leap beyond" is a "return upon", since the surpassing opens a place for beings: "all transcendence is as such essentially receptive", and its finitude lies in its receptivity, in the need of what will be received. "Transcendence is the need of beings." No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay supplies a different reception. Its first step of absolute knowing is "to receive and take up" (hin- und aufzunehmen) the absolute in its being-near-us (H 120 / GA 5 131), and the inability "to take in the Parousia" (die Parusie ... hinzunehmen) is the inability for science (H 126 / GA 5 138). The essay's receptivity receives the absolute. Henry's, taken from the Kantbuch, receives beings. In the essay the stance that merely receives what is present belongs to δόξα: "receiving (δέχεσθαι) means here: simply taking in and letting the matter rest with what is present" (Annehmen bedeutet hier: ohne weiteres hinnehmen und es beim Anwesenden bewenden lassen, H 162 / GA 5 176), which "does not think further on the presencing of what is present". The receptivity Henry makes the finitude of transcendence would be, in the essay's terms, the receiving that stays with beings. Henry's "need" has its counterpart in the absolute that "needs the throne" (H 188 / GA 5 204), but the essay's absolute needs "us", not beings.

**¶5.** It is no accident that finitude was first affirmed with full force in Kant: "Kant had to seek finitude in the rational being itself" (Heidegger, n. 94: *K* 224). Pure rationality is finite as transcendence, receptive and turned toward the beings it is not; it constitutes sensibility in itself. No GA 5 locus is engaged; the GA 3 run treats this reading of the Kantbuch. The essay's Kant is the critic who measures knowing "by its nature and limits" (H 118 / GA 5 129), whose concern Hegel's first section dismisses as presupposing knowledge separated from the absolute. The Kant of finitude that Henry draws from 1929 has no place in the 1942–43 essay. There finitude is not the theme, and Hegel's absolute is read without it.

**¶6.** Surpassing beings is identically maintaining them in being; the world is transcendent to "phenomena" only as related to them, and transcendence is finitude itself. The task of ontology is not to break the bond between transcendence and phenomena but to understand it. "In the reversal, says Heidegger, consciousness must not ... abandon its sojourn among beings, it must take it over expressly in its truth" (n. 95). To think that sojourn in its truth is to understand why it is always, for us, a sojourn alongside beings. The quotation (R §13 ¶6, n. 95 / H 190 / GA 5 206) renders "Aber in der Umkehrung soll es den Aufenthalt inmitten des Seienden auch nicht verlassen. Es soll ihn eigens in seiner Wahrheit übernehmen" (But in the reversal it should not leave its sojourn in the midst of beings either. It should take it over expressly in its truth). The subject "es" is natural consciousness, named in the sentence before: "natural consciousness cannot be introduced to where it already is" (Das natürliche Bewußtsein kann nicht dorthin eingeführt werden, wo es schon ist). Henry writes "consciousness". His ellipsis takes out "auch" (either), which ties the sentence to the one before: natural consciousness is not to be led elsewhere, and not to leave either. The reversal here is Heidegger's frame, not Hegel's text: "the lasting need" (die bleibende Not) that consciousness turn from its not-turning-toward the being of beings (H 189–190 / GA 5 206). Hegel's own "reversal of consciousness itself", from the fifteenth section, has a narrower sense. Henry sets the sentence in an argument about Dasein, transcendence and finitude drawn from *Being and Time*, *Vom Wesen des Grundes* and the Kantbuch. The essay's Dasein is Hegel's existence, not the being-there of *Being and Time*, and the sojourn is that of natural consciousness, "we who constantly sojourn in the manner of natural consciousness in the midst of beings" (die wir ständig in der Weise des natürlichen Bewußtseins inmitten des Seienden uns aufhalten, H 148 / GA 5 161). Henry's gloss is nonetheless close to the essay's next paragraph, which he does not quote. The point of the sixteen sections is "that we ourselves be in the experience that is our very being, and this in the old, traditional sense of being: present alongside ... what is present" (damit wir in der Erfahrung, die unser Sein selbst mit *ist*, selbst seien und dies im alten überlieferten Sinn des Seins: anwesend bei ... dem Anwesenden, H 190 / GA 5 206). Henry's "sojourn alongside beings" is Heidegger's "present alongside what is present", and the essay calls it "the old, traditional sense of being", the metaphysical sense it expounds. Henry's use of the sentence is supported by the context he omits. The context also marks the sense as traditional and so belonging to the epoch, a mark Henry takes for the eidetic truth of transcendence. The GA 3 run found that Section II keeps the essay's "already" and drops its need to turn (at R §17 ¶4). Section I keeps the sentence about the reversal but reads it as the finitude of a transcendence that never left beings, so the turn drops out here as well.

**¶7.** The task of thinking the essence cannot be accomplished outside the relation of transcendence to phenomena; hence "the necessity for ontology to give itself an ontic foundation": since being is the being of beings, the question of being is first a question about the being questioned in its being. The finitude of ontology's procedure is the finitude of transcendence. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The thesis is *Being and Time* §4, where Dasein is ontically distinguished by being ontological. The essay has its own form of it: natural consciousness is "ontic-preontological" (H 163 / GA 5 177), and the reversal "does not swerve from straightforward representing onto a side path, but goes, within natural representing, into that from which what it perceives as present first comes to it" (geht innerhalb des natürlichen Vorstellens auf das ein, H 174 / GA 5 189). The essay's ontology stays inside natural representing; Henry's stays tied to beings. Both refuse a pure ontology without ontic anchor. In the essay the anchor is natural consciousness as a stance. In Henry it is transcendence's need of beings.

**¶8.** Phenomenologically, that being is always the being of beings means that manifestation is always manifestation of something that manifests itself: the essence does not accomplish its work of bringing into presence "with regard to itself". "The essence of manifestation is not the manifestation of itself. It is not the essence that manifests itself, it is beings"; Boehme already held that a manifestation can manifest only something other. Manifestation, as manifestation of the other, is essentially finite. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's thesis contradicts the essay's central formula without naming it. The essay says of Hegel's consciousness that in the testing comparison "consciousness is its own self-appearing in appearing. It is present to itself" (ist das Bewußtsein sein eigenes Sicherscheinen im Erscheinen. Es ist sich anwesend, H 164 / GA 5 179); experience is "the reaching-attaining arrival as which appearing appears to itself" (als welches das Erscheinen sich erscheint, H 175 / GA 5 190); the absolute's appearing is "its appearing in absolving self-appearing" (sein Erscheinen im absolventen Sicherscheinen, H 171 / GA 5 186). Henry's "the essence of manifestation is not self-manifestation" denies to the monist essence what the essay asserts of Hegel's absolute. Section I does not engage the essay's formula. Section II will render it as "s'apparaître de l'apparaître" (the self-appearing of appearing, R §§17–21) and argue that this self-appearing is appearing at a distance, hence no self-manifestation in Henry's sense. Here the thesis stands on Boehme and on Henry's own analysis. The essay is the strongest text against it in Henry's corpus, and Section I passes it by.

**¶9.** The essence is what makes being for-itself; but in its act of appearing it is not this act that appears, so the for-itself is not self-manifestation, not "selbst-ständig" (self-standing). It is being-in-itself that becomes for-itself (Sartre), whence the for-itself's contingency; being is "being-for-unveiling and not being unveiled" (Sartre on Heidegger, n. 98). No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's "for-itself" is Sartre's. The essay's Hegelian for-itself is the absolute's self-presence: "bringing itself near in itself, it is for itself" (H 130 / GA 5 141). Its "for us" means "in itself", "appearing out of the absoluteness of the absolute into the pure site of its appearing" (»Für uns« bedeutet: »an sich«, H 191 / GA 5 207). In the essay the for-itself is not the in-itself's becoming-apparent to another. It is the absolute's coming to itself. Henry's critique here is aimed at Sartre and through him at Heidegger's being "for unveiling"; it has no purchase on the essay's Hegel without the Section II argument that this coming-to-itself is itself a distance.

**¶10.** The for-itself seems autonomous only when it is taken as a being opposed to the in-itself; understood as pure manifestation, it is nothing but the appearance of beings themselves, "the presence of being to the for-itself", the *nothing* that realizes this presence (Sartre, n. 100). "It is at the very moment when the essence is understood in its purity that its indissoluble bond to beings is understood." No GA 5 locus is engaged. The unit continues the debate with Sartre. Its conclusion is the essay's on natural consciousness and real knowledge, which are the same without being equal and are not separated (H 138 / GA 5 150). Henry draws it as a consequence of purity: the purer the essence, the more bound to beings. The essay draws it from the structure of consciousness, which is "the original unity of both" (H 145 / GA 5 158).

**¶11.** Because the being of the phenomenon escapes the phenomenal condition, the essence of manifestation conceals itself while doing its work, and this self-concealment is the self-manifestation of beings. "The untruth of the essence is the truth of beings." The light of manifestation shines only on beings; or rather, beings shine in a light that is their own brilliance. Hegel: light is vivifying only "if it applies itself to what is different from itself" (n. 101: *Philosophy of History*). No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's formula takes up Heidegger's thinking of untruth as concealment, which the essay names in passing: being "reveals and conceals itself" (H 143 / GA 5 155), and the site the reversal opens is covered over "*as* the open of that relation" (*als* das Offene jenes Verhältnisses gerade verdeckt, H 182 / GA 5 198). Henry inverts the essay's image of light. The essay speaks of "the light of being" without which natural consciousness "could not even lose itself in beings" (H 136 / GA 5 148). Henry writes that "it is not the light of being that shines on beings, it is beings that shine in it". His inversion makes the light depend on what it lights, which is the finitude of the preceding paragraphs. In the essay the light comes from the absolute, whose ray "shines on us" (H 130 / GA 5 141). Henry's Hegel here is the *Philosophy of History*, not the Introduction.

**¶12.** Only the totality of being and beings is concrete: "being is bound to beings as light to the thing on which it becomes visible". In the work of art light joins the earth: the chthonic element tears itself from night and shines for a moment, and the artist needs stone as the surface on which manifestation can shine. "Earth is the place of light." No GA 5 locus is cited. Henry's earth, stone, column and temple recall the essay on the work of art that opens the same volume of *Holzwege*. That essay is outside this run's range and has not been checked here, so the resemblance is noted, not asserted as a source. If Henry draws on it, he reads Heidegger's earth, which in the artwork essay is what shelters and refuses itself, as the support on which light must shine. That is the finitude of manifestation in his own sense. Within the Hegel essay the nearest counterpart is the absolute that needs "the throne" in order not to be "the lifeless solitary" (H 188 / GA 5 204). Both images make a height depend on a support. In the essay the support is the conceived history of spirit; in Henry it is the opaque being.

**¶13.** The essence can show itself only on the ontic determination and through it; yet "the essence is not the determination, nor is it the phenomenon". The essence of phenomenality is other than its effectivity and finds in it its own suppression; the determination manifests the essence by concealing it, and "the truth of the essence is the untruth of its untruth". No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's denial that the essence ever coincides with its effectivity again runs against the goal of Hegel's Introduction, as at §10 ¶8: appearance "becomes equal to essence" (Hegel's sixteenth section, GA 5 128), and "the pure appearing of the absolute coincides with its essence" (H 181 / GA 5 197). The essay's Hegel has the coincidence; Henry's monism forbids it. Henry's paragraph does not argue with Hegel. It shows, from monism's essence, why no such coincidence can occur, and that derivation will become the critique of Hegelian experience at R §20.

**¶14.** To reach effectivity the essence had to alienate itself, and more originally to lose itself in the realization where it hides by manifesting. The determination is the enigma (n. 103: Kierkegaard): it is semblance (*Schein*), but an appearance (*Erscheinung*) such that what appears in it refers inevitably to what does not appear. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's appearance, which refers to what does not appear, is the one the essay attributes to natural representing and rejects as the sense of Hegel's "appearing knowledge". "Natural representing, which has crept into philosophy here, takes appearing knowledge as merely appearing knowledge, behind which a non-appearing one keeps itself hidden" (nimmt das erscheinende Wissen als das nur erscheinende, hinter dem ein nicht erscheinendes sich verborgen hält, H 131 / GA 5 143); and appearing in the sense of "the advance glimpse of something that has not itself appeared" is inappropriate to science (H 128–129 / GA 5 140). Henry's definition of the determination as *Erscheinung* in the sense of reference to the non-appearing is, by the essay's account, the natural consciousness's notion of appearance, which Hegel's title excludes. Henry's reply would be that his non-appearing is not a thing behind the appearance but the essence itself, which hides in appearing. The essay's Hegel would answer that the essence appears as appearing itself. Here Henry and the essay's Hegel disagree outright. Henry argues from monism's structure, and the essay asserts against him the self-appearing he has just denied (¶8).

**¶15.** The determination can manifest the concealment of the essence only by disappearing: the death of the determination is the manifestation of pure manifestation, and since the essence's element disappears with it, the essence is nothing until a new entity appears. The essence reaches us only through the time of things, and the consciousness that seeks it, led astray by it, can only wander "from experience to experience, from object to object, without finding rest" (n. 104: the Appendix). No GA 5 locus is cited. The last sentence restates Hegel's eighth section, printed in the essay, without its goal. Hegel writes that the progress is "unstoppable" and finds "no satisfaction at any earlier station" (auf keiner frühern Station Befriedigung zu finden); the anxious consciousness "can find no rest" (kann aber keine Ruhe finden, GA 5 122). The tearing-out is the death of what is limited (GA 5 121). The same section opens with the goal: "where knowledge finds itself, and concept corresponds to object" (wo es sich selbst findet und der Begriff dem Gegenstande, der Gegenstand dem Begriffe entspricht, GA 5 121). Henry keeps the unrest and the death and removes the goal. Heidegger's gloss makes the movement come "from the goal" (H 146 / GA 5 159), so that the unrest is already the goal's attraction. Henry's wandering consciousness, led astray by the essence, is the essay's natural consciousness without the will of the absolute that, for Heidegger, exerts the force. The note sends the reader to the Appendix, where Henry works out the critique on Hegel directly. Within Section I, the paragraph is the first statement of the Section II verdict that Hegelian experience has no end because its essence is distance.

### § 14. The relation of essence and ontic determination in the philosophy of consciousness

**¶1.** As the two philosophies exchange their themes they exchange their problems: the bond of being and beings becomes in the philosophy of consciousness the bond of subject and object. The subject relates necessarily to the object because the essence of consciousness refers to the determination as its truth; classical philosophy says that "the subject knows itself only on the object". The object belongs to the internal structure of the essence as what alone lets it realize itself. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's formula supplies the return to self that his §11 ¶9 left out of representation, but makes it depend on the object. The essay states the same structure for the subject of modern metaphysics: the subject "essentially unfolds in the representing relation to the object. But as this relation it is already the representing relation to itself" (H 121 / GA 5 132), and in representing the object "the subject itself presents itself as such" (H 121–122 / GA 5 133). Henry and the essay agree that the subject's self-relation runs through the object. They differ on what that shows. For the essay, it is the structure of subjectity, which completes itself in the absolute's self-certainty. For Henry, it shows that the subject's self-relation is borrowed, and so not a self-manifestation.

**¶2.** In Boehme the absolute manifests itself through opposition, but opposition alone draws only the empty space of a possible manifestation, a diffuse light not conscious of itself. A mirror in which the absolute sees only itself is transparent and reflects nothing; "the opacity of the ontic determination" is the radically other through which opposition is realized, as light is realized only in fire and God bears an eternal body. The ontic belongs to the eidetic structure of the pure essence of manifestation (n. 105: Koyré). No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's Boehme gives the mirror a condition the essay's Hegel does not. In the essay knowing "gives back" the ray and "reflects it", and is "in its essence the ray itself" (H 120 / GA 5 131). No opaque other is required for the reflection. The essay's absolute appears to itself without an opaque surface, through the representation in which the subject presents itself. Henry's thesis that light needs an opaque other is the premise he will turn against that self-appearing. The essay offers no argument for or against it.

**¶3.** Schelling thinks the object as the condition of actual consciousness, "the act by which the thinking subject immediately becomes object" (n. 106); objectification realizes consciousness only in a real, limited object, and "to arrive at consciousness and to be limited are one and the same" (n. 107). In the late philosophy the other of consciousness is a real being, heterogeneous to the essence; creation and the "autonomy of the offspring" (n. 108) have their motive in the conditions of effective phenomenality. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Schelling's equation of consciousness and limitation is the reverse of Hegel's eighth section, printed in the essay. There consciousness is, as its own concept, "immediately the going beyond the limited" (unmittelbar das Hinausgehen über das Beschränkte, GA 5 121), and the limited is natural life, driven beyond by another. For Hegel consciousness is not limitation but the going beyond it. For Henry's Schelling consciousness is limitation itself. Henry gathers both under monism, as the need of the other.

**¶4.** Hegel in turn understood these conditions as those of the realization of the absolute: alienation must include the radically other. As long as it does not, "this alienation is still imperfect; it expresses the relation of self-certainty with the object, which, just because it is in the relation, has not yet gained its full freedom" (n. 109: *PhE* II 311). The early writings speak of a "nostalgic need for a reality" (n. 110) and the "intrusion" of a "real historical element" (n. 111); the alienation constitutive of the Logos becomes effective only in nature. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The *Phenomenology* quotation, from the chapter on absolute knowledge, is outside the essay; the GA 3 run verified Hyppolite's page on the scan. Henry's Hegel requires alienation to release the other into "full freedom". The essay's Heidegger reads Hegel's externalization the other way: as "the holding-in-itself of the fullness of appearing" (das Ansichhalten der Fülle des Erscheinens), "recollection", becoming at home (H 176 / GA 5 192). Two Hegels, the one Henry reads in Hyppolite and the one the essay expounds, diverge on whether externalization releases or retains. Henry's reading of Hegel's alienation, worked out in the Appendix (R §76), is closer to the letter of *PhE* II 311 on this point than the essay's gloss, which concerns the Introduction and its "for us".

**¶5.** Consciousness is always consciousness of something, the external consciousness of the object, which Hegel calls "consciousness". The object is the becoming-conscious of the essence of consciousness, what lets it become conscious of itself: "self-consciousness is identical with consciousness", which is why being-there (être-là) has "the meaning of pure thought" (n. 112: *PhE* II 267). No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's identity of consciousness and self-consciousness is stated in the essay's exposition of Hegel's thirteenth section. "Natural consciousness is at the same time a knowledge of its knowledge of the object, even if it does not turn back expressly to this knowledge. Consciousness of the object and consciousness of knowledge is the same" (Das Bewußtsein vom Gegenstand und das Bewußtsein vom Wissen ist das Selbe, H 159 / GA 5 173). Henry's "être-là" is again Hyppolite's word for Hegel's *Dasein*. The essay's Hegel draws from this identity the possibility of testing: consciousness is the comparison of the two. Henry draws its dependence: self-consciousness exists only through the object.

**¶6.** Fichte poses the problem of the manifestation of "existence": in grasping itself, existence becomes this or that; knowledge divides itself by being evident to itself "as this and that" (n. 116). What does consciousness contain in this state? "The world, and nothing but the world" (n. 117), the sum of beings. "Nothing else is produced in the effective becoming of phenomenality but the ontic determination alone: effective consciousness is the transcendent entity." No GA 5 locus is engaged. Fichte's consciousness that contains "nothing but the world" is, in the essay's terms, natural consciousness, which "finds only beings". Henry makes it the necessary result of objectification. The essay makes it the habitual stance of a consciousness that "does not heed" the being it represents. Henry turns the essay's description of a stance into a law of manifestation.

**¶7.** Does not the pure essence appear in the real content? No: consciousness can only transform the divine life into a world (n. 118), and "absolute consciousness is by itself the immediate and therefore no longer conscious accomplishment of this transformation" (n. 119). "Absolute consciousness is unconscious." The philosophy of consciousness must posit the unconsciousness of the absolute consciousness by which it thinks the essence; in Schelling the limiting activity "never reaches consciousness" (n. 121). "The appearing of consciousness in objectification is its own disappearing." No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay has the same structure for natural consciousness and denies it for the "we". Natural consciousness "sees itself placed in another light, without ever being able to catch sight of this light as such" (ohne doch jemals dieses Licht als solches erblicken zu können, H 138 / GA 5 150), and the formal, the beingness of beings, "is never 'for it'" (H 173 / GA 5 189). But the formal "is 'for us'" (ist »für uns«, H 174 / GA 5 189), who perform the reversal. The essay's Hegel admits Henry's unconsciousness for natural consciousness and lifts it in the "for us". Henry's next paragraph attacks the device by which the lifting is done.

**¶8.** Can the manifestation of pure consciousness be exhibited? Schelling reaches the original act only "by induction" (n. 122), reflectively, from the object. His transcendental point of view seems to lift the paradox, since it "considers the object only through the act of intuition" (n. 123) and "brings to consciousness and makes objective what is absolutely non-objective" (n. 124). But it can bring the non-objective to consciousness only by making it objective, the object of inner sense: the phenomenal becoming of the essence is again its self-suppression. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The objection Henry makes to Schelling's transcendental point of view applies, term for term, to the essay's "for us". Heidegger says that the reversal of consciousness "is one of representing" (die eine solche des Vorstellens ist, H 174 / GA 5 189); that through it "the appearing of what appears comes into the exposition" (H 174 / GA 5 189); and that the exposition "represents the being of beings" (Die Darstellung stellt das Sein des Seienden vor, ibid.). The essay's "for us" brings appearing to view by representing it. If Henry's objection to Schelling holds, it holds against the essay's reversal: a representing of appearing makes the essence an object and so loses it. Henry does not draw this consequence in Section I. Section II draws it at R §§17–21, against "our contribution" and the reversal (H 189–190, 173–181). The unit prepares that engagement without citing the essay.

**¶9.** The classical essence of manifestation carries a double demand: consciousness must objectify itself to become effective, a "philosophy of effectivity"; yet the essence's purity requires recognizing that its manifestation in the entity is inadequate, a cipher pointing elsewhere. Freedom endlessly displaces the limit it posits (Fichte); the contradiction remains Kant's "we know only phenomena", and the philosophy of consciousness has saved the absolute only by banishing it to a "world behind". No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's "world behind" (arrière-monde) is what the essay calls the derived form of metaphysics. Metaphysics as the separation of "a supersensible and a sensible world", the former truly being and the latter only seemingly, is "the non-essence (*Unwesen*) of metaphysics" (H 162–163 / GA 5 176–177). Hegel's appearing knowledge, on the essay's account, has no hidden behind (H 131 / GA 5 143). Henry and the essay reject the two-world solution together. They differ on who escapes it: for the essay, Hegel; for Henry, no philosophy of consciousness.

**¶10.** But do these difficulties remain obstacles if the essence is understood concretely, in the effectivity of its realization? It is the claim to grasp the essence outside its reference to the determination that must be questioned: "the pure essence is the abstraction of being-present"; "it is beings that appear"; effective being-there is ontic and ontological at once. If Dasein designates presence in its real accomplishment, is it not legitimate to call transcendence "the essential way of being of this being" that Dasein also is (n. 127: *SZ* 132), and to speak of "an ontic possibility of the understanding of being" (n. 128: *SZ* 212)? Is not the ambiguity of Dasein grounded in the structure of effective phenomenality? No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry here states, as a position to be weighed, the essay's Hegelian solution. "What appears is thereby neither eliminated nor separated from real knowledge" (Das Erscheinende wird dadurch weder beseitigt, noch vom realen Wissen abgetrennt, H 138 / GA 5 150); natural consciousness is "ontic-preontological" (H 163 / GA 5 177); the actuality of the actual is appearing itself (H 134 / GA 5 146). Effective being-there that is ontic and ontological at once is the essay's natural consciousness taken in its unity with real knowledge. Henry asks his question in the vocabulary of *Being and Time*, but the position he sketches is closest to Hegel as the essay presents him. §16 will refuse it.

### § 15. The ontological signification of the problematic that aims at the essence and the originary concept of finitude

**¶1.** A problematic aimed at the essence means that the essence is by itself the condition of appearance. If the ontic element were a condition of that possibility, it would belong to the essence and be ontological; the distinction between ontic and ontological would have to be rethought. "Are beings, yes or no, a condition of manifestation?" No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry and the essay both find the distinction of ontic and ontological unsettled, from opposite sides. Henry asks whether beings might belong to the act of appearing, which would collapse the distinction. Heidegger says the ambiguity of ὄν "names both at once and neither as such" (H 162 / GA 5 176), and that Hegel "distinguishes all this but levels the differences" (H 161 / GA 5 175). For Heidegger the distinction is unthought; for Henry it is in danger of being erased. Henry will restore it in the next paragraph.

**¶2.** The thesis that beings are a condition of the essence's effectivity is absurd: beings could be there only if appearing had already done its work, so they always come too late. The in-itself has nothing in common with the for-itself and returns to its night; the light that manifests is indifferent to the singular content. "It is not the determinate character of the object but its objective character that founds its phenomenal character." No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's distinction of the object's determinate content from its objectness is the essay's distinction of object and objectness (*Gegenstand*, *Gegenständlichkeit*). Natural consciousness "has a general representation of its object as object", but does not enter into the "as" (H 160 / GA 5 174); the "new object" of experience is objectness, which "arises on the old object" (am alten Gegenstand der neue, die Gegenständlichkeit, entsteht, H 172 / GA 5 188). Henry's reply to the question of ¶1 is the essay's distinction. The essay draws the distinction inside a single movement of experience; Henry uses it to set beings outside the essence's work.

**¶3.** Does the indifference of appearing to its content imply the self-standing (*Selbständigkeit*) of the essence? Perhaps contingency presupposes some content: Kant's categories require an empirical manifold for their real use, so that the ontic may belong necessarily to the essence's accomplishment despite its contingency. No GA 5 locus is engaged; the Kantian premise belongs to the GA 3 run. The essay does not ask whether being needs some content or other. Its Hegel holds that the absolute appears only in the shapes of consciousness, and that the conceived history of spirit is the throne without which the absolute would be lifeless (H 188 / GA 5 204). The essay's answer to Henry's question would be Hegelian: the absolute needs its history, not an arbitrary manifold. Henry does not take up that answer.

**¶4.** The essence is thought, by both philosophies, as opposition, and what arises in it is set "before". What comes forth through opposition "in a foreground of light" (dans un avant-plan de lumière) as the condition of manifestation is not beings but this foreground itself, the transcendental horizon of being. What stands at a distance in transcendence is the horizon of nothingness: "transcendence objects to itself not beings but their place." No GA 5 locus is cited, but Henry's phrase is the essay's. "The foreground of the light within which every kind of knowledge and consciousness already stands" (der Vordergrund des Lichtes, innerhalb dessen jede Art von Wissen und Bewußtsein als ein Gesehenhaben schon steht, H 164 / GA 5 178) names the truth of natural consciousness: what philosophy's opinion takes for a "behind" is in truth a "before". Henry takes up the word and assigns it to transcendence's objecting of the horizon. He also adds what the essay does not say. Heidegger's foreground is the light of being, not nothingness. The essay's "nothing" is what natural consciousness takes being for: from its standpoint "the appearing of what appears ... counts as something null" (etwas Nichtiges, H 144 / GA 5 157), while Hegel's determinate negation is "the nothing of that from which it comes" (H 145 / GA 5 157). Henry's horizon of nothingness comes from *What is Metaphysics?* and the Kantbuch. Joined to the essay's foreground, it makes the light of being a nothing objected by transcendence, which is Henry's thesis and not the essay's.

**¶5.** Consciousness-philosophy falls into equivocation when it describes "objectification", because it does not separate in the object what is ontic from what is ontological. The content of the object is the ontic determination; its condition as ob-ject is the essence. "To this effective becoming beings are wholly foreign: they neither contribute to it nor result from it." No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's charge that the philosophy of consciousness fails to separate object and objectness is not the essay's charge against Hegel. Heidegger says that Hegel "distinguishes all this", object and knowledge, object and concept in the "as", "but levels the differences into a general distinguishing" (H 161 / GA 5 175). Heidegger's Hegel has the distinction and flattens it; Henry's philosophy of consciousness lacks it. The difference matters for Section II, where Henry contests the essay's own use of the distinction (R §17, at H 161–163).

**¶6.** Until that distinction is made, the claim that consciousness creates the object is inevitable and paradoxical. Consciousness does create the object, the being-placed-before; but absolute idealism confuses beings with the effective conditions of phenomenality and so believes it can deduce beings, taking an ideal suppression for a real one (Marx, n. 129). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay connects idealist objectification with a claim over beings in its own way. The modern technical world "is nothing other than natural consciousness, which ... carries out the unconditioned, self-securing producibility of all beings in the unstoppable objectification of everything" (H 137–138 / GA 5 150); and within subjectity "every being as such becomes object" (H 177 / GA 5 192). Heidegger adds that absolute metaphysics is "not the cause" of what is established in technology (H 138 / GA 5 150). Marx, quoted by Henry, charges idealism with confusing objectification with the positing of beings. Heidegger traces the objectification of beings, which is real, to the epoch of subjectity. Henry's charge is logical: a confusion of levels. Heidegger's account is historical: an epoch in which the confusion becomes the world. Henry does not consider the essay's version.

**¶7.** Finitude must be understood in its relation to being. Ontic knowledge is finite because receptive, its content not being what it objects; but it receives beings only as objects, so its finitude is that of a knowledge that needs the object, the finitude of ontological knowledge. No GA 5 locus is engaged; the analysis is the Kantbuch's, treated in the GA 3 run. The essay is silent about finitude. Its Hegel's absolute knowledge is not finite, and Heidegger expounds it without once opposing finitude to it. The silence matters for Henry's joining of the two texts. The Kantbuch makes the understanding of being the ground of human finitude; the Hegel essay makes the absolute's being-near-us the ground of our essence. When Henry reads both as one philosophy of transcendence, the essay's silence on finitude has to be read as consent or as a turn, and Henry does not say which.

**¶8.** Ontological knowledge creates its own object, the horizon; it is finite because the horizon is finite. "It is the place where light arises, it is the place itself as such that is finite. Light is this finitude of a place." Finitude is an eidetic structure of the essence of phenomenality. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay's site (*Stätte*) of appearing, which Henry has read as world and horizon (§9 ¶3), is not finite in this sense. The reversal "opens the compass of the site in which appearing appears to itself" and thereby "secures the completeness and the progress of the experience of consciousness" (sichert sie die Vollständigkeit und den Fortgang, H 176 / GA 5 191); "for us" means "appearing out of the absoluteness of the absolute into the pure site of its appearing" (H 191 / GA 5 207). The site of the essay is complete, not finite. Henry's "light is the finitude of a place" can be applied to it only by the Section II argument that the absolute's completeness is obtained in distance.

**¶9.** "What is at issue at bottom is to bring to light the essential intertwining of being (not of beings) as such and finitude in man" (Heidegger, n. 130: *K* 278). What is finite is being itself, not beings, which by themselves are not finite: beings are finite only as objects, encountered by a being that must unfold the finite horizon of being to receive them (*Gegenstand* as against the infinite intuition's *Enstand*). No GA 5 locus is engaged; this too is the Kantbuch. The essay's counterpart to the intertwining of being and man is the "not without us": the absolute's being-near-us "has revealed itself as the 'not without us'" (hat sich enthüllt als das »nicht ohne uns«, H 188 / GA 5 204). There the dependence is not finitude. It is the absolute's will to be near us, and it requires us as those who "sceptically heed the being of beings". The 1929 intertwining of being and finitude in man becomes in 1942–43 the absolute's need of us, without finitude. Henry quotes the first, and the second does not appear in Section I.

**¶10.** Transcendence is finite because receptive, but what it receives is what it forms: freedom "cannot escape what thus arises from it" (n. 131: *Vom Wesen des Grundes*), that is, the world. The limit of transcendence is not beings, as obstacle or resistance against which freedom becomes conscious of itself; existence is limited because transcendence bears its limit in itself as what it produces (n. 132). No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's internal limit has the structure the essay finds in Hegel's eighth section. The force that tears natural consciousness beyond itself "comes from consciousness itself" (kommt jedoch aus dem Bewußtsein selbst, H 147–148 / GA 5 160–161); consciousness "suffers this violence from itself" (Hegel, GA 5 122). Henry and the essay agree that the limit is not an external obstacle. They disagree about what the internal limit does. For Henry it binds transcendence to its finite world; for Hegel and Heidegger it drives consciousness beyond every limited shape toward the goal.

**¶11.** Husserl already saw, in *Experience and Judgment*, that finitude lies not in the ontic content of representation but in its structure: the absolute substrate, nature, is never the theme of a simple grasp, and every object carries a "horizon of indeterminate determinability" (n. 134). Finitude lies in the mode of givenness, the being as object; the infinite diversity of beings is its consequence, and "as the horizon is beyond beings, beings are beyond the horizon". No GA 5 locus is engaged. The unit closes the loop opened at §8 ¶4. There Henry asked whether an appearance's endless reference to what is not given points to a deeper finitude. That reference is the essay's second and third modes of appearing (H 128–129 / GA 5 140), and here Henry grounds it in the finitude of the horizon of being. The essay removed science's appearing from those modes. Henry makes them the trace of the finitude of being itself. The two answers to the same structure part company here. Heidegger's Hegel overcomes horizonal appearing in the appearing of absolute knowledge. Henry makes horizonal appearing the truth of monism's essence.

**¶12.** The ontological character of finitude confirms that beings are foreign to the essence's work: the effective becoming of phenomenality is accomplished without them, and its finitude has its principle elsewhere. This confirms the radical independence of the essence in its effective reality from every ontic determination. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay agrees that being is not a being: experience, the being of beings, "is nowhere to be found as a component among beings" (nirgends als ein seiendes Bestandstück innerhalb des Seienden vorfindlich, H 172 / GA 5 187). It denies that this makes being independent of the stance among beings, since the ontological "does not separate itself from natural consciousness" (H 164 / GA 5 179). Henry's §13 sided with the second claim and his §15 with the first. He reconciles them by the distinction between objectness, to which the essence is bound, and beings, from which it is free. The essay's distinction of object and objectness permits that reconciliation (¶2), but the essay does not make it.

### § 16. The idea of the formal structure of the autonomy of the essence and the task of a repetition of the ontological elucidation of the concept of phenomenon

**¶1.** The effective becoming of the essence is manifestation manifesting itself, and its independence from beings means that phenomenal becoming is realized in and by the essence: the horizon of being must show itself. "The horizon of being considered in its purity must be perceptible in and for itself. Being must be able to show itself." Because the essence includes phenomenal becoming, it is autonomous. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's demand is what the essay asserts of Hegel's absolute. Appearing "appears to itself" (H 175 / GA 5 190); the formal, the beingness of beings, is "for us" (H 174 / GA 5 189); consciousness in the testing is "its own self-appearing in appearing" (H 164 / GA 5 179). Henry's "in and for itself" is Hegel's phrase for the absolute's being-near-us (an und für sich schon bei uns, GA 5 116). The demand Henry makes of the monist essence, that it show itself, is satisfied in the essay's Hegel by self-appearing. Henry's Section II will have to show that this satisfaction is only apparent, and it begins at once with the essay (R §17 ¶3, at H 126).

**¶2.** Autonomy means the self-standing of ontological knowledge, which is the condition of ontic knowledge; whether being is always the being of beings cannot yet be decided. "Because the nothing is revealed, science can make beings themselves the object of its research" (n. 135: *What is Metaphysics?*, emphasis Henry's). The determination of beings by being presupposes the manifestation of the determining element itself: "the determining element itself must show itself with a certain clarity" (n. 136: *K* 279). No GA 5 locus is engaged. The Kantbuch sentence, and the translation on which Henry's reading of it rests, are examined in the GA 3 run at R §17 ¶2. The essay has its own version of the demand that the determining show itself. Its skepsis "has seen in advance the being of beings" (Ihr Zusehen hat im vorhinein das Sein des Seienden gesehen, H 140 / GA 5 152), and "the having-seen of the skepsis is the knowledge of absoluteness" (H 177 / GA 5 193). On the essay's terms, being shows itself in the having-seen, the *vidi* that it has identified with representing (H 133 / GA 5 145). Henry's demand is met there by representation, which is the reason his Section II will reject it.

**¶3.** The understanding of ontological knowledge as the condition of ontic knowledge does not impair the essence's self-standing if the manifestation of beings presupposes that of the horizon. Self-standing lies in the immanence of phenomenal becoming to the essence of phenomenality; its meaning and possibility must be clarified. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay's counterpart to that immanence is its thesis that the exposition belongs to experience: "the experience to be thought here does not belong to the exposition as a mark of its character; the exposition belongs to the essence of experience" (die Darstellung gehört in das Wesen der Erfahrung, H 168 / GA 5 182), and "appearing knowledge's appearing includes representing itself in its presence, that is, exhibiting itself" (H 171 / GA 5 186). In the essay phenomenal becoming is immanent to the being of consciousness as its exposition. Henry will render that exposition (*Darstellung*) as "représentation" (representation) in Section II, a rendering that is his own and that turns the essay's immanence into the representation he has already defined as alienation.

**¶4.** Autonomy makes the designation of Dasein as a being still more incomprehensible: if Dasein designates the effective reality of manifestation, beings have been excluded from it. "The effective becoming of manifestation is being-there (l'être-là) ... Dasein as such is ontological." No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's conclusion agrees with the essay's use of the word, by a coincidence across two senses of the word. In the essay *Dasein* is Hegel's existence, glossed as presencing: "to presence ... : appearing in the immediate sense of coming forth into unconcealment: presencing, existence (Dasein)" (H 134 / GA 5 146), and phenomenology is "the name for the Dasein of spirit" (der Name für das Dasein des Geistes, H 185 / GA 5 201). On the essay's terms Dasein is purely ontological, a presencing, not a being. Henry reaches the same sentence about *Being and Time*'s Dasein against its author. The agreement is verbal. It shows, though, that the essay's Hegelian Dasein is the effective becoming of manifestation that Henry describes, and Henry's "être-là", which is also Hyppolite's word for Hegel's *Dasein*, joins the two.

**¶5.** The positive meaning of autonomy is that the pure essence cannot be separated from the phenomenal becoming in which it realizes itself: as included in the essence, phenomenal becoming is its reality, and the essence finds its reality in itself. No GA 5 locus is engaged. Henry's formula of autonomy describes, in form, the essay's Hegelian absolute: experience "is the absoluteness of the absolute, its appearing in absolving self-appearing" (H 171 / GA 5 186), and "at this point the pure appearing of the absolute coincides with its essence" (H 181 / GA 5 197). An essence that finds its reality in its own appearing is the absolute of the essay. Henry asks whether the monist essence can be such an essence. The essay answers for Hegel that it is, and Henry's book will argue that it is not.

**¶6.** The possibility of self-standing lies in the essence: under monism, the immanence of phenomenal becoming to the essence means that the horizon opened by the essence manifests itself in its purity. "With the manifestation of the horizon, being shows itself." The problem of the phenomenal becoming of the essence is that of its internal structure. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay locates the possibility in the reversal: "the reversal opens and delimits the site of the history of the formation of consciousness" (Die Umkehrung öffnet und umgrenzt die Stätte, H 176 / GA 5 191). For the essay the horizon, or site, shows itself because the reversal turns toward appearing. Henry places the possibility in the essence's internal structure, with no "us" and no contribution. The difference anticipates Section II's verdict on "our contribution" (*Zutat*), which Henry will read as a sign that monism's essence cannot show itself unaided.

**¶7.** The essence is autonomous in the ultimate sense that the effective reality of manifestation produced in it also finds its ground in it. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The essay's Hegel has the same self-grounding, in the propositions "the absolute alone is true, the true alone is absolute", which "cannot be grounded" because "they have posited what itself first grounds" (H 124 / GA 5 135). Henry's autonomous essence and the essay's absolute are both self-grounding. Henry's question is whether a self-grounding obtained through the opposition of monism is a self-grounding at all.

**¶8.** Only the elucidation of the ground of this immanence can say whether phenomenal becoming coincides wholly with the essence, "whether the originary and pure essence is the truth or whether it is also untruth". With beings set aside in their claim to manifest or hide the essence, that double possibility must be asked of the essence itself. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The question of the essence's own untruth is Heidegger's question of concealment. In the essay it surfaces as the hidden way "in which being itself reveals and conceals itself" (H 143 / GA 5 155), and as the open that the reversal opens and covers over "*as* the open" (H 182 / GA 5 198). Henry turns the question away from the beings in which §13 located concealment and toward the essence. That is where the essay locates it, in being's own self-concealment, though the essay does not say whether that concealment belongs to the essence of manifestation or to an epoch.

**¶9.** The task of phenomenology, the elucidation of the essence of the phenomenon, has been shown to find its possibility in the essence. The first elucidation, under monism's presuppositions, showed at least that the essence must be able to manifest itself: "being must be able to show itself". Understanding this possibility requires a repetition of the elucidation, which will put monism's presuppositions in question and introduce us to the originary essence of revelation. No GA 5 locus is engaged. The repetition begins, in Section II, with the essay: Henry's first move there takes the essay's word Parousia for the manifestation of being that is "already" effective (R §17 ¶3, at H 126). The demand with which Section I closes, that being show itself, is the essay's Hegelian demand that science be "with its first step already in the Parousia of the absolute" (H 126 / GA 5 137). Henry inherits the demand from the essay and will turn it against the essay. On the essay's own account its Parousia is presence "in the manner of representation".

Across Section I Henry makes the essay load-bearing in two ways, by citation and by vocabulary. The nine citations do four jobs. The first is to define the essence of consciousness as representation and subjectity as presence in the manner of representation (H 133–134 at §11 ¶¶9, 17, 25). The second is to supply, from the essay's ontic consciousness (H 161, 173 at §11 ¶3, §12 ¶2), the omission of being for which Heidegger is said to reject the philosophy of consciousness. The third is to fix the bond of transcendence to beings (H 190 at §13 ¶6). The fourth is to give the essence a definition (H 129 at §8 ¶7). The quotations are mostly exact. The departures fall in a pattern, and each serves Henry's thesis. At H 161 Henry cuts the clause that gives ὄν a history and the frame that marks "ontic consciousness" as Heidegger's own earlier distinction, applied to Hegel with reservations. He renders subjectity as subjectivity, adds "human" to the subject of H 134, and drops the actuality of the actual that ties the sentence to Hegel's real knowledge. He collapses shining, appearing, seeming and semblance at H 129 into one "act of appearing". The essay's history, its marking of voice, and its distinctions within appearing are what Henry removes, and they are what would stop the essay's account of modern metaphysics from becoming an eidetic of manifestation as such. Once, at §11 ¶25, Henry marks the voice ("commenting on classical philosophy") and argues the transfer. Even there the argument rests on his own §10, where representation is grounded in an alienation that the essay does not ascribe to it: the essay's representation returns to the subject, and its externalization is a becoming-at-home. The uncited presence of the essay is larger than the cited. The Parousia sought on the ground of the Parousia (§8 ¶9), real knowledge (§8 ¶8), the formal (§8 ¶6), the foreground of light (§15 ¶4) and the immanence of absolute knowledge in natural consciousness (§8 ¶12) all come from it, and are absorbed into Henry's voice without a reference. Where Henry's reading holds against the text, it holds on the essay's own ground. The essay does define modern consciousness as representation and subjectity as presence in representation. It does license "being" for objectivity (GA 5 154). It does place the truth of natural consciousness in a light that natural consciousness cannot see. And the sentence on the sojourn among beings is borne out by the "present alongside what is present" that follows it. Where it does not hold, the failure is not exegetical carelessness but a refusal to let the essay's history count. Heidegger presents presence in representation as one destiny of being and limits beingness-as-presence to "one, though decisive, way of being". Henry needs the identity of the philosophy of consciousness and the philosophy of being to be eidetic, and he dissolves the history into an unchanging presupposition. The section's sharpest tension with the essay is left unaddressed: Henry denies that the monist essence manifests itself (§13 ¶8), while the essay's central formula, the self-appearing of appearing, asserts exactly that of Hegel's absolute. Section I passes the formula by. Section II takes it up, and the GA 3 run's treatment of that engagement is now to be re-read against GA 5.

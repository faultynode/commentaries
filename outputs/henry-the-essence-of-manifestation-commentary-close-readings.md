# Michel Henry, *L'essence de la manifestation*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the block of the [main commentary](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md) that it extends. The French is quoted from the PUF text in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/henry/henry-lessence-de-la-manifestation.md`, an ebook conversion of the 2011 reprint). That file keeps Henry's italics, so emphasis can be argued from. It has no page numbers, so passages are cited, as in the commentary, by section and by the commentary's paragraph labels. All English renderings of Henry, Hegel and Heidegger are working renderings, kept close to the French so that its structure shows.

Two sigla recur. *K* is the 1953 French translation of Heidegger's *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* by de Waelhens and Biemel. A *K* page resolves only in `heidegger-ga-3-kant-et-le-probleme-de-la-metaphysique-biemel.md`, where it equals the pdf page minus two, and each *K* passage is given with its place in GA 3. *H* is the separate edition of *Holzwege*, which Henry quotes in his own French for Heidegger's essay on Hegel's concept of experience. The corpus copy of that essay (`heidegger-ga-5-hegels-begriff-der-erfahrung.md`) prints the *Holzwege* page numbers in the margin, so *H* pages can be checked against the German.

One rendering needs saying in advance. Henry's French pair *existentiel* / *existential* is the French rendering of Heidegger's *existenziell* / *existenzial*. These readings render it "existentiell" / "existential", as the English translation of *Sein und Zeit* does. So what the main commentary calls Henry's "existential determinations" appear here as "existentiell determinations".

## §17 ¶2, K 279: the future tense the translators supplied

Reads [§17, Paragraph 2](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-originary-character-of-the-manifestation-of-being-and-the-problem-of-natural-consciousness).

> No less ambiguous is the proposition cited earlier, according to which "in order that one may understand the essential determination of the existent by being, the determining element itself will have to show itself with a certain clarity". The manifestation of being, understood as the element that determines the existent (as constitutive of its ontological structure), is here interpreted as having to come about in the future, and so is referred to a work of elucidation that it calls for, and calls for precisely in order to come about, at least "with a certain clarity". *The determination of the existent by being is nonetheless realized prior to the philosopher's understanding of this determination, and this determination, prior to every philosophical understanding, nonetheless presupposes the manifestation of being, inasmuch as it is in fact nothing other than this manifestation itself.* For it is on the ground of this manifestation that the existent is what it is. To natural consciousness, which grasps the existent each time with its own characters, being is already given: the manifestation of being is originary.

French:

> Non moins ambiguë est la proposition précédemment citée selon laquelle « pour que l’on puisse comprendre l’essentielle détermination de l’étant par l’être il faudra que l’élément déterminant lui-même se montre avec une certaine clarté ». La manifestation de l’être compris comme l’élément déterminant l’étant (en tant que constitutif de la structure ontologique de celui-ci) est ici interprétée comme devant se produire dans le futur, référée par conséquent à un travail d’élucidation qu’elle appelle, et cela justement pour se produire, du moins « avec une certaine clarté ». *La détermination de l’étant par l’être se réalise pourtant antérieurement à la compréhension de cette détermination par le philosophe, et cette détermination antérieure à toute compréhension philosophique présuppose néanmoins la manifestation de l’être en tant qu’elle n’est rien d’autre, en fait, que cette manifestation elle-même*. Car c’est sur le fond de cette manifestation que l’étant est ce qu’il est. A la conscience naturelle qui saisit chaque fois l’étant avec ses caractères propres, l’être est d’ores et déjà donné : la manifestation de l’être est originaire.

The whole charge hangs on a tense. Henry reads "will have to" (*il faudra*) as a future and glosses it at once: the manifestation of being is interpreted "as having to come about in the future" (*comme devant se produire dans le futur*). The future is the translators'. At GA 3 §40, 223 Heidegger writes that the determining "must be sufficiently graspable" (*muß das Bestimmende selbst hinreichend faßbar*), a present necessity, and goes on to say that being "must first be conceived" (*muß zuvor begriffen werden*). The French (K 279) made two changes. It put the necessity into the future, and it turned "sufficiently graspable" into "shows itself with a certain clarity" (*se montre avec une certaine clarté*). Between them they convert a condition on a question, namely which concept the question of being must form first, into a sequence in time in which being shows itself later, once philosophy has done its work. Henry's reading takes over both changes. His "in the future" restates the French tense, and his second quotation, "at least 'with a certain clarity'" (*du moins « avec une certaine clarté »*), restates the French verb. The English translation in the corpus (`heidegger-ga-3-kant-and-the-problem-of-metaphysics.md`) follows the German: "the determining itself must be sufficiently comprehensible". Read in that version, the sentence gives Henry nothing to correct.

The attribution is also handled with care. When Henry first quoted the sentence, at §16, he wrote "says Heidegger" (src L585). Here it is "the proposition cited earlier", with no name, and the note points back to §16 and not to Heidegger. The charge is laid against a proposition and its grammar. That is also the only level on which it holds.

Henry's own thesis runs on a different word. "At least" (*du moins*) concedes that only the clarity is deferred. The italic sentence then drops that concession and asserts more: the determination of the existent by being is realized before the philosopher understands it, and it "presupposes" the manifestation of being "inasmuch as it is in fact nothing other than this manifestation itself". The step from determining to manifesting is carried by "in fact" (*en fait*) and by the identity it introduces, not by an argument. That identity of being with appearing is the premise of the monism Section I took apart, and Henry turns it here against a sentence that does not need it. GA 3 §41, 228 already says that the understanding of being pervades existence without being conceived. So the thesis stands without the polemic, as the main commentary says. The close reading adds where the polemic came from: a French future tense.

The vocabulary of "understanding" (*compréhension*) slips too. In this paragraph "understanding" is the philosopher's thematic grasp, and it comes after being has manifested itself. Two pages later (§18 ¶7) "ontological understanding" (*compréhension ontologique*) names the originary manifestation itself, which comes before every grasp. The French had already rendered Heidegger's *begreifen* (conceive) by "understand" (*comprendre*), so the word Henry inherits from *K* covers both the concept and the understanding that GA 3 keeps apart. The paragraph's closing word, "already" (*d'ores et déjà*), picks up the contrast of ¶1 between the "at last" of accomplished phenomenology and the "already" of its condition. It prepares the claim at §21 ¶7 that philosophy always comes too late.

## §17 ¶6, H 163 and 144: "forget" in quotation marks

Reads [§17, Paragraph 6](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-originary-character-of-the-manifestation-of-being-and-the-problem-of-natural-consciousness).

> Because the manifestation of being is originary, because natural consciousness holds itself, in conformity with its essence, in the truth, it cannot be said to turn away from the truth. *Consciousness cannot forget the being that constitutes its very essence.* When it is nonetheless asserted that this consciousness lives, as natural consciousness, in the forgetting of being, what is meant is that it keeps to the existent and, in doing so, holds for nothing what is not of this nature, that is, being, the appearing of what appears. Natural consciousness does indeed direct itself toward the existent, and in its exclusive preoccupation with the existent it does not concern itself with being. But natural consciousness can be preoccupied with the existent only if the existent shows itself to it. And the manifestation of the existent is, as such, the manifestation of being. *Not for an instant has being ceased to show itself to natural consciousness at the very moment when it is said to "forget" it.*

French:

> Parce que la manifestation de l’être est originaire, parce que la conscience naturelle se tient, conformément à son essence, dans la vérité, elle ne saurait être dite se détourner de celle-ci. *La conscience ne peut oublier l’être qui constitue son essence même*. Quand on affirme cependant que cette conscience vit, comme conscience naturelle, dans l’oubli de l’être, on veut dire qu’elle s’en tient à l’étant et, ce faisant, tient pour rien ce qui n’est pas de cette nature, c’est-à-dire l’être, l’apparaître de l’apparaissant. La conscience naturelle, en effet, se dirige vers l’étant et dans sa préoccupation exclusive à l’égard de celui-ci, elle ne se soucie pas de l’être. La conscience naturelle ne peut cependant se préoccuper de l’étant que si celui-ci se montre à elle. Mais la manifestation de l’étant est, comme telle, la manifestation de l’être. *Pas un instant l’être n’a cessé de se montrer à la conscience naturelle au moment même où elle est dite l’ « oublier »*.

The paragraph uses four verbs for natural consciousness's relation to being. It refuses two: consciousness cannot "turn away" (*se détourner*) from the truth, and it cannot "forget" (*oublier*) being. It grants two: consciousness "holds for nothing" (*tient pour rien*) and "does not concern itself" (*ne se soucie pas*). It reconciles the two pairs by translating the refused verb into a granted one: to say that consciousness forgets being is to say that it holds being for nothing. The quotation marks around "forget" in the last sentence mark this. The word is kept at a distance because it is someone else's word, and because, once translated, it no longer means forgetting.

Henry's notes show whose words these are. Both cite Heidegger's essay on Hegel. Note 8 (*H* 163) quotes natural consciousness as "always already brought onto the way of its truth", which "on the way, always already too, constantly turns back". The German has "on the way it also keeps turning back" (*unterwegs kehrt es auch schon ständig um*). Henry's main text sharpens turning back into turning away. Yet the sentence he cites grants what he asserts, since consciousness is "already brought onto the way to its truth" (*schon auf den Weg zu seiner Wahrheit gebracht*). Henry's quotation also stops before the clause that follows, "and remains for itself" (*und bleibt für es*), which is the Hegelian "for it" he takes up at §19. Note 9 (*H* 144) supplies the granted verb: "the appearing of what appears, the reality of the real, passes in the purview of natural consciousness for something null" (*gelten … als etwas Nichtiges*). Henry's "holds for nothing … the appearing of what appears" (*tient pour rien … l'apparaître de l'apparaissant*) is that sentence turned into a definition. "Forget", on the other hand, is not in the essay. The corpus text of it has neither *Vergessenheit* (forgetfulness) nor any form of *vergessen* (forget). Henry brings the general Heideggerian doctrine of the forgetting of being to a text that says something narrower, and then defines the doctrine by that narrower text. The quotation marks mark the import.

The grammar keeps the doctrine without an author. The main text says "when it is asserted" (*quand on affirme*), "what is meant" (*on veut dire*), and "it is said to 'forget' it" (*elle est dite l'« oublier »*). Heidegger appears only in the notes, and there he is cited with "cf.", as a pointer to compare and not as the source of the quoted word. As at ¶2, Henry argues against a proposition and leaves its author in the apparatus.

The argument turns on "as such" (*comme telle*): "the manifestation of the existent is, as such, the manifestation of being". From that identity, the final "not for an instant" follows at once. But the identity proves that being is manifest. It does not prove that being is manifest *as being*, and that is the problem Heidegger's sentence names: in natural consciousness the appearing appears as null. Henry needs a reply to this, and §18 gives it, splitting "taking heed of being" into a thematic and an ontological sense. Henry's ground for refusing forgetting, that being "constitutes its very essence", also cuts both ways. If consciousness cannot forget its essence because it is that essence, neither can it hold that essence before itself. Henry draws this consequence himself at §45, whose title speaks without quotation marks of the dissimulation of the originary essence of revelation "and its forgetting" (*et son oubli*). The word refused here returns there, but with a new object: the immanence that Henry's own Section II will put in place of transcendence.

## §18 ¶5–6, H 163: the ontological and the existentiell, and Heidegger's "not yet"

Reads [§18, Paragraphs 5 and 6](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-concept-of-representation-ontological-structure-and-existential-understanding).

> Let us make here a distinction important for the rest of our inquiry, a distinction between what is ontological and what is existentiell. The "taking heed of being" of true knowledge and the "not taking heed of being" of natural consciousness are modes of the life of consciousness, determinations of its existence. We shall call these determinations *existentiell determinations*. The "taking heed of being" inherent in the essence of consciousness, and as such indifferent both to the "taking heed of being" of true knowledge and to the "not taking heed of being" of natural consciousness, is on the contrary, as that originary manifestation of self of being which constitutes consciousness itself in its universal essence, an *ontological structure*. The relation between the ontological structure of consciousness and the existentiell determinations it can take on in its life is easy to understand, at least as far as what is said here goes: the ontological structure of consciousness is at once indifferent and immanent to its existentiell determinations. Indifferent, because this structure does not prescribe that this or that existentiell determination of consciousness occur, for instance that consciousness, turning back on its own essence, direct itself upon it in order to grasp it in true knowledge. In conformity with this indifference, consciousness can exist without true knowledge occurring in it. *Existentiell determinations are inessential*: they are, in relation to the essence, contingent and variable predicates. But the inessential character of these determinations does not mean that they are separated from the essence. On the contrary, the essence is immanent to them as what makes them possible, as their very essence. That is why natural knowledge *is* ontological. The distinction in consciousness between its existentiell determinations and its ontological structure lays bare the ambiguity of a proposition such as the one by which Heidegger still tries to characterize natural knowledge and to underline its contradiction: "natural consciousness is and is not… ontological". Natural consciousness is ontological inasmuch as the essence of consciousness in its universal structure is present in it. It is improper to say that natural consciousness *is* not ontological. Considered as an existentiell determination, natural consciousness is no less ontological, inasmuch as it is precisely a mode of the life of consciousness, a mode of existence in its universal ontological structure, inasmuch as, in general, the universal ontological structure of existence is immanent to every existentiell determination as constituting its very essence.

French:

> Faisons ici une distinction importante pour la suite de nos recherches, une distinction entre ce qui est ontologique et ce qui est existentiel. Le « prendre garde à l’être » du savoir vrai et le « ne pas prendre garde à l’être » de la conscience naturelle sont des modes de la vie de la conscience, des déterminations de son existence. Nous appellerons ces déterminations des *déterminations existentielles*. Le « prendre garde à l’être » inhérent à l’essence de la conscience et, comme tel, indifférent au « prendre garde à l’être » du savoir vrai comme au « ne pas prendre garde à l’être » de la conscience naturelle est au contraire, comme cette manifestation de soi originaire de l’être qui constitue la conscience elle-même dans son essence universelle, une *structure ontologique*. Le rapport entre la structure ontologique de la conscience et les déterminations existentielles que celle-ci est susceptible de revêtir dans sa vie est facile à comprendre, du moins pour ce qui en est dit ici : la structure ontologique de la conscience est à la fois indifférente et immanente aux déterminations existentielles de celle-ci. Indifférente, parce que cette structure ne prescrit pas que telle ou telle détermination existentielle de la conscience se produise et, par exemple, que la conscience, se retournant sur sa propre essence, se dirige sur elle pour la saisir dans le savoir vrai. Conformément à cette indifférence, la conscience peut exister sans que le savoir vrai se produise en elle. *Les déterminations existentielles sont inessentielles*, ce sont, par rapport à l’essence, des prédicats contingents et variables. Mais le caractère inessentiel de ces déterminations ne signifie pas qu’elles soient séparées de l’essence : bien au contraire, l’essence leur est immanente comme ce qui les rend possibles, comme leur essence même. C’est pourquoi le savoir naturel *est* ontologique. La distinction dans la conscience entre ses déterminations existentielles et sa structure ontologique met à nu l’ambiguïté d’une proposition comme celle par laquelle Heidegger s’efforce encore de caractériser le savoir naturel et de souligner sa contradiction : « la conscience naturelle est et n’est pas… ontologique ». La conscience naturelle est ontologique en tant qu’est présente en elle l’essence de la conscience dans sa structure universelle. Il est impropre de dire que la conscience naturelle n’*est* pas ontologique. Considérée comme une détermination existentielle, la conscience naturelle n’en est pas moins ontologique en tant qu’elle est justement un mode de la vie de la conscience, un mode de l’existence dans sa structure ontologique universelle – en tant que, en général, la structure ontologique universelle de l’existence est immanente à toute détermination existentielle comme constituant son essence même.

The distinction is made by turning a verb into a noun. "Taking heed of being" (*le « prendre garde à l'être »*) comes from Henry's quotation of *H* 136 at ¶4. Here it is set in quotation marks and given an article, so that it can be counted and sorted: twice as a "mode" of the life of consciousness, once as an "ontological structure". The new term is introduced with the formula "we shall call" (*nous appellerons*) and printed in italics, *existentiell determinations*. A second italic, *ontological structure*, gives it its opposite. Everything that follows in Sections II and III uses these two italics.

The verb Henry nominalizes was already divided in the German he quotes. At *H* 136 Heidegger writes that natural consciousness "does not attend to being" (*achtet … nicht auf das Sein*) "and must nonetheless heed it" (*muß es gleichwohl achten*). The first is *achten auf* with a preposition (attend to). The second is *achten* with a direct object (heed, respect). Henry's French at ¶4 gives one phrase twice, "does not take heed of being and must nonetheless take heed of it" (*il ne prend pas garde à l'être et doit pourtant y prendre garde*). It then discovers that "to take heed does not mean the same in the two cases". Heidegger's syntax had already marked the difference that Henry's distinction claims to uncover. Heidegger's next sentence states Henry's thesis outright: natural consciousness "cannot help co-representing the being of beings in general" (*kann nicht umhin, das Sein des Seienden im allgemeinen mit vorzustellen*), since without the light of being it could not even lose itself among beings.

The proposition Henry corrects in ¶6 has been cut to fit. He quotes *H* 163 as "natural consciousness is and is not… ontological". The German reads that natural consciousness "is ontological and yet is not yet so" (*ist … ontologisch und ist dies doch noch nicht*). Heidegger then names the state: "we say that ontic consciousness is pre-ontological" (*vorontologisch*). The ellipsis removes "yet … not yet" (*doch noch*), and Henry's framing word "contradiction" (*contradiction*) occurs nowhere in the essay. What Heidegger wrote is a developmental "not yet": natural consciousness is on its way, as the sentence about turning back on the same page says (see the previous reading). Henry's French makes it a flat "is and is not", which a distinction can then dissolve. The italic copula, "natural knowledge *is* ontological" and "it is improper to say that natural consciousness *is* not ontological", turns the question from one of time to one of being. Consciousness either is ontological or is not, and Henry answers that it is.

The real disagreement survives the cut, and it lies in the word "indifferent" (*indifférente*). For Heidegger the pre-ontological is "brought onto the way to its truth", and its "not yet" belongs to it as the impulse of the path. Henry's structure "does not prescribe" (*ne prescrit pas*) any determination, true knowledge included: "consciousness can exist without true knowledge occurring in it". The essence has no goal. That is what lets §19 deny the becoming-for-itself any ontological signification, and §21 deny the absolute a history. Where Heidegger had "pre-ontological", Henry writes "ontological" without the prefix. It is the same refusal of a "not yet" that the next reading finds in his "ontological understanding".

The inference is weakest at its last clause, which says that the essence is immanent to the determinations "as their very essence". The word "essence" here does two jobs, and Henry's note 16 concedes it. One may speak of the essence of an existentiell determination, perception for instance, as a typical structure. But that is not the essence of consciousness in general that grounds all determinations. "Natural knowledge is, if you like, an essence." The paragraph's "that is why" (*c'est pourquoi*) slides from the second sense to the first.

## §18 ¶8: "existentiell or, better said, existential"

Reads [§18, Paragraph 8](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-concept-of-representation-ontological-structure-and-existential-understanding).

> In the ontological understanding of being resides absolute knowledge. True or philosophical knowledge is an existentiell or, better said, existential understanding of the ontological understanding. But not every existentiell understanding of the ontological understanding is necessarily true or necessarily philosophical. If it does not confine itself to the understanding of an existentiell determination considered in its particularity, existence's implicit existentiell understanding of itself is an existentiell understanding of the ontological understanding, the implicit and most often improper sketch of an existential understanding. In truth, and precisely because an existentiell determination is a determination of existence, existence's implicit existentiell understanding of itself is always implicitly an existentiell understanding of the essence of existence in its universal ontological structure, that is, an existentiell understanding of the ontological understanding. Philosophical knowledge is only a thematization, carried out with the conceptual material required and worked out for this purpose, of this implicit existentiell self-understanding of existence, that is, of the ontological understanding itself.

French:

> Dans la compréhension ontologique de l’être réside le savoir absolu. Le savoir vrai ou philosophique est une compréhension existentielle ou, pour mieux dire, existentiale de la compréhension ontologique. Mais toute compréhension existentielle de la compréhension ontologique n’est pas forcément vraie ni forcément philosophique. Si elle ne se limite pas à la compréhension d’une détermination existentielle considérée dans sa particularité, la compréhension existentielle implicite de soi de l’existence est une compréhension existentielle de la compréhension ontologique, l’esquisse implicite et le plus souvent impropre d’une compréhension existentiale. A vrai dire, et précisément parce qu’une détermination existentielle est une détermination de l’existence, la compréhension existentielle implicite de soi de l’existence est toujours implicitement une compréhension existentielle de l’essence de l’existence dans sa structure ontologique universelle, c’est-à-dire une compréhension existentielle de la compréhension ontologique. Le savoir philosophique n’est qu’une thématisation, conduite avec le matériel conceptuel exigé et élaboré à cette fin, de cette compréhension existentielle implicite de soi de l’existence, c’est-à-dire de la compréhension ontologique elle-même.

Henry corrects himself in mid-sentence, and the correction places Heidegger. "Existentiell or, better said, existential" (*existentielle ou, pour mieux dire, existentiale*) uses the French pair for *existenziell* and *existenzial*. Henry knows the pair and uses it strictly. Earlier in the book he writes of the "existential analytic" (*analytique existentiale*) of *Sein und Zeit*, and of an "existential" as a structure that belongs to the ontological constitution of Dasein (src L277–287, L401). "Better said" (*pour mieux dire*) does not withdraw the first adjective. Philosophical knowledge is existentiell, a determinate mode of life of the kind ¶5 defined. In content it is existential, an understanding of the structures of existence. The paragraph then shows which of the two weighs more. "Existentiell" (*existentielle*) occurs ten times in it. "Existential" (*existentiale*) occurs twice: once in the correction, and once more as the goal of which implicit self-understanding is "the implicit and most often improper sketch". The closing sentence decides it: "philosophical knowledge is *only* a thematization" (*n'est qu'une thématisation*).

Laid over Heidegger's terms, the paragraph has three tiers where *Sein und Zeit* has two. In *Sein und Zeit* §4 the existential is already ontological. Its structures, the existentials, are the ontological characters of Dasein, and the existential analytic is the way into fundamental ontology. Henry places a tier above it, the "ontological understanding" (*compréhension ontologique*), which is the originary manifestation-of-self of being. He then files the existential below it, as the thematizing of that understanding, which is one existentiell mode among others. The analytic that *Sein und Zeit* offers as the access to ontology becomes, on Henry's arrangement, a mode of life with ontology as its object. The demotion repeats Henry's thesis of §§6–7 that the existential analytic presupposes the essence it was meant to reach. Here it is carried by a single adjective and its correction.

The top tier's name is also chosen. What *Sein und Zeit* calls the pre-ontological understanding of being Henry calls the "ontological understanding of being", without the prefix. The previous reading found the same move against the essay on Hegel, where Heidegger's "pre-ontological" (*vorontologisch*) consciousness became a consciousness that simply *is* ontological. In both places Henry declines to treat the understanding that every consciousness has as an early stage of a later, explicit ontology. "In it resides absolute knowledge": the paragraph's first sentence gives the unthematized understanding the title Hegel kept for the end of the path. The argument depends on this. If philosophy only thematizes an understanding that is already absolute, it can add no truth to it, and the paragraph's "not necessarily true" (*pas forcément vraie*) can only concern the thematization. The ¶9 that follows, with Marx, presses the consequence: understanding, of whatever kind, changes nothing in the reality it understands.

The main commentary reverses the pair. It renders *existentiale* as "existentiell" and then glosses "existential" as *existenziell*. With the French restored, the paragraph says the opposite of what the gloss attributes to it: the correction moves philosophy *toward* Heidegger's ontological term, and the next sentences hold it below the ontological understanding.

## §19 ¶3–4: *für es*, *für sich*, *für uns*, and the irreal

Reads [§19, Paragraphs 3 and 4](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#being-for-itself-from-the-ontological-point-of-view-and-from-the-existential-point-of-view-consciousness-and-truth).

> The unhappy consciousness of Judaism does not represent to itself the unity of essence and existence: this unity is not for it *(für es)*, it is on the contrary for us *(für uns)*, who understand existence in its truth, that is, as the essence. When natural consciousness has risen through the whole series of its experiences, through the experience of the unhappy consciousness, to the philosophical knowledge that is ours, the unity of essence and existence which constitutes the essence of this consciousness will become present to it itself, will no longer be only a unity for us. Consciousness will be brought before its truth. Its truth, namely the unity in it of essence and existence, will be a truth for it *(für sich)*. Yet, as we have just seen, the unity of essence and existence which is for the consciousness that comes to understand its essence *(für sich)*, as it is for us *(für uns)* who move within philosophical knowledge, is no less irreal than the separation of existence and essence which is for the unhappy consciousness, for natural consciousness in general *(für es)*. What is for consciousness, "for it", "for itself", "for us", what it represents to itself in understanding itself in a determinate mode of its life, is always, whatever this mode may be, something irreal. To the irreal separation of essence and existence which is for natural consciousness *(für es)* is opposed, not the unity, itself irreal, of essence and existence which is for us *(für uns)* or for the consciousness that understands itself in its essence *(für sich)*, but only *the real unity of existence and essence*. It matters little in the end whether existence understands or does not understand itself as being the essence: it is the essence, that is, reality. It is the manifestation of self of the essence of manifestation, *the originary being for-itself of being itself*.
>
> Although it seems to bring the problematic into the original dimension of being for-itself, the distinction instituted by Hegel between what is for consciousness and what is for us in fact remains radically foreign to it: *it does not concern the ontological structure of being-for-itself*. The *Phenomenology of Spirit*, which keeps to the description of existence as it is for the consciousness that represents it to itself, whether this consciousness be natural or philosophical, therefore has at no moment to do with reality. The examination of the various modalities within which existence understands itself proceeds in abstraction because, as Hegel himself says, "abstraction is precisely what is not authentically, what only *is for consciousness*". It is true that by "what only is for consciousness" Hegel means what holds for non-philosophical consciousness, which has not yet reached self-knowledge. But, as we have seen, what is for philosophical consciousness *(für sich, für uns)* is no less irreal: the unity of existence and essence that this consciousness represents to itself in understanding itself in its truth is still only an abstract unity. We see more precisely the fundamental ambiguity in the obligation laid on the in-itself to become for-itself when, as is constantly the case in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, the in-itself designates the reality of consciousness, that is, in fact, the originary ontological structure of being-for-itself. The becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness has, strictly speaking, no ontological signification. The essence is wholly contained in the being-in-itself of consciousness, where it has already accomplished itself. The becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness concerns only the manner in which this consciousness understands itself and has, as such, only an existentiell signification. Far from being able to contribute to the realization of spirit, true knowledge is in fact wholly foreign to it, because it stands on a quite different plane from that of reality. *The becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness is not the phenomenology of spirit*. *The phenomenology of spirit, the manifestation of pure manifestation, resides in the being-in-itself of consciousness, which is the originary being-for-itself of being and, as such, the manifestation of pure manifestation, the manifestation of spirit*. The "manifestation of spirit" in true knowledge, what Hegel improperly calls the phenomenology of spirit, is not the manifestation of spirit which constitutes its very essence, the essence of consciousness and of existence in general; it is not reality, but only a representation of it within a determinate act of consciousness. The expression "manifestation of spirit" is therefore fundamentally ambiguous: spirit is as such manifestation. The "manifestation of spirit" is therefore the essence itself; it is the originary and pure ontological element as it is in itself. The "manifestation of spirit" in true knowledge, on the contrary, does not belong to the internal structure of the essence; it is not something ontological. The "manifestation of spirit" in true knowledge is the strict correlate of a determinate representation of consciousness; it is only one "manifestation" among others, which, far from constituting it, presupposes on the contrary the universal and pure essence of manifestation in general. The becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness is a modality of the existentiell self-understanding of existence. But manifestation as such is not the doing of this understanding; it is the doing of the essence. The phenomenology of spirit is an ontological structure.

Henry needs the German because his French cannot keep Hegel's three standpoints apart. French "consciousness" is feminine, so "for it" (*pour elle*) renders Hegel's neuter "for it" (*für es*, consciousness as it experiences). A few lines later the same *pour elle* renders "for itself" (*für sich*): "its truth … will be a truth for it *(für sich)*". Only the parentheses tell the reader which Hegelian term is meant. Henry keeps the German nine times in one paragraph. He does so to register three differences that the main clause will annul, not to dwell on them. The annulling sentence gives the three as one list in quotation marks, "'for it', 'for itself', 'for us'", and assigns them a single predicate: "always … something irreal". The parentheses keep Hegel's distinctions, and the list in quotation marks collapses them.

"Irreal" (*irréel*) is the word that does the collapsing, and it has a technical sense. At §18 ¶1 Henry used it for the noematic correlate of an act (*corrélat noématique irréel*), the object as meant, which is not a real component of the experience. In that sense the paragraph before this one says plainly that irreal does not mean false: the separation is irreal "not because it is false, but because it occurs within representation", as a meaning aimed at by consciousness (src L931). Hegel's "for us" is thus classed as a noematic sense, which is a classification, not a refutation. The paragraphs then let the word slide into its common sense. The *Phenomenology* "has at no moment to do with reality". The unity grasped in philosophical knowledge is "only an abstract unity". The only thing set against all three standpoints is "*the real unity*". The verdict that Hegel's book never touches reality draws on the everyday force of "irreal". Its warrant covers only the technical one. Henry's note 19 promises to ground the distinction of real and irreal later (§31, and §§66–67).

The definition of the in-itself carries the decisive step, and once more it is introduced by "in fact" (*en fait*). The obligation laid on the in-itself to become for-itself is "fundamentally ambiguous" when the in-itself "designates the reality of consciousness, that is, in fact, the originary ontological structure of being-for-itself". With this "that is, in fact" (*c'est-à-dire en fait*), the in-itself of the *Phenomenology*, which is by definition what is *not yet* for itself, becomes for-itself already. From there it follows at once that becoming-for-itself adds nothing ontological. The conclusion is in the definition, as at §17 ¶2 the conclusion was in the "in fact" that made determination equal manifestation. The quotation offered as Hegel's own warrant needs a qualification, and Henry gives it. "Abstraction is precisely what is not authentically, what only *is for consciousness*" comes from the shape of the virtuous consciousness in conflict with the way of the world (note 22). Hegel says it of a non-philosophical standpoint, and Henry extends it to Hegel's "for us" by appeal to what "we have seen".

Typography finishes the argument. The italic title *Phénoménologie de l'Esprit* (with capitals) is set against the roman "phénoménologie de l'esprit" (without them). The italic sentence "*the becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness is not the phenomenology of spirit*" means that the book is not what its title names. The second italic sentence gives the title to the ontological structure, and the paragraph's last words restate it: "the phenomenology of spirit is an ontological structure". The chain of hyphens, "becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself" (*le devenir-pour-soi de l'être-en-soi*), returns four times. It names the dialectic as a single compound noun, a thing that can be classed as "a modality of existentiell self-understanding" and no longer a movement. Henry's argument against the path of the *Phenomenology* is partly carried out in its grammar: verbs of becoming become nouns, and a predicate is attached to the noun.

The passage applies the distinction of §18 ¶5 (see the reading above) to Hegel's own vocabulary. It prepares §20, where what happens "behind the back" of consciousness is shown to be "for us" and hence irreal, and §21, where the absolute is denied a history. The "originary being for-itself of being itself" (*l'être pour-soi originaire de l'être lui-même*) is the first form of the phrase that §§30–31 will fill with content as auto-affection.

## §21 ¶4, H 140: "not the modesty of an obscure *Grund*, … the Word"

Reads [§21, Paragraph 4](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-reaffirmation-of-the-originary-character-of-the-manifestation-of-being-in-the-bringing-into-light-of-its-non-historical-character).

> It is improperly, on the other hand, that one speaks of an equality between "consciousness" and its essence. With the essence that constitutes the ontological structure of being-for-itself and of absolute truth, the "consciousness" that designates the representation of this essence cannot coincide, even in philosophical knowledge, for, as we have seen, *the difference between reality and its representation cannot be suppressed*. Self-consciousness, which Hegel says, against Fichte, arises only after a preliminary process, therefore has strictly speaking no ontological signification; it could neither designate the structure of the essence nor belong to it. The absolute, if one is willing to understand by it the originary being-for-itself of the essence which makes it reality, is not produced in history. It is therefore, once again, the mere representation of the essence that one may refuse to "that enthusiasm which, like a pistol shot, begins immediately with absolute knowledge and disposes of different positions by declaring that it wants to know nothing of them". The absolute that this enthusiasm posits is, in effect, only a position; another position is then opposed to it, notably the one this enthusiasm will reach only after changing itself into its contrary, and that on the path of despair which is the path of consciousness. Absolute knowledge, however, is the doing of this enthusiasm, and that as what allows it to *be*, as that first act of appearing of the originary and pure essence of manifestation which has already accomplished its work for it, at least so as to allow it to manifest itself. History is therefore not the "movement by which appearing comes about", for appearing has already come near us, the self-appearing of appearing has already been produced, and that as the originary upsurge of an effective dimension of phenomenality in which this history can be accomplished and first begin. Transcendental knowledge is not to be acquired; it is a priori. What is at the beginning is not the modesty of an obscure *Grund*; it is the Word. The reality of the absolute is presupposed as the condition of all possible reality in general, as the condition of a history. But the reality of the absolute is its absoluity, its originary presence to itself in the Parousia. The presence of the absolute to itself, the gathering of its essence in its unity with itself, such is the strong nature of the origin itself. *The Parousia occurs at the origin because it constitutes the origin's essence*. Because it constitutes the essence of the origin, the Parousia results from no progress; it is its presupposition. In the Parousia resides the reality of the absolute. Because it finds its reality in the Parousia, the absolute finds it in the origin. The destiny of the absolute is not played out in history.

The passage answers a progressive verb with a perfect. Henry quotes *H* 140 as "the movement by which appearing comes about" (*mouvement par lequel advient l'apparaître*). Heidegger's German is "that movement of the coming of appearing" (*jene Bewegung des Kommens des Erscheinens*), and it "is the happening" that brings consciousness, shape by shape, into the image of its essence. Henry turns the noun "coming" (*Kommen*) into the verb "comes about" (*advient*) and denies it with three perfects: appearing "has already come near us" (*est d'ores et déjà venu près de nous*), the self-appearing of appearing "has already been produced" (*s'est d'ores et déjà produit*), and the essence "has already accomplished its work" (*a d'ores et déjà accompli son œuvre*). The formula "already" (*d'ores et déjà*) occurs three times in two sentences. "Come near us" answers Heidegger with the Hegel sentence his essay keeps returning to: the absolute "is already, in and for itself, with us" (*an und für sich schon bei uns*), quoted in the essay at its opening and twice more (GA 5 file, lines 7, 61, 129). The essay asks how a way to the absolute is possible if the absolute is already with us. Henry's answer is that it is not, so there is no way.

"Not to be acquired" answers the sentence that follows Henry's quotation at *H* 140. There Heidegger calls the history of consciousness, with Hegel, "the history of the *formation* of consciousness itself into science" (*Bildung*). "Transcendental knowledge is not to be acquired; it is a priori" (*n'est pas à acquérir, il est à priori*) sets a gerund of acquisition, the formation that the *Phenomenology* narrates, against the Kantian term for what is had before all acquisition. Henry recasts Hegel's education of consciousness as the acquiring of something that was never missing.

The pistol shot is turned, not vindicated. Hegel's image of an enthusiasm that "begins immediately with absolute knowledge" is directed against Schelling. Henry first agrees with Hegel: the absolute such an enthusiasm posits "is only a position", open to the counter-position it will reach "on the path of despair which is the path of consciousness". Then "however" (*pourtant*) reverses the scene. Absolute knowledge "is the doing of this enthusiasm", but only "as what allows it to *be*". The italic copula is the point. What the enthusiasm asserts is a representation, refused like every other. What the enthusiasm *is*, an act of appearing, is already absolute knowledge, as natural consciousness was at §17. The enthusiast has no privilege over the Hegelian, and the passage takes neither side in the quarrel between Hegel and Schelling. It moves the absolute from the content of either position to the appearing of both.

The image of the beginning is a pair of nouns from two languages. On one side is "an obscure *Grund*" (*un* Grund *obscur*), the German noun in italics, qualified by "modesty" (*modestie*). On the other is "the Word" (*le Verbe*), the French noun with a capital and the article of the Johannine prologue in French Bibles (*au commencement était le Verbe*). The text gives no note for the *Grund*, and the corpus holds no Schelling to settle whose ground it is. The main commentary's gloss, Schelling's *Ungrund* (groundlessness), names a word Henry does not write. What the wording itself shows is a contrast of strength. The obscure ground is "modest", a poor beginning that needs development, like Hegel's absolute that is "essentially result". The Word belongs to "the strong nature of the origin" (*la forte nature de l'origine*) two sentences later: complete, self-present, gathered "in its unity with itself". A beginning in the dark would make manifestation the outcome of a history. A beginning in the Word makes it the presupposition of one.

The inference the passage rests on is stated in its first sentence. "The difference between reality and its representation cannot be suppressed" (italic), therefore self-consciousness, which arises after a process, "has strictly speaking no ontological signification". The phrase "against Fichte" (*contre Fichte*) takes a side: Hegel's self-consciousness, reached at the end of a process, is set against Fichte's, posited at the start. Henry's rejection of the first is no rejection of the second, and §38 turns to Fichte as the thinker of the internal structure of immanence. The step is weaker than it looks. The premise shows that a *represented* self-consciousness cannot be the essence. It does not show that self-consciousness cannot be anything but represented. That is the question §§30–31 answer, with an auto-affection that is a self-relation without representation, and it is why this passage can only refuse here and assert later. The closing sentence, "the destiny of the absolute is not played out in history", sums up the sub-division and prepares §21 ¶7: "philosophy always comes too late, for what it says was at the beginning."

## §22 ¶3, K 98: the seemingly non-finite, cut

Reads [§22, Paragraph 3](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-interpretation-of-the-essence-of-phenomenality-within-the-fundamental-presuppositions-of-monism-and-the-problem-of-receptivity-ontological-signification-of-this-problem).

> The ontological character of receptivity could be masked inasmuch as receptivity is first thought in the light of the problem of the reception of the existent, and is for that reason bound to the concept of finitude, at least as that concept first presents itself. Since the reception of the existent is grounded on the essence, it presupposes, prior to itself, and so prior to the receptive act itself, a knowledge of another order: an ontological knowledge which, in opposition to this receiving knowledge, is first interpreted as a knowledge that is no longer receiving but, on the contrary, creative. "If finite knowledge of the existent is possible," says Heidegger, "it will have to be grounded on a knowing of the being of the existent *prior to every receptive act*. Finite knowledge of the existent therefore requires, in order to be possible, *a non-receptive knowing*, … a sort of 'creative' intuition." In what, however, does the ontological knowledge consist that makes the reception of the existent possible? If such knowledge, no longer concerning the existent, cannot be receiving *with regard to it*, is it not receiving, and essentially so, with regard to being itself, inasmuch as it is determined as the process by which the essence objectifies itself in the form of a horizon that manifests itself to the essence only precisely inasmuch as the essence receives it? To show that the problem of the reception of the existent refers back to a problem of the ontological order is not to solve the problem of receptivity, but only to pose it on the plane that is originally its own, and all the more urgently since in the essence of ontological receptivity reside the foundation and, finally, the essence of ontic receptivity itself. That receptivity has an essentially ontological character does not mean that it is foreign to the reception of the existent, since, quite the contrary, it is only as object that the existent can be received. Objectivation must be possible for the reception of the existent to be accomplished. But the possibility of objectivation resides in the ontological essence of receptivity. Far from suppressing the problem of receptivity, the reference of ontic knowledge to ontological knowledge makes it arise in all its acuteness.

He cuts the parenthesis and keeps the quotation marks, so the quotation still hesitates, but in a weaker way. Henry's frame then puts the hesitation in time and not in appearance: ontological knowledge "is *first* interpreted" (*est d'abord interprétée*) as creative. "First" is accurate as a description of the step Heidegger takes, and Henry's next question takes it beyond that step. The German goes on to sharpen the result into exactly that question: how can a finite being, "delivered over to beings and dependent on taking them in" (*an das Seiende ausgeliefert und auf die Hinnahme desselben angewiesen*), know beings before all taking-in "without being their 'creator'"? Henry's quotation stops where Heidegger's question begins. He then asks it himself, in his own words: "is it not receiving … with regard to being itself?"

Henry's own emphasis marks where his question gets its leverage. His note 39 says the italics are his ("souligné par nous"). They fall on "*prior to every receptive act*" and "*a non-receptive knowing*". In both places the French says "receptive", and the German has two different words. "Prior to every receptive act" renders "lying before all receiving" (*vor allem Empfangen liegendes*). "Non-receptive" renders "not-taking-in" (*nicht-hinnehmendes*). Heidegger's *Hinnahme* (taking-in) is the technical term of the Kant book for the finite intuition's way of having what it intuits. *Empfangen* (receiving) is the ordinary verb. The translation turns both into the adjective of Kant's *Rezeptivität*. Henry's emphasis falls on that one word, and his thesis needs it to be one word: a single receptivity that reaches from the ontic reception of beings to the ontological reception of being, so that "in the essence of ontological receptivity reside the foundation and … the essence of ontic receptivity itself". A reader of the German would have to argue that *Empfangen* and *Hinnahme* name one thing. A reader of the French finds them already the same word, and Henry's italics lean on that.

The French also supplies a future tense again: "it will have to be grounded" (*elle devra se fonder*) renders "then it must ground itself" (*dann muß sie sich … gründen*). Here, unlike at §17 ¶2, Henry builds nothing on it. The weight of the paragraph is on its last three sentences, which follow one another with no "therefore": the existent can be received only as object; objectivation must be possible for that reception; the possibility of objectivation lies in the ontological essence of receptivity. The third sentence is the thesis of §22 restated, and it does not follow from the first two. They show that ontic reception requires objectivation. They do not show that objectivation is itself a reception. Henry's "but" (*mais*) is where he asserts it. §22 ¶2 had prepared the assertion by definition: "to be received means … to manifest oneself", so that "the receptivity of the horizon is identically its manifestation". The present paragraph rests on that equation. Heidegger's answer to the same question, pure intuition as a taking-in that gives itself what it takes in (§9, 44; §28, 141), keeps forming and taking-in in one act. Henry takes it up at §23, in the next reading but one, and splits it.

## §22 ¶4–5, K 92: *Ent-stand*, *Gegen-stand*, and Henry's hyphens

Reads [§22, Paragraphs 4 and 5](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-interpretation-of-the-essence-of-phenomenality-within-the-fundamental-presuppositions-of-monism-and-the-problem-of-receptivity-ontological-signification-of-this-problem).

> The understanding of the ontological character of receptivity lifts the ambiguity weighing on the opposition, instituted by traditional philosophical thought, between a "creative" knowledge on the one hand and a "receiving" knowledge on the other. Such an opposition holds, in fact, only relative to the existent. Knowledge will be called creative or receiving, in its relation to the existent, according to whether the existent springs from the very act by which knowledge intuits it, or whether, on the contrary, knowledge is constrained to find it as an existent that it does not create but that exists independently of it. *But ontological knowledge, which forms the horizon of being at the same time as it receives it, is creative and receiving at once, and that in the indissoluble unity of its very essence*. Thus appears the inessential character of the Kantian distinction between the *Entstand* and the *Gegenstand*. In truth this distinction is not only inessential; it proves fundamentally obscure. In opposing the creative intuition, of which we have at least the idea, to an intuition constrained to receive a foreign existent, Kant claims to hold the foundation of the metaphysical opposition between a finite and an infinite knowledge. But the true problem is not whether the existent proceeds from the power of knowledge itself or not; it is to understand how this existent, whether knowledge creates it or not, can nonetheless manifest itself to it. Even if the existent were posited in existence by the very act of the power that knows it, it is necessary, if this power is precisely that of a knowledge, that the existent manifest itself to it. And how could it do so, if not through the mediation of the originary and pure essence of manifestation? But *finitude is*, as has been shown, *an ontological category; it finds its foundation in the essence inasmuch as what arises in the work of the essence is the finite horizon of being*. That the existent is created changes nothing in the finitude of its manifestation, in the essential finitude that concerns it in its very being. It is no doubt as object that the existent is finite. But the existent is not an ob-ject because it exists prior to a knowledge that does not create it. Intuition is not finite because it receives an existent that does not emanate from it. It is finite in itself, as intuition. It is the ontological power constrained to receive the finite place (which it itself forms) of being that is finite, and that independently of any consideration of the nature of the existent that arises in this place, whether this existent is produced by the ontological power of manifestation or not.
>
> That the existent is finite as object means: it is finite inasmuch as it is intuited. For intuition finds its foundation in the process by which the essence sets over against itself the pure field of phenomenality in the form of a finite horizon. *It is clear, then, that the antithetical concept of an originary infinite intuition arises in the problematic without any context*. That such an intuition is called originary and infinite because it is creative of the existent changes nothing in the finite nature of intuition in it. *Or else, with this idea of an infinite intuition, it is another mode of manifestation that is thought, another manner of appearing and of giving itself that seeks to assert its concept before philosophical reflection*. But by what right is this other mode of revelation still called an intuition? On what title can philosophy admit it, in the absence of any genuine and explicit problematic directed upon it? Such a mode of revelation, which constitutes a pure formal ontological element, could not in any case be defined by its relation to an ontic content, by the fact that this content is supposed to emanate from it. It is the internal ontological structure of the essence of manifestation, considered in itself and understood in its purity, that is in question. So long as this essence is interpreted as that of transcendence, the effective, pure phenomenality it promotes must, as such, be finite. The existent that manifests itself through the mediation of this essence is finite too, and that whether or not it is created by the power of knowledge, that is, by this essence itself. It is in every case an existent that manifests itself within the finite horizon of being. *The "Entstand" too is an ob-ject, at least so long as it is intuited*. Only a confusion allows one to oppose it, from this ontological point of view, to the existent that intuition does not create but simply finds before it. Heidegger commits this confusion when he writes: "to finite knowledge alone can a reality of the type object *(Gegenstand)* be given. It alone must expose itself to the existent that already is." The character in virtue of which the existent is prior to the exercise of a power of knowledge that does not create it is an ontic character. The character in virtue of which the existent is the ob-ject is an ontological character that determines a priori the structure of the being of every possible existent as intuited existent. The confusion of these two characters leads to giving an ultimate sense to the opposition between a "creative" and a "receiving" intuition. But this opposition loses every ontological signification, and perhaps every possible kind of signification in general, for the thought that thinks the object. To such a thought it is given to understand that the ob-ject is not before *(ob, gegen)* because the existent it represents is found and not created by the power of knowledge, but because it manifests itself in an intuition, and that this is also why it is finite.

French:

> La compréhension du caractère ontologique de la réceptivité lève l’ambiguïté qui pèse sur l’opposition instituée par la pensée philosophique traditionnelle entre une connaissance « créatrice », d’une part, et, d’autre part, une connaissance « réceptrice ». Une telle opposition ne vaut, en fait, que relativement à l’étant. La connaissance sera dite créatrice ou réceptrice, dans sa relation à l’étant, selon que celui-ci jaillit de l’acte même par lequel elle l’intuitionne ou que, au contraire, elle est astreinte à le trouver comme un étant qu’elle ne crée pas mais qui existe indépendamment d’elle. *Mais la connaissance ontologique qui forme l’horizon de l’être en même temps qu’elle le reçoit est créatrice et réceptrice à la fois, et cela dans l’unité indissoluble de son essence même*. Ainsi apparaît le caractère inessentiel de la distinction kantienne de l’*Entstand* et du *Gegenstand*. A vrai dire, cette distinction n’est pas seulement inessentielle, elle se révèle foncièrement obscure. En opposant l’intuition créatrice dont nous avons du moins l’idée et, d’autre part, une intuition astreinte à recevoir un étant étranger, Kant prétend tenir le fondement de l’opposition métaphysique entre une connaissance finie et une connaissance infinie. Mais le vrai problème n’est pas de savoir si l’étant procède ou non du pouvoir de connaissance lui-même, il est de comprendre comment cet étant, que la connaissance crée ou qu’elle ne crée pas, peut cependant se manifester à elle. Quand bien même l’étant serait posé dans l’existence par l’acte même du pouvoir qui le connaît, il faut, si justement ce pouvoir est celui d’une connaissance, que l’étant se manifeste à lui. Et comment pourrait-il le faire, sinon par la médiation de l’essence originaire et pure de la manifestation ? Mais *la finitude est*, comme il a été montré, *une catégorie ontologique, elle trouve son fondement dans l’essence en tant que ce qui surgit dans l’œuvre de celle-ci est l’horizon fini de l’être*. Que l’étant soit créé ne change rien à la finitude de sa manifestation, à la finitude essentielle qui le concerne dans son être même. C’est sans doute en tant qu’objet que l’étant est fini. Mais l’étant n’est pas un ob-jet parce qu’il existe préalablement à une connaissance qui ne le crée pas. L’intuition n’est pas finie parce qu’elle reçoit un étant qui n’émane pas d’elle. Elle est finie en elle-même, en tant qu’intuition. C’est le pouvoir ontologique astreint à recevoir le lieu fini (qu’il forme lui-même) de l’être qui est fini, et cela abstraction faite de toute considération concernant la nature de l’étant qui surgit dans ce lieu, que cet étant soit produit ou non par le pouvoir ontologique de la manifestation.
>
> Que l’étant soit fini en tant qu’objet veut dire : il est fini en tant qu’il est intuitionné. Car l’intuition trouve son fondement dans le processus par lequel l’essence s’objecte le champ pur de la phénoménalité sous la forme d’un horizon fini. *Il est clair alors que le concept antithétique d’une intuition originaire infinie surgit dans la problématique sans aucun contexte*. Qu’une telle intuition soit dite originaire et infinie parce qu’elle est créatrice de l’étant, ne change rien à la nature finie de l’intuition en elle. *Ou alors, avec cette idée d’une intuition infinie, c’est un autre mode de manifestation qui est pensé, c’est une autre manière d’apparaître et de se donner qui veut faire valoir son concept devant la réflexion philosophique*. Mais de quel droit cet autre mode de révélation est-il encore appelé une intuition ? A quel titre la philosophie peut-elle l’accueillir en l’absence de toute problématique véritable et explicite dirigée sur lui ? Un tel mode de révélation qui constitue un élément ontologique formel pur ne saurait, en tout cas, être défini par sa relation à un contenu ontique, par le fait que celui-ci est censé émaner de lui. C’est de la structure ontologique interne de l’essence de la manifestation considérée en elle-même et comprise dans sa pureté qu’il s’agit. Aussi longtemps que cette essence est interprétée comme celle de la transcendance, la phénoménalité effective, pure, qu’elle promeut, doit, en tant que telle, être finie. L’étant qui se manifeste par la médiation de cette essence est fini, lui aussi, et cela qu’il soit créé ou non par le pouvoir de la connaissance, c’est-à-dire par cette essence même. C’est dans tous les cas un étant qui se manifeste à l’intérieur de l’horizon fini de l’être. *L’« Entstand » est lui aussi un ob-jet, aussi longtemps du moins qu’il est intuitionné*. Seule une confusion permet de l’opposer, de ce point de vue ontologique, à l’étant que l’intuition ne crée pas mais trouve simplement devant elle. Cette confusion, Heidegger la commet lorsqu’il écrit : « à la connaissance finie seule peut être donnée une réalité du type objet *(Gegenstand).* Elle seule doit s’exposer à l’étant qui est déjà ». Le caractère en vertu duquel l’étant est antérieur à l’exercice d’un pouvoir de la connaissance qui ne le crée pas est un caractère ontique. Le caractère en vertu duquel l’étant est l’ob-jet est un caractère ontologique qui détermine à priori la structure de l’être de tout étant possible en tant qu’étant intuitionné. La confusion de ces deux caractères amène à donner un sens ultime à l’opposition d’une intuition « créatrice » et d’une intuition « réceptrice ». Mais cette opposition perd toute signification ontologique, et peut-être toute espèce de signification possible en général, pour la pensée qui pense l’objet. A une telle pensée il est donné de comprendre que l’ob-jet n’est pas devant *(ob, gegen)* parce que l’étant qu’il représente est trouvé et non créé par le pouvoir de la connaissance, mais parce qu’il se manifeste dans une intuition – et que c’est pour cela aussi qu’il est fini.

Henry removes the hyphen from Heidegger's word and adds one to his own. In GA 3 §5, 31 the coinage is hyphenated: absolute knowing has beings "as *Ent-stand*" (*als Ent-stand*), as what springs forth in being let spring forth (*im Entstehenlassen*). The hyphen makes the word a counterpart to *Gegen-stand* (object, what stands against): two prefixes on one "standing". The French keeps the German in brackets, "as creation [*Ent-stand*]" (*en tant que création [Ént-stand]*, K 92, Biemel file L999). Henry writes *Entstand* in one piece throughout §22, in italics or in quotation marks, and calls the pair "the Kantian distinction of the *Entstand* and the *Gegenstand*". Unhyphenated and credited to Kant, the pair reads as two received terms of a doctrine, not as a morphological device by which Heidegger reads Kant's original intuition (*intuitus originarius*). The opposite happens to the object. The 1953 French writes "a reality of the type ob-ject" (*du type ob-jet*), hyphenated, after Heidegger's *Gegenstand*. When Henry quotes the sentence he prints it without the hyphen, "du type objet *(Gegenstand)*". In his own sentences he writes "ob-ject" four times. He takes the hyphen from Heidegger's quoted sentence and uses it for his argument.

That argument is a claim about a prefix. "The ob-ject is not before *(ob, gegen)* because the existent it represents is found and not created …, but because it manifests itself in an intuition". Henry glosses the Latin *ob* and the German *gegen* together by "before" (*devant*). In GA 3 the *gegen* is a relation of resistance to a finite knower: only finite knowing "is delivered over to what already is" (*an das schon Seiende ausgeliefert*), and only for it is there "anything like an object". The French, "it alone must expose itself to the existent that already is" (*elle seule doit s'exposer*), had already turned that condition into an obligation, as the main commentary notes. Henry's "before" makes the prefix a matter of visibility. The object stands before because it is seen, and anything seen stands before, whether it was created or found. With that one gloss, *against* becomes *in front of*. The argument then follows by necessity: "the 'Entstand' too is an ob-ject".

The italic thesis carries a condition that GA 3 refuses: "*at least so long as it is intuited*" (*aussi longtemps du moins qu'il est intuitionné*). Heidegger says on the same page that infinite knowing is "strictly speaking not even reached" by the statement that its intuiting first produces "the object" (K 92 / GA 3 §5, 31). For Heidegger, the Ent-stand is precisely not intuited in Henry's sense of being set before. Henry's "at least" admits that his conclusion holds only on his premise, which is the premise of Section I: every intuiting sets what it intuits before itself. So the charge that Heidegger "commits this confusion" does not touch GA 3. What does touch it is the second italic sentence, which GA 3 has no answer to: "*it is another mode of manifestation that is thought*", and "by what right is this other mode of revelation still called an intuition?" Heidegger keeps the word "intuiting" (*Anschauen*) for infinite knowing. Henry asks why a revelation that sets nothing before itself should be called by the name of the act that does. The question survives the misreading, and §30 answers it with immanence.

The paragraph also changes Henry's vocabulary. "Mode of revelation" (*mode de révélation*) appears here for the first time in Section II, in place of "manifestation". It is used for what an infinite intuition would have to be if it were not an intuition. The word that will name the original essence at §29 and §30 first enters the book as the name of something Heidegger could not have called intuition.

## §23, K 199: "*in itself* a forming act", and the italic *also*

Reads [§23](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-internal-possibility-of-the-receptivity-of-being-and-the-problem-of-schematism), third block.

> It is again a purely negative, and therefore illusory, solution that should be set aside by noting that one cannot give as the foundation of imagination's receptivity toward the horizon the fact that the horizon is formed by imagination itself. This solution seems to offer itself when, for instance, Heidegger says, admittedly with regard to the pure intuitions but precisely in order to show that their essence lies in imagination, that they receive the pure view of space and time, but that "this reception is *in itself* a forming act that gives itself what offers itself". That the pure ontological content that the essence sets over against itself is formed by the essence in no way explains how such a content is *also* received by it. Quite the contrary: such a formation of the pure horizon of being by the ontological power of knowledge no doubt has a sense, a phenomenological sense, only inasmuch as what it forms manifests itself, that is, is received by it. Thus the capacity of imagination to produce the pure content that it objectifies for itself in the form of a horizon, its very possibility of objectifying *to itself* such a content, finds its foundation in its aptitude to receive it. *The essence of spontaneity resides in receptivity*. It is no small thing, assuredly, to show that pure intuition, as forming, is imagination. That pure intuition is imagination does not, however, solve the problem of the ultimate possibility of the essence of objectivation, inasmuch as objectivation always receives what it forms, and receives it as the very condition of this formation.

French:

> C’est encore une solution purement négative, et partant illusoire, qu’il convient d’écarter en remarquant qu’on ne peut donner, comme fondement de la réceptivité de l’imagination à l’égard de l’horizon, le fait que celui-ci est formé par elle-même. Cette solution semble se proposer lorsque, par exemple, Heidegger dit, à propos sans doute des intuitions pures, mais justement pour montrer que leur essence réside dans l’imagination, qu’elles reçoivent la vue pure de l’espace et du temps mais que « cette réception est *en elle-même* un acte formateur qui se donne à lui-même ce qui s’offre ». Que le contenu ontologique pur que s’objecte l’essence soit formé par celle-ci n’explique en aucune façon comment un tel contenu est *aussi* reçu par elle. Bien au contraire, une telle formation de l’horizon pur de l’être par le pouvoir ontologique de la connaissance n’a sans doute un sens, un sens phénoménologique, que pour autant que ce qu’elle forme se manifeste, c’est-à-dire est reçu par elle. Ainsi la capacité qu’a l’imagination de produire le contenu pur qu’elle s’objective sous la forme d’un horizon, la possibilité pour elle justement de *s*’objecter un tel contenu, trouvent-elles leur fondement dans l’aptitude qu’elles ont de le recevoir. *L’essence de la spontanéité réside dans la réceptivité*. Ce n’est pas rien, assurément, de montrer que l’intuition pure est, en tant que formatrice, imagination. Que l’intuition pure soit imagination ne résout pas cependant le problème de la possibilité la plus ultime de l’essence de l’objectivation en tant que toujours celle-ci reçoit ce qu’elle forme, et cela comme la condition même de cette formation.

The German sentence Henry quotes asserts an identity, and the French had already loosened it. GA 3 §28, 141 says of the pure intuitions: "They take in the look, but this taking-in is in itself precisely the forming self-giving of what gives itself" (*aber dieses Hinnehmen ist in sich gerade das bildende Sichselbstgeben des sich Gebenden*). The 1953 French (K 199) reads "this reception is in itself a forming act that gives itself what offers itself" (*cette réception est en elle-même un acte formateur qui se donne à lui-même ce qui s'offre*). Three changes happen there. "Precisely" (*gerade*), the word that makes the taking-in and the self-giving one, is gone. The nominalized infinitive "self-giving" (*Sichselbstgeben*) becomes an "act" with a relative clause, a subject that does something. And the genitive "of what gives itself" (*des sich Gebenden*), in which giver and given are the same, becomes a direct object, "what offers itself" (*ce qui s'offre*), standing over against the act that gives it. The German has one event. The French has an act and its object. Henry takes the French as it stands and puts his question into the gap it leaves.

His italics, which note 43 marks as his own, place that question in the quotation: "*in itself*" (*en elle-même*). Heidegger's "in itself" (*in sich*) claimed that the taking-in is, as such, the forming. Henry's emphasis puts that claim on trial. The next sentence names the duality he finds behind it with a second italic: the content is formed by the essence, but how is it "*also* received" (*aussi reçu*) by it? "Also" is the split itself. Forming and receiving become two operations joined by a conjunction, and "precisely" (*gerade*) said there was no conjunction to make. A third italic falls on a single letter: "its very possibility of *s*'objecter" (objectifying *to itself*). The reflexive pronoun is the place where Henry locates the receiving. The essence sets the content before *itself*, and to be "for itself" in that way is already to be receptive.

Henry's edition had presented the sentence as a doctrine, and his thesis turns that doctrine around. The translators' introduction quotes the same sentence (K 33, Biemel file L255) as the key to pure intuition: "their receptivity is creative or, to use Heidegger's terms, 'this reception is in itself a forming act …'". "Receptivity is creative" is the formula Henry has been taking apart since §22. His italic thesis, "*the essence of spontaneity resides in receptivity*", inverts it: where the introduction made receiving a mode of creating, Henry makes creating rest on receiving.

The inference turns on a "that is" (*c'est-à-dire*). Formation has a phenomenological sense "only inasmuch as what it forms manifests itself, *that is*, is received by it". The equation of manifesting with being received is the definition laid down at §22 ¶1, and it does all the ranking. If to manifest is to be received, then whatever has phenomenological sense has it through receiving, and forming comes second by definition. Heidegger's "precisely" asserts an identity, and Henry's "that is" asserts a priority. Neither explains how a forming can be a taking-in, and Henry grants as much two pages later, at §24, when he calls intuition "only a name". The concessive clauses show he knows the context. "Admittedly with regard to the pure intuitions, but precisely in order to show that their essence lies in imagination" (*à propos sans doute …, mais justement …*) concedes that Heidegger's sentence answers a different question. The "seems" of "this solution *seems* to offer itself" (*semble se proposer*) concedes that Heidegger does not quite offer it. The split made here is repeated in §24, where self-affection divides into affection by itself and affection of itself, and in §31, where receptivity divides in two: "to receive in the first sense is to create the content one receives; … in the second sense, not to create the content but to *be* it."

## §23, K 157 and 159–160: the rule cut out of the schema-image

Reads [§23](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-internal-possibility-of-the-receptivity-of-being-and-the-problem-of-schematism), eleventh block.

> The question of schematism in fact covers two distinct problems. The first is that of the possibility of the union of thought and intuition, and its solution consists in bringing to light their common essence. But the true problem of schematism is not that of grounding the union of thought and intuition; it arises in fact once this first problem has been solved. It is then a matter of understanding the internal possibility of this common essence of thought and intuition, that is, the possibility of transcendence itself. This possibility resides in the originary manifestation of the pure horizon of being. It alone, in fact, allows transcendence to hold near itself and to keep what it has itself formed. In the act of keeping what it has itself formed resides the internal possibility of transcendence, the possibility of its effective accomplishment. *It is of schematism that it is asked, without equivocation, to ground this ultimate possibility*: "The accomplishment of transcendence will have to be at bottom schematism." And again: "Kant therefore necessarily comes upon transcendental schematism as soon as he seeks to bring to light the ground of the intrinsic possibility of transcendence." The accomplishment of transcendence in the manifestation of the horizon alone makes "originary" and "transcendental" truth effective. "This truth," says Heidegger again, "is explicated in its essence by transcendental schematism." Thus schematism finally counts as a solution to the problem of the manifestation of being, inasmuch as it claims to ground transcendence itself in its intrinsic possibility, that is, again, to explicate transcendental truth in its essence. How, though, does schematism secure the manifestation of the pure horizon of being? *The manifestation of the pure horizon of being by schematism consists in a sensibilization of the horizon. The horizon manifests itself inasmuch as it is brought into relation with sensibility, that is, inasmuch as it is intuited*. Thus the perceptibility of the horizon credited to schematism in fact finds its foundation in intuition itself. It is intuition that allows schematism to accomplish its work. The "pure view", the "image-schema" that it procures, is an open place in the milieu of transcendence. But this place is visible, is truly an "image", only by reason of its intuitive character. This character of the image that makes it visible "comes from the fact that the image-schema springs … from a possible presentation", and this presentation consists in the fact that the pure place it presents can be brought "into the sphere of a possible intuition". *It is therefore the possibility for what the image presents, that is, for the transcendental horizon of being, to be brought into the sphere of a possible intuition that makes this horizon an "image" and so grounds its phenomenological character*. Inasmuch as schematism consists in the *formation of an image*, it finds its possibility in intuition.

French:

> La question du schématisme couvre en fait deux problèmes distincts : le premier est celui de la possibilité de l’union de la pensée et de l’intuition, et sa solution consiste dans la mise à jour de leur essence commune. Mais le vrai problème du schématisme n’est pas celui de fonder l’union de la pensée et de l’intuition, il se pose en fait lorsque ce premier problème a été résolu. Il s’agit alors, en effet, de comprendre la possibilité interne de cette essence commune de la pensée et de l’intuition, c’est-à-dire la possibilité de la transcendance elle-même. Cette possibilité réside dans la manifestation originaire de l’horizon pur de l’être. Celle-ci permet seule, en effet, à la transcendance de maintenir près de soi et de conserver ce qu’elle a elle-même formé. Dans l’acte de conserver ce qu’elle a elle-même formé, réside la possibilité interne de la transcendance, la possibilité de son accomplissement effectif. *C’est au schématisme qu’il est demandé sans équivoque de fonder cette possibilité ultime* : « L’accomplissement de la transcendance devra être foncièrement schématisme. » Et encore : « Kant touche donc nécessairement au schématisme transcendantal dès qu’il veut mettre au jour le fondement de la possibilité intrinsèque de la transcendance. » L’accomplissement de la transcendance dans la manifestation de l’horizon rend seul effective la vérité « originelle » et « transcendantale ». « Cette vérité, dit encore Heidegger, est explicitée en son essence par le schématisme transcendantal. » Ainsi le schématisme vaut-il finalement comme une solution au problème de la manifestation de l’être, et cela en tant qu’il prétend fonder la transcendance elle-même dans sa possibilité intrinsèque, c’est-à-dire encore expliciter en son essence la vérité transcendantale. Comment, cependant, le schématisme assure-t-il la manifestation de l’horizon pur de l’être ? *La manifestation par le schématisme de l’horizon pur de l’être consiste dans une sensibilisation de l’horizon. L’horizon se manifeste en tant qu’il est mis en relation avec la sensibilité, c’est-à-dire en tant qu’il est intuitionné*. Ainsi la perceptibilité de l’horizon mise au compte du schématisme trouve-t-elle en fait son fondement dans l’intuition elle-même. C’est l’intuition qui permet au schématisme d’accomplir son œuvre. La « vue pure », l’« image-schème » qu’il procure, est une place ouverte dans le milieu de la transcendance. Mais cette place n’est visible, n’est véritablement une « image », qu’en raison du caractère intuitif de celle-ci. Ce caractère de l’image qui la rend visible « provient du fait que l’image-schème surgit… d’une présentation possible » et cette présentation consiste dans le fait que la place pure qu’elle présente peut être amenée « dans la sphère d’une intuition possible ». *C’est donc la possibilité pour ce que présente l’image, c’est-à-dire pour l’horizon transcendantal de l’être, d’être amené dans la sphère d’une intuition possible qui fait de cet horizon une « image » et fonde ainsi son caractère phénoménologique*. En tant que le schématisme consiste dans la *formation d’une image*, il trouve sa possibilité dans l’intuition.

What gets brought "into the sphere of a possible intuition" changes between Heidegger's sentence and Henry's. The French of K 157 reads in full: "This intuitive character comes from the fact that the image-schema springs, and from the manner in which it springs, from a possible presentation, represented in its function as rule, so bringing this rule into the sphere of a possible intuition" (Biemel file L2074). Henry quotes it in two pieces. His ellipsis removes "and from the manner in which it springs". His first quotation then stops at "a possible presentation", before "represented in its function as rule". His second picks up only "into the sphere of a possible intuition", and gives it a new subject: "the pure place it presents can be brought" there. In the French it is "this rule" that is brought into the sphere of possible intuition. In Henry it is "the pure place", and his italic conclusion says what that place is: "what the image presents, *that is*, the transcendental horizon of being". The German is plainer still. The schema-image has its character "from the fact that and how it springs from the possible presentation represented in its regulation, and so holds the rule, as it were, into the sphere of possible intuitability" (*die Regel in die Sphäre der möglichen Anschaulichkeit hineinhält*, GA 3 §21, 99). For Heidegger the image is visible because it presents a rule for presenting. For Henry the horizon is visible because it can be intuited. The rule disappears from the chain, and with it the reason GA 3 gives for the image being an image.

The translators had prepared the substitution in their nouns. Where Heidegger writes look-character (*Anblickcharakter*), the French has "intuitive character" (*caractère intuitif*), with the German in brackets. Where he writes "possible intuitability" (*mögliche Anschaulichkeit*), it has "a possible intuition". On the next pages, where he writes beholdability (*Erblickbarkeit*), it has "intuitivity" (*intuitivité*), again with the German in brackets (K 160, Biemel file L2113). Henry quotes that last sentence at the end of the following paragraph: the pure concepts "need an essentially pure intuitivity, *if what is opposed in pure ob-jectivation is to be perceptible as opposed*". Heidegger's three words come from the vocabulary of looking and beholding (*Blick*, *erblicken*) and of the image. In the French all three become words of intuition. Henry's own gloss shows the German sense coming back through: "this character of the image that makes it *visible*" (*qui la rend visible*). The phrase is closer to *Anblick* than to *Anschauung*. Henry calls the character visible, and argues from the French noun that names it intuitive.

Henry's coinage does the rest. "*A sensibilization of the horizon*" (*une sensibilisation*, italic) is his word. The French has "sensible transposition" (*transposition sensible*) for Kant's *Versinnlichung*, and Henry quotes it in quotation marks in the next paragraph. Henry's noun is followed by two "that is"s: the horizon is manifest "inasmuch as it is brought into relation with sensibility, *that is*, inasmuch as it is intuited". Sensibility, then, is intuition. In GA 3 the sensible transposition is the procuring of an image according to a rule, and the image that the pure concepts need is time (§22, 103). Henry's "sensibilization" leaves out the rule and the time, and keeps only the relation to an intuiting.

The conclusion, "inasmuch as schematism consists in the *formation of an image*, it finds its possibility in intuition", is what Henry needs for the circle that follows: intuition grounds the image, the image grounds the manifestation of the horizon, and the horizon grounds intuition. The circle is real in Henry's reconstruction. It is produced by the cut, since GA 3's schema, a rule for forming a look in time, is not grounded in an intuition of the horizon. GA 3 has its own open question at this point: what makes the rule's look visible? It answers with time as pure self-affection, "failing which its function in forming the schema remains wholly obscure" (K 255 / §35, 200). That is the answer Henry meets in §24, in the next two readings.

## §24, K 244: affection *by* itself and affection *of* itself

Reads [§24](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#reaffirming-the-central-place-of-the-problem-of-receptivity-and-the-ontological-interpretation-of-time-as-auto-affection), third block. The passage is the opening of the first paragraph on the double sense and the paragraph that ranks the two senses.

> Auto-affection has a double signification. It designates first affection by itself. Inasmuch as it designates an affection by itself, the auto-affection of time means that it is time itself that affects itself. This means in the first place that time is not affected by anything other than itself, that is, essentially, that it is not affected by the existent.
>
> The auto-affection that constitutes the essence of time is not, however, identical with affection by itself; it is also, more fundamentally, affection of itself. Time is not only, in effect, what affects. Inasmuch as it is what affects, time presupposes what is affected by it. What is affected by time, however, is time itself. *Affection by itself finds its foundation in affection of itself*.

French:

> L’auto-affection a une double signification. Elle désigne d’abord l’affection par soi. En tant qu’elle désigne une affection par soi, l’auto-affection du temps signifie que c’est le temps lui-même qui s’affecte. Cela veut dire en premier lieu que le temps n’est pas affecté par autre chose que lui, c’est-à-dire essentiellement qu’il n’est pas affecté par l’étant.
>
> L’auto-affection qui constitue l’essence du temps ne s’identifie pas, toutefois, avec l’affection par soi, elle est aussi, plus fondamentalement, affection de soi. Le temps n’est pas seulement, en effet, ce qui affecte. En tant qu’il est ce qui affecte, le temps présuppose ce qui se trouve affecté par lui. Ce qui se trouve affecté par le temps, toutefois, est le temps lui-même. *L’affection par soi trouve son fondement dans l’affection de soi*.

The distinction is made with two prepositions, and only one of them comes from the text Henry is reading. In K 244 the translators render Heidegger's pure self-affection (*reine Selbstaffektion*) as "pure affection of self" (*affection pure de soi*) and, once, as "pure auto-affection" (*auto-affection pure*). Heidegger's "pure affection of itself" (*reine Affektion ihrer selbst*) becomes "pure affection of itself" (*pure affection de lui-même*). Henry's "affection of itself" (*affection de soi*) is thus the translators' phrase, and in their usage it names the whole of *Selbstaffektion*. His "affection by itself" (*affection par soi*) is not a phrase of theirs. He takes the French genitive *de soi*, which can be read as "by self" or "of self", fixes it on the second reading, where the self is what is affected, and coins *par soi* for the first. One term in GA 3 becomes a pair in Henry, and the more fundamental member of the pair is the one that kept the translators' words.

Henry's first sense corresponds to a sentence on the same page of *K*. "This pure intuition solicits itself by [the object] that it intuits, forming it without the help of experience" (*se sollicite elle-même par [l'objet] qu'elle intuitionne*, Biemel file L3499). The German reads "goes at itself with what is intuited, formed in it" (*geht mit dem in ihr gebildeten Angeschauten sich selbst an*, GA 3 §34, 189). The French "by" (*par*), followed by the translators' bracketed "[the object]", is Henry's *par soi*: time affected by the horizon it has formed. The next French sentence, "time is by nature pure affection of itself" (*pure affection de lui-même*), is his *de soi*. Heidegger wrote the two sentences as a description and its summary, one movement of going-at-oneself. Henry reads them as two senses and ranks them.

The ranking rests on a step that the paragraph asserts rather than argues. "Inasmuch as it is what affects, time presupposes what is affected by it. What is affected by time, however, is time itself." Affecting and being affected are correlatives, and each presupposes the other. To conclude that "affection by itself finds its foundation in affection of itself" turns a correlation into a priority. The italic sentence states the result as a chiasmus. It mirrors the grammar of §23's thesis, "the essence of spontaneity resides in receptivity", but it does not give the reason. Henry gives the reason three paragraphs later, and it is phenomenological. The horizon is manifest only if it is received, so the raising of an opposition "belongs to the essence's receptivity", and the time that receives grounds the time that forms. That is the §23 equation of manifesting with being received, applied to time.

The word "co-constitutes" (*co-constitue*, src L1029) marks how far this paragraph goes. "Affection by itself, which co-constitutes auto-affection" names affection by itself as one half, its hyphenated prefix granting it a share. At §31 the share is withdrawn. The affection of transcendence by the horizon "cannot rigorously be called auto-affection", and the "ambiguity" of the concept "means nothing at all". Between §24 and §31 the translators' *de soi* becomes the only rigorous sense, and Henry's own *par soi* the confused one. Heidegger's text sets the same two poles in the opposite order. Because time as pure self-affection "forms the essence of anything like going-at-oneself", the finite subject can "be gone at as a self" (*als ein Selbst angegangen werden*). For Heidegger, the self's being affected follows from time's self-affecting. Henry reverses this, and the French preposition is what lets him do so.

## §24, K 229–231: "not its temporal character but its intuitive character"

Reads [§24](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#reaffirming-the-central-place-of-the-problem-of-receptivity-and-the-ontological-interpretation-of-time-as-auto-affection), tenth block.

> If time, which is affection of itself, constitutes the most ultimate possibility of transcendence, it is inasmuch as it secures the reception of the horizon that transcendence has itself formed. *How, though, can time secure the reception of the horizon of transcendence? What is it in the nature of time that finally makes it possible as affection of itself?* Time secures the reception of the horizon of transcendence *inasmuch as it intuits* the pure ontological content that transcendence sets over against itself in the form of this horizon. *It is inasmuch as time is by nature intuition that it is possible as affection of itself*. *What matters in the time that ultimately makes possible the essence of manifestation is not its temporal character but its intuitive character.* Time is the "pure succession of the series of *nows*"; as such it has a pure ontological content that is opposed to that of space by reason of its determinate properties. *But the time of succession can no more be called an intuition than space can*. Space and pure time are each only the content of an intuition. That such a content is called intuitive means precisely that it gives itself only to the intuition that receives it. Intuition is neither time nor pure space, but the power that receives them and, as such, what confers on them a phenomenological signification and constitutes, on that account, their essence. Inasmuch as it is the power of receiving the horizon of pure time, within which space too manifests itself on the ground of the reception of this horizon, intuition is what secures the internal possibility of the originary and pure essence of manifestation, the possibility of transcendence itself. It is as intuition that the originary time of affection of itself fulfils the central role that is its own within the problematic concerning the universal essence of manifestation; it is on that account that it appears as what makes this essence possible in its internal structure.

French:

> Si le temps qui est l’affection de soi constitue la possibilité la plus ultime de la transcendance, c’est en tant qu’il assure la réception de l’horizon que la transcendance a elle-même formé. *Comment, cependant, le temps peut-il assurer la réception de l’horizon de la transcendance ? Qu’est-ce qui, dans la nature du temps, le rend finalement possible comme affection de soi ?* Le temps assure la réception de l’horizon de la transcendance *en tant qu’il intuitionne* le contenu ontologique pur que la transcendance s’objecte sous la forme de cet horizon. *C’est dans la mesure où le temps est en sa nature intuition qu’il est possible comme affection de soi*. *Ce qui importe dans le temps qui rend ultimement possible l’essence de la manifestation, ce n’est pas son caractère temporel, c’est son caractère intuitif.* Le temps est la « pure succession de la série des *maintenant* » ; il a, comme tel, un contenu ontologique pur qui s’oppose à celui de l’espace en raison de ses propriétés déterminées. *Mais le temps de la succession ne peut, pas plus que l’espace, être appelé une intuition*. L’espace et le temps pur ne sont l’un et l’autre que le contenu d’une intuition. Qu’un tel contenu soit appelé intuitif, cela signifie précisément qu’il ne se donne qu’à l’intuition qui le reçoit. L’intuition n’est ni le temps ni l’espace pur, mais le pouvoir qui les reçoit et, comme telle, ce qui leur confère une signification phénoménologique et constitue, à ce titre, leur essence. En tant qu’elle est le pouvoir de recevoir l’horizon du temps pur, à l’intérieur duquel se manifeste aussi l’espace sur le fond de la réception de cet horizon, l’intuition est ce qui assure la possibilité interne de l’essence originaire et pure de la manifestation, la possibilité de la transcendance elle-même. C’est en tant qu’il est intuition que le temps originaire de l’affection de soi remplit le rôle central qui est le sien à l’intérieur de la problématique qui concerne l’essence universelle de la manifestation, c’est à ce titre qu’il apparaît comme ce qui rend possible cette essence dans sa structure interne.

The paragraph argues by emphasis. It has five italic stretches in a dozen sentences: the two questions, the phrase "*inasmuch as it intuits*", and three theses. The decisive one is built on a noun phrase taken over from *K*. "Not its temporal character but its intuitive character" (*ce n'est pas son caractère temporel, c'est son caractère intuitif*) reuses the translators' "intuitive character" (*caractère intuitif*). At K 157 that phrase rendered Heidegger's look-character of the schema-image (see the previous reading but one). There it named an image's visibility. Here it names time's power to receive. The phrase stays the same while what it names changes, and the earlier translation had already made *Anblick* (look) into *intuition*.

Henry borrows the syntax of the sentence he is answering and turns it around. K 231 renders GA 3 §32, 175: "Time as pure intuition is neither only what is intuited in the pure act of intuition, nor only the act of intuiting deprived of its 'object'. Time as pure intuition spontaneously forms what it intuits" (Biemel file L3268). The German of the second sentence is "in one, the forming intuiting of what it intuits" (*ist in einem das bildende Anschauen seines Angeschauten*). The French drops "in one", adds "spontaneously", and turns a single nominal phrase into a subject, a verb and an object. Henry's answer has the same frame: "Intuition is *neither* time *nor* pure space, *but* the power that receives them". Heidegger's "neither … nor" refuses both one-sided readings of time, as only the intuited or only the intuiting. Henry's "neither … but" chooses the second. The French had already separated the subject from its object. Henry calls the object "the content of an intuition" and the subject "the power that receives", and once they are apart nothing temporal remains on the side of the power.

The quotation "pure succession of the series of *nows*" carries an italic that Henry's note (n. 80, *K* 229) does not mark as his. The corpus *K* file, an unreviewed OCR, shows no italics at that line (Biemel file L3234), so it cannot settle whether the emphasis is the translators' or Henry's, and nothing should be built on it. What the line does settle is the next sentence of *K*, which Henry does not quote: "to intuit means: to receive what offers itself. Pure intuition gives itself, in the receptive act, what is capable of being received". In GA 3 the receiving that Henry assigns to intuition alone is already a self-giving of the succession. On the same page Heidegger warns that the "still narrow notion of the receptive act", taken from empirical intuition, cannot apply.

What made time easy to empty of its temporal character was a vocabulary of looking that GA 3 itself uses. Earlier in §24 (src L1029) Henry quotes K 230: the "originarily forming act" is "in itself and at once an act of seeing, fore-seeing and re-seeing" (*acte de voir, de pré-voir et de re-voir*). The German is "looking-at, looking-ahead and looking-back" (*anblickend, vor- und rückblickend*). The three dimensions of time are named as kinds of looking. But in GA 3 the looking is the manner of a forming: "this originary *forming* is in itself and at once looking-at, looking-ahead and looking-back". Henry keeps the looking and gives the forming to the content. Once time's dimensions are seeings, the claim that what matters in time is its intuitive character follows almost from the vocabulary.

The italic sentence "*the time of succession can no more be called an intuition than space can*" denies a position that GA 3 does not hold. Heidegger never calls the bare succession an intuition. He calls time as pure intuition the forming intuiting *of* the succession, "in one". Henry's denial is aimed at the now-sequence, which GA 3 also calls "by no means time in its originality" (§32, 175). The thesis Henry actually has to meet is the "in one", and the paragraph does not meet it. That is why the circle Henry exposes at the end of §24 is, as he says, only "made more conspicuous" by the appeal to time, and why §31 must reopen auto-affection from the side of the content. The §24 block of the main commentary already calls this Henry's sharpest departure from the Kant book. The wording shows how the departure was made: through a French nominal phrase reused, a "neither … nor" turned into "neither … but", and Heidegger's own vocabulary of looking separated from the forming it described.

## §28–§29, *H* 134: *Erscheinen*, "of it and by it"

Reads [§28](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-abstractness-of-the-essence-of-manifestation-within-monisms-ontological-presuppositions-and-the-problem-of-constructing-a-phenomenology-of-the-ground), eighth block, and [§29](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#exhibiting-the-ontological-motive-behind-the-problematics-inability-to-construct-a-phenomenology-of-the-ground-and-to-give-content-to-the-idea-of-the-formal-structure-of-autonomy), first to third blocks. The passage quoted is the §28 paragraph and the third §29 paragraph. The first two §29 paragraphs are cited in the discussion.

> The dialectical situation in which thought loses itself is not suppressed but only brought to its highest degree of tension when the perpetual referral from one term to the other is ended in no other way than by their identification, by the flattening of one onto the other and the rigorous overlapping of essence and exteriority. Such an overlapping takes place, the fallacious identification it promotes is effective, when it is said that "the field where the *Erscheinen* comes to the intuition of itself is made of the *Erscheinen* itself and by it". Truth to tell, considered in its indeterminate generality, such a proposition is true. *In it the decisive character of the essence announces itself: that of being the foundation of its own manifestation*. To be the foundation of its own manifestation is precisely what it is, for the essence of manifestation, to be a foundation. But the character in virtue of which the essence is the foundation cannot be purely and simply affirmed. Such a character must, on the contrary, be grasped in its very possibility. *This possibility has been brought to light: it resides in the manifestation of the foundation itself, that is, of transcendence as such*. To the thought that understands the essence as the foundation of its own manifestation, the problem of this possibility does not escape. The manifestation of the act of appearing is included in the assertion according to which what becomes phenomenal in the pure field of phenomenality is this act of appearing itself. "The field where the *Erscheinen* comes to the intuition of itself *is made of the 'Erscheinen' itself*." It is precisely because the phenomenological field where the act of appearing manifests itself is made of the act of appearing itself that this field is the one where such an act manifests itself, that it is the field where the act of appearing comes to the intuition of itself. Of this field it is also said, however, that it is not only made of the *Erscheinen* itself but also by it. That the field of the *Erscheinen* is also "made by it" invites us to reflect on the nature of the *Erscheinen* inasmuch as it is not only what becomes phenomenal in this field as constituting its very phenomenality, *inasmuch as it is not only this field itself but also what creates it*. For what creates the phenomenological field of appearance is not this field itself but transcendence. To the thought that meditates on the nature of the *Erscheinen* this evidence then presents itself: the determination of the field where the act of appearing comes to the intuition of itself as made of this act and by it rests on a confusion, more exactly on the ambiguity of "appearing" itself, inasmuch as *it designates at once the phenomenality of the transcendental horizon of being and transcendence itself*. For us the sense of this confusion is clear: the attempt to determine the reality of the foundation by conferring on it a phenomenological status is what announces itself in it. With the phenomenological identification of transcendence and the horizon, however, this determination is badly accomplished. Far from being lifted by it, the fundamental phenomenological indeterminacy of the foundation is what makes it possible, for transcendence can be identified with the horizon of being *that it is not* only inasmuch as its originary and proper mode of revelation remains wholly unclarified. Because such a mode remains unclarified or, better said, wholly unknown, the mode of manifestation of the horizon itself is purely and simply substituted for it. Thus appears clearly the phenomenological origin of the identification of transcendence and the horizon. What is implied by such an identification is nothing less than an ontological identity that the whole context of the problematic belies. On the phenomenological plane itself, however, such an identification proves illusory. With the manifestation of the horizon, in which it seeks in vain the principle of a phenomenology of transcendence, that is, of the foundation itself, the problematic gives itself something it does not have, for the manifestation of the horizon is possible only through transcendence, that is, precisely on the ground of something that escapes it.
>
> The elaboration of the formal structure of the idea of autonomy remains formal, however; the conditions it enumerates as constituting together concrete phenomenality remain in fact empty presuppositions so long as this question is not answered: *what is appearing?* That the act of appearing appears, that it is the foundation of its own appearing, and that it appears precisely as this foundation, gives us to think, no doubt, that this act of appearing suffices to itself; but it still says nothing so long as the signification of the word "appearing" remains, in fact and each time, wholly indeterminate. Does the distinction instituted by the problematic between the *Erscheinen* and its manifestation, between the act of appearing understood as what becomes phenomenal in the phenomenological field of being and the same act considered in itself as founding the phenomenality of this field where it appears to itself, have a sense, and can it be maintained, if what is to be understood by "appearing" remains not only indeterminate but also, and in consequence, completely undifferentiated? Can the division, in the work of manifestation, between what accomplishes this work and what is accomplished by it claim any legitimacy, despite its *logical* appearance, if what is to be thought under each of the two terms it separates turns out in fact to be *the same thing*, "appearing"? Is such a division not, precisely, purely "logical"? Is the effort of the problematic, on the contrary, to identify with the visibility of the open field of phenomenality the power that opens this field and makes it "visible" not quite useless, if the unity this problematic seeks to promote and ground is in fact nothing other than the empty identity of tautology? Behind the overlapping of the *Erscheinen* and the phenomenal field where this *Erscheinen* comes to the intuition of itself, what else is there, in effect, if this field is made of the *Erscheinen* itself, if it is in fact itself this *Erscheinen*, than the pure and simple act of appearing, wholly indeterminate besides, which one merely sees fit to name twice? Why so many distinctions? When they prove illusory, is it not the very idea of a formal structure of the *Selbständigkeit* of the essence that appears needlessly complicated or, better said, empty and without foundation?

French:

> La situation dialectique où se perd la pensée n’est pas supprimée mais seulement portée à son degré de tension le plus haut, lorsqu’au perpétuel renvoi d’un terme à l’autre il n’est pas mis fin autrement que par leur identification, par l’aplatissement l’un sur l’autre et le recouvrement rigoureux de l’essence et de l’extériorité. Un tel recouvrement s’opère, l’identification fallacieuse qu’il promeut est effective, lorsqu’il est dit que « le champ où l’*Erscheinen* parvient à l’intuition de soi est fait de l’*Erscheinen* lui-même et par lui ». A vrai dire, considérée dans sa généralité indéterminée, une telle proposition est vraie. *En elle s’annonce le caractère décisif de l’essence, celui d’être le fondement de sa propre manifestation*. Être le fondement de sa propre manifestation, c’est justement cela, pour l’essence de la manifestation, être un fondement. Mais le caractère en vertu duquel l’essence est le fondement ne saurait être purement et simplement affirmé. Un tel caractère doit au contraire être saisi dans sa possibilité même. *Cette possibilité a été mise en lumière, elle réside dans la manifestation du fondement lui-même, c’est-à-dire de la transcendance comme telle*. A la pensée qui comprend l’essence comme le fondement de sa propre manifestation, le problème de cette possibilité n’échappe pas. La manifestation de l’acte d’apparaître est incluse dans l’affirmation selon laquelle ce qui se phénoménalise dans le champ pur de la phénoménalité est cet acte d’apparaître lui-même. « Le champ où l’*Erscheinen* parvient à l’intuition de soi *est fait de l’« Erscheinen » lui-même*. » C’est précisément parce que le champ phénoménologique où se manifeste l’acte d’apparaître est fait de l’acte d’apparaître lui-même que ce champ est celui où se manifeste un tel acte, qu’il est le champ où l’acte d’apparaître parvient à l’intuition de soi. De ce champ il est dit aussi, toutefois, qu’il n’est pas fait seulement de l’*Erscheinen* lui-même mais encore par lui. Que le champ de l’*Erscheinen* soit encore « fait par lui », cela nous invite à réfléchir sur la nature de l’*Erscheinen* en tant qu’il n’est pas seulement ce qui se phénoménalise dans ce champ comme constituant sa phénoménalité même, *en tant qu’il n’est pas seulement ce champ lui-même mais encore ce qui le crée*. Car ce qui crée le champ phénoménologique de l’apparence n’est pas ce champ lui-même, mais la transcendance. A la pensée qui médite sur la nature de l’*Erscheinen* se présente dès lors cette évidence : la détermination du champ où l’acte d’apparaître parvient à l’intuition de soi comme fait de cet acte et par lui, repose sur une confusion, plus exactement sur l’ambiguïté de l’« apparaître » lui-même en tant que *celui-ci désigne à la fois la phénoménalité de l’horizon transcendantal de l’être et la transcendance elle-même*. Pour nous le sens de cette confusion est clair : la tentative de déterminer la réalité du fondement en lui conférant un statut phénoménologique est ce qui s’annonce en elle. Avec l’identification phénoménologique de la transcendance et de l’horizon, toutefois, cette détermination s’opère mal. Loin d’être levée par elle, l’indétermination phénoménologique foncière du fondement est ce qui la rend possible, car la transcendance ne peut être identifiée avec l’horizon de l’être *qu’elle n’est pas* que pour autant que son mode originaire et propre de révélation demeure totalement inéclairci. Parce qu’un tel mode demeure inéclairci ou, pour mieux dire, totalement ignoré, il lui est purement et simplement substitué le mode de manifestation de l’horizon lui-même. Ainsi apparaît clairement l’origine phénoménologique de l’identification de la transcendance et de l’horizon. Ce qui est impliqué par une telle identification, ce n’est rien de moins qu’une identité ontologique que tout le contexte de la problématique dément. Sur le plan phénoménologique lui-même, toutefois, une telle identification se révèle illusoire. Avec la manifestation de l’horizon où elle cherche vainement le principe d’une phénoménologie de la transcendance, c’est-à-dire du fondement lui-même, la problématique se donne quelque chose qu’elle n’a pas, car la manifestation de l’horizon n’est possible que par la transcendance, c’est-à-dire justement sur le fond de quelque chose qui lui échappe.
>
> L’élaboration de la structure formelle de l’idée d’autonomie reste cependant formelle, les conditions qu’elle énumère comme constituant ensemble la phénoménalité concrète demeurent en fait des présuppositions vides aussi longtemps qu’il n’est pas répondu à cette question : *qu’est-ce qu’apparaître* ? Que l’acte d’apparaître apparaisse, qu’il soit le fondement de sa propre apparition, et qu’il apparaisse justement en tant qu’il est ce fondement, cela donne sans doute à penser que cet acte d’apparaître se suffit à lui-même, mais cela ne veut encore rien dire aussi longtemps que la signification du mot « apparaître » demeure en fait et chaque fois totalement indéterminée. La distinction instituée par la problématique entre l’*Erscheinen* et sa manifestation, entre l’acte d’apparaître compris comme ce qui se phénoménalise dans le champ phénoménologique de l’être et ce même acte considéré en lui-même en tant qu’il fonde la phénoménalité de ce champ où il s’apparaît, a-t-elle un sens et peut-elle être maintenue si ce qu’il convient d’entendre par « apparaître » reste non seulement indéterminé mais aussi et en conséquence complètement indifférencié ? Le partage dans l’œuvre de la manifestation entre ce qui accomplit cette œuvre et ce qui se trouve par elle accompli, peut-il se prévaloir, malgré son apparence *logique*, de quelque légitimité si ce qu’il convient de penser sous chacun des deux termes qu’il sépare se trouve être en fait *la même chose*, l’« apparaître » ? Un tel partage n’est-il pas, justement, purement « logique » ? L’effort, au contraire, de la problématique pour identifier avec la visibilité du champ ouvert de la phénoménalité le pouvoir qui ouvre ce champ et qui le rend « visible », n’est-il pas bien inutile si l’unité que cette problématique cherche à promouvoir et à fonder n’est en fait rien d’autre que l’identité vide de la tautologie ? Derrière le recouvrement de l’*Erscheinen* et du champ phénoménal où cet *Erscheinen* parvient à l’intuition de soi, qu’y a-t-il d’autre, en effet, si ce champ est fait de l’*Erscheinen* lui-même, s’il est en fait lui-même cet *Erscheinen*, que le pur et simple acte d’apparaître, d’ailleurs totalement indéterminé, qu’on trouve seulement bon de nommer deux fois ? Pourquoi tant de distinctions ? Quand celles-ci se révèlent illusoires, n’est-ce pas l’idée même d’une structure formelle de la *Selbständigkeit* de l’essence qui apparaît inutilement compliquée ou, pour mieux dire, vide et sans fondement ?

Henry keeps the German word because he has to divide it. Across these paragraphs *Erscheinen* (appearing) appears in German nineteen times, alongside the French "appearing" (*apparaître*) and "act of appearing" (*acte d'apparaître*). The German is the word of the sentence he quotes. The French is the word of his analysis, which shows that one term covers two things: "the phenomenality of the transcendental horizon of being and transcendence itself". The German is kept as the name of the undivided word, and the French takes over as Henry divides it. By §29 ¶3 the French has won: the question that ends the formal analysis, "*what is appearing?*" (*qu'est-ce qu'apparaître ?*), is asked in French. The German returns only as the thing "one merely sees fit to name twice".

The quotation comes from Heidegger's essay on Hegel, and Henry's French changes it at the point where his argument enters. Henry's note 99 gives *H* 134. The German there reads: "The immediate existence of consciousness or of knowing is appearing (*das Erscheinen*), in such a way that the site of appearing, as its stage, is formed *in* the appearing and by it itself" (*so zwar, daß die Stätte des Erscheinens als dessen Schauplatz* im *Erscheinen und durch dieses selbst gebildet wird*, GA 5 file L81). Henry renders: "the field where the *Erscheinen* comes to the intuition of itself is made of the *Erscheinen* itself and by it". Three things change. Heidegger's "site" and "stage" (*Stätte*, *Schauplatz*, literally a place for viewing) become "the field where the *Erscheinen* comes to the intuition of itself". Henry's version introduces the self-intuition that he will then demand to see. Heidegger's italic "*in*", a locative (the stage is formed within appearing), becomes "made of" (*fait de*), a material. Heidegger's "by it itself" (*durch dieses selbst*) becomes "by it" (*par lui*). The split Henry exploits, "of it" against "by it", is the split between his "made of" and the "by". In the German the pair is "in" and "through", which together say only that the stage arises within appearing and through it. Henry's French turns this into two claims, one about what the field is made of and one about what makes it, and that is what lets him ask which of the two the act of appearing is.

The sentence is also not Heidegger's thesis. In the essay it explicates Hegel's "presentation of appearing knowledge" (*Darstellung des erscheinenden Wissens*). It comes in a stretch that sets out the being of the subject as "subjectity" (*Subjektität*), presence "in the manner of representation". It belongs to Heidegger's account of the metaphysics of representation. Henry introduces it with an impersonal "when it is said that" (*lorsqu'il est dit que*), and that is in a way accurate. The proposition belongs to the monism of representation whose last form Heidegger is describing, and Henry is right to treat it as that monism's formula. The main commentary leaves the quotation without a source. Restoring the source shows that Henry's opponent here is a description of Hegel in Heidegger's words, not Heidegger in his own.

Henry's verdict on the formula is in two steps, and the wording marks each. First he concedes: "considered in its indeterminate generality, such a proposition is true", and the italic sentence names its truth, that the essence is "the foundation of its own manifestation". §29 ¶1 restates that truth in a chain of italics: "*the essence is active if it shows itself*", "*the essence is the foundation of its manifestation*". Then comes the charge, carried by quotation marks around the word itself: "the ambiguity of 'appearing'" (*l'ambiguïté de l'« apparaître »*), "the same thing, 'appearing'" (*la même chose, l'« apparaître »*). In §29 ¶3 the quotation marks extend to Henry's own categories. The division is called "*logical*" and then "purely 'logical'". The field's visibility is put in quotation marks too, "makes it 'visible'". The formal structure of autonomy that Section I built is put in doubt by Henry himself, and the doubt is carried out by scare quotes.

The last sentence of the quoted §28 paragraph names the mistake, and its italic carries it: transcendence is identified "with the horizon of being *that it is not*" (*qu'elle n'est pas*). The relative clause is the whole objection in three words. What follows in §29 does not refute the formula. It shows that the formula is empty unless the two modes of appearing it confuses can be told apart, which requires "the originary and proper mode of revelation" of transcendence. "Better said, wholly unknown" (*ou, pour mieux dire, totalement ignoré*) is the same self-correcting formula as at §18 ¶8, and here too it raises the stakes. What the problematic has not clarified, it does not know at all. The immanence introduced at §30 is Henry's name for that unknown mode, which is why the next reading begins there.

## §30: immanence defined by what it does not do

Reads [§30](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-ontological-determination-of-the-original-essence-of-revelation-as-immanence-immanent-content-and-transcendent-content), second block.

> The reason why the being of the foundation escapes the problematic that understands this foundation as transcendence lies in the fact that the manifestation of transcendence is not the work of transcendence itself. What is implied in this fact, as a positive determination of the being of the foundation, must now be thought. What does it mean, for a manifestation, "not to be the work of transcendence"? To work, for transcendence, is to open a horizon. The phenomenological formation of the transcendental horizon of being in the act that creates exteriority: such is the work of transcendence. *Not to be the work of transcendence therefore means, for a manifestation, to arise and be accomplished independently of the movement by which the essence launches itself and projects itself forward in the form of a horizon; to arise, be accomplished and be maintained independently of the ontological process of objectivation, that is, precisely in the absence of all transcendence*. The manifestation that occurs in the absence of all transcendence is, however, the manifestation of transcendence itself. That a manifestation, the manifestation of the essence understood as transcendence, occurs in the absence of all transcendence therefore means: *the originary act of transcendence reveals itself independently of the movement by which it launches itself forward and projects itself out of itself*. *The act that reveals itself independently of its own forward launch, independently of the movement by which it projects itself out of itself, reveals itself in itself, in such a way that this "in itself" means*: *without surpassing itself, without going out of itself*. *What does not surpass itself, what does not launch itself out of itself but remains in itself without leaving itself or going out of itself, is, in its essence, immanence. Immanence is the originary mode in which the revelation of transcendence itself is accomplished and, as such, the originary essence of revelation*.

French:

> La raison pour laquelle l’être du fondement échappe à la problématique qui comprend ce fondement comme la transcendance, réside dans le fait que la manifestation de la transcendance n’est pas l’œuvre de la transcendance elle-même. Ce qui est impliqué dans ce fait, au titre d’une détermination positive de l’être du fondement, doit maintenant être pensé. Que signifie, pour une manifestation, « ne pas être l’œuvre de la transcendance » ? Œuvrer, pour celle-ci, c’est ouvrir un horizon. La formation phénoménologique de l’horizon transcendantal de l’être dans l’acte créateur de l’extériorité, telle est l’œuvre de la transcendance. *Ne pas être l’œuvre de la transcendance, cela signifie donc, pour une manifestation, surgir et s’accomplir indépendamment du mouvement par lequel l’essence s’élance et se projette en avant sous la forme d’un horizon, surgir, s’accomplir et se maintenir indépendamment du processus ontologique de l’objectivation, c’est-à-dire précisément en l’absence de toute transcendance*. La manifestation qui se produit en l’absence de toute transcendance est cependant la manifestation de la transcendance elle-même. Qu’une manifestation, la manifestation de l’essence comprise comme la transcendance, se produise en l’absence de toute transcendance, cela veut donc dire : *l’acte originaire de la transcendance se révèle indépendamment du mouvement par lequel il s’élance en avant et se projette hors de soi*. *L’acte qui se révèle indépendamment de son propre élan en avant, indépendamment du mouvement par lequel il se projette hors de soi, se révèle en lui-même, de telle manière que cet « en lui-même » signifie* : *sans se dépasser, sans sortir de soi*. *Ce qui ne se dépasse pas, ce qui ne s’élance pas hors de soi mais demeure en soi-même sans se quitter ni sortir de soi est, dans son essence, immanence. L’immanence est le mode originaire selon lequel s’accomplit la révélation de la transcendance elle-même et, comme telle, l’essence originaire de la révélation*.

The term the rest of the book rests on is defined almost entirely by negation. Henry lists transcendence's verbs: to launch itself (*s'élancer*), to project itself forward (*se projeter en avant*), to surpass itself (*se dépasser*), to go out of itself (*sortir de soi*). Then he denies each one: "without surpassing itself, without going out of itself", "does not launch itself out of itself", "without leaving itself or going out of itself". The one positive verb, "remains in itself" (*demeure en soi-même*), is a reflexive with no motion in it. The definition is fixed by quotation marks around Henry's own phrase: "this 'in itself' means: without surpassing itself, without going out of itself". The quotation marks mark a phrase being given a meaning by stipulation, and the meaning given is a list of what the act does not do. Immanence enters the book as transcendence with its verbs negated.

The wording takes a risk that §33 has to repair. "In itself" (*en soi*) is the phrase of Sartre's being-in-itself, the thing that stays shut in itself, and §28 has just used Sartre against himself. A reader could take "remains in itself without going out of itself" as the description of a thing. §33 says this is precisely the false sense: there immanence is no "ontic category", and the table "does *not* remain in itself". The §30 definition depends on that later correction, because its own vocabulary of staying put is the vocabulary §33 rejects as the sense immanence gets when it is read off beings.

Most of the paragraph is in italics, and the roman sentences carry the premises. The italics hold the derivations: what "not the work of transcendence" means, what follows for the act, what "in itself" means, what immanence is. The roman sentences hold the two claims the derivations need. The first is the definition "to work, for transcendence, is to open a horizon" (*œuvrer, pour celle-ci, c'est ouvrir un horizon*). The second is the identity "the manifestation that occurs in the absence of all transcendence is, however, the manifestation of transcendence itself". Each carries a weakness. The definition limits transcendence's work to opening a horizon. If transcendence could manifest itself in some other way that was still its own, "not its work" would not mean "in the absence of all transcendence". Heidegger's own answer at GA 3 §34, a going out toward and back to itself that first constitutes the self (K 246), is such a way. The first italic sentence bridges the gap with two phrases, "independently of" and "that is, precisely in the absence of" (*c'est-à-dire précisément en l'absence*). "Independently of the movement" is one claim, and "in the absence of all transcendence" is a stronger one. The "that is" treats them as the same.

The last word changes the book's vocabulary. Section II began with "manifestation". From here on the originary essence is "revelation" (*révélation*). The phrase "mode of revelation" first entered the section at §22 (see the reading on *Ent-stand*), as the name of what an infinite intuition would have to be if it were not an intuition. Here that mode gets a name, immanence, and a status, "the originary essence of revelation". The italic thesis repeats "revelation" (*révélation*) and "reveals itself" (*se révèle*) five times in four sentences. What transcendence could not do for itself, manifest itself, is done by something whose definition is that it does nothing transcendence does. §31 turns this into auto-affection, and §32 into the thesis that immanence is the essence of transcendence. Section III's title takes the word that the definition implies: immanence is "the invisible".

## §31, K 244–245: "as one should, and as Heidegger does"

Reads [§31](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-fundamental-ambiguity-in-the-concept-of-auto-affection-auto-affection-and-affection-by-itself), fourteenth block.

> Does the bringing to light of an originary affection, in its radical ontological opposition to the affection of transcendence by the horizon that it raises for itself, not give a sense to the ambiguity weighing on the concept of affection? Does it not introduce this ambiguity into the problematic as a positive element of it, or, better said, a decisive one? Must such an ambiguity be lifted, or only deepened? Or else is the deepening of this ambiguity, which is discovered as the analysis advances and gives itself finally as its most positive result, not identically the bringing to light of another ambiguity, this one purely negative, according to which *the concept of affection has constantly been confused, by the problematic that moves within the ontological presuppositions of monism, with that of auto-affection?* For the affection of transcendence by the horizon it projects is not an affection of transcendence by itself, if the ontological dissociation of the essence and the pure content it represents to itself is to be maintained, if it is true to say, in conformity with this dissociation, that in receiving the pure ontological content it represents to itself, it is not itself that the essence receives. Because it is not an affection of transcendence by itself, the affection of transcendence by the horizon could not be understood by the problematic, if it wishes at least to keep some rigour, as an auto-affection. *Auto-affection resides neither in transcendence nor in representation*. For this reason it is false to say that "the pure affection of self … determines … the deep essence of transcendence", if, as one should and as Heidegger does, one understands by pure affection of self auto-affection itself. Nor, for the same reason, can auto-affection be interpreted, as Heidegger would still have it, as constituting the essence of time itself inasmuch as it "is implied in the intrinsic possibility of the act of objectivation", that is, precisely, as transcendence.

French:

> La mise en lumière d’une affection originaire dans son opposition ontologique radicale avec l’affection de la transcendance par l’horizon qu’elle se suscite à elle-même, ne donne-t-elle pas un sens à l’ambiguïté qui pèse sur le concept de l’affection ? N’introduit-elle pas cette ambiguïté dans la problématique comme un élément positif de celle-ci ou, pour mieux dire, décisif ? Une telle ambiguïté doit-elle être levée ou seulement approfondie ? Ou bien l’approfondissement de cette ambiguïté qui se découvre au progrès de l’analyse et se donne finalement comme son résultat le plus positif, n’est-il pas identiquement la mise en lumière d’une autre ambiguïté, celle-là purement négative, conformément à laquelle *le concept de l’affection a constamment été confondu par la problématique qui se meut à l’intérieur des présuppositions ontologiques du monisme avec celui de l’auto-affection ?* Car l’affection de la transcendance par l’horizon qu’elle projette n’est pas une affection de la transcendance par elle-même si la dissociation ontologique de l’essence et du contenu pur qu’elle se représente doit être maintenue, s’il est vrai de dire, conformément à cette dissociation, qu’en recevant le contenu ontologique pur qu’elle se représente, ce n’est pas elle-même que l’essence reçoit. Parce qu’elle n’est pas une affection de la transcendance par elle-même, l’affection de la transcendance par l’horizon ne saurait être comprise par la problématique, si celle-ci veut du moins garder quelque rigueur, comme une auto-affection. *L’auto-affection ne réside ni dans la transcendance ni dans la représentation*. Pour cette raison, il est faux de dire que « l’affection pure de soi… détermine… l’essence profonde de la transcendance »   si, comme il convient de le faire et comme le fait Heidegger, on entend par affection pure de soi l’auto-affection elle-même. Celle-ci, pour la même raison, ne peut non plus être interprétée, ainsi que le veut encore Heidegger  , comme constituant l’essence du temps lui-même en tant qu’il « est impliqué dans la possibilité intrinsèque de l’acte d’objectivation », c’est-à-dire précisément comme transcendance.

The concession clause decides what the charge is. "If, as one should and as Heidegger does, one understands by pure affection of self auto-affection itself" (*si, comme il convient de le faire et comme le fait Heidegger*). Henry grants that Heidegger's "pure affection of self" means auto-affection, and he grants that this is the right reading. He does not accuse Heidegger of confusing two senses of one term. The confusion is named, in italics, as being between two concepts: "*the concept of affection*" and "that of auto-affection". The agent of the confusion is not Heidegger but "the problematic that moves within the ontological presuppositions of monism", given as the agent of a passive verb, "has been confused". Heidegger's name appears only beside two quotations. So the fact that *Selbstaffektion* is one German word does not answer Henry. His claim is that what Heidegger calls self-affection is, analysed, an affection of transcendence by its horizon, that is, affection, not auto-affection. The 1953 French's "pure auto-affection" (*auto-affection pure*, K 244), which Henry's note 103 cites, is not the ground of this charge. Note 103 sets that phrase beside "pure affection of self" in "the same passage" and treats the two as one. The main commentary's block reads the charge as Henry's distinction "projected onto a single term". The concession clause rules that reading out, and the key-passage row for this passage started from the same mistake (see the list's Corrections).

The quotation Henry refutes has been taken out of a claim about Kant's text. K 245 reads: "It is therefore not only in the second edition of the *Critique* that Kant introduced the idea of the pure affection of self, which determines, as has now become manifest, the deep essence of transcendence" (Biemel file L3518). The German is "the idea of pure self-affection, which, as has now become clear, determines the innermost essence of transcendence" (*das innerste Wesen der Transzendenz bestimmt*, GA 3 §34, 190). Henry's two ellipses remove the relative pronoun and "as has now become manifest". His quotation starts after the frame, which is a claim about the history of the *Critique*. Heidegger's sentence says that a result already shown is present in Kant's first edition. In Henry's version it becomes a bare thesis, and "is false" can be predicated of it. "Has now become manifest" was Heidegger's claim to have shown it, and the cut removes it. The French "deep essence" (*essence profonde*) for "innermost essence" (*innerste Wesen*) also matters. Heidegger's superlative of inwardness, the innermost, is the very dimension Henry claims for immanence. The French softened it to depth, and Henry quotes the softened word.

The paragraph's three opening questions stage the alternative and then close it. Is the ambiguity "a positive element … or, better said, a decisive one" (*ou, pour mieux dire, décisif*)? The self-correcting formula seen at §18 ¶8 and §28 returns, and as there it raises the stakes in mid-sentence. Henry then gives the ambiguity two values: one "positive", the deepening of the analysis, and one "purely negative", monism's confusion. He keeps the first as his result and charges the second to the opponent. The step that does the work is conditional: the horizon's affection is not self-affection "if the ontological dissociation of the essence and the pure content it represents … is to be maintained". The verdict "it is false to say" rests on that "if". The dissociation was argued earlier in §31: the horizon is the essence's imaginary product, not the essence. Granted that, the verdict follows. Heidegger's answer is that the movement "from itself toward … and back to itself" first constitutes the self (K 246). That answer denies the dissociation's starting point: the self that would be dissociated from its horizon does not exist before the movement. The paragraph does not engage it.

"As Heidegger would still have it" (*ainsi que le veut encore Heidegger*) gives the second quotation, from K 244, the verb of a wish. The previous paragraph (src L1207) brought back the K 244 sentence on raising an opposition with "Heidegger said" in the imperfect (*disait Heidegger*). The verbs set Heidegger at a distance, as someone who used to say and still wants. The italic thesis, "*auto-affection resides neither in transcendence nor in representation*", settles where §24 had ranked. Affection "by itself" (*par soi*), which §24 still let "co-constitute" auto-affection (see the reading on affection by and of itself), is now excluded from it. The next paragraphs say what remains: an auto-affection whose content is the essence itself, which §32 calls the essence of transcendence.

## §32: "immanence is the essence of transcendence"

Reads [§32](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#immanence-and-transcendence), second block.

> It is in this way, in effect, that the subordination of reality to the essence of the power that receives it now takes place: when transcendence is not present in the internal structure of this essence, the reality received by it does not take before it the form of a horizon; it is no longer anything that would be before, and in this absence of all distance it is identified, on the contrary, with the essence itself. Such a mode of receptivity, which does not transcend itself toward its content but rather finds it in itself, that is, in the essence, exists. For the exclusion of transcendence from the originary essence of receptivity has proved, in the eyes of the problematic, to have a positive signification, which is expressed in the determination of a specific mode of receptivity whose internal structure is constituted by immanence. The subordination of reality to this originary power of receptivity means the possibility of a pure ontological reality that is no longer defined in its essence by exteriority, the possibility of a reality other than the horizon, *the originary ontological possibility of transcendence itself.* What makes a thing possible, however, is what is properly called its essence. With the bringing to light of the originary essence of receptivity, the problematic encounters the ultimate possibility it aims at inasmuch as it seeks the last foundation of all manifestation. *Immanence is the essence of transcendence*.

French:

> C’est de cette manière, en effet, que s’opère maintenant la subordination de la réalité à l’essence du pouvoir qui la reçoit : quand la transcendance n’est pas présente dans la structure interne de cette essence, la réalité reçue par celle-ci ne prend pas devant elle la forme d’un horizon, elle n’est plus rien qui soit devant et, dans cette absence de toute distance, s’identifie au contraire avec l’essence même. Un tel mode de réceptivité qui ne se transcende pas vers son contenu mais trouve bien plutôt celui-ci en lui-même, c’est-à-dire dans l’essence, existe. Car l’exclusion de la transcendance hors de l’essence originaire de la réceptivité s’est révélée aux yeux de la problématique avoir une signification positive qui s’exprime dans la détermination d’un mode spécifique de réceptivité dont la structure interne est constituée par l’immanence. La surbordination de la réalité à ce pouvoir originaire de réceptivité signifie la possibilité d’une réalité ontologique pure qui ne se trouve plus définie en son essence par l’extériorité, la possibilité d’une réalité autre que l’horizon, *la possibilité ontologique originaire de la transcendance elle-même.* Ce qui rend possible une chose, c’est là, toutefois, ce qu’on appelle proprement son essence. Avec la mise en lumière de l’essence originaire de la réceptivité, la problématique rencontre la possibilité ultime qu’elle vise en tant qu’elle cherche le fondement dernier de toute manifestation. *L’immanence est l’essence de la transcendance*.

(The source file reads "surbordination" in the fifth sentence, apparently a typographical error; the first sentence has "subordination". It is left as the file has it.)

The thesis is reached by substitution. The italic phrase before it calls immanence "*the originary ontological possibility of transcendence itself*". The roman sentence after it supplies a definition: "what makes a thing possible, however, is what is properly called its essence" (*ce qu'on appelle proprement son essence*). The italic thesis puts the defined word in place of the defining one: possibility of transcendence, therefore essence of transcendence. The definition is introduced as common usage, "what is called" (*ce qu'on appelle*), and the "however" (*toutefois*) marks it as a borrowing. It is the transcendental sense of essence that Heidegger also uses. In *Vom Wesen des Grundes*, which Henry cites as *WG*, transcendence is the domain within which the essence of ground is to be treated, and "transcendence designates the essence of the subject" (*die Transzendenz bezeichnet das Wesen des Subjekts*, GA 9 file L20, L100). Henry's thesis takes the same step one level down: the essence of transcendence is immanence. *WG* used transcendence to name the essence of other things. Henry asks for the essence of transcendence, and the grammar, the essence of X is Y, is the same.

The sentence that asserts the existence of immanent receptivity ends with its verb. "Such a mode of receptivity, which does not transcend itself toward its content but rather finds it in itself, that is, in the essence, exists" (*… existe*). After a long subject the claim arrives in one word, and the next sentence gives its warrant, introduced by "for" (*car*). The warrant is §30: the exclusion of transcendence "has proved … to have a positive signification". The existence of the mode is grounded on a positive reading of an exclusion. A negation has been read as a determination, and what the negation determines is said to exist. The main commentary's §32 blocks show that Henry knows this. The next block asks whether the determination is "positive … or a purely negative one", and Henry answers there with an appeal to revelation: a receptivity reveals its content, so the content is a phenomenological reality. That answer is not in this paragraph. Here the existence of immanent receptivity is asserted and its warrant comes from §30.

The first sentence carries the vocabulary of Section I and of the reading on *Ent-stand* in one phrase. The reality received in immanence "is no longer anything that would be before" (*n'est plus rien qui soit devant*), "and in this absence of all distance it is identified … with the essence itself". "Before" (*devant*) is the word Henry used at §22 to gloss the prefix of the ob-ject, "(ob, gegen)". "Distance" (*distance*) is the "phenomenological distance" of Section I's §9. Immanence is defined here as the absence of both: of the prefix and of the interval. The object was "before" because it was seen. What is received in immanence is not seen, because nothing stands before it. §36 sharpens the thesis into a chiasmus, "the immanent essence is the essence of immanence", and Section III calls what is not seen "the invisible".

## §33, *der reine Bezug*: the capital letter

Reads [§33](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-ontological-interpretation-of-the-essence-of-transcendence-as-immanence-and-the-internal-possibility-of-surpassing), eleventh block.

> The abstract moment of this negation, in which transcendence finds itself deprived of its essence, is also that of an affirmation no less abstract and empty, in which the relation of transcendence finds itself simply posited. The more immediate this positing, the more categorical the manner in which it is carried out. The affirmation of the relation of transcendence outside any philosophical context containing at least an indication of the internal possibility of such a relation does not easily let itself be recognized in its gratuitousness, for this gratuitousness is interpreted no less immediately as a character of the real element *and, at the same time, as what grounds its reality*. In the relation's absence of foundation resides, in effect, the character in virtue of which it is the foundation, the "absolute". When the absolute has no foundation, that is, in fact, *when its positive internal possibility cannot be exhibited*, its advent is only the more surprising. The relation surges forth like lightning; it is the simple Trait, *der reine Bezug.* This simplicity, which is the absence of all foundation, not only designates the relation as the foundation; it qualifies it further as a foundation without foundation. To be a foundation without foundation: such is the essence of the relation, such is *the contingent nature of the absolute*. In this contingency the absolute acquires the pathos that it precisely lacks. The ontological deficiency of the problematic is the source of the existential prestige with which it adorns itself. Existence, writes a commentator, is "a sort of absolute without origin and without destiny, *the Relation itself*, at once irreplaceable … and unjustifiable." Thus one sees the problematic's own powerlessness to "justify" the relation, that is, to grasp it in what makes it possible, lead it to absolutize the relation of transcendence by making the absence of foundation the character and essence of the foundation itself. The existential moment in which the absolute "without origin and without destiny" surges forth in the nakedness of its contingency is also the moment of realism. Realism is the realization of what is not reality but presupposes it. Realism is abstraction. The abstract realization of the absolute in the "Relation" does not suffice, however, to make it the real, living relation, the relation that *relates itself* to what it relates to. The addition of a capital letter does not replace the ontological work of elucidation that lays bare the concrete structure of reality. Reality is not the relation; it is presupposed by it. It is the being of the relation, what makes it possible. With the omission of the essence of transcendence, the philosophy of being keeps, despite appearances, to the level of the philosophy of consciousness, when the latter declares simply: "Consciousness (is) precisely only relation, referral, signification toward or for…"

French:

> Le moment abstrait de cette négation où la transcendance se voit privée de son essence est aussi celui d’une affirmation non moins abstraite et vide dans laquelle le rapport de transcendance se trouve simplement posé. D’autant plus immédiate est cette position, d’autant plus catégorique la manière dont elle s’opère. L’affirmation du rapport de transcendance en dehors de tout contexte philosophique contenant au moins une indication sur la possibilité interne d’un tel rapport ne se laisse pas aisément reconnaître dans sa gratuité, car celle-ci est interprétée non moins immédiatement comme un caractère de l’élément réel *et, en même temps, comme ce qui fonde sa réalité*. Dans l’absence de fondement du rapport réside en effet le caractère en vertu duquel celui-ci est le fondement, l’« absolu ». Quand l’absolu n’a pas de fondement, c’est-à-dire en fait *quand sa possibilité positive interne ne peut être exhibée*, son avènement n’en est que plus surprenant. Le rapport surgit comme l’éclair, il est le simple Trait, *der reine Bezug.* Cette simplicité qui est l’absence de tout fondement ne désigne pas seulement le rapport comme le fondement, elle le qualifie encore comme fondement sans fondement. Être un fondement sans fondement, telle est l’essence du rapport, telle est *la nature contingente de l’absolu*. Dans cette contingence l’absolu acquiert le pathétique qui lui fait justement défaut. La carence ontologique de la problématique est la source des prestiges existentiels dont elle se pare. L’existence, écrit un commentateur, est « une sorte d’absolu sans origine et sans destin, *la Relation elle-même*, à la fois irremplaçable… et injustifiable  . » Ainsi voit-on sa propre impuissance à « justifier » la relation, c’est-à-dire à saisir celle-ci dans ce qui la rend possible, conduire la problématique à absolutiser le rapport de transcendance en faisant de l’absence de fondement le caractère et l’essence du fondement lui-même. Le moment existentiel où l’absolu « sans origine et sans destin » surgit dans la nudité de sa contingence est aussi le moment du réalisme. Le réalisme est la réalisation de ce qui n’est pas la réalité mais la présuppose. Le réalisme est l’abstraction. La réalisation abstraite de l’absolu dans la « Relation » ne suffit pas cependant à faire de celle-ci la relation réelle vivante, la relation qui *se rapporte elle-même* à ce à quoi elle se rapporte. L’adjonction d’une majuscule ne remplace pas le travail ontologique d’élucidation qui met à nu la structure concrète de la réalité. Celle-ci n’est pas le rapport, elle est présupposée par lui. Elle est l’être du rapport, ce qui le rend possible. Avec l’omission de l’essence de la transcendance la philosophie de l’être s’en tient, malgré l’apparence, au niveau de la philosophie de la conscience, lorsque celle-ci déclare simplement : « La conscience (n’est) précisément que rapport, renvoi, signification vers ou pour…   »

The paragraph criticizes typography and uses it. Henry's verdict is "the addition of a capital letter does not replace the ontological work of elucidation", aimed at the commentator's "*la Relation elle-même*" (the Relation itself, capital R). Earlier in the same paragraph he writes "the simple Trait" (*le simple Trait*) with a capital T, puts "absolute" in quotation marks, and gives "*der reine Bezug*" (the pure relation) in German and in italics. He copies the style he is attacking before he condemns it. The capitals and quotation marks carry the pathos he attributes to the opponent: "in this contingency the absolute acquires the pathos (*pathétique*) that it precisely lacks". The word will return in Section IV. There affectivity is the originary essence and "suffering" (*souffrir*) its effectivity (§53), and pathos is the substance of revelation. Here it is the false pathos of a relation without ground, the pathos of advent, of the lightning (*comme l'éclair*). Henry sets the true pathos against it later, without lightning: an affectivity that never arrives because it never left.

The German phrase has no note. Its source is Rilke. Heidegger discusses "the pure relation" (*der reine Bezug*) in Rilke's poetry in *Wozu Dichter?* in *Holzwege* (GA 5, 283, as quoted in the corpus volume `heidegger/zaborowski-ed-martin-heidegger-holzwege.md`, L1553). The corpus has no text of that essay, and Henry does not say where he takes the phrase from, so the attribution stops at the phrase. What the wording shows is that Henry quotes it in Rilke's and Heidegger's German, next to a French capitalized noun, inside a paragraph whose named target is a French commentator on Sartre. His notes 108–109 give G. Varet, *L'ontologie de Sartre* (1948), 177 and 113. The paragraph thus gathers poet, philosopher and commentator under one formula, a relation that is absolute because it has no ground. "Foundation without foundation" (*fondement sans fondement*) has its Heideggerian form in *WG*, where freedom is "the ab-yss of Dasein" (*der Ab-grund des Daseins*). Henry does not cite it here, and the resemblance is not a citation. It is enough to show that the formula he mocks is not only existentialist.

The quotation marks around the opponent's words become Henry's own terms. Varet's "unjustifiable" is picked up as "to 'justify' the relation" (*à « justifier » la relation*), and Henry glosses it immediately: "that is, to grasp it in what makes it possible". Varet's "without origin and without destiny" is quoted a second time, in quotation marks, in the sentence that joins the existential moment to realism. The final quotation, from Varet 113, carries a verb in parentheses: "Consciousness (is) precisely only relation, referral, signification toward or for…" (*La conscience (n'est) précisément que rapport …*). The parenthetical "(n'est)" (is only) supplies a verb the quoted phrase lacks. Henry turns a verbless formula into a definition with a copula and a restriction, and so into a thesis he can place at "the level of the philosophy of consciousness".

The argument is carried by two copulas that define. "Realism is the realization of what is not reality but presupposes it." "Realism is abstraction." "Reality is not the relation; it is presupposed by it." These are the paragraph's own theses. They depend on the definition of reality as "what makes possible", given at §32 (see the previous reading), and they do not argue it again. The italic phrase that answers the capital letter is "the relation that *relates itself* to what it relates to" (*qui se rapporte elle-même*). Its emphasis falls on the reflexive verb, the same reflexive that §33 began from, *relating-itself-to*. A relation that relates itself must first be near itself. That is the immanence §32 called the essence of transcendence. The capital letter names the relation without its self, and the italic reflexive puts the self back.

## §34: "the not-being-in-the-world of being-in-the-world"

Reads [§34](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#consciousness-of-the-world-and-consciousness-without-world), first block.

> With the determination of the essence of transcendence as immanence, the transcendental relation of being-in-the-world is no longer simply affirmed but grasped, on the contrary, in its intrinsic possibility. This possibility resides in the manifestation of the relation itself. It is this possibility, understood as phenomenological possibility, that is determined in the bringing to light of the "essence" of transcendence. Because the originary phenomenological possibility of the relation constitutes its very reality, the determination of the internal structure of this possibility is identically that of the reality of the relation; it is the grasp of the relation in its true nature. To grasp the transcendental relation of being-in-the-world in its true nature, starting from the internal structure of its most ultimate phenomenological possibility, is to understand it in its radical opposition to the being of the "world" and to the phenomenality that constitutes it. *Transcendence is not in the world*. Not to be in the world means for it, not simply not to occur within the world in the manner of the existent, but, more precisely and in a way more decisive for the understanding of the nature of the "Being-in", *not to occur as that very thing which is the world*. The not-being-in-the-world of being-in-the-world constitutes, however, its ownmost possibility. This possibility, the possibility of an occurring of transcendence, the ultimate phenomenological possibility for transcendence to be, resides entirely in the effective phenomenality of what is not the world, *in the originary immanent revelation of the movement itself*. With the originary immanent revelation of the movement, as it is accomplished in its radical independence with regard to the world and to the phenomenality that belongs to it, the phenomenological foundation of the decisive opposition between the concepts of movement and of objectivity is brought to light. In conformity with this opposition it appears that *movement is essentially possible as non-objective.* Possible means real. The possibility of movement is the phenomenological reality of its originary manifestation. That movement is possible as non-objective means that it always holds itself short of the world, outside the field of the horizon; that, although it is the movement of relating to the world, movement nevertheless is never in the world.

French:

> Avec la détermination de l’essence de la transcendance comme immanence, le rapport transcendantal de l’être-au-monde n’est plus affirmé simplement mais saisi au contraire dans sa possibilité intrinsèque. Celle-ci réside dans la manifestation du rapport lui-même. C’est cette possibilité comprise comme possibilité phénoménologique qui se trouve déterminée dans la mise en lumière de l’« essence » de la transcendance. Parce que la possibilité phénoménologique originelle du rapport constitue sa réalité même, la détermination de la structure interne de cette possibilité est identiquement celle de la réalité du rapport, elle est la saisie de celui-ci dans sa vraie nature. Saisir le rapport transcendantal de l’être-au-monde dans sa vraie nature, à partir de la structure interne de sa possibilité phénoménologique la plus ultime, c’est le comprendre dans son opposition radicale à l’être du « monde » et à la phénoménalité qui le constitue. *La transcendance n’est pas dans le monde*. Ne pas être dans le monde, cela signifie pour elle, non pas simplement ne pas survenir à l’intérieur du monde à la manière de l’étant, mais, plus précisément et d’une manière plus décisive pour la compréhension de la nature de l’« Être-dans », *ne pas survenir comme cela même qui est le monde*. Le n’être-pas-dans-le-monde de l’être-dans-le-monde constitue cependant sa possibilité la plus propre. Celle-ci, la possibilité d’un survenir de la transcendance, la possibilité phénoménologique ultime pour que la transcendance soit, réside tout entière dans la phénoménalité effective de ce qui n’est pas le monde, *dans la révélation originaire immanente du mouvement lui-même*. Avec la révélation originaire immanente du mouvement telle qu’elle s’accomplit dans son indépendance radicale à l’égard du monde et de la phénoménalité qui lui appartient, se trouve mis en lumière le fondement phénoménologique de l’opposition décisive des concepts du mouvement et de l’objectivité. Conformément à cette opposition il apparaît que *le mouvement est essentiellement possible comme non objectif.* Possible signifie réel. La possibilité du mouvement est la réalité phénoménologique de sa manifestation originaire. Que le mouvement soit possible comme non objectif veut dire qu’il se tient toujours en deçà du monde, hors du champ de l’horizon, – que, bien qu’il soit celui de se rapporter au monde, le mouvement pourtant n’est jamais dans le monde.

Henry puts a negation inside Heidegger's compound. The French rendering of *In-der-Welt-sein* (being-in-the-world) is hyphenated, *être-dans-le-monde*. In *Sein und Zeit* the hyphens mark a unitary phenomenon, one that cannot be put together from a being, a world and an "in". Henry nominalizes the compound once more and inserts the negative particles: "le n'être-pas-dans-le-monde de l'être-dans-le-monde" (the not-being-in-the-world of being-in-the-world). Inside the same hyphens, then, he places what is not in the world. The coinage gives being-in-the-world a genitive relation to its own negation, and the sentence calls that negation "its ownmost possibility" (*sa possibilité la plus propre*). That is the superlative *Sein und Zeit* keeps for Dasein's own possibilities, and above all for death. The main commentary paraphrases the coinage away. It is the paragraph's thesis in a single word.

The quoted "Being-in" (*« Être-dans »*), with a capital and in quotation marks, is the French for Heidegger's *In-Sein*. Heidegger had already separated it from spatial inclusion, at the page Henry cites for the table and the wall (*SZ* 55, note 104). Two things merely present can be said to be "by" each other, and the chair "touches" the wall, but "strictly speaking there can never be any talk of 'touching'" (*Sein und Zeit* file L1007). So when Henry says that "not to be in the world" does not mean "not to occur within the world in the manner of the existent", he is repeating *Sein und Zeit*. The step that is his own is the italic one that follows: "*not to occur as that very thing which is the world*". Heidegger denied that being-in is being inside. Henry also denies that it is being the world, that is, that the phenomenality of the world is the phenomenality of transcendence. The "more precisely and in a way more decisive" (*plus précisément et d'une manière plus décisive*) marks the point where he goes beyond *Sein und Zeit*.

Three words reverse a maxim of *Sein und Zeit*. "Possible means real" (*Possible signifie réel*). In *Sein und Zeit* §7 Heidegger writes that "higher than actuality stands *possibility*" (*Höher als die Wirklichkeit steht die* Möglichkeit, *Sein und Zeit* file L797). He sets possibility above the actual as what is to be seized and not merely found. Henry identifies the two, and the next sentence gives the reason: "the possibility of movement is the phenomenological reality of its originary manifestation". A possibility that is the self-manifestation of what it makes possible is not ahead of the actual, because it is the actual already revealed to itself. The copula "means" (*signifie*) is the same short definitional copula as at §33 ("realism is abstraction"). The section's large reversals are made in sentences of three or four words.

"Movement" (*mouvement*) is the word Henry keeps for transcendence once it is grasped from its immanent essence, and the italic thesis opposes it to "objectivity": "*movement is essentially possible as non-objective*". The opposition is the one §33 drew between the hand that relates to nothing, because it is objective, and the surpassing that relates because it is not. Its formula here, "it always holds itself short of the world" (*il se tient toujours en deçà du monde*), answers the *Humanismus* sentence quoted at §33, "man is never short of the world, man first" (*Ueber den Humanismus* 35, Henry's note 107). Heidegger denied that man is first short of the world and then in it. Henry agrees that man is not first short of the world, but the movement that relates man to the world always is. The paragraph thus completes Section II's argument at the point where it began, with natural consciousness at §17 already in the presence of being. The presence that §17 called the Parousia is now located in a movement that is never in the world it opens. §34 ¶2 draws the conclusion in Husserl's phrase: imagination's consciousness is a "consciousness without world".

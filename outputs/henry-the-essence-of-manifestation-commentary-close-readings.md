# Michel Henry, *L'essence de la manifestation*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the block of the [main commentary](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md) that it extends. The French is quoted from the PUF text in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/henry/henry-lessence-de-la-manifestation.md`, an ebook conversion of the 2011 reprint). That file keeps Henry's italics, so emphasis can be argued from. It has no page numbers, so passages are cited, as in the commentary, by section and by the commentary's paragraph labels. All English renderings of Henry, Hegel and Heidegger are working renderings, kept close to the French so that its structure shows.

Two sigla recur. *K* is the 1953 French translation of Heidegger's *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* by de Waelhens and Biemel. A *K* page resolves only in `heidegger-ga-3-kant-et-le-probleme-de-la-metaphysique-biemel.md`, where it equals the pdf page minus two, and each *K* passage is given with its place in GA 3. *H* is the separate edition of *Holzwege*, which Henry quotes in his own French for Heidegger's essay on Hegel's concept of experience. The corpus copy of that essay (`heidegger-ga-5-hegels-begriff-der-erfahrung.md`) prints the *Holzwege* page numbers in the margin, so *H* pages can be checked against the German.

One rendering needs saying in advance. Henry's French pair *existentiel* / *existential* is the French rendering of Heidegger's *existenziell* / *existenzial*. These readings render it "existentiell" / "existential", as the English translation of *Sein und Zeit* does. So what the main commentary calls Henry's "existential determinations" appear here as "existentiell determinations".

## §17 ¶2, K 279: the future tense the translators supplied

Reads [§17, Paragraph 2](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-originary-character-of-the-manifestation-of-being-and-the-problem-of-natural-consciousness).

> No less ambiguous is the proposition cited earlier, according to which "in order that one may understand the essential determination of the existent by being, the determining element itself will have to show itself with a certain clarity". The manifestation of being, understood as the element that determines the existent (as constitutive of its ontological structure), is here interpreted as having to come about in the future, and so is referred to a work of elucidation that it calls for, and calls for precisely in order to come about, at least "with a certain clarity". *The determination of the existent by being is nonetheless realized prior to the philosopher's understanding of this determination, and this determination, prior to every philosophical understanding, nonetheless presupposes the manifestation of being, inasmuch as it is in fact nothing other than this manifestation itself.* For it is on the ground of this manifestation that the existent is what it is. To natural consciousness, which grasps the existent each time with its own characters, being is already given: the manifestation of being is originary.

French:

> Non moins ambiguë est la proposition précédemment citée selon laquelle « pour que l’on puisse comprendre l’essentielle détermination de l’étant par l’être il faudra que l’élément déterminant lui-même se montre avec une certaine clarté ». La manifestation de l’être compris comme l’élément déterminant l’étant (en tant que constitutif de la structure ontologique de celui-ci) est ici interprétée comme devant se produire dans le futur, référée par conséquent à un travail d’élucidation qu’elle appelle, et cela justement pour se produire, du moins « avec une certaine clarté ». *La détermination de l’étant par l’être se réalise pourtant antérieurement à la compréhension de cette détermination par le philosophe, et cette détermination antérieure à toute compréhension philosophique présuppose néanmoins la manifestation de l’être en tant qu’elle n’est rien d’autre, en fait, que cette manifestation elle-même*. Car c’est sur le fond de cette manifestation que l’étant est ce qu’il est. A la conscience naturelle qui saisit chaque fois l’étant avec ses caractères propres, l’être est d’ores et déjà donné : la manifestation de l’être est originaire.

The whole charge hangs on a tense. Henry reads "will have to" (*il faudra*) as a future and glosses it at once: the manifestation of being is interpreted "as having to come about in the future" (*comme devant se produire dans le futur*). The future is the translators'. At GA 3 §40, 223 Heidegger writes that the determining "must be sufficiently graspable" (*muß das Bestimmende selbst hinreichend faßbar*), a present necessity, and goes on to say that being "must first be conceived" (*muß zuvor begriffen werden*). The French (K 279) made two changes. It put the necessity into the future, and it turned "sufficiently graspable" into "shows itself with a certain clarity" (*se montre avec une certaine clarté*). Between them they convert a condition on a question, namely which concept the question of being must form first, into a sequence in time in which being shows itself later, once philosophy has done its work. Henry's reading takes over both changes. His "in the future" restates the French tense, and his second quotation, "at least 'with a certain clarity'" (*du moins « avec une certaine clarté »*), restates the French verb. The English translation in the corpus (`heidegger-ga-3-kant-and-the-problem-of-metaphysics.md`) follows the German: "the determining itself must be sufficiently comprehensible". Read in that version, the sentence gives Henry nothing to correct.

The attribution is also handled with care. When Henry first quoted the sentence, at §16, he wrote "says Heidegger" (src L585). Here it is "the proposition cited earlier", with no name, and the note points back to §16 and not to Heidegger. The charge is laid against a proposition and its grammar. That is also the only level on which it holds.

Henry's own thesis runs on a different word. "At least" (*du moins*) concedes that only the clarity is deferred. The italic sentence then drops that concession and asserts more: the determination of the existent by being is realized before the philosopher understands it, and it "presupposes" the manifestation of being "inasmuch as it is in fact nothing other than this manifestation itself". The step from determining to manifesting is carried by "in fact" (*en fait*) and by the identity it introduces, not by an argument. That identity of being with appearing is the premise of the monism Section I took apart, and Henry turns it here against a sentence that does not need it. GA 3 §41, 228 already says that the understanding of being pervades existence without being conceived. So the thesis stands without the polemic, as the main commentary says. The close reading adds where the polemic came from: a French future tense.

The vocabulary of "understanding" (*compréhension*) slips too. In this paragraph "understanding" is the philosopher's thematic grasp, and it comes after being has manifested itself. Two pages later (§18 ¶7) "ontological understanding" (*compréhension ontologique*) names the originary manifestation itself, which comes before every grasp. The French had already rendered Heidegger's *begreifen* (conceive) by "understand" (*comprendre*), so the word Henry inherits from *K* covers both the concept and the understanding that GA 3 keeps apart. The paragraph's closing word, "already" (*d'ores et déjà*), picks up the contrast of ¶1 between the "at last" of accomplished phenomenology and the "already" of its condition. It prepares the claim at §21 ¶7 that philosophy always comes too late.

## §17 ¶6, H 163 and 144: "forget" in quotation marks

Reads [§17, Paragraph 6](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-originary-character-of-the-manifestation-of-being-and-the-problem-of-natural-consciousness).

> Because the manifestation of being is originary, because natural consciousness holds itself, in conformity with its essence, in the truth, it cannot be said to turn away from the truth. *Consciousness cannot forget the being that constitutes its very essence.* When it is nonetheless asserted that this consciousness lives, as natural consciousness, in the forgetting of being, what is meant is that it keeps to the existent and, in doing so, holds for nothing what is not of this nature, that is, being, the appearing of what appears. Natural consciousness does indeed direct itself toward the existent, and in its exclusive preoccupation with the existent it does not concern itself with being. But natural consciousness can be preoccupied with the existent only if the existent shows itself to it. And the manifestation of the existent is, as such, the manifestation of being. *Not for an instant has being ceased to show itself to natural consciousness at the very moment when it is said to "forget" it.*

French:

> Parce que la manifestation de l’être est originaire, parce que la conscience naturelle se tient, conformément à son essence, dans la vérité, elle ne saurait être dite se détourner de celle-ci. *La conscience ne peut oublier l’être qui constitue son essence même*. Quand on affirme cependant que cette conscience vit, comme conscience naturelle, dans l’oubli de l’être, on veut dire qu’elle s’en tient à l’étant et, ce faisant, tient pour rien ce qui n’est pas de cette nature, c’est-à-dire l’être, l’apparaître de l’apparaissant. La conscience naturelle, en effet, se dirige vers l’étant et dans sa préoccupation exclusive à l’égard de celui-ci, elle ne se soucie pas de l’être. La conscience naturelle ne peut cependant se préoccuper de l’étant que si celui-ci se montre à elle. Mais la manifestation de l’étant est, comme telle, la manifestation de l’être. *Pas un instant l’être n’a cessé de se montrer à la conscience naturelle au moment même où elle est dite l’ « oublier »*.

The paragraph uses four verbs for natural consciousness's relation to being. It refuses two: consciousness cannot "turn away" (*se détourner*) from the truth, and it cannot "forget" (*oublier*) being. It grants two: consciousness "holds for nothing" (*tient pour rien*) and "does not concern itself" (*ne se soucie pas*). It reconciles the two pairs by translating the refused verb into a granted one: to say that consciousness forgets being is to say that it holds being for nothing. The quotation marks around "forget" in the last sentence mark this. The word is kept at a distance because it is someone else's word, and because, once translated, it no longer means forgetting.

Henry's notes show whose words these are. Both cite Heidegger's essay on Hegel. Note 8 (*H* 163) quotes natural consciousness as "always already brought onto the way of its truth", which "on the way, always already too, constantly turns back". The German has "on the way it also keeps turning back" (*unterwegs kehrt es auch schon ständig um*). Henry's main text sharpens turning back into turning away. Yet the sentence he cites grants what he asserts, since consciousness is "already brought onto the way to its truth" (*schon auf den Weg zu seiner Wahrheit gebracht*). Henry's quotation also stops before the clause that follows, "and remains for itself" (*und bleibt für es*), which is the Hegelian "for it" he takes up at §19. Note 9 (*H* 144) supplies the granted verb: "the appearing of what appears, the reality of the real, passes in the purview of natural consciousness for something null" (*gelten … als etwas Nichtiges*). Henry's "holds for nothing … the appearing of what appears" (*tient pour rien … l'apparaître de l'apparaissant*) is that sentence turned into a definition. "Forget", on the other hand, is not in the essay. The corpus text of it has neither *Vergessenheit* (forgetfulness) nor any form of *vergessen* (forget). Henry brings the general Heideggerian doctrine of the forgetting of being to a text that says something narrower, and then defines the doctrine by that narrower text. The quotation marks mark the import.

The grammar keeps the doctrine without an author. The main text says "when it is asserted" (*quand on affirme*), "what is meant" (*on veut dire*), and "it is said to 'forget' it" (*elle est dite l'« oublier »*). Heidegger appears only in the notes, and there he is cited with "cf.", as a pointer to compare and not as the source of the quoted word. As at ¶2, Henry argues against a proposition and leaves its author in the apparatus.

The argument turns on "as such" (*comme telle*): "the manifestation of the existent is, as such, the manifestation of being". From that identity, the final "not for an instant" follows at once. But the identity proves that being is manifest. It does not prove that being is manifest *as being*, and that is the problem Heidegger's sentence names: in natural consciousness the appearing appears as null. Henry needs a reply to this, and §18 gives it, splitting "taking heed of being" into a thematic and an ontological sense. Henry's ground for refusing forgetting, that being "constitutes its very essence", also cuts both ways. If consciousness cannot forget its essence because it is that essence, neither can it hold that essence before itself. Henry draws this consequence himself at §45, whose title speaks without quotation marks of the dissimulation of the originary essence of revelation "and its forgetting" (*et son oubli*). The word refused here returns there, but with a new object: the immanence that Henry's own Section II will put in place of transcendence.

## §18 ¶5–6, H 163: the ontological and the existentiell, and Heidegger's "not yet"

Reads [§18, Paragraphs 5 and 6](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-concept-of-representation-ontological-structure-and-existential-understanding).

> Let us make here a distinction important for the rest of our inquiry, a distinction between what is ontological and what is existentiell. The "taking heed of being" of true knowledge and the "not taking heed of being" of natural consciousness are modes of the life of consciousness, determinations of its existence. We shall call these determinations *existentiell determinations*. The "taking heed of being" inherent in the essence of consciousness, and as such indifferent both to the "taking heed of being" of true knowledge and to the "not taking heed of being" of natural consciousness, is on the contrary, as that originary manifestation of self of being which constitutes consciousness itself in its universal essence, an *ontological structure*. The relation between the ontological structure of consciousness and the existentiell determinations it can take on in its life is easy to understand, at least as far as what is said here goes: the ontological structure of consciousness is at once indifferent and immanent to its existentiell determinations. Indifferent, because this structure does not prescribe that this or that existentiell determination of consciousness occur, for instance that consciousness, turning back on its own essence, direct itself upon it in order to grasp it in true knowledge. In conformity with this indifference, consciousness can exist without true knowledge occurring in it. *Existentiell determinations are inessential*: they are, in relation to the essence, contingent and variable predicates. But the inessential character of these determinations does not mean that they are separated from the essence. On the contrary, the essence is immanent to them as what makes them possible, as their very essence. That is why natural knowledge *is* ontological. The distinction in consciousness between its existentiell determinations and its ontological structure lays bare the ambiguity of a proposition such as the one by which Heidegger still tries to characterize natural knowledge and to underline its contradiction: "natural consciousness is and is not… ontological". Natural consciousness is ontological inasmuch as the essence of consciousness in its universal structure is present in it. It is improper to say that natural consciousness *is* not ontological. Considered as an existentiell determination, natural consciousness is no less ontological, inasmuch as it is precisely a mode of the life of consciousness, a mode of existence in its universal ontological structure, inasmuch as, in general, the universal ontological structure of existence is immanent to every existentiell determination as constituting its very essence.

French:

> Faisons ici une distinction importante pour la suite de nos recherches, une distinction entre ce qui est ontologique et ce qui est existentiel. Le « prendre garde à l’être » du savoir vrai et le « ne pas prendre garde à l’être » de la conscience naturelle sont des modes de la vie de la conscience, des déterminations de son existence. Nous appellerons ces déterminations des *déterminations existentielles*. Le « prendre garde à l’être » inhérent à l’essence de la conscience et, comme tel, indifférent au « prendre garde à l’être » du savoir vrai comme au « ne pas prendre garde à l’être » de la conscience naturelle est au contraire, comme cette manifestation de soi originaire de l’être qui constitue la conscience elle-même dans son essence universelle, une *structure ontologique*. Le rapport entre la structure ontologique de la conscience et les déterminations existentielles que celle-ci est susceptible de revêtir dans sa vie est facile à comprendre, du moins pour ce qui en est dit ici : la structure ontologique de la conscience est à la fois indifférente et immanente aux déterminations existentielles de celle-ci. Indifférente, parce que cette structure ne prescrit pas que telle ou telle détermination existentielle de la conscience se produise et, par exemple, que la conscience, se retournant sur sa propre essence, se dirige sur elle pour la saisir dans le savoir vrai. Conformément à cette indifférence, la conscience peut exister sans que le savoir vrai se produise en elle. *Les déterminations existentielles sont inessentielles*, ce sont, par rapport à l’essence, des prédicats contingents et variables. Mais le caractère inessentiel de ces déterminations ne signifie pas qu’elles soient séparées de l’essence : bien au contraire, l’essence leur est immanente comme ce qui les rend possibles, comme leur essence même. C’est pourquoi le savoir naturel *est* ontologique. La distinction dans la conscience entre ses déterminations existentielles et sa structure ontologique met à nu l’ambiguïté d’une proposition comme celle par laquelle Heidegger s’efforce encore de caractériser le savoir naturel et de souligner sa contradiction : « la conscience naturelle est et n’est pas… ontologique ». La conscience naturelle est ontologique en tant qu’est présente en elle l’essence de la conscience dans sa structure universelle. Il est impropre de dire que la conscience naturelle n’*est* pas ontologique. Considérée comme une détermination existentielle, la conscience naturelle n’en est pas moins ontologique en tant qu’elle est justement un mode de la vie de la conscience, un mode de l’existence dans sa structure ontologique universelle – en tant que, en général, la structure ontologique universelle de l’existence est immanente à toute détermination existentielle comme constituant son essence même.

The distinction is made by turning a verb into a noun. "Taking heed of being" (*le « prendre garde à l'être »*) comes from Henry's quotation of *H* 136 at ¶4. Here it is set in quotation marks and given an article, so that it can be counted and sorted: twice as a "mode" of the life of consciousness, once as an "ontological structure". The new term is introduced with the formula "we shall call" (*nous appellerons*) and printed in italics, *existentiell determinations*. A second italic, *ontological structure*, gives it its opposite. Everything that follows in Sections II and III uses these two italics.

The verb Henry nominalizes was already divided in the German he quotes. At *H* 136 Heidegger writes that natural consciousness "does not attend to being" (*achtet … nicht auf das Sein*) "and must nonetheless heed it" (*muß es gleichwohl achten*). The first is *achten auf* with a preposition (attend to). The second is *achten* with a direct object (heed, respect). Henry's French at ¶4 gives one phrase twice, "does not take heed of being and must nonetheless take heed of it" (*il ne prend pas garde à l'être et doit pourtant y prendre garde*). It then discovers that "to take heed does not mean the same in the two cases". Heidegger's syntax had already marked the difference that Henry's distinction claims to uncover. Heidegger's next sentence states Henry's thesis outright: natural consciousness "cannot help co-representing the being of beings in general" (*kann nicht umhin, das Sein des Seienden im allgemeinen mit vorzustellen*), since without the light of being it could not even lose itself among beings.

The proposition Henry corrects in ¶6 has been cut to fit. He quotes *H* 163 as "natural consciousness is and is not… ontological". The German reads that natural consciousness "is ontological and yet is not yet so" (*ist … ontologisch und ist dies doch noch nicht*). Heidegger then names the state: "we say that ontic consciousness is pre-ontological" (*vorontologisch*). The ellipsis removes "yet … not yet" (*doch noch*), and Henry's framing word "contradiction" (*contradiction*) occurs nowhere in the essay. What Heidegger wrote is a developmental "not yet": natural consciousness is on its way, as the sentence about turning back on the same page says (see the previous reading). Henry's French makes it a flat "is and is not", which a distinction can then dissolve. The italic copula, "natural knowledge *is* ontological" and "it is improper to say that natural consciousness *is* not ontological", turns the question from one of time to one of being. Consciousness either is ontological or is not, and Henry answers that it is.

The real disagreement survives the cut, and it lies in the word "indifferent" (*indifférente*). For Heidegger the pre-ontological is "brought onto the way to its truth", and its "not yet" belongs to it as the impulse of the path. Henry's structure "does not prescribe" (*ne prescrit pas*) any determination, true knowledge included: "consciousness can exist without true knowledge occurring in it". The essence has no goal. That is what lets §19 deny the becoming-for-itself any ontological signification, and §21 deny the absolute a history. Where Heidegger had "pre-ontological", Henry writes "ontological" without the prefix. It is the same refusal of a "not yet" that the next reading finds in his "ontological understanding".

The inference is weakest at its last clause, which says that the essence is immanent to the determinations "as their very essence". The word "essence" here does two jobs, and Henry's note 16 concedes it. One may speak of the essence of an existentiell determination, perception for instance, as a typical structure. But that is not the essence of consciousness in general that grounds all determinations. "Natural knowledge is, if you like, an essence." The paragraph's "that is why" (*c'est pourquoi*) slides from the second sense to the first.

## §18 ¶8: "existentiell or, better said, existential"

Reads [§18, Paragraph 8](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-concept-of-representation-ontological-structure-and-existential-understanding).

> In the ontological understanding of being resides absolute knowledge. True or philosophical knowledge is an existentiell or, better said, existential understanding of the ontological understanding. But not every existentiell understanding of the ontological understanding is necessarily true or necessarily philosophical. If it does not confine itself to the understanding of an existentiell determination considered in its particularity, existence's implicit existentiell understanding of itself is an existentiell understanding of the ontological understanding, the implicit and most often improper sketch of an existential understanding. In truth, and precisely because an existentiell determination is a determination of existence, existence's implicit existentiell understanding of itself is always implicitly an existentiell understanding of the essence of existence in its universal ontological structure, that is, an existentiell understanding of the ontological understanding. Philosophical knowledge is only a thematization, carried out with the conceptual material required and worked out for this purpose, of this implicit existentiell self-understanding of existence, that is, of the ontological understanding itself.

French:

> Dans la compréhension ontologique de l’être réside le savoir absolu. Le savoir vrai ou philosophique est une compréhension existentielle ou, pour mieux dire, existentiale de la compréhension ontologique. Mais toute compréhension existentielle de la compréhension ontologique n’est pas forcément vraie ni forcément philosophique. Si elle ne se limite pas à la compréhension d’une détermination existentielle considérée dans sa particularité, la compréhension existentielle implicite de soi de l’existence est une compréhension existentielle de la compréhension ontologique, l’esquisse implicite et le plus souvent impropre d’une compréhension existentiale. A vrai dire, et précisément parce qu’une détermination existentielle est une détermination de l’existence, la compréhension existentielle implicite de soi de l’existence est toujours implicitement une compréhension existentielle de l’essence de l’existence dans sa structure ontologique universelle, c’est-à-dire une compréhension existentielle de la compréhension ontologique. Le savoir philosophique n’est qu’une thématisation, conduite avec le matériel conceptuel exigé et élaboré à cette fin, de cette compréhension existentielle implicite de soi de l’existence, c’est-à-dire de la compréhension ontologique elle-même.

Henry corrects himself in mid-sentence, and the correction places Heidegger. "Existentiell or, better said, existential" (*existentielle ou, pour mieux dire, existentiale*) uses the French pair for *existenziell* and *existenzial*. Henry knows the pair and uses it strictly. Earlier in the book he writes of the "existential analytic" (*analytique existentiale*) of *Sein und Zeit*, and of an "existential" as a structure that belongs to the ontological constitution of Dasein (src L277–287, L401). "Better said" (*pour mieux dire*) does not withdraw the first adjective. Philosophical knowledge is existentiell, a determinate mode of life of the kind ¶5 defined. In content it is existential, an understanding of the structures of existence. The paragraph then shows which of the two weighs more. "Existentiell" (*existentielle*) occurs ten times in it. "Existential" (*existentiale*) occurs twice: once in the correction, and once more as the goal of which implicit self-understanding is "the implicit and most often improper sketch". The closing sentence decides it: "philosophical knowledge is *only* a thematization" (*n'est qu'une thématisation*).

Laid over Heidegger's terms, the paragraph has three tiers where *Sein und Zeit* has two. In *Sein und Zeit* §4 the existential is already ontological. Its structures, the existentials, are the ontological characters of Dasein, and the existential analytic is the way into fundamental ontology. Henry places a tier above it, the "ontological understanding" (*compréhension ontologique*), which is the originary manifestation-of-self of being. He then files the existential below it, as the thematizing of that understanding, which is one existentiell mode among others. The analytic that *Sein und Zeit* offers as the access to ontology becomes, on Henry's arrangement, a mode of life with ontology as its object. The demotion repeats Henry's thesis of §§6–7 that the existential analytic presupposes the essence it was meant to reach. Here it is carried by a single adjective and its correction.

The top tier's name is also chosen. What *Sein und Zeit* calls the pre-ontological understanding of being Henry calls the "ontological understanding of being", without the prefix. The previous reading found the same move against the essay on Hegel, where Heidegger's "pre-ontological" (*vorontologisch*) consciousness became a consciousness that simply *is* ontological. In both places Henry declines to treat the understanding that every consciousness has as an early stage of a later, explicit ontology. "In it resides absolute knowledge": the paragraph's first sentence gives the unthematized understanding the title Hegel kept for the end of the path. The argument depends on this. If philosophy only thematizes an understanding that is already absolute, it can add no truth to it, and the paragraph's "not necessarily true" (*pas forcément vraie*) can only concern the thematization. The ¶9 that follows, with Marx, presses the consequence: understanding, of whatever kind, changes nothing in the reality it understands.

The main commentary reverses the pair. It renders *existentiale* as "existentiell" and then glosses "existential" as *existenziell*. With the French restored, the paragraph says the opposite of what the gloss attributes to it: the correction moves philosophy *toward* Heidegger's ontological term, and the next sentences hold it below the ontological understanding.

## §19 ¶3–4: *für es*, *für sich*, *für uns*, and the irreal

Reads [§19, Paragraphs 3 and 4](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#being-for-itself-from-the-ontological-point-of-view-and-from-the-existential-point-of-view-consciousness-and-truth).

> The unhappy consciousness of Judaism does not represent to itself the unity of essence and existence: this unity is not for it *(für es)*, it is on the contrary for us *(für uns)*, who understand existence in its truth, that is, as the essence. When natural consciousness has risen through the whole series of its experiences, through the experience of the unhappy consciousness, to the philosophical knowledge that is ours, the unity of essence and existence which constitutes the essence of this consciousness will become present to it itself, will no longer be only a unity for us. Consciousness will be brought before its truth. Its truth, namely the unity in it of essence and existence, will be a truth for it *(für sich)*. Yet, as we have just seen, the unity of essence and existence which is for the consciousness that comes to understand its essence *(für sich)*, as it is for us *(für uns)* who move within philosophical knowledge, is no less irreal than the separation of existence and essence which is for the unhappy consciousness, for natural consciousness in general *(für es)*. What is for consciousness, "for it", "for itself", "for us", what it represents to itself in understanding itself in a determinate mode of its life, is always, whatever this mode may be, something irreal. To the irreal separation of essence and existence which is for natural consciousness *(für es)* is opposed, not the unity, itself irreal, of essence and existence which is for us *(für uns)* or for the consciousness that understands itself in its essence *(für sich)*, but only *the real unity of existence and essence*. It matters little in the end whether existence understands or does not understand itself as being the essence: it is the essence, that is, reality. It is the manifestation of self of the essence of manifestation, *the originary being for-itself of being itself*.
>
> Although it seems to bring the problematic into the original dimension of being for-itself, the distinction instituted by Hegel between what is for consciousness and what is for us in fact remains radically foreign to it: *it does not concern the ontological structure of being-for-itself*. The *Phenomenology of Spirit*, which keeps to the description of existence as it is for the consciousness that represents it to itself, whether this consciousness be natural or philosophical, therefore has at no moment to do with reality. The examination of the various modalities within which existence understands itself proceeds in abstraction because, as Hegel himself says, "abstraction is precisely what is not authentically, what only *is for consciousness*". It is true that by "what only is for consciousness" Hegel means what holds for non-philosophical consciousness, which has not yet reached self-knowledge. But, as we have seen, what is for philosophical consciousness *(für sich, für uns)* is no less irreal: the unity of existence and essence that this consciousness represents to itself in understanding itself in its truth is still only an abstract unity. We see more precisely the fundamental ambiguity in the obligation laid on the in-itself to become for-itself when, as is constantly the case in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, the in-itself designates the reality of consciousness, that is, in fact, the originary ontological structure of being-for-itself. The becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness has, strictly speaking, no ontological signification. The essence is wholly contained in the being-in-itself of consciousness, where it has already accomplished itself. The becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness concerns only the manner in which this consciousness understands itself and has, as such, only an existentiell signification. Far from being able to contribute to the realization of spirit, true knowledge is in fact wholly foreign to it, because it stands on a quite different plane from that of reality. *The becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness is not the phenomenology of spirit*. *The phenomenology of spirit, the manifestation of pure manifestation, resides in the being-in-itself of consciousness, which is the originary being-for-itself of being and, as such, the manifestation of pure manifestation, the manifestation of spirit*. The "manifestation of spirit" in true knowledge, what Hegel improperly calls the phenomenology of spirit, is not the manifestation of spirit which constitutes its very essence, the essence of consciousness and of existence in general; it is not reality, but only a representation of it within a determinate act of consciousness. The expression "manifestation of spirit" is therefore fundamentally ambiguous: spirit is as such manifestation. The "manifestation of spirit" is therefore the essence itself; it is the originary and pure ontological element as it is in itself. The "manifestation of spirit" in true knowledge, on the contrary, does not belong to the internal structure of the essence; it is not something ontological. The "manifestation of spirit" in true knowledge is the strict correlate of a determinate representation of consciousness; it is only one "manifestation" among others, which, far from constituting it, presupposes on the contrary the universal and pure essence of manifestation in general. The becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness is a modality of the existentiell self-understanding of existence. But manifestation as such is not the doing of this understanding; it is the doing of the essence. The phenomenology of spirit is an ontological structure.

Henry needs the German because his French cannot keep Hegel's three standpoints apart. French "consciousness" is feminine, so "for it" (*pour elle*) renders Hegel's neuter "for it" (*für es*, consciousness as it experiences). A few lines later the same *pour elle* renders "for itself" (*für sich*): "its truth … will be a truth for it *(für sich)*". Only the parentheses tell the reader which Hegelian term is meant. Henry keeps the German nine times in one paragraph. He does so to register three differences that the main clause will annul, not to dwell on them. The annulling sentence gives the three as one list in quotation marks, "'for it', 'for itself', 'for us'", and assigns them a single predicate: "always … something irreal". The parentheses keep Hegel's distinctions, and the list in quotation marks collapses them.

"Irreal" (*irréel*) is the word that does the collapsing, and it has a technical sense. At §18 ¶1 Henry used it for the noematic correlate of an act (*corrélat noématique irréel*), the object as meant, which is not a real component of the experience. In that sense the paragraph before this one says plainly that irreal does not mean false: the separation is irreal "not because it is false, but because it occurs within representation", as a meaning aimed at by consciousness (src L931). Hegel's "for us" is thus classed as a noematic sense, which is a classification, not a refutation. The paragraphs then let the word slide into its common sense. The *Phenomenology* "has at no moment to do with reality". The unity grasped in philosophical knowledge is "only an abstract unity". The only thing set against all three standpoints is "*the real unity*". The verdict that Hegel's book never touches reality draws on the everyday force of "irreal". Its warrant covers only the technical one. Henry's note 19 promises to ground the distinction of real and irreal later (§31, and §§66–67).

The definition of the in-itself carries the decisive step, and once more it is introduced by "in fact" (*en fait*). The obligation laid on the in-itself to become for-itself is "fundamentally ambiguous" when the in-itself "designates the reality of consciousness, that is, in fact, the originary ontological structure of being-for-itself". With this "that is, in fact" (*c'est-à-dire en fait*), the in-itself of the *Phenomenology*, which is by definition what is *not yet* for itself, becomes for-itself already. From there it follows at once that becoming-for-itself adds nothing ontological. The conclusion is in the definition, as at §17 ¶2 the conclusion was in the "in fact" that made determination equal manifestation. The quotation offered as Hegel's own warrant needs a qualification, and Henry gives it. "Abstraction is precisely what is not authentically, what only *is for consciousness*" comes from the shape of the virtuous consciousness in conflict with the way of the world (note 22). Hegel says it of a non-philosophical standpoint, and Henry extends it to Hegel's "for us" by appeal to what "we have seen".

Typography finishes the argument. The italic title *Phénoménologie de l'Esprit* (with capitals) is set against the roman "phénoménologie de l'esprit" (without them). The italic sentence "*the becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself of consciousness is not the phenomenology of spirit*" means that the book is not what its title names. The second italic sentence gives the title to the ontological structure, and the paragraph's last words restate it: "the phenomenology of spirit is an ontological structure". The chain of hyphens, "becoming-for-itself of the being-in-itself" (*le devenir-pour-soi de l'être-en-soi*), returns four times. It names the dialectic as a single compound noun, a thing that can be classed as "a modality of existentiell self-understanding" and no longer a movement. Henry's argument against the path of the *Phenomenology* is partly carried out in its grammar: verbs of becoming become nouns, and a predicate is attached to the noun.

The passage applies the distinction of §18 ¶5 (see the reading above) to Hegel's own vocabulary. It prepares §20, where what happens "behind the back" of consciousness is shown to be "for us" and hence irreal, and §21, where the absolute is denied a history. The "originary being for-itself of being itself" (*l'être pour-soi originaire de l'être lui-même*) is the first form of the phrase that §§30–31 will fill with content as auto-affection.

## §21 ¶4, H 140: "not the modesty of an obscure *Grund*, … the Word"

Reads [§21, Paragraph 4](../commentaries/henry/henry-the-essence-of-manifestation-commentary.md#the-reaffirmation-of-the-originary-character-of-the-manifestation-of-being-in-the-bringing-into-light-of-its-non-historical-character).

> It is improperly, on the other hand, that one speaks of an equality between "consciousness" and its essence. With the essence that constitutes the ontological structure of being-for-itself and of absolute truth, the "consciousness" that designates the representation of this essence cannot coincide, even in philosophical knowledge, for, as we have seen, *the difference between reality and its representation cannot be suppressed*. Self-consciousness, which Hegel says, against Fichte, arises only after a preliminary process, therefore has strictly speaking no ontological signification; it could neither designate the structure of the essence nor belong to it. The absolute, if one is willing to understand by it the originary being-for-itself of the essence which makes it reality, is not produced in history. It is therefore, once again, the mere representation of the essence that one may refuse to "that enthusiasm which, like a pistol shot, begins immediately with absolute knowledge and disposes of different positions by declaring that it wants to know nothing of them". The absolute that this enthusiasm posits is, in effect, only a position; another position is then opposed to it, notably the one this enthusiasm will reach only after changing itself into its contrary, and that on the path of despair which is the path of consciousness. Absolute knowledge, however, is the doing of this enthusiasm, and that as what allows it to *be*, as that first act of appearing of the originary and pure essence of manifestation which has already accomplished its work for it, at least so as to allow it to manifest itself. History is therefore not the "movement by which appearing comes about", for appearing has already come near us, the self-appearing of appearing has already been produced, and that as the originary upsurge of an effective dimension of phenomenality in which this history can be accomplished and first begin. Transcendental knowledge is not to be acquired; it is a priori. What is at the beginning is not the modesty of an obscure *Grund*; it is the Word. The reality of the absolute is presupposed as the condition of all possible reality in general, as the condition of a history. But the reality of the absolute is its absoluity, its originary presence to itself in the Parousia. The presence of the absolute to itself, the gathering of its essence in its unity with itself, such is the strong nature of the origin itself. *The Parousia occurs at the origin because it constitutes the origin's essence*. Because it constitutes the essence of the origin, the Parousia results from no progress; it is its presupposition. In the Parousia resides the reality of the absolute. Because it finds its reality in the Parousia, the absolute finds it in the origin. The destiny of the absolute is not played out in history.

(The source file reads "Ge qui est au début", a conversion error; the bracket marks the correction.)

The passage answers a progressive verb with a perfect. Henry quotes *H* 140 as "the movement by which appearing comes about" (*mouvement par lequel advient l'apparaître*). Heidegger's German is "that movement of the coming of appearing" (*jene Bewegung des Kommens des Erscheinens*), and it "is the happening" that brings consciousness, shape by shape, into the image of its essence. Henry turns the noun "coming" (*Kommen*) into the verb "comes about" (*advient*) and denies it with three perfects: appearing "has already come near us" (*est d'ores et déjà venu près de nous*), the self-appearing of appearing "has already been produced" (*s'est d'ores et déjà produit*), and the essence "has already accomplished its work" (*a d'ores et déjà accompli son œuvre*). The formula "already" (*d'ores et déjà*) occurs three times in two sentences. "Come near us" answers Heidegger with the Hegel sentence his essay keeps returning to: the absolute "is already, in and for itself, with us" (*an und für sich schon bei uns*), quoted in the essay at its opening and twice more (GA 5 file, lines 7, 61, 129). The essay asks how a way to the absolute is possible if the absolute is already with us. Henry's answer is that it is not, so there is no way.

"Not to be acquired" answers the sentence that follows Henry's quotation at *H* 140. There Heidegger calls the history of consciousness, with Hegel, "the history of the *formation* of consciousness itself into science" (*Bildung*). "Transcendental knowledge is not to be acquired; it is a priori" (*n'est pas à acquérir, il est à priori*) sets a gerund of acquisition, the formation that the *Phenomenology* narrates, against the Kantian term for what is had before all acquisition. Henry recasts Hegel's education of consciousness as the acquiring of something that was never missing.

The pistol shot is turned, not vindicated. Hegel's image of an enthusiasm that "begins immediately with absolute knowledge" is directed against Schelling. Henry first agrees with Hegel: the absolute such an enthusiasm posits "is only a position", open to the counter-position it will reach "on the path of despair which is the path of consciousness". Then "however" (*pourtant*) reverses the scene. Absolute knowledge "is the doing of this enthusiasm", but only "as what allows it to *be*". The italic copula is the point. What the enthusiasm asserts is a representation, refused like every other. What the enthusiasm *is*, an act of appearing, is already absolute knowledge, as natural consciousness was at §17. The enthusiast has no privilege over the Hegelian, and the passage takes neither side in the quarrel between Hegel and Schelling. It moves the absolute from the content of either position to the appearing of both.

The image of the beginning is a pair of nouns from two languages. On one side is "an obscure *Grund*" (*un* Grund *obscur*), the German noun in italics, qualified by "modesty" (*modestie*). On the other is "the Word" (*le Verbe*), the French noun with a capital and the article of the Johannine prologue in French Bibles (*au commencement était le Verbe*). The text gives no note for the *Grund*, and the corpus holds no Schelling to settle whose ground it is. The main commentary's gloss, Schelling's *Ungrund* (groundlessness), names a word Henry does not write. What the wording itself shows is a contrast of strength. The obscure ground is "modest", a poor beginning that needs development, like Hegel's absolute that is "essentially result". The Word belongs to "the strong nature of the origin" (*la forte nature de l'origine*) two sentences later: complete, self-present, gathered "in its unity with itself". A beginning in the dark would make manifestation the outcome of a history. A beginning in the Word makes it the presupposition of one.

The inference the passage rests on is stated in its first sentence. "The difference between reality and its representation cannot be suppressed" (italic), therefore self-consciousness, which arises after a process, "has strictly speaking no ontological signification". The phrase "against Fichte" (*contre Fichte*) takes a side: Hegel's self-consciousness, reached at the end of a process, is set against Fichte's, posited at the start. Henry's rejection of the first is no rejection of the second, and §38 turns to Fichte as the thinker of the internal structure of immanence. The step is weaker than it looks. The premise shows that a *represented* self-consciousness cannot be the essence. It does not show that self-consciousness cannot be anything but represented. That is the question §§30–31 answer, with an auto-affection that is a self-relation without representation, and it is why this passage can only refuse here and assert later. The closing sentence, "the destiny of the absolute is not played out in history", sums up the sub-division and prepares §21 ¶7: "philosophy always comes too late, for what it says was at the beginning."

# Husserl, *Erste Philosophie I*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the lecture of the [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md) that holds the block it extends, and names the block by its place under that lecture ("block 4" is the fourth paragraph). The German is quoted from the Husserliana edition (Hua VII, ed. Rudolf Boehm, 1956) in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-7-erste-philosophie-i-kritische-ideengeschichte.md`): the main text of the lecture course of winter 1923/24, *Kritische Ideengeschichte* (Critical History of Ideas), Lectures 1–27, Hua VII 3–199. The commentary gives no page numbers for the main text. Headings here add the Hua VII page from the running heads in the file, which sit irregularly at the tops and bottoms of pages, so a page can be off by one. Where the editor's textual notes (*Textkritische Anmerkungen*) give a page and line for a word in the passage, that page is used instead.

All English renderings of Husserl are working renderings. They stay close to the German so that its structure shows, and they are not taken from a published translation.

Four cautions about the file affect more than one reading. First, the file was cleaned by hand after its conversion filled blank leaves with invented prose. The cuts left no markers, and in one place real text went with them: about a page of the Twenty-seventh Lecture (c. Hua VII 198) is missing, so the end of the Kant discussion cannot be read here. Smaller faults are noted where they touch a passage: a probable dropped "not" on p. 68, main text absorbed into a footnote in the Second Lecture, and a footnote tail set into the body of the Twenty-sixth. No gap is filled from memory. Second, Husserl's spaced type comes through as italic or, sometimes, bold. Emphasis can be argued from, but the converter may have dropped some, so its absence is weak evidence. Third, much of the file is set line by line as on the printed page, with words broken at line ends ("ent- / deckte"); quotations join the halves. Fourth, the footnote markers are often misnumbered, so which note belongs to which sentence is sometimes uncertain. The edition's textual notes record the manuscript (A), Landgrebe's typescript (B) and Husserl's corrected copies of it (C and D). The readings use them where a variant bears on the wording.

## Lecture 1, Hua VII 3–4: the name as formal foreshadowing

Reads [Lecture 1, blocks 1–2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#first-lecture-on-the-historical-task-of-giving-phenomenology-the-developmental-shape-of-a-first-philosophy).

Husserl takes the title "first philosophy" for what its words say and for nothing it has come to mean. A name coined for a discipline, he argues, carries at its origin only a "formal foreshadowing" (*formale Vorzeichnung*): a sketch of the intention the discipline is to realize, drawn before its content is fixed. Such a sketch can be lifted off the doctrine that first filled it and given to a different science. The course borrows Aristotle's name and nothing else, and the first paragraph is built to show that borrowing the name commits it to nothing else.

> "First philosophy" was, as is well known, introduced by Aristotle as the name of a philosophical discipline, but in the post-Aristotelian period it was displaced by the expression "metaphysics", which came into use by chance. If I take up again the expression Aristotle coined, I draw from its very disuse the very welcome advantage that it awakens in us only its word-for-word meaning, and not the manifold sediments of historical tradition which, as the vague concepts of metaphysics, let memories of the manifold metaphysical systems of the past run together in confusion. This word-for-word sense once served, as is quite understandable in an original terminological coinage, as a formal foreshadowing of the theoretical intention that the new discipline was to realize with its problem-content, which was only to be defined more determinately afterwards. However far the science to which our lectures are to be devoted may depart in its problem-content from that of Aristotle's first philosophy, that formal foreshadowing can serve us too excellently, and therefore we take over the word and attach our first reflections to it.

German:

> ,,Erste Philosophie" ist, wie bekannt, als Name einer philosophischen Disziplin von *Aristoteles* eingeführt, in der nacharistotelischen Zeit aber durch den zufällig in Gebrauch geratenen Ausdruck ,,Metaphysik" verdrängt worden. Wenn ich den von *Aristoteles* geprägten Ausdruck wiederaufnehme, so ziehe ich gerade aus seiner Ungebräuchlichkeit den sehr erwünschten Vorteil, daß er nur die wortwörtliche Bedeutung in uns weckt, und nicht die vielfältigen Sedimente der historischen Überlieferung, die als die vagen Begriffe von Metaphysik Erinnerungen an die mannigfaltigen metaphysischen Systeme der Vorzeit durcheinandergehen lassen. Dieser wortwörtliche Sinn diente dereinst, wie es bei einer ursprünglichen terminologischen Prägung wohlverständlich ist, als formale Vorzeichnung der theoretischen Absicht, welche die neue Disziplin mit ihrem nachher erst bestimmter zu definierenden Problemgehalt verwirklichen sollte. Wie weit auch die Wissenschaft, der unsere Vorlesungen gewidmet sein sollen, in ihrem Problemgehalt von demjenigen der aristotelischen Ersten Philosophie sich entfernt, jene formale Vorzeichnung kann auch uns vortrefflich dienen, und darum übernehmen wir das Wort und knüpfen daran unsere ersten Überlegungen.

The paragraph sets two kinds of word against each other. "First philosophy" was "coined" (*geprägt*), a "terminological coinage" (*terminologische Prägung*) made on purpose by a named author. "Metaphysics" "came into use by chance" (*zufällig in Gebrauch geraten*), a word that befell the tradition and has no author. The coined word keeps a "word-for-word meaning" (*wortwörtliche Bedeutung*); the accidental one gathers "sediments" (*Sedimente*), deposits of tradition that blur into "vague concepts". The same contrast decides the value of disuse. A word nobody uses has gathered nothing, so it "awakens in us only" its literal sense. The verb matters: the literal sense is not defined by Husserl but woken in the hearer, as if it lay in the word ready to be roused. The whole argument depends on that assumption, that a word's literal sense survives intact beneath two thousand years of non-use and can be heard without the history that dropped it.

The second sentence turns the literal sense into a sketch. It "once served" (*diente dereinst*), at the origin, as a foreshadowing "of the theoretical intention" (*der theoretischen Absicht*), and the problem-content was "only to be defined more determinately afterwards" (*nachher erst bestimmter zu definierenden*). The order matters more than the claim about Aristotle: even for him, on Husserl's account, the name came before the doctrine and did not contain it. That lets the third sentence detach the one from the other. However far the new science departs "in its problem-content", the foreshadowing "can serve us too", "and therefore" (*und darum*) "we take over the word". The "therefore" is stronger than the premise. That a sketch is formal shows it can be reused; it does not show that the new filling is the one the sketch intended. The paragraph claims the second while arguing only the first.

The edition's textual notes show Husserl moving away from Aristotle as he revised. The manuscript (A) qualified the departure: the new science departs "in its problem-content, notwithstanding essential commonality" (*unbeschadet wesentlicher Gemeinsamkeit*), and the clause is gone from the printed text (apparatus to Hua VII 3,17, src L7039). A also tied the borrowed foreshadowing more tightly to its source: "that formal foreshadowing which the original word-sense awakens" (4,1), and in the next paragraph it read "word-for-word" where the print has "literal" (4,4). The revision keeps the name and cuts the common ground. The literal sense remains the only bridge to Aristotle, and the commentary's "clean vessel, prized for its emptiness" (L1.1) states the result: the name is valued because it commits to nothing.

The next paragraph reads the sketch out. "First philosophy—what must be read out of the literal sense (*aus dem wörtlichen Sinn*)?" A philosophy first "in itself", from "inner essential grounds" (src L301). Reading 2 follows the step from rank to grounding. The paragraph after that closes the loop: "With these expositions oriented by the word-sense (*Wortsinn*) 'first philosophy' there is given at the same time the first formal foreshadowing for the intention of my lectures" (src L309). The phrase that described Aristotle's coinage now describes Husserl's own course, and it does so for the same reason: the content is still to come. Twenty-five lectures later, the course has not settled its dispute with the other word. The Twenty-seventh Lecture allows that there can be final philosophical truth only after a transcendental grounding, "or, if metaphysics remains the title for it and for what is ultimate in principle, only then can there be an actual metaphysics" (*wenn Metaphysik der Titel für sie … bleibt*, src L3112, Hua VII 194). The word rejected for its sediments returns as an acceptable title once its content has been redefined, which is the operation the first paragraph refused to perform on it.

Two later texts in the corpus bear on the image. Landgrebe's summary of the course (Beilage I) gives Lecture 1 as "Origin of the name. First philosophy as philosophy of the beginning" (src L4122): the name and its reading are the lecture's first two topics. The *Crisis* takes up "sediment" as a concept. The autonomous philosopher must see "that all his self-evidences are prejudices, that all prejudices are unclarities out of a traditional sedimentation" (*Unklarheiten aus einer traditionalen Sedimentierung*, Hua VI §15, 72–73). In 1923 sediments are what a disused word escapes; by the 1930s the philosopher must dig through them, because they are in his own self-evidences and not only in other people's vocabulary. The first paragraph of *Erste Philosophie* trusts that a word can be found clean. The later Husserl no longer trusts that.

## Lecture 1, Hua VII 4: first in the order of grounding

Reads [Lecture 1, block 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#first-lecture-on-the-historical-task-of-giving-phenomenology-the-developmental-shape-of-a-first-philosophy).

"First" in "first philosophy" means first in the order in which a science is built, not first in rank. Husserl derives that order from what a science is: a work made by purposive labour, whose single purpose arranges everything done for it into a sequence. A sequence has a beginning, so philosophy, if it is a science, needs a discipline of its beginning, and that discipline is the first philosophy. The passage reaches this sense by setting aside a rival one, and the way it sets it aside matters for what the course will later mean by "first".

> It can be meant thereby that it is first in value and dignity: carrying within itself, as it were, the holy of holies of philosophy, while the remaining, "second" philosophies would only have to represent the necessary preliminary stages, as it were the antechambers of that holy of holies. But the meaning can also be another, and one that is even nearer to hand on essential grounds. In any case let it be the one to be preferred by us here. Sciences are works that have sprung from purposive labour; unity of a purpose creates, in the rational sequence of the purposive activities belonging to it, unity of order. Each science by itself offers us an endless multiplicity of spiritual formations; we call them truths.

German:

> Es kann dabei gemeint sein, daß sie die erste sei an Wert und Würde: gleichsam das Allerheiligste der Philosophie in sich tragend, während die übrigen, „zweiten“ Philosophien, nur die notwendigen Vorstufen, gleichsam die Vorgemächer jenes Allerheiligsten darzustellen hätten. Die Bedeutung kann aber auch eine andere sein, und eine aus Wesensgründen sogar näherliegende. Jedenfalls sei sie die hier von uns zu bevorzugende. Wissenschaften sind aus zwecktätiger Arbeit entsprungene Werkgebilde; Einheit eines Zweckes schafft in der rationalen Folge zugehöriger Zwecktätigkeiten Einheit der Ordnung. Jede Wissenschaft für sich bietet uns eine endlose Mannigfaltigkeit geistiger Gebilde, wir nennen sie Wahrheiten.

The rejected sense is given only in images, and both are marked as images: "as it were" (*gleichsam*) introduces the "holy of holies" (*Allerheiligste*) and again the "antechambers" (*Vorgemächer*). The figure is a temple, and in a temple "first" comes last: one passes through the antechambers to reach the sanctuary. On the rank reading, the "second" philosophies are "preliminary stages" (*Vorstufen*) on the way up, and first philosophy is where the way ends. Husserl puts "second" in quotation marks because on this reading the numbering runs against the path. The sense he prefers puts first what is first in the sequence of work. The two readings do not just weigh dignity against order. They run the same series in opposite directions.

The step between them is less argued than the commentary's "decisive shift" (L1.4) suggests. The other meaning is "even nearer to hand on essential grounds" (*aus Wesensgründen sogar näherliegende*), a comparative that grants the rank reading some essential ground of its own. Then comes a subjunctive of decision: "In any case let it be the one to be preferred by us here" (*Jedenfalls sei sie die hier von uns zu bevorzugende*). "In any case" (*jedenfalls*) cuts off the comparison before it is finished, and "by us here" limits the choice to this course. Only after the choice does the reason arrive, in the indicative: sciences "are" works sprung from purposive labour. The textual notes show the hedging was added in revision. The manuscript stated the rank reading flatly, "It can thereby be the first" (*Sie kann dabei die erste sein*), where the print has "It can be meant thereby" (apparatus to Hua VII 4,12f., src L7039). The contrastive "but" (*aber*) in "But the meaning can also be another" was inserted in C (4,17). Revision made the first sense a meaning someone might intend and sharpened the turn away from it.

The reason itself is teleological. "Works" (*Werkgebilde*) are formations made by work, and "unity of a purpose creates … unity of order". Truths are the products: "we call them truths", a naming that makes truth a kind of made thing. The next paragraph lifts the order from single truths to inferences, proofs and theories, up to an "ideal all-unity of theory" (src L303), and the one after draws the consequence for philosophy, "insofar as we think of it as a science": it "must have a theoretical beginning", and its name "would then point to a scientific discipline of the beginning" (*Disziplin des Anfangs*, src L307, Hua VII 5). Every step there is conditional (*würde … hindeuten*, would point). The passage has not shown that philosophy is a science; it has shown what first philosophy must be if it is one. The lectures that follow, beginning with Plato, are meant to supply the "if".

The image of the temple does not return, but the numbering does. In the Second Lecture, Plato's dialectic is said to foreshadow "a necessary grounding and articulation of philosophy in two stages, a so-to-speak 'first' and a 'second' philosophy" (src L414, Hua VII 14). The quotation marks are still there, but the series now runs in the order of grounding. First philosophy "goes ahead" (*geht voran*) as "a universal methodology justifying itself absolutely", and the second philosophy is the whole of the factual sciences, which draw their rationality from it (src L416). The "second" philosophies of the First Lecture were antechambers; here they are the applications. Landgrebe's summary records the outcome of the First Lecture in four words: "First philosophy as philosophy of the beginning" (src L4122).

The passage's last turn is about the person, not the discipline. If first philosophy is a discipline of the beginning, the true "beginner" (*Anfänger*) of philosophy is whoever first carries it out in perfect insight. Until that happens, "there is in this sense as yet no beginner of philosophy" (src L307). Once it has happened, there can be beginners in the ordinary sense, apprentices who re-create in their own insight what others thought first. The second half of the course starts from that figure. *Erste Philosophie II* opens its analysis with what is "familiar to the philosophizing beginner, or rather to the philosopher *in statu nascendi*" (Hua VIII 8–9, src L385–386 of that file). The order of grounding that this passage chose for the discipline becomes, in Hua VIII, an order in the life of whoever begins.

## Lecture 1, Hua VII 10: the example and the essence

Reads [Lecture 1, block 22](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#first-lecture-on-the-historical-task-of-giving-phenomenology-the-developmental-shape-of-a-first-philosophy).

What Husserl credits to Socrates here is the passage from a clarified single case to a universal norm. Once a case of the genuine has been seen clearly, it "offers itself" as an example, and seeing it as an example already sees the essence of which it is one. The universal is not gathered from many cases. It is seen in one case whose contingent features have become variable. That is essential intuition, the method of Husserl's own eidetics, and the passage attributes it to Socrates' practice of clarifying opinions. The attribution decides how the history will run, because the whole Platonic discovery of the apriori is made to grow from it.

> Every such clarification at once gains exemplary significance. What comes to be seen, in the individual single case of life, of history, of myth, as the true or genuine itself and as the measure of the unclear mere opinion, offers itself without further ado as an example for something universal. In the pure intuition of essence that sets in as a matter of course—in which everything empirically contingent takes on the character of the extra-essential and freely variable—it is seen as what is essentially genuine überhaupt. In this pure (or apriori) universality it functions as a valid norm for all conceivable single cases of such essence überhaupt.

German:

> Jede solche Klärung gewinnt alsbald exemplarische Bedeutung. Was im individuellen Einzelfall des Lebens, der Geschichte, des Mythos als das Wahre oder Echte selbst und als Maß der unklaren bloßen Meinung zur Erschauung kommt, das bietet sich ohne weiteres als Exempel für ein Allgemeines dar. Es wird in der naturgemäß sich einstellenden reinen Wesensintuition — in der alles empirisch Zufällige den Charakter des Außerwesentlichen und frei Variablen annimmt — als wesensmäßig Echtes überhaupt erschaut. In dieser reinen (oder apriorischen) Allgemeinheit fungiert es als gültige Norm für alle erdenklichen Einzelfälle von solchem Wesen überhaupt.

(*Überhaupt*, "as such" or "in general", is left in German here as in the commentary: it marks the shift from a case to its whole kind.)

Every verb of the passage takes the step away from the one who takes it. A clarification "gains" (*gewinnt*) exemplary significance, "at once" (*alsbald*). The case "offers itself" (*bietet sich … dar*) as an example, "without further ado" (*ohne weiteres*). The intuition of essence "sets in as a matter of course" (*naturgemäß sich einstellend*). The contingent "takes on" (*annimmt*) the character of the freely variable. Nobody varies anything; no Socratic interlocutor is said to compare or generalize. The step from case to essence happens by itself once the case is clear. That is what licenses the attribution. If essential seeing is what clarity naturally turns into, then whoever clarified, as Socrates did, was already seeing essences, whether or not he knew the method.

The wording keeps two claims apart that the attribution merges. "Exemplary significance" belongs to the clarified case: it becomes an "example" (*Exempel*), the Latinate word Husserl uses for the instance that carries an eidos, rather than the everyday *Beispiel*. "Freely variable" (*frei Variablen*) belongs to the method: it is the vocabulary of eidetic variation, in which the contingent features of an example are changed at will to see what stays invariant. Socrates' practice, as the paragraphs before describe it, is clarification: the "beautiful and good itself" set against what was merely opined as beautiful and good (src L377). Free variation is not in that description. It enters in a dash-enclosed clause, the one part of the sentence that describes a procedure rather than a result. The paragraph before this one had marked its translation into modern terms, "expressed in our language" (*in unserer Sprache ausgedrückt*), when it glossed Socratic clarity as "insight" and "evidence" (src L377). This paragraph has no such marker, although its vocabulary is the more technical.

The textual notes show the same caution being added late. The parenthesis in "pure (or apriori) universality" was set in brackets in C (apparatus to Hua VII 10,29, src L7039). The equation of pure universality with the apriori, the word the whole history will turn on, is the revision's gloss. The passage itself, like everything from Hua VII 8 to this sentence, was taken "with slight changes" from Husserl's 1923 essay *Die Idee einer philosophischen Kultur* (The Idea of a Philosophical Culture), as the editor's note says (src L393; apparatus to 8,23–10,31). The essay appeared in a Japanese-German journal as an account of the Greek origin of a philosophical culture, not as a lecture on method. The essay's reprint in the supplementary texts omits this stretch with a row of dots (src L3189), so its earlier wording cannot be compared in the file.

The summary that follows states what the passage made possible, and hedges it. Socrates "first saw the in-itself subsistence of pure and general essences as absolute self-givennesses of a pure intuition of essence" (src L398, Hua VII 11), and he recognized the sense of his method, "expressed in modern terms" (*modern ausgedrückt*), "as intuitive and apriori critique of reason". The next paragraph concedes that all this lacked a scientific form in Socrates and claims only that the "germ forms" (*Keimformen*) were there (src L400). Plato receives the discovery as theory: the Fifth Lecture calls "the discovery of the cognition of ideas" the "greatest and earliest success" of reflection on cognition, the source of pure mathematics as "pure science of ideas" (src L726, Hua VII 34–35). The circle closes in the Nineteenth Lecture. Transcendental consciousness, in its basic forms and achievements, "can and must be considered in the method of *intuition of essence*, that is, as we can say, in the purely grasped *Platonic method*" (*in der rein gefaßten platonischen Methode*, src L2532–2534, Hua VII 139–140). The method read into Socrates in the First Lecture comes back as Plato's name for Husserl's own procedure. The history leads to phenomenology because phenomenology was put into its beginning.

The lectures of 1925 give the method the description the First Lecture withholds. In *Phänomenologische Psychologie* the intuition of the apriori is a "great achievement" of "fictive free choice" (*fingierende Willkür*), and the question is what "remains, in such free variations of a prototype, … as the invariant", without which a thing "would be unthinkable as an example (*Exempel*) of its kind" (Hua IX §9a, c. 72, src L1686–1691 of that file). There the variation is an activity of phantasy, done step by step. In Hua VII it "sets in" by nature. The difference is the measure of what the attribution to Socrates needs: a version of essential seeing in which nobody has to do the varying, so that a moralist in the marketplace could have done it without knowing.

## Lecture 3, Hua VII 19: a logic of consequence, not of truth

Reads [Lecture 3, block 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#third-lecture-the-aristotelian-stoic-traditional-logic-as-a-logic-of-consequence-or-consistency).

Traditional formal logic, Husserl claims, governs only whether judgments can be held together without contradiction, and says nothing about whether they are true. Its laws state the conditions under which someone who has once judged can go on holding those judgments consistently, by their "merely analytic sense". Truth is a further question that this logic never reaches. The claim reframes two thousand years of logic as a lower stage, and it opens the space the rest of the course fills: a logic of truth must turn to the cognizing subject, because only there are judgments brought to the things they are about.

> Essentially, this logic, and the whole logic of the tradition, was not a proper logic of truth but a mere logic of non-contradiction, of concordance, of consequence. More precisely: it was the rational theories propagating themselves through the millennia that made up the core stock of logic, however it otherwise changed, restricted to the formal conditions of the possibility of holding fast consistently to judgments once made according to their merely analytic sense, prior to all asking about their material truth or possibility. Since this is a highly significant distinction, at which Kant's doctrine of analytic thinking already aimed, but which was brought neither by him nor by those after him to the very necessary scientific clarification, I will add a systematic excursus here that should satisfy every need for transparency in principle.

German:

> Im wesentlichen war diese und war die ganze Logik der Tradition nicht eine eigentliche Logik der Wahrheit sondern eine bloße Logik der Widerspruchslosigkeit, der Einstimmigkeit, der Konsequenz. Genauer gesprochen, es waren die durch die Jahrtausende sich fortpflanzenden rationalen Theorien, welche den Kernbestand der wie immer sonst sich abwandelnden Logik ausmachten, eingeschränkt auf die formalen Bedingungen der Möglichkeit, die einmal gefällten Urteile ihrem bloß analytischen Sinn gemäß konsequent festzuhalten, vor allem Fragen nach ihrer sachlichen Wahrheit oder Möglichkeit. Da es sich hier um eine höchst bedeutsame Unterscheidung handelt, auf die schon Kants Lehre vom analytischen Denken zwar abzielte, die aber weder von ihm noch von den späteren zu der sehr nötigen wissenschaftlichen Klärung gebracht worden ist, will ich hier einen systematischen Exkurs folgen lassen, der allen Bedürfnissen nach prinzipieller Durchsichtigkeit genugtun dürfte.

The first sentence denies with one adjective and concedes with another. Traditional logic was "not a proper" (*nicht eine eigentliche*) logic of truth, "but a mere" (*bloße*) logic of non-contradiction. "Proper" leaves room for an improper logic of truth, and the next lecture supplies it: the logic of consequence is "a valuable lower stage of the logic of truth; but still only a lower stage" (*Unterstufe*, src L590, Hua VII 24). The three names that follow are not synonyms piled up for effect. Non-contradiction (*Widerspruchslosigkeit*) is negative, the absence of conflict; concordance (*Einstimmigkeit*) is the positive agreement of judgments with each other; consequence (*Konsequenz*) is the relation of following. The series runs from the weakest condition to the one that carries inference, and the course keeps the last as its name: *Konsequenzlogik* (src L585), "logic of consequence" in the lecture title (src L443).

The "more precisely" sentence says what the restriction is a restriction to, and its verbs are the verbs of a judging life. Judgments are "once made" (*einmal gefällt*); the laws govern how to "hold fast" (*festzuhalten*) to them "consistently" (*konsequent*). Logic is described here not as a theory of propositions but as the norm of someone who has committed himself and must go on in a way that keeps the commitment. The next paragraph builds the excursus on exactly that figure: someone judging "one after another", for whom earlier judgments "go on holding inwardly" (*innerlich fortgelten*, src L473). The final phrase marks the boundary in time as well as in logic: "prior to all asking" (*vor allem Fragen*), with *Fragen* a nominalized infinitive, the activity of asking. Consequence logic works before anyone asks whether what was judged is so. The commentary's "prior to all questions" (L3.8) is right on sense but loses the activity. The point matters because a logic of truth will be a logic of that asking, carried out by a subject who brings judgments to evidence.

The textual notes show that this paragraph was rewritten, and that the rewriting both hardened the verdict and softened the credit to Kant. Pages 19,3–19,36 follow the typescript C. The manuscript (A) said the traditional logic, "considered primarily" (*primär betrachtet*), was not a logic of truth, and that its core theories were "restricted to the formal conditions of the possibility of consistent judgments" (apparatus, src L7045). C dropped "considered primarily", wrote "essentially" (*im wesentlichen*), and added the phrase on which the excursus depends: holding fast to judgments "according to their merely analytic sense, prior to all asking about their material truth". The manuscript had the notion of an "analytic sense of judgment" three pages later, in the excursus, where Husserl says that with it he goes "beyond the tradition" (A's wording at 22,16–18, src L7051). Only in revision was it written into the thesis itself. On Kant, C had said the distinction was one "which Kant already saw and introduced into philosophy"; D reduced this to a distinction "at which Kant's doctrine of analytic thinking already aimed" (*abzielte*, 19,13f., src L7051). Kant moved from discoverer to someone who aimed.

The thesis governs the next four lectures. The Fourth Lecture rests the "separating off" (*Abscheidung*) of a consequence logic on the fact that judgments can be taken as mere "judgment-senses" by "mere making-distinct" (src L596; reading 5). The Seventh Lecture's title names the "full idea of logic—a logic of truth—as a science of the subjectivity that cognizes" (src L936). The path from Aristotle's analytics to the transcendental subject runs through the word *eigentlich*: a proper logic of truth cannot stay with sense; it must ask how sense is fulfilled, and that is a question about consciousness.

Four years later, *Formal and Transcendental Logic* (1929) makes the same cut and claims it as new. "Our distinction between a formal logic of non-contradiction and of truth is something fundamentally new, however well known it is in its words" (FTL §22, Hua XVII c. 76, src L1804 of that file; the file is machine-converted and unreviewed). The old words, Husserl says there, meant the difference between formal problems in general and problems that take material content into account. The claim of novelty is the claim of Hua VII, now made against the tradition's use of its own words: the tradition had the phrase and not the distinction. The 1923 paragraph is less sure on one point. It still credits Kant with having aimed at the distinction, and it took two revisions to arrive at even that much.

## Lecture 4, Hua VII 25–26: analytic making-distinct

Reads [Lecture 4, block 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#fourth-lecture-excursus-on-the-universal-logic-of-consequence-as-analytic-mathematics-the-correlative-treatment-of-formal-ontology-and-the-problem-of-a-logic-of-truth).

"Analytic making-distinct" (*analytische Verdeutlichung*) is the evidence in which a judgment is grasped as what it means, without any turn to the things it is about. It brings out an identical "analytic sense" that can be recognized again in every repetition of the judgment, and it decides nothing about truth. Husserl names it here to fix the two concepts of sense on which the logic of consequence depends: a sense every well-formed judgment has, and a material sense that only intuition can show to be there or missing. The term secures the domain of formal logic by cutting it off from evidence of things.

> Distinguishing terminologically, we can set over against each other *analytic making-distinct*, which brings out the identical "analytic" sense of the assertion, e.g. in the assertion "2 is smaller than 3", as against material clarification or confirmation and the possibility or truth that comes forth in it. Here a wholly different concept of sense designates itself; in particular, in negative speech it is then said, e.g., "2 is greater than 3" "has no sense"; that is, it naturally has an analytic sense, it is a proposition that is wholly and fully distinct according to what would be meant in the judging assertion; but the material sense, possibility and truth, is missing here, as becomes evident in the clarification, in the return to the material intuition of the "2" and "3" and "greater". For the evidence directed at the analytic sense, that of analytic making-distinct, there suffices, we could also say, a merely symbolic, merely verbal judging, which for its part yields nothing at all for possibility and truth, and likewise nothing for necessity or probability of validity, or for their opposite.

German:

> Terminologisch unterscheiden können wir gegenübersetzen *analytische Verdeutlichung*, welche den identischen „analytischen“ Sinn der Aussage herausstellt, z.B. in der Aussage „2 ist kleiner als 3“, gegenüber der sachlichen Klärung oder Bewährung und der darin hervortretenden Möglichkeit oder Wahrheit. Hier bezeichnet sich ein ganz anderer Begriff von Sinn; insbesondere in der negativen Rede heißt es dann z.B. „2 ist größer als 3“, das „hat keinen Sinn“, d.h. es hat natürlich einen analytischen Sinn, es ist ein Satz, der nach dem, was da im urteilenden Aussagen gemeint wäre, ganz und gar deutlich ist; aber der sachliche Sinn, Möglichkeit und Wahrheit, ist hier zu vermissen, wie in der Klärung, im Rückgang zur sachlichen Veranschaulichung des „2“ und „3“ und „größer“ evident wird. Für die auf den analytischen Sinn gerichtete Evidenz, die der analytischen Verdeutlichung, genügt, könnten wir auch sagen, ein bloß symbolisches, bloß verbales Urteilen, das seinerseits gar nichts für Möglichkeit und Wahrheit, ebenso nichts für Notwendigkeit, Wahrscheinlichkeit der Geltung ergibt und für deren Gegenteil.

The two kinds of evidence differ by the preposition of their movement. Making-distinct "brings out" (*herausstellt*) what is already in the judgment; clarification is a "return" (*Rückgang*) to the intuition of the things. The paragraph before had put the same contrast as "passing over" (*übergeht*) to an intuitive presentation, which would be "a making-evident of a wholly different kind and direction" (src L602). Distinctness stays inside the judgment and clarity leaves it. That is why the scare quotes in the opening of the lecture matter. Judgments are said to be grasped by "mere making-distinct" (*„bloße Verdeutlichung“*, src L600), in quotation marks, which point back to the Third Lecture's "making judgments merely 'analytically distinct'" (*„analytisch deutlich“*, src L556) and keep the phrase as Husserl's term, not a description of ordinary clarity of mind. The Fourth Lecture turns the adverb into a name.

"Sense" is the word under strain, and the passage says so: "here a wholly different concept of sense designates itself" (*bezeichnet sich*), as if the word sorted itself out under the example. The Third Lecture had called "sense" an "equivocal expression" (*äquivoken Ausdrucks „Sinn“*, src L566). The example shows the equivocation at work in ordinary speech. "'2 is greater than 3' 'has no sense'" carries two pairs of quotation marks: the first quotes the judgment, the second quotes what people say about it. What people say is wrong in one sense and right in the other. The proposition is "wholly and fully distinct" (*ganz und gar deutlich*), so it has analytic sense; its material sense "is missing" (*ist … zu vermissen*), and that is "evident" only "in the return" to the intuition of 2, 3 and greater. The example is chosen so that the failure is a priori: no fact could make 2 greater than 3, so the missing material sense is a matter of essence, not of luck. Even so, the intuition needed is intuition of numbers and of "greater", which is not sensory. The material clarification of the Fourth Lecture is already the categorial intuition that the Nineteenth Lecture will name an "extension of the idea of intuition" (reading 22).

The last sentence gives the strongest claim and the weakest wording. For analytic evidence, "there suffices, we could also say (*könnten wir auch sagen*), a merely symbolic, merely verbal judging". The conditional "we could also say" offers this as one way of putting it, and the textual notes show Husserl uneasy with it. In his corrected copy he wrote in the margin at this point: "only it must of course be carried out in explicit distinctness" (*nur muß es freilich in expliziter Deutlichkeit vollzogen sein*, apparatus to Hua VII 25,36, src L7051). Merely verbal judging is not enough by itself; it must be articulated step by step. The note opens a gap between two things the printed sentence runs together: words merely read or heard, and words judged through explicitly. The commentary's L4.5 treats symbolic-verbal thinking as what makes consequence logic "so powerful and so portable", without the reservation.

The term organizes the rest of the excursus. "The whole syllogistic, purely grasped, is … 'Analytic'", because consequence and inconsistency concern judgments only "as holdings of analytic making-distinct" (*Bestände analytischer Verdeutlichung*, src L604). And the tradition's fault is stated in its terms: it never "appropriated theoretically" the "double evidence belonging to judging", and so could not separate what belongs to consequence from what belongs to truth (src L606).

*Formal and Transcendental Logic* rebuilds the distinction with the marginal note's correction inside it. §16 is titled "Evidence of clarity and evidence of distinctness", and its first subsection, "Distinctness and confusion" (*Deutlichkeit und Verworrenheit*), sets a third mode below both: the judgment that occurs as "a wholly vague idea" or as the vague meaning of a sentence read and accepted, with no explicit act of judging (FTL §16a, Hua XVII c. 61–62, src L1423–1428 of that file). Merely verbal judging now has a name, confusion, and distinctness is the explicit carrying-out that the 1923 margin required. Hua VII has two evidences, of the sense and of the things. The C note shows a third, the passive reception of words, entering under the first, and FTL makes it a level of its own.

## Lecture 4, Hua VII 30: the judged in judging activity

Reads [Lecture 4, block 23](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#fourth-lecture-excursus-on-the-universal-logic-of-consequence-as-analytic-mathematics-the-correlative-treatment-of-formal-ontology-and-the-problem-of-a-logic-of-truth).

Every judgment, Husserl now insists, is something judged, and it is judged in an activity that belongs to a subject's life. The logic that treats judgments as identical senses and objects as identical substrates has taken one side of a correlation and left the other unexamined: the acts in which senses are meant and truths are seen. Truth and true being are correlates of each other, and the pair has a further correlate in the subjective life where both "give themselves". The passage introduces this second correlation, and with it the course's turn from logic to the cognizing subject.

> If logic in fact, and in carrying out the great intentions of Platonic dialectic, wanted to be a universal and radical methodology for attaining truth, then research could not be directed thematically only at the plane of correlation just designated, truth and true being; another correlative pair, itself standing in correlation with the former, had to become theme. Judgment is what is judged in judging activity, and this is a subjective life. Originally judging truly is a judging that confirms itself in insight, and truly existing objectivity is objectivity giving itself to the experiencing subject in experiencing, or otherwise self-beholding and grasping, lived experience, and determining itself in insightful judging. Objectively true judging is a judging that necessarily confirms itself, or can confirm itself, insightfully for everyone, etc. There is need of a research into judgment and truth, into objects and actuality, not only with regard to judgments as identical assertion-senses and with regard to objects as identical substrate-senses, but also with regard to the subjective of judging, of insight, of intersubjective and definitive self-confirmation, of object-positing and object-experiencing, and therein especially with regard to the subjective modes in which, in cognizing lived experience, in consciousness, all such things as the meant and true object itself, judgment as proposition, and truth give themselves.

German:

> Wenn die Logik in der Tat, und in Auswirkung der großen Intentionen der Platonischen Dialektik, eine universale und radikale Methodenlehre für die Erzielung der Wahrheit sein wollte, dann durfte die Forschung nicht bloß thematisch gerichtet sein auf die vorhin bezeichnete Korrelationsebene Wahrheit und wahres Sein, sondern noch ein anderes korrelatives Paar, selbst mit dem vorigen in Korrelation stehend, mußte zum Thema werden. Urteil ist Geurteiltes im urteilenden Tun, und dieses ist ein subjektives Leben. Ursprünglich wahr urteilen ist in Einsicht sich bestätigendes, und wahrhaft seiende Gegenständlichkeit ist im erfahrenden oder sonstwie selbst erschauenden und erfassenden Erleben dem erfahrenden Subjekt sich gebende Gegenständlichkeit, und sich bestimmende in einsichtigem Urteilen. Objektiv wahres Urteilen ist notwendig für jedermann sich einsichtig bestätigendes oder bestätigen könnendes usw. Es bedarf einer Urteils- und Wahrheitsforschung, einer Gegenstands- und Wirklichkeitsforschung nicht nur in Hinsicht auf Urteile als identische Aussagesinne und in Hinsicht auf Gegenstände als identische Substratsinne, sondern auch in Hinsicht auf das Subjektive des Urteilens, des Einsehens, des intersubjektiv und endgültig sich Bewährens, des Gegenstandsetzens und Gegenstanderfahrens, und dabei insbesondere auf die subjektiven Modi, in welchen sich im erkennenden Erleben, im Bewußtsein alles dergleichen, wie vermeinter und wahrer Gegenstand selbst, Urteil als Satz und Wahrheit, selbst gibt.

*Note on the text.* The file has "ist notwendig" at the fourth sentence. The editor's note on this sentence gives "*Objektiv wahres Urteilen <ist> ein notwendig für jedermann …*", with an editorial supplement in angle brackets and an article the file lacks (apparatus to Hua VII 30,22–24, src L7056). Which wording the printed main text has cannot be settled from the file; the difference does not touch the reading.

The key sentence is a definition by grammar. "Judgment is what is judged" (*Urteil ist Geurteiltes*): a bare noun, then a past participle made into a noun, with no article on either. The judgment is identified with a product, and the product is placed "in judging activity" (*im urteilenden Tun*). The sentence is not passive, as the commentary renders it (L4.23, "Judgment is judged in judging activity"; see the list's Corrections). A passive would say what happens to judgments. The noun predicate says what a judgment is: something that exists as the result of a doing and is found within it. The same construction defined the Stoic *lekton* a lecture earlier, as "the judgment judged in judging (judgment in the noematic sense)" (*des im Urteilen geurteilten Urteils*, src L455, Hua VII 19). There the phrase separated the proposition from the act so that formal logic could have its object. Here the same phrase points back to the act. What made a pure logic of consequence possible becomes the reason it cannot be the whole of logic.

The next clause names the activity's home: "and this is a subjective life" (*und dieses ist ein subjektives Leben*). The demonstrative *dieses* is neuter and takes up the doing (*Tun*), not the judgment. Then the subject of the sentences changes. The first sentence has the noun "judgment" (*Urteil*); the following ones have the verbal noun "judging" (*urteilen*, *Urteilen*): "originally judging truly", "objectively true judging". Logic's object has been turned from the proposition to the act that produces it.

The predicates of those sentences are nearly all reflexive. Judging "confirms itself" (*sich bestätigendes*); objectivity is "giving itself" (*sich gebende*) to the subject and "determining itself" (*sich bestimmende*) in judging; at the end everything "gives itself" (*sich … selbst gibt*) in consciousness. The reflexives do work the commentary's "constituted in the *subjective* accomplishments" (L4.23) passes over. The subject does not confer truth; truth confirms itself in the subject's insight, and objects give themselves in his experience. The turn to subjectivity is stated so that the object remains the agent of its own givenness. The sentences are also elliptical. "Originally judging truly is in insight self-confirming" has no noun after the adjective, and the fourth sentence ends "etc." (*usw.*). This is a lecturer's shorthand, and it shows how far the thesis is from being argued: it is asserted as a series of correlations to be worked out later.

The textual notes show that the intersubjective clauses were added afterwards. "Objectively true judging is … insightfully for everyone …" is a "later addition, only in A", Husserl's pencil in his manuscript after the typescript was made (30,22–24), and so is "of intersubjective and definitive self-confirmation" (30,29f., src L7056). The lecture as first written moved from judgment to the judging subject and its insight. The step from insight to "everyone", which the commentary makes the culmination of the passage ("objectivity grounded in intersubjective re-confirmability"), was pencilled in later and never reached the typescript.

The second correlation shapes the next three lectures. The Fifth Lecture begins with how logic, after Aristotle, settled into the objective dimension once "a piece of subjective-reflective reflection had done its service" (src L630, Hua VII 31). The Seventh Lecture names the missing science a "logic of cognizing" (reading 8). And the course's critical history becomes, from here on, a history of how the "subjective modes" of givenness were glimpsed and missed: by Descartes, who found the subjectivity, and by Locke, who looked at it as a natural thing.

## Lecture 6, Hua VII 40: living in what one does not see

Reads [Lecture 6, blocks 10–11](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#sixth-lecture-the-demand-for-a-theory-of-cognition-implied-in-the-platonic-idea-of-dialectic).

The scientist at work, Husserl says, "lives in" the processes of his cognizing and does not see them. What he sees are their results: the same thing across changing perspectives, the same proposition across repeated assertions, the same correctness in every check. The life that produces these identities is the medium of his seeing and so is not among the things seen. Reflection brings parts of it into view, but only now and then, for a practical purpose, and never as a theme. This hiddenness is what a science of cognizing subjectivity must overcome, and the passage states it as a fact about the structure of cognition, not as a failing of particular scientists.

> Thinking theoretically and carrying out theoretical achievements, he lives in these processes, which he does not himself see in doing so. What he has in view are the results and paths to results that shape themselves in them: the experienced, giving itself as one and the same thing in changing experiencing, in changing subjective views and perspectives; or the judgment, giving itself as an identical one in changing asserting and judging activity, as one and the same proposition, say "2 × 2 = 4", to which one can always come back; then, in exhibiting cognition, the propositions in their adaptation to what is objectively beheld, the character of correctness that comes out identically in every exhibiting, etc. Only when the scientist passes over from this naively performing thinking into the new reflective attitude, which he also needs for the purpose of subjectively justifying what he does, does something step out of the subjective life hidden until then: these or those moments that interest him, of the subjective manner of givenness of his experienced objectivities, of the judgments, of the correctness of these judgments, step into the grasping gaze; but, as we said before, merely case by case, in concrete singularity, and anything but as a theoretical theme.

German:

> Theoretisch denkend und theoretische Leistungen vollziehend, lebt er in diesen Prozessen, die er dabei selbst nicht sieht. Was er im Blick hat, sind die in ihnen sich gestaltenden Ergebnisse und Ergebniswege: das im wechselnden Erfahren, in wechselnden subjektiven Ansichten, Perspektiven als ein und selbiges Ding sich gebende Erfahrene oder das im wechselnden aussagend urteilenden Tun als ein identisches sich gebende Urteil, als ein und derselbe Satz, etwa ,,$2 \times 2 = 4$’’, auf den man immer wieder zurückkommen kann; dann, im ausweisenden Erkennen, die Sätze in ihrer Anpassung an gegenständlich Geschautes, der in jeder Ausweisung sich identisch herausstellende Charakter der Richtigkeit usw. Nur wenn der Wissenschaftler von diesem naiv vollziehenden Denken in die neue reflektive Einstellung übergeht, deren er für den Zweck subjektiver Rechtfertigung seines Tuns auch bedarf, tritt einiges aus dem vordem verborgenen subjektiven Leben, treten diese oder jene ihn interessierenden Momente der subjektiven Gegebenheitsweise seiner Erfahrungsgegenständlichkeiten, der Urteile, der Richtigkeit dieser Urteile in den erfassenden Blick; aber, wie wir vorhin sagten, bloß fallweise, in konkreter Einzelheit, und nichts weniger denn als theoretisches Thema.

*Note on the text.* The file sets the arithmetical example in LaTeX markup ("$2 \times 2 = 4$"); it is quoted as the file has it. The file shows no emphasis in the first sentence. The commentary's italics on "*lives*" and "*not see*" (L6.10) are its own.

The first sentence splits the scientist into two relations to his own activity, and its prepositions carry the split. He lives "in" (*in*) the processes; he has the results "in view" (*im Blick*). To live in something is to be where it happens; to have something in view is to stand over against it. The two cannot hold of the same thing at once: what one lives in is what one sees *through*. The relative clause "which he does not himself see in doing so" (*die er dabei selbst nicht sieht*) is not a reproach. "In doing so" (*dabei*) ties the not-seeing to the doing: while he thinks, the thinking is the one thing not in front of him. The sentence before had said the same of the "living life" (*lebendigen Lebens*) in which the scientist's achieving consists "while it remains hidden from him" (*während es ihm verborgen bleibt*, src L833, Hua VII 39).

The list of results is built from one grammatical form. The thing is "the experienced giving itself as one and the same" (*als ein und selbiges Ding sich gebende Erfahrene*); the proposition is a judgment "giving itself as an identical one" (*als ein identisches sich gebende Urteil*); correctness is a character "coming out identically" (*sich identisch herausstellende*). Each result is the subject of a reflexive participle, set against a "changing" (*wechselnd*) series: changing experiencing, changing perspectives, changing judging activity. Identity is what gives itself; change is what the subject does. The scientist sees the identities and not the changes that present them, and the passage's description of his attention is also the programme of the science he lacks: a study of how the one gives itself in the many. The example "2 × 2 = 4, to which one can always come back" adds time. The proposition is identical because it can be returned to, and the returning is again something done and not seen.

The second sentence grants a way out and limits it at once. Reflection does occur, and the scientist "also needs" it "for the purpose of subjectively justifying what he does". The motive is practical: he checks his work. What comes into view is selected by that interest, "these or those moments that interest him" (*ihn interessierenden*), and arrives "merely case by case, in concrete singularity" (*bloß fallweise, in konkreter Einzelheit*). The final phrase, "anything but as a theoretical theme" (*nichts weniger denn als theoretisches Thema*), is a strong negation, not a "nothing less than" as the commentary renders it (L6.11): the occasional glimpses are as far as can be from a theme. The page before had called them "glancing lights" (*Streiflichter*) on the processes of cognizing subjectivity (src L831). The verb of the main clause, "steps out of" (*tritt … aus*), makes the hidden life itself the agent once more: something steps out when the scientist turns, not because he digs.

The passage locates the hiddenness in the structure of cognition, and that is how the course uses it. The Twelfth Lecture states it as a law of experience: "in experiencing the experienced becomes known, but not the experiencing", and this is "quite natural, since the subjective life that is called lived experience is hidden in its own essence and has never been studied" (src L1503, Hua VII 81–82). The processes of reason, likewise, "are hidden in natural performance, unknown and not themselves cognized again" (src L1499, Hua VII 81). The Twenty-sixth Lecture gives the condition its final name. The transcendental subjectivity of the community of subjects "remained unseen, in the state of naive anonymity" (*im Stande naiver Anonymität*), and phenomenology must make visible "the transcendental subjectivity hidden from itself in naturalness" (*die in der Natürlichkeit sich selbst verborgene*, src L3039, Hua VII 184). The Sixth Lecture's scientist who lives in what he does not see has become a subjectivity hidden from itself. "Naive" links the two: the Sixth Lecture's "naively performing thinking" (*naiv vollziehenden Denken*) is the first use of the adjective for the attitude that the theory of the reduction in *Erste Philosophie II* sets out to suspend.

What the passage contributes at its place is the motive for a science that does not yet exist. The question that follows asks whether a research that makes "cognizing activity überhaupt" its theoretical theme would not give the scientist "a justification of a higher style" (src L833). The occasional reflection of the working scientist is the germ of that science and its opposite: the same turn of the gaze, but driven by a practical interest and confined to single cases.

## Lecture 7, Hua VII 45: a logic of cognizing

Reads [Lecture 7, block 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#seventh-lecture-systematic-sketch-of-the-full-idea-of-logic--a-logic-of-truth--as-a-science-of-the-subjectivity-that-cognizes-and-accomplishes-berhaupt).

Formal logic, however far its idea is widened, is the science of objectivity as such. Husserl postulates its counterpart: a science of the subjectivity that cognizes, standing to formal logic in "necessary correlation". He proposes to call it a "logic of cognizing" (*Logik des Erkennens*), and he defends the name by the double sense of the Greek word behind "logic": *logos* means what is thought and said, but also reason, and reason belongs to the side of the subject. The name claims for the new science the authority of logic while turning logic's gaze around. The passage is hedged at every step, and the hedges show how much of the correlation is still a postulate.

> Expressed otherwise, a logic as rational science of objectivity überhaupt—however far its idea is to be widened (and perhaps even beyond a *mathesis universalis*)—would have as its necessary counterpart a logic of cognizing, a science, and perhaps also a rational science, of cognition-subjectivity überhaupt; both sciences, and perhaps both articulating themselves into groups of single disciplines that belong together, would stand in the relation of necessary correlation. The word "logic" would fit insofar as *logos* indicates not only, in the objective respect, what is cognized, the meaning of an assertion, the true concept and the like, but also reason, and so the subjective side, the side of cognition.

German:

> Anders ausgedrückt, eine Logik als rationale Wissenschaft von der Objektivität überhaupt — wie weit ihre Idee zu erweitern wäre (und vielleicht noch über eine *mathesis universalis* hinaus) — hätte als notwendiges Gegenstück eine Logik des Erkennens, eine Wissenschaft, und vielleicht auch eine rationale Wissenschaft von der Erkenntnissubjektivität überhaupt; beide Wissenschaften, und vielleicht beide in zusammengehörige Gruppen von einzelnen Disziplinen sich gliedernd, ständen im Verhältnis notwendiger Korrelation. Das Wort „Logik“ würde insofern passen, als *Logos* nicht nur in der objektiven Hinsicht das Erkannte, die Aussagebedeutung, den wahren Begriff u.dgl. andeutet, sondern auch Vernunft, und so die subjektive, erkenntnismäßige Seite.

The two sentences make one firm claim and surround it with conditionals. What is firm is the word "necessary", used twice: a "necessary counterpart" (*notwendiges Gegenstück*), a "relation of necessary correlation". Everything else is subjunctive or qualified. Logic "would have" (*hätte*) its counterpart, the two sciences "would stand" (*ständen*) in correlation, the word "would fit" (*würde passen*). "Perhaps" (*vielleicht*) occurs three times: logic may reach "perhaps even beyond" the *mathesis universalis*; the new science is a science, "and perhaps also a rational science"; the two sciences "perhaps" divide into groups of disciplines. The second "perhaps" matters most. A science of cognizing subjectivity is certain; that it can be rational, an a priori science of essences like formal logic and not an empirical psychology, is left open. The rest of the First Section turns on that question: the Eighth Lecture asks why psychology, which took subjectivity as its object, never became such a science (reading 9). The commentary's "a formula Husserl now states with care" (L7.2) catches the care but not the openness: the correlation is necessary, the rationality of its subjective side is not yet secured.

The argument from the word is limited by its own connective. The word "would fit insofar as" (*insofern … als*) *logos* also indicates reason: the fit holds only in this respect. And *logos* "indicates" (*andeutet*) its senses, a weaker verb than "means". The list of objective senses is the list of logic's existing objects ("what is cognized, the meaning of an assertion, the true concept"). Reason stands alone on the other side, followed by "and so" (*und so*), which converts a sense of the word into a side of the correlation. That conversion is the real step. Reason could be taken objectively, as the rational order of things, as easily as subjectively. Husserl takes it as the subject's faculty, and the etymology supports the assignment only because he has already divided the field into an objective and a subjective side. The appeal to Greek does not prove the correlation; it shows that the old name can be stretched to cover it.

The paragraph that follows draws the consequence that marks the new science off from its counterpart. A logic of cognizing must take as its theme the cognizing in which it is itself carried out, so it "relates to itself" (src L944). Objective logic relates to itself too, since every logical law is itself a proposition and falls under the laws of propositions (src L946); the subjective logic's self-reference is the "correlative" of this. The name "logic" therefore brings with it the self-application the lectures will need: the science of cognizing must justify the cognizing through which it is known.

Within Hua VII the name does not return. The Seventh Lecture's title, the editor's, speaks of "a logic of truth" as a science of "the subjectivity that cognizes and achieves" (src L936), and the later lectures speak of "transcendental philosophy" and "phenomenology". *Logos* itself recurs on the objective side. In the Seventeenth Lecture, Locke's chapters on language, thought and knowledge raise "the problematic of the specific *logos*, the formal framework of logical categories that the experienced world must take on" (src L2288, Hua VII 127). The subjective sense that the Seventh Lecture claimed for the word is left unused.

*Formal and Transcendental Logic* begins where this sentence stops. Its §1 is titled "Starting from the meanings of the word *logos*: speech, thinking, what is thought". *Logos* means word and speech, the matter spoken of, the thought proposition, and "in some phrases" the "mental act itself" of asserting; and all these meanings take a "pregnant sense" when "the idea of a norm of reason" enters them, so that "*logos* then means now reason itself, as faculty, but then rational, namely insightful, thinking" (FTL §1, Hua XVII c. 22, src L809–815 of that file, which is machine-converted and unreviewed). The single "and so" of Hua VII becomes a numbered analysis, and the act sense of *logos*, absent from the 1923 list, now stands between the objective senses and reason. The book's subtitle, *Critique of Logical Reason*, keeps the second half of the 1923 justification. The 1923 "logic of cognizing" is the project; the name under which it was carried out became "transcendental logic".

## Lecture 8, Hua VII 53: the standing cross of psychology

Reads [Lecture 8, block 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#eighth-lecture-aristotles-grounding-of-psychology-and-the-basic-problem-of-a-psychology-berhaupt).

Psychology was meant to be the science of the subject, and from Aristotle on it failed at the one task that would have made it so: to bring out consciousness as consciousness *of* something, systematically and by description. Husserl calls psychology a "standing cross" for philosophers because the failure was not an accident of its development but a lack of method present from the start, and because every later attempt to found logic and ethics on psychology inherited it. The elementary fact it missed is intentionality. Husserl calls an analysis of it the "ABC" of soul-life, and the image lays down the standard by which the course will judge Locke and his successors.

> To be sure, as psychology comes onto the scene, it is properly a *standing cross* of philosophical minds. From the beginning it was unable to master precisely the problematic that has accrued to us in the last lectures, in our starting from cognition and the unities of cognition and in connection with the methodological disciplines of logic and then also of ethics. However much one spoke of the faculties of cognizing and practical reason, there was lacking the *method* to bring out *systematically and descriptively*, in the right way, the *act-spheres* to which these faculties after all relate, and therefore *consciousness as consciousness of* something in general, and to get them into a theoretical grip. That, however, was a radical lack, which had to make impossible the development of psychology into the firm shape of a genuine science progressing in rational descriptions and explanations. In every pulse of life, after all, human and animal soul-life is consciousness of this and that. As a whole it is to be characterized as a continuously unitary stream of ever newly shaping consciousness, presenting, judging, feeling, striving, acting consciousness, a consciousness that has exceedingly manifold forms, in which, ever changing according to objects and subjective modes of appearance, on the one hand subjective lived experiences themselves become conscious, such as sense data, feelings, volitions, but on the other hand also, and at one with them, things in space, plants and animals, mythical powers, gods or demons, manifold cultural shapes, socialities, values, goods, ends, etc. How could a *psychology* come onto the right path without penetrating to a systematic *elementary analysis of consciousness as consciousness of something*, as, so to speak, the ABC of soul-life!

German:

> Freilich so, wie die Psychologie auf den Plan tritt, ist sie eigentlich ein *beständiges Kreuz* der philosophischen Geister. Von Anfang an vermochte sie sich gerade der Problematik nicht zu bemächtigen, welche uns in den letzten Vorlesungen in unserem Ausgang von der Erkenntnis und den Erkenntniseinheiten und im Zusammenhang mit den methodologischen Disziplinen der Logik und dann auch der Ethik zugewachsen ist. Es fehlte, so viel man von den Vermögen erkennender und praktischer Vernunft auch sprach, an der *Methode*, die *Aktsphären*, auf welche sich diese Vermögen doch beziehen, und demnach überhaupt das *Bewußtsein als Bewußtsein von* etwas in richtiger Weise *systematisch deskriptiv* *herauszustellen* und in einen theoretischen Griff zu bekommen. Das war aber ein radikaler Mangel, der die Entwicklung der Psychologie zur festen Gestalt einer echten, in rationalen Beschreibungen und Erklärungen schreitenden Wissenschaft unmöglich machen mußte. In jedem Lebenspuls ist doch menschliches und tierisches Seelenleben Bewußtsein von dem und jenem. Als ganzes ist es zu charakterisieren als kontinuierlich einheitlicher Strom sich immer neu gestaltender Bewußtseins, vorstellenden, urteilenden, fühlenden, strebenden, handelnden Bewußtseins, eines Bewußtseins, das überaus mannigfache Formen hat, in dem, immerfort wechselnd nach Gegenständen und subjektiven Erscheinungsweisen, einerseits subjektive Erlebnisse selbst bewußt werden, wie Empfindungsdaten, Gefühle, Wollungen, andererseits aber auch, und in eins damit, Dinge im Raum, Pflanzen und Tiere, mythische Mächte, Götter oder Dämonen, mannigfaltige Kulturgestalten, Sozialitäten, Werte, Güter, Zwecke usw. Wie könnte eine *Psychologie* auf die rechte Bahn kommen, ohne zu einer systematischen *Elementaranalyse des Bewußtseins als Bewußtseins von etwas* durchzudringen, als gewissermaßen dem ABC des Seelenlebens!

*Note on the text.* The file reads "Strom sich immer neu gestaltender Bewußtseins", where the grammar wants "gestaltenden" (a stream of ever newly shaping consciousness). It is quoted as the file has it; the slip does not touch the reading.

"Cross" (*Kreuz*) is the colloquial German for an affliction one has to bear, and "standing" (*beständig*) makes it permanent. The image is not of an error to be corrected once but of a burden that philosophy carries whenever it relies on psychology. "Properly" (*eigentlich*) says the burden is psychology's real character, behind its success as one science among others. The verdict is placed at the moment of psychology's founding, "as it comes onto the scene" (*so, wie … auf den Plan tritt*), and the next sentence repeats the timing: "from the beginning" (*von Anfang an*). The lack is original.

What was lacking is stated with the file's densest run of emphasis in the lecture: the *method*, the *act-spheres*, *consciousness as consciousness of*, *systematically descriptively*, *to bring out*. The emphasized words form a programme. The faculties (*Vermögen*) of reason were much talked about; the acts to which faculties "after all" (*doch*) relate were not described. Husserl's complaint is that a faculty psychology names powers and skips what they do. The connective "and therefore" (*und demnach*) then widens the act-spheres to "consciousness as consciousness of something überhaupt". Intentionality is introduced not as a discovery about some acts but as what any description of acts would have met. The commentary marks this as the first full discussion of intentionality (L8.3); its formula is here tied from the start to method. What psychology lacked was not the concept but a way of bringing it "into a theoretical grip".

The catalogue that follows shows why the grip is hard to get. Consciousness is a "stream" of presenting, judging, feeling, striving, acting, and in it "on the one hand" subjective experiences become conscious, sense data, feelings, volitions, "but on the other hand also, and at one with them" (*und in eins damit*), things in space, plants, animals, "mythical powers, gods or demons", cultural shapes, values. The list deliberately includes objects that do not exist. Gods and demons are as much what consciousness is "of" as plants are. The "of" in "consciousness of" is therefore not a real relation between two existing things, a point the Fifteenth Lecture makes explicit against every assertion of relation between existents (reading 18). The passage plants it in a list.

The closing rhetorical question names the analysis "so to speak, the ABC of soul-life" (*gewissermaßen dem ABC des Seelenlebens*). An ABC is elementary in two senses: it is learned first, and everything written is composed from its letters. The image commits the science of consciousness to both: an analysis of elements, and one that must come before any explanatory psychology. The image recurs at two turning points of the Locke lectures. In the Fourteenth, Locke's "significant thought" is that if one could exhibit all elementary ideas in pure consciousness, one would have "the ABC of elementary ideas and legitimate concepts" (src L1734, Hua VII 99). Locke has the image but fills it with sensory data. In the Seventeenth, the ABC is placed where the Eighth Lecture demanded it: "Phenomenological elementary analyses and descriptions create the ABC" for psychology, and the study of this ABC and of the forms to be built from it *a priori* "makes up a whole science, and an apriori one at that", which "with necessity" precedes every empirical psychology (src L2269, Hua VII 126; reading 20). The ABC that Aristotle's psychology lacked, and Locke misspelled, turns out to be a priori. That answers the question left open in the Seventh Lecture, whether the science of cognizing subjectivity could be a rational science (reading 8).

The verdict's scope is set by the next paragraph. The lack does not interest Husserl "as a mere lack" of one objective science, but because psychology claims to ground logic and ethics (src L1142–1144). The standing cross falls on the philosophers, not on the psychologists: it is philosophy that must bear the consequences of founding its norms on a science that never learned its ABC.

## Lecture 9, Hua VII 60: the world considered "transcendentally"

Reads [Lecture 9, block 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#ninth-lecture-skepticism--the-fundamental-significance-of-its-immortality-in-the-history-of-philosophy-the-decisive-step-of-descartes).

To consider the world "transcendentally", Husserl says, is to consider it as a possible object of consciousness, purely in its relation to the subjectivity for which it is there, and to consider that subjectivity purely as what gives every object its content and sense. The passage defines the term without using Kant and without naming any discipline. It locates its first appearance in the Sophists, who did not want it: their paradoxes made the naive pre-givenness of the world into a problem for the first time. Transcendental philosophy begins, on this account, as an unintended consequence of skepticism.

> In such ingenious paradoxes, in skeptical argumentations of which one does not rightly know how far they are really meant in earnest, there enters, still in primitive and vague form, a wholly new motive of most universal significance into the philosophical consciousness of humanity. For the first time the naive pre-givenness of the world becomes problematic, and from there the world itself, as to the possibility in principle of its cognition and the sense in principle of its being-in-itself. Expressed otherwise: for the first time the real universe, and in later sequence the totality of possible objectivity überhaupt, is considered "transcendentally", as object of possible cognition, of possible consciousness überhaupt. It is considered in relation to the subjectivity for which it is to be able to be there in the manner of consciousness, and purely in this relation: that is, the subjectivity too is considered purely as exercising transcendental functions, and its consciousness, the transcendental function itself, as that in which or through which all conceivable objects as such receive, for a subject of consciousness, whatever content and sense they are to be able to have for this subject.

German:

> In dergestalt geistreichen Paradoxien, in skeptischen Argumentationen, von denen man nicht recht weiß, inwieweit sie wirklich ernst gemeint sind, tritt, noch in primitiver und vager Form, ein völlig neues Motiv von universalster Bedeutung in das philosophische Bewußtsein der Menschheit. Zum ersten Male wird die naive Vorgegebenheit der Welt problematisch, und von da her sie selbst nach prinzipieller Möglichkeit ihrer Erkenntnis und nach dem prinzipiellen Sinn ihres Ansichseins. Anders ausgedrückt: zum ersten Mal wird das reale Weltall und wird in späterer Folge die Allheit möglicher Objektivität überhaupt „transzendental“ betrachtet, als Gegenstand möglicher Erkenntnis, möglichen Bewußtseins überhaupt. Es wird betrachtet in Beziehung auf die Subjektivität, für die es bewußtseinsmäßig soll dasein können, und rein in dieser Beziehung: d.i., auch die Subjektivität wird rein als solche transzendentalen Funktionen übend betrachtet, und ihr Bewußtsein, die transzendentale Funktion selbst, als dasjenige, in dem oder wodurch alle erdenklichen Objekte als solche für ein Bewußtseinssubjekt jedweden Gehalt und Sinn erhalten, den sie für dieses Subjekt sollen haben können.

The motive "enters" (*tritt … in*) consciousness; nobody introduces it. The Sophists are named only through their products, "ingenious paradoxes" (*geistreichen Paradoxien*) and arguments "of which one does not rightly know how far they are really meant in earnest". The clause is a hedge about intention, and it frees the history from needing one. Whether Gorgias meant his argument or played with it, its content outran him. This is the pattern of the whole course: an idea is credited to a figure who did not grasp it, and its "sense" is read off later. The Sophists receive the most extreme version, since they meant to destroy what they founded. The commentary's "the reader should weigh how strong the claim is" (L9.4) can be answered from the wording: the claim is strong about the idea and weak about the people.

The definition comes in two steps marked by "for the first time" (*zum ersten Male*), said twice; the file shows no emphasis on either. First the world's "naive pre-givenness" (*naive Vorgegebenheit*) becomes problematic, and "from there" (*von da her*) the world itself, as to the possibility of its cognition and the sense of its being-in-itself. The order matters. What is questioned first is not the world's existence but its being given in advance, before any question. Only through that does the world's being-in-itself become a question of "sense" (*Sinn*). This is the course's version of the skeptical problem: not whether the world exists but what "existing in itself" can mean for a world that is there only as pre-given to someone. Then "expressed otherwise" (*anders ausgedrückt*) introduces the word "transcendental" in quotation marks. The quotation marks are the only distancing marks in the passage, and they mark a term Husserl is about to define in his own way. The definition is entirely relational: the universe "as object of possible consciousness", "in relation to the subjectivity for which it is to be able to be there", "and purely in this relation". "Purely" (*rein*) excludes every other consideration of the world, as nature, as cause, as fact.

The second half turns the relation around, and its "too" (*auch*) shows that the definition is a correlation, not a one-sided reduction. "The subjectivity too is considered purely": not as a soul in the world, but as "exercising transcendental functions". Its consciousness is "the transcendental function itself", that "in which or through which" (*in dem oder wodurch*) objects receive "whatever content and sense they are to be able to have". The alternative "in which or through which" leaves open whether consciousness is the place of sense or its means; the later lectures speak of constitution "in" consciousness. The modal verbs keep the claim formal: the world "is to be able to be there" (*soll dasein können*), objects "are to be able to have" (*sollen haben können*) sense. Nothing in the definition says what consciousness does to give sense. It says only that whatever sense an object can have, it has for a subject.

The passage needs this definition at its point in the history because the next lectures measure Descartes by it. The following paragraph says that in antiquity "precisely this transcendental impulse" of the skepsis "did not come to effect" (src L1194–1195), and the Ninth Lecture then makes the modern age a working-out of it: modern philosophy has the task "to make true in a higher sense the radical subjectivism of the skeptical tradition", to overcome a "frivolous" subjectivism by a serious one, "in short, by the transcendental subjectivism" (src L1226, Hua VII 61–62). The formula anticipates the Twentieth Lecture's thesis that to overcome skepticism is to "make it true in a good sense" (reading 24). The First Lecture had already announced the line from the other end: Descartes' meditations are the source of "the tendency, running through the whole modern age, to reshape all philosophy into a transcendental philosophy" (src L363, Hua VII 8). Between the two the Ninth Lecture supplies what "transcendental" will mean.

The quotation marks return once more in the course, around a phrase Husserl rejects. When the nineteenth century spoke of "transcendental method" (*„transzendentaler Methode“*, src L3079), it meant the regressive search for conditions of possibility (reading 30). The Ninth Lecture's "transcendentally" is the other sense, the one Husserl claims: not a method for deducing conditions but a way of considering everything as there for consciousness. The two pairs of quotation marks bracket the course's quarrel with Kant's heirs over the word.

## Lecture 10, Hua VII 63–64: Descartes as Columbus

Reads [Lecture 10, block 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#tenth-lecture-the-cartesian-meditations).

Descartes, Husserl says, made the discovery without which no transcendental philosophy could begin, and did not know what he had found. He found pure subjectivity, self-enclosed and certain of itself, and took it for a new means to an old end: a secure foundation for the objective sciences and their world. Husserl's image is Columbus, who reached a new continent and believed he had found a new sea route to the old India. The verdict separates discovery from understanding, and it traces the failure to a specific lack: Descartes had not learned from the skeptics what the problem was.

> In the first two and most important of the six Meditations there lies, to be sure, a great discovery, and precisely the one that first had to be made so that a transcendental philosophy could begin: namely the discovery of the transcendentally pure subjectivity, absolutely closed in itself, which can become aware of itself at any time, in absolute indubitability. But of the proper sense of this discovery Descartes himself could not take possession. Behind the apparent triviality of its all-too-familiar pronouncement *ego cogito, ego sum* there open in fact all-too-great and dark depths. It went with Descartes as with Columbus, who discovered the new continent but knew nothing of it and believed he had merely discovered a new sea route to the old India. In Descartes' case this lay in his not grasping the deepest sense of the problem of a philosophy to be grounded anew and radically, or, what is essentially one with it, the genuine sense of a transcendental grounding of cognition and science rooted in the *ego cogito*. That, however, again has its ground in his not having gone to school with the skepsis in the right way.

German:

> In den beiden ersten und wichtigsten der sechs Meditationen liegt zwar eine große Entdeckung, und gerade diejenige, die allererst gemacht sein mußte, damit eine Transzendentalphilosophie beginnen konnte: nämlich die Entdeckung der transzendental reinen, in sich absolut geschlossenen Subjektivität, die ihrer selbst jederzeit innewerden kann, in absoluter Zweifellosigkeit. Aber des eigentlichen Sinnes dieser Entdeckung konnte sich Descartes selbst nicht bemächtigen. Hinter der scheinbaren Trivialität ihres allbekannten Ausspruches *ego cogito, ego sum* eröffnen sich in der Tat allzu große und dunkle Tiefen. Es ging Descartes wie Columbus, der den neuen Kontinent entdeckte, aber davon nichts wußte und bloß einen neuen Seeweg nach dem alten Indien entdeckt zu haben meinte. Das lag bei Descartes daran, daß er den tiefsten Sinn des Problems einer neu und radikal zu begründenden Philosophie nicht erfaßte, oder, was im wesentlichen damit eins ist, den echten Sinn einer im *ego cogito* verwurzelten transzendentalen Erkenntnis- und Wissenschaftsbegründung. Das aber hat wieder seinen Grund darin, daß er nicht in der rechten Weise bei der Skepsis in die Lehre gegangen war.

*Note on the text.* The file breaks "ent- / deckte" across a page break (Hua VII 63/64, src L1287–1290); the sentence reads on intact.

The passage turns on the difference between finding something and taking possession of it. The concessive "to be sure" (*zwar*) grants the discovery in full, and in Husserl's own terms: "transcendentally pure" subjectivity, "absolutely closed in itself", aware of itself "in absolute indubitability". Descartes is given the words of the Ninth Lecture's definition (reading 10) before he is denied their sense. "But of the proper sense of this discovery Descartes himself could not take possession" (*konnte sich … nicht bemächtigen*). The verb is the one used of psychology two lectures earlier, which "was unable to master" (*vermochte sich … nicht zu bemächtigen*) the problematic of cognition (reading 9). Landgrebe's summary takes the sentence as the lecture's heading: "Descartes could not take possession of the sense of his discovery" (src L4155). The failure is not of seeing but of appropriating: the thing was in his hands, its sense was not.

The Columbus image maps the failure precisely. Columbus "discovered" (*entdeckte*) the continent, "but knew nothing of it" (*aber davon nichts wußte*), and "believed" (*meinte*) he had found something else. What he believed he had found has two parts, and the image depends on both: a "new sea route" (*neuen Seeweg*) to the "old India" (*alten Indien*). The route is new and the destination old. For Descartes the ego is the new route; the destination is what philosophy had always wanted, a secure knowledge of the objective world, of God and of nature. The recapitulation that follows shows the route being travelled: from the certain *sum cogitans* Descartes asks what entitles a merely subjective evidence to count as a criterion of truth beyond subjective life, and what could justify belief in an "allegedly extra-subjective world" (src L1300, Hua VII 65). The ego is used to reach the old world, not explored as the new one. "Merely" (*bloß*) carries the judgment: what was a continent was taken for a passage.

The image also limits the blame. Columbus is not a fool; nobody could have known the continent was there. "Apparent triviality" (*scheinbaren Trivialität*) and "all-too-great and dark depths" (*allzu große und dunkle Tiefen*) say the same of the *cogito*: its familiarity hides its scope, and the depths are dark, not merely unnoticed. But the last two sentences give a reason after all, in a chain of grounds marked by its connectives: "this lay in" (*das lag … daran*) his not grasping the problem of a radically grounded philosophy, and "that, however, again has its ground in" (*das aber hat wieder seinen Grund darin*) his not having "gone to school with the skepsis in the right way" (*nicht in der rechten Weise bei der Skepsis in die Lehre gegangen*). *In die Lehre gehen* is the idiom of apprenticeship to a master. The master is the skeptic. Descartes used skeptical doubt as a tool, a means to certainty, and did not learn what the Sophists had, against their will, made visible: that the world's being is a question of its sense for consciousness (reading 10). Had he learned it, the ego would not have been a route to the world but the place where the world's sense is to be clarified. "In the right way" concedes that he was in some way a pupil of the skeptics: his doubt is theirs; his lesson was the wrong one.

The verdict governs the rest of the First Section. The Eleventh Lecture gives the image a second form: Descartes "remained standing before the gate" of transcendental philosophy (reading 13), and Locke's "displacement" of the ego into a soul in the world follows from the Cartesian misunderstanding (reading 14). The Twentieth Lecture states the positive counterpart of the apprenticeship: to overcome skepticism is to "make it true in a good sense" (reading 24).

*Cartesian Meditations* (1929/31) keeps the verdict and changes its reason. §10 is titled "Descartes' failure to make the transcendental turn" (*Descartes' Verfehlen der transzendentalen Wendung*). There the failure is traced to "how much Scholasticism" lies hidden in the Meditations, following Gilson and Koyré, and to the prejudice, born of admiration for mathematical natural science, that the *ego cogito* is an apodictic axiom for a deductive world-science, or that in the pure ego one had saved "a little end of the world" (*ein kleines Endchen der Welt*, Hua I 63, src L1498–1520 of that file). The 1929 Descartes misreads his discovery because of what he brought to it from science and tradition. The 1923 Descartes misreads it because of what he failed to take from the skeptics. Both are versions of Columbus's mistake, the old India of a deductive science of the world; Hua VII alone names skepticism as the teacher he did not follow.

## Lecture 10, Hua VII 68: objectivity constituting itself in a "having"

Reads [Lecture 10, block 10](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#tenth-lecture-the-cartesian-meditations).

Transcendental research, as Husserl defines its task here, does not determine objects that are already given; it asks how anything comes to be given at all. Objectivity "constitutes itself" (*sich … konstituiert*) "for the cognizer and in his cognizing 'having'": the object, and the very fact of its being there in advance, is an achievement of conscious life, beginning with the plainest perception. This is the first use of the verb "constitute" in the main text of the course, and it arrives as the statement of a task, not as a doctrine. The passage also requires that the new research set aside the pre-givenness that objective research relies on; the file's text of that requirement is damaged.

> Only the Cartesian bringing-out of pure subjectivity, and thereby of the connection of consciousness to be considered purely in itself, in its immanent closedness, made it possible to keep the sense of this task unconfused as against the task of all objective research. If the latter wants to determine theoretically objects pre-given to the cognizer, the transcendental research that has now become necessary wants something so very different that it may in principle let a having-pre-given, a sheer being-there of objects, **count**. Its task, after all, consists in investigating, generally and in every kind and stage, how in cognition objectivity as such and objectivity of every category constitutes itself as such subjectively, for the cognizer and in his cognizing "having": how cognition, then, already as the plainest perception, achieves pre-givenness of this and that object, and how it thereupon carries out higher achievements of cognition.

German:

> Erst die Cartesianische Herausstellung der reinen Subjektivität und damit des rein in sich, in seiner immanenten Abgeschlossenheit zu betrachtenden Zusammenhangs des Bewußtseins ermöglichte es, den Sinn dieser Aufgabe unverwirrt zu erhalten gegenüber der Aufgabe aller objektiven Forschung. Will diese dem Erkennenden vorgegebene Gegenstände theoretisch bestimmen, so will die jetzt notwendig gewordene transzendentale Forschung so sehr anderes, daß sie ein Vorgegeben-haben, ein Schlechthin-da-sein von Objekten prinzipiell **gelten** lassen darf. Ihre Aufgabe besteht ja darin, allgemein und in jeder Art und Stufe zu erforschen, wie in der Erkenntnis Objektivität als solche und Objektivität jeder Kategorie sich als solche subjektiv, für den Erkennenden und in seinem erkennenden ,,Haben'', konstituiert: wie also Erkenntnis schon als schlichteste Wahrnehmung Vorgegebenheit von dem und jenem Objekte leistet und wie sie daraufhin höhere Erkenntnisleistungen vollzieht.

*Note on the text.* The second sentence, as the file has it, says that transcendental research "may in principle let … count" (*prinzipiell* ***gelten*** *lassen darf*) a having-pre-given. Its own "so very different" (*so sehr anderes*) and the task stated in the next sentence require the opposite: research that wants something so different from objective research that it may *not* let the pre-givenness of objects count. A "not" (*nicht*) has very probably dropped out, and the bold on "gelten" may be a misplaced rendering of spaced type that covered the negation as well. A whole-file search finds the phrase only here, and the editor's textual notes have no entry for this line. The translation above keeps the file's wording. This reading does not argue from the emphasis or from the exact form of the negation, and it quotes the clause only for what its context secures: that the research sets aside, in principle, the pre-givenness of objects. The print must be checked before the clause is cited. The editor's notes do record a C insertion at 68,16f. whose lemma reads "als solche und für den Erkennenden und in seinem erkennenden 'Haben'", where the file has "als solche subjektiv, für den Erkennenden"; which is the printed wording cannot be told from the file.

The defining sentence has an unusual subject for a verb of making. Not the subject but "objectivity as such and objectivity of every category" is what "constitutes itself" (*sich … konstituiert*). The reflexive keeps the grammar of the earlier lectures, in which judgments "confirm themselves" and objects "give themselves" (readings 6 and 7). Constitution is not something the cognizer does to the object; it is something the object undergoes "for" (*für*) him and "in" (*in*) his having. The two prepositions divide the work. "For the cognizer" says whose objectivity it is; "in his cognizing having" says where it is achieved. And "subjectively" (*subjektiv*) qualifies how it constitutes itself, not what it is: objectivity "as such" constitutes itself subjectively. The commentary's warning against a "fabrication-reading" (L10.10) is carried by the wording: the verb never has the subject as its agent.

"Having" (*Haben*) is in quotation marks, and the quotation marks point back. The preceding paragraph had said that no science before had made its theme how cognizing subjectivity "comes to this having" (*zu diesem Haben kommt*), "for it has only what it achieves in itself" (*sie hat nur, was sie in sich leistet*, src L1319). The Sixth Lecture had put the same thought in a play of participles: what the cognizer has, he has "only as the had of his having, as the experienced of his experiencing" (src L841, Hua VII 41). The quotation marks set the ordinary verb apart as a term. Having an object is not a relation between two things already there, but an achievement whose structure is to be described. The colon then names the two levels of that achievement: "already as the plainest perception" (*schon als schlichteste Wahrnehmung*) cognition "achieves pre-givenness" (*Vorgegebenheit … leistet*), and "thereupon" (*daraufhin*) carries out higher achievements. Pre-givenness itself is achieved. This is the point the damaged clause turns on. Objective research takes the object's being there in advance as its starting point; transcendental research asks how that being-there-in-advance is produced, and so cannot use it as a premise.

The hyphenated coinages carry that refusal. "A having-pre-given" (*ein Vorgegeben-haben*) and "a sheer being-there" (*ein Schlechthin-da-sein*) are nominalizations made for the occasion. The hyphens turn the ordinary attitude into an object of reference: what the natural attitude simply does, having things given and finding them there, becomes a named thing that one can let count or not. The coinages are the grammatical form of a suspension. The commentary hears in the clause "the seed of the *epoché*" (L10.10), and the wording supports the hearing, with one caution: the clause speaks of what the research "may" (*darf*) let count, a permission or prohibition of method, not yet a named operation. The method itself, bracketing and reduction, is the work of *Erste Philosophie II*.

The passage's first word limits Descartes' share. "Only" (*erst*) the Cartesian bringing-out of pure subjectivity made it possible to keep this task "unconfused". Descartes is credited with the condition of the task, not with the task, which, by the previous lecture, he did not grasp (reading 11). The next paragraph adds that the Cartesian discovery and its method are still "essentially to be purified" (*wesentlich zu reinigende*, src L1351). The course's later critique of Locke uses the passage's own term against him. The Sixteenth Lecture says that it "never occurs" to Locke and his successors "to take this having seriously into view once and to describe it as what it essentially is" (*dieses Haben einmal ernstlich in Augenschein zu nehmen*, src L2024, Hua VII 111). The natural scientist rightly looks only at what he has; the theorist of knowledge who does the same has missed the "having" that the Tenth Lecture made the theme. The Twelfth Lecture states the positive thesis that follows: genuine cognition is to "constitute" objectivity for the cognizer, so that objectivity can have "its site nowhere else" than in consciousness (reading 15).

## Lecture 11, Hua VII 73: before the gate, outside the realm of the Mothers

Reads [Lecture 11, block 7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#eleventh-lecture-first-real-prospect-of-transcendental-science-transition-from-the-cartesian-meditations-to-locke).

Descartes opened the way into transcendental philosophy and stopped at its threshold. Husserl gives the field he did not enter a borrowed name, Goethe's "realm of the Mothers" (*Reich der Mütter*) from the second part of *Faust*, and glosses it in the next sentence as "the primal grounds of all cognition in transcendental subjectivity". The image places transcendental subjectivity not as one more region of objects but as the origin of all of them, a realm one goes down into. It also states the charge exactly: not that Descartes failed to find the entrance, but that, having opened it, he turned back to the objective world.

> Descartes remained standing before the gate, opened by himself, of transcendental philosophy, the only truly radical philosophy; the passage into the never-trodden but very well to-be-trodden "realm of the Mothers" he did not undertake. His philosophical radicalism failed. His conviction that one must go back to the primal grounds of all cognition in transcendental subjectivity did not bring the right fruit for him and for the time after, because he was precisely unable to do justice to the deeper sense of such a radicalism. He misunderstands his own good beginning, because he does not drive the clarifying reflection on to the fulfilling end. Therefore he at once succumbs to problems that would otherwise have been recognizable to him as countersensical.

German:

> Descartes ist vor der von ihm eröffneten Pforte der transzendentalen Philosophie, der allein wahrhaft radikalen Philosophie, stehen geblieben; den Gang in das nie betretene aber sehr wohl zu betretende „Reich der Mütter“ hat er nicht angetreten. Sein philosophischer Radikalismus versagte. Seine Überzeugung, man müsse auf die Urgründe aller Erkenntnis in der transzendentalen Subjektivität zurückgehen, brachte für ihn und die Folgezeit nicht die rechte Frucht, weil er es eben nicht vermochte, dem tieferen Sinn eines solchen Radikalismus genugzutun. Er mißversteht seinen eigenen guten Anfang, weil er die klärende Besinnung nicht bis zum erfüllenden Ende forttreibt. Daher erliegt er alsbald Problemen, die für ihn sonst als widersinnige erkennbar gewesen wären.

The image has two parts, and they are not the same image. The first is architectural: a "gate" (*Pforte*) that Descartes "opened" (*eröffnet*) and before which he "remained standing" (*stehen geblieben*). The second is a journey: a "passage" (*Gang*) into a "realm" that he "did not undertake" (*nicht angetreten*). The gate belongs to the course's own vocabulary. In the First Lecture the beginning of first philosophy was "the entrance gate" (*die Eingangspforte*), "the beginning of all philosophy überhaupt" (src L307, Hua VII 5). Descartes stands where the First Lecture placed the beginning, and the sentence before has said as much: he is "the genuine beginner of philosophy … but only in the beginning of the beginning" (*nur im Anfange des Anfanges*, src L1422–1423, Hua VII 72). The gate is the beginning reached; the realm is the beginning carried through.

The realm is set in quotation marks, the only marked borrowing in the passage, and is given two attributes in an emphatic contrast: "never trodden but very well to be trodden" (*nie betretene aber sehr wohl zu betretende*). "Very well" (*sehr wohl*) answers a denial: someone has held that this realm cannot be entered. In the course's terms that someone is the dogmatic objectivist, for whom there is nothing behind the objective world for consciousness to investigate, and also the Kantian, whose transcendental conditions are inferred rather than entered and seen (reading 30). (Goethe's scene is not in the corpus, so how the phrase stands to his wording cannot be checked here.) The next sentence supplies the literal content of the image: "the primal grounds of all cognition in transcendental subjectivity" (*die Urgründe aller Erkenntnis*). The Mothers are the primal grounds. The commentary's gloss, "the maternal ground from which all objective sense is born" (L11.7), is the right direction, but "born" adds a generation the passage does not state. The wording says only that the realm is where cognition has its grounds, and that one must "go back" (*zurückgehen*) to them, a descent like the one in *Faust*.

Three sentences give the reasons, and each is a failure of carrying through. "His philosophical radicalism failed" (*versagte*). His conviction "did not bring the right fruit", because he could not "do justice to the deeper sense of such a radicalism". He misunderstands "his own good beginning" because he does not "drive the clarifying reflection on to the fulfilling end" (*bis zum erfüllenden Ende forttreibt*). "Fulfilling" is the word for intuition that gives what was meant: reflection that stops short leaves its intention empty. The diagnosis matches the Columbus image of the Tenth Lecture (reading 11), but changes the cause. There Descartes had not learned from the skeptics; here he stops reflecting too soon. The two are one failure seen from two sides: without the skeptic's question about the sense of the world's being, the reflection on the ego has nowhere further to go.

The consequence follows at once, and its wording names what the gate kept out. Descartes "succumbs" to problems that would have been recognizable "as countersensical" (*als widersinnige*). The next sentences list them: the "perverse two-substance doctrine", the "objectivistic prejudice", and the use of the ego to rescue the objective world against skepticism (src L1429). The pure ego becomes "the pure soul", "the little piece of the objective world" (*Stückchen der objektiven Welt*) given indubitably, from which the rest of the world is to be secured (src L1430, Hua VII 73). What Descartes did instead of entering the realm was to treat its threshold as part of the world outside it. The Eleventh Lecture's verdict is that the "objectivistic basic attitude" of Cartesian philosophy gave the positive sciences an apparent right to count themselves absolute, and so called forth "psychologistic and naturalistic theories of reason" (src L1433, Hua VII 74). That is the Second Section's programme: the lectures on Locke show what is built on the threshold.

*Cartesian Meditations* keeps the diminutive. Its §10 warns against the prejudice that "in our apodictic pure *ego* we had saved a little end of the world" (*ein kleines Endchen der Welt*, Hua I 63, src L1518–1519 of that file). "Little piece" in 1923, "little end" in 1929: the image of the ego as a scrap of the world survives, and with it the diagnosis that Descartes' mistake was to keep the transcendental inside the objective. The realm of the Mothers does not recur in the corpus.

## Lecture 11, Hua VII 75: Locke's displacement

Reads [Lecture 11, block 11](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#eleventh-lecture-first-real-prospect-of-transcendental-science-transition-from-the-cartesian-meditations-to-locke).

Locke, in Husserl's account, did what Descartes failed to do and in doing it lost what Descartes had found. He made the Cartesian *ego* the theme of a science, but took it as the human soul, a part of a world that stands firm. Husserl calls the change a "displacement" (*Verschiebung*): the same theme, moved from the ground of transcendental questioning onto the ground of the pre-given world. The word names the double movement that governs the Second and Third Sections. Every later empiricist advance in the study of consciousness is, by this account, an advance on displaced ground.

> A remarkable and instructive displacement accomplishes itself here. Of a radical philosophy, of a systematic grounding, on absolutely secure ground, of a science radically put in question in advance, there is seriously no longer any talk. The world stands firm, and at bottom so does the possibility of objective science. But it is a matter of studying its tool, the human understanding, in the right way, in order to be able to further it. What must thereby be the self-evident theme, taken concretely, is according to Locke nothing other than the Cartesian *ego*, only, to be sure, grasped in a naturally objective way as the human soul, purely in itself, our human mind as it finds itself in evident inner experience. If Descartes had omitted, which in the context of his radical grounding of cognition was fateful enough, to make the *ego cogito* the theme of a science of its own, it is what is new in Locke that he does this, but, wholly naturalistically disposed, takes the *ego* as soul in the pre-given world.

German:

> Eine merkwürdige und lehrreiche Verschiebung vollzieht sich hier. Von einer radikalen Philosophie, von einer systematischen Begründung einer im voraus radikal in Frage gestellten Wissenschaft auf absolut sicherem Grunde ist ernstlich keine Rede mehr. Die Welt steht fest, und die Möglichkeit objektiver Wissenschaft im Grunde auch. Aber es gilt, ihr Werkzeug, den menschlichen Verstand, in rechter Weise zu studieren, um sie fördern zu können. Was dabei das selbstverständliche und konkret genommene Thema sein muß, ist nach Locke nichts anderes als das Cartesianische *ego*, nur freilich in natürlich objektiver Weise gefaßt als die menschliche Seele, rein in sich, unser menschlicher Geist, wie er sich selbst in evidenter innerer Erfahrung finde. Hatte Descartes es unterlassen, was im Zusammenhang seiner radikalen Erkenntnisbegründung verhängnisvoll genug war, das *ego cogito* zum Thema einer eigenen Wissenschaft zu machen, so ist es das Neue Lockes, daß er dies tut, aber, ganz naturalistisch eingestellt, das *ego* als Seele in der vorgegebenen Welt.

A displacement moves something without changing what it is, and that is the claim. The theme "is according to Locke nothing other than (*nichts anderes als*) the Cartesian *ego*". The identity is asserted first and the difference added after "only, to be sure" (*nur freilich*): grasped "in a naturally objective way as the human soul". The same object, then, in a different place. "Remarkable and instructive" (*merkwürdig und lehrreich*) are the words for a case that teaches by its structure: the *ego* can be lost without being forgotten, merely by being relocated. The commentary's "Locke advances by one step … and relapses by a deeper one" (L11.11) states the result. The wording shows how it happens. Nothing in the passage reports an argument by Locke for naturalizing the *ego*. The relocation is presented as the cost of a starting point taken for granted.

The starting point is stated in four short words: "The world stands firm" (*Die Welt steht fest*). It is the shortest sentence in the passage and the only one without qualification. Descartes had put the world in question "in advance" (*im voraus*); for Locke the question is "seriously no longer" (*ernstlich … mehr*) raised. "Seriously" grants that Locke still talks about the limits of knowledge; what he no longer does is let the world's existence become a question. With the world standing, cognition has a role inside it. The understanding is "its tool" (*ihr Werkzeug*), the tool of objective science, to be studied "in order to be able to further it". The aim is practical, like the scientist's occasional reflection in the Sixth Lecture (reading 7). Locke reflects on cognition for the sake of the sciences, and so his reflection takes over their attitude.

The last sentence measures Locke against Descartes in a conditional that is really a comparison. "If Descartes had omitted … to make the *ego cogito* the theme of a science of its own, it is what is new in Locke that he does this". Descartes' omission is called "fateful enough" (*verhängnisvoll genug*), the same adjective used of Descartes' "fateful unclarities" at the opening of the Tenth Lecture (src L1286). Locke's novelty is real: he is the first to make consciousness a field of systematic work. But the sentence ends on the place he gives it: "the *ego* as soul in the pre-given world" (*in der vorgegebenen Welt*). "Pre-given" is the word whose validity the Tenth Lecture's transcendental research was to suspend (reading 12). Locke's *ego* is located exactly where transcendental research may not locate it. The adverb that describes him, "wholly naturalistically disposed" (*ganz naturalistisch eingestellt*), names an attitude (*Einstellung*), not a doctrine. The course will make the change of attitude the method that corrects the displacement.

The word itself does not recur in the lectures, but the double movement organizes them. The Twelfth Lecture states what Locke "would have had to" do if he had kept the theme in place (reading 15). The *tabula rasa* of the Fourteenth Lecture is the displaced *ego* imagined as a space for thing-like contents (reading 17). And the Twentieth Lecture turns the displacement into a history of progress: Locke's naturalized *ego*, purified of its world by Berkeley and Hume, becomes a "purely immanent philosophy" that is the skeptical pre-form of phenomenology (reading 24). The displacement is also the condition of that history, since without Locke's thematizing of the *ego* there would be no line from the *Essay* to the *Treatise*.

Husserl's 1924 address for Kant's bicentenary, printed in the same volume, uses the plural. Genuine insight is needed into "the ways in which objective sense of every kind and objective truth arise in pure consciousness". The solution of this task, "which already moves Locke's doctrine of origins in its depths, but works itself out there through fateful displacements (*verhängnisvolle Verschiebungen*) in a perverted shape", leads into the most difficult investigations (*Kant und die Idee der Transzendentalphilosophie*, Hua VII 279, src L3895). The 1924 text adds what the lecture implies: Locke's question was the right one, the question of origins, and the displacement perverted its sense without removing it. "Fateful" is now attached to Locke's displacements, as in the lecture it was attached to Descartes' omission. The two failures are one history.

## Lecture 12, Hua VII 83–84: objectivity has its site in consciousness

Reads [Lecture 12, block 10](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#twelfth-lecture-the-naive-dogmatism-of-objectivism).

The proper achievement of cognition, Husserl argues, is to constitute objectivity for the cognizer, and so objectivity, as something to be attained in subjectivity, can have its site nowhere but in actual and possible consciousness. To place it outside all possible consciousness, as something consciousness copies or signals, makes no sense, because whatever consciousness is directed to can show itself only in consciousness. The thesis is stated as what Locke "would have had to see". It is the course's positive doctrine at its most compressed, and the form of an unfulfilled obligation is part of its content: the doctrine is presented as what the question of cognition itself demands, once its sense is clear.

> If Locke wanted, as he and all later theorists of cognition undoubtedly wanted, to lay clear the general essence of the achievement of cognition; if he wanted the imperfect to be perfected, so as to win principled norms for cognizing activity, and so for genuine science, science out of principled self-responsibility, to become possible for the first time: then he had to bring the sense of his project to a clarity that could not be confused, and keep it there; that is, to unconfusable clarity about what the question of the essence, achievement or validity of cognition properly means in this context. He had to see that the proper achievement of cognition, of genuine, so-called rational cognition, is nothing less than to constitute for the cognizer, by attaining it, objectivity of every kind and shape, truly existing being, true assertion, true theory and science. It had to be seen, and held fast in a radical grip, that objectivity, as something *attainable* in subjectivity, can have its site nowhere else than in its domain of consciousness itself (that of actual and possible consciousness), and that it can have no sense to place it outside all possible consciousness, as something by which consciousness could direct itself, say through imaging or indicating. As if that by which consciousness directs itself, even an imaging and indicating consciousness, could show itself anywhere other than in consciousness, and as if the directing itself were actualized otherwise than in syntheses of fulfilling identification. It must above all also be seen that everything pre-given plainly and as a matter of course as existing is this only as experienced of experiencing acts, and draws sense and validity only from them.

German:

> Wollte Locke, was er und alle nachkommenden Erkenntnistheoretiker unzweifelhaft wollten, das generelle Wesen der Erkenntnisleistung klarlegen, wollte er, daß, um prinzipielle Normen für das erkennende Tun zu gewinnen, Unvollkommenes vervollkommnet und somit allererst echte Wissenschaft, Wissenschaft aus prinzipieller Selbstverantwortung ermöglicht werden konnte: dann mußte er den Sinn seines Vorhabens zu unverwirrbarer Klarheit bringen und ihn darin halten; also in unverwirrbarer Klarheit, was die Frage nach Wesen, Leistung oder Geltung der Erkenntnis in diesem Zusammenhang eigentlich meint. Er mußte sehen, daß die eigene Leistung der Erkenntnis, der echten, sogenannten vernünftigen Erkenntnis, nichts minderes ist, als für den Erkennenden Objektivität jeder Art und Gestalt, wahrhaft Seiendes, wahre Aussage, wahre Theorie und Wissenschaft erzielend zu konstituieren. Es mußte gesehen und in radikalem Zugriff festgehalten sein, daß Objektivität als ein in der Subjektivität *Erzielbares* nirgendwo sonst seine Stätte haben kann als eben in ihrem Bewußtseinsbereich selbst (dem des wirklichen und möglichen Bewußtseins) und daß es keinen Sinn haben kann, sie außer alles mögliche Bewußtsein zu stellen, als etwas, wonach sich das Bewußtsein, etwa durch Abbildung oder Indizierung, richten könnte. Als ob das, wonach sich Bewußtsein richtet, sei es auch ein abbildendes und indizierendes, sich woanders ausweisen könnte denn im Bewußtsein und als ob das Sichrichten selbst sich anders aktualisierte denn als in Synthesen erfüllender Identifizierung. Es muß dabei vor allem auch gesehen sein, daß alles schlicht und selbstverständlich als daseiend Vorgegebene das nur ist als Erfahrenes erfahrender Akte und nur aus ihnen Sinn und Geltung schöpft.

The conditional frame does more than reproach Locke. Its antecedent states what Locke "undoubtedly" (*unzweifelhaft*) wanted, and it extends the wish to "all later theorists of cognition". Its consequent states what anyone with that wish "had to" (*mußte*) see. The modal is repeated four times: "he had to bring", "he had to see", "it had to be seen", "it must above all also be seen". The doctrine is derived from the question, not from phenomenology. That is the force of the commentary's "the conditional sharpens the failure into a precise list" (L12.10). The list is not of Locke's errors but of what the question of cognition, once clear about what it "properly means" (*eigentlich meint*), commits its asker to. Locke's failure is a failure to understand his own question.

The central sentence turns on one emphasized word, the only emphasis in the file's passage: objectivity is something "*attainable*" (*Erzielbares*) in subjectivity. The verb "attain" (*erzielen*) has already appeared in the sentence before, where cognition constitutes objectivity "by attaining it" (*erzielend*). The word comes from the aim (*Ziel*) and keeps the teleology of the First Lecture's "purposive labour" (reading 2): objectivity is the goal toward which cognizing strives, and a goal has its place in the striving that aims at it. From this follows the spatial claim. Objectivity "can have its site nowhere else" (*nirgendwo sonst seine Stätte haben kann*) than in consciousness. "Site" (*Stätte*) is a strong word, a place of dwelling, and "nowhere else" excludes the alternative before it is named. The parenthesis then widens the site: "actual and possible consciousness". The thesis is not that objects exist only while someone is aware of them. It is that their being, as something to be attained, refers to possible attainment.

The alternative is named only as a countersense, in two "as if" (*als ob*) clauses that are grammatically fragments. "As if that by which consciousness directs itself … could show itself anywhere other than in consciousness": a thing beyond all consciousness that consciousness copies (*Abbildung*) or that its states indicate (*Indizierung*) would have to be checked against the copy, and the checking is again consciousness. "And as if the directing itself were actualized otherwise than in syntheses of fulfilling identification": being directed to an object is not a relation to something outside, but a series of identifications in which a meant object is fulfilled. The two terms of the rejected theory recall the Eleventh Lecture's charge against Descartes, who took evidence for "a criterion, a mere index of truth" (src L1433, Hua VII 74). Imaging and indicating are the image theory and the sign theory of perception, which the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Lectures refute in detail. Here they are dismissed in advance by the form of the sentence: an "as if" states the rejected view only as an absurdity.

The last sentence extends the thesis from objectivity in science to the world of ordinary life. "Everything pre-given plainly and as a matter of course as existing" is so "only as experienced of experiencing acts", and draws "sense and validity" from them. The words of the Tenth Lecture return: pre-givenness, which transcendental research may not let count (reading 12), is now said to draw its validity from the acts that experience it. The thesis has been extended from what science attains to what life finds already there.

The opening clause carries the norm for which all this matters: "science out of principled self-responsibility" (*Wissenschaft aus prinzipieller Selbstverantwortung*). It is the first use of the word in the lectures, and the course measures its later figures by it. Hume's radicalism is "not that genuine radicalism which means ultimate self-responsibility out of ultimate self-reflection and self-clarification" (src L2740, Hua VII 160). The supplementary texts of *Erste Philosophie II* draw the conclusion for life as a whole, in the essay "Meditation on the Idea of an Individual and Communal Life in Absolute Self-Responsibility": a philosophical life "must be understood as a life überhaupt out of absolute self-responsibility" (Hua VIII 197, src L3050 of that file). The Twelfth Lecture holds the two ends together. Self-responsibility is possible only if the objects for which one answers have their site where one can answer for them, in consciousness.

## Lecture 13, Hua VII 92: the natural attitude "inhibited"

Reads [Lecture 13, block 10](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#thirteenth-lecture-the-empiricist-prejudices--the-psychologism-in-the-theory-of-cognition).

Pure inner experience, Husserl says, is not simply available. Before it can describe anything, the natural attitude that takes the world as given must be "inhibited" (*unterbunden*), so that nothing objectively experienced is mixed into what is described. Only then does inner life show itself as what it is, "through and through consciousness and at once conscious". The passage names the method, the phenomenological reduction, in the sentence before, and then describes its effect in the vocabulary of prevention: it stops something from happening. The understanding the passage carries is of the reduction as a condition of seeing, not yet as an operation with steps.

> It needs, as we shall show in detail later, in order to come about at all, to keep its purity, and to carry with it scientific security that this purity is kept, a method of its own, the method of the phenomenological reduction. For only when the natural objectivistic attitude with all its givennesses is inhibited (which this very method teaches how to achieve), only when accordingly the otherwise unavoidable mixing-in of what is objectively experienced (or, what is the same, of what is transcendent to pure inwardness) is made quite impossible, can this difficulty be overcome. Only then can it in fact be seen what lies before us under the title of the "inner", that is, of what is enclosed purely in the evidence of the *ego cogito*. And only then does it come forth, can and must it be seen, provided that prejudices do not, in a kind of hypnosis, make us blind again to what is actually seen, that the whole inner life is through and through consciousness and at once conscious, and therefore to be described only as such.

German:

> Sie bedarf, wie wir später ausführlich zeigen werden, um überhaupt erst zustandezukommen, um ihre Reinheit zu erhalten und wissenschaftliche Sicherheit der Erhaltung dieser Reinheit mit sich zu führen, einer eigenen Methode, der Methode der phänomenologischen Reduktion. Nämlich nur, wenn die natürliche objektivistische Einstellung mit allen ihren Gegebenheiten unterbunden ist (was eben diese Methode zu leisten lehrt), nur, wenn demgemäß die sonst unvermeidliche Einmengung von objektiv Erfahrenem (oder, was dasselbe ist, der reinen Innerlichkeit Transzendentem) ganz unmöglich gemacht wird, kann diese Schwierigkeit überwunden werden. Erst dann kann in der Tat gesehen werden, was unter dem Titel des ,,Innerlichen", also <des> rein in der Evidenz des *ego cogito* Beschlossenen, vorliegt. Und dann erst tritt hervor, kann und muß gesehen werden, sofern nicht Vorurteile in einer Art Hypnose für das wirklich Gesehene wieder blind machen, daß das ganze Innenleben durch und durch Bewußtsein und zugleich Bewußtes und daher nur als solches zu beschreiben ist.

*Note on the text.* "<des>" is the editor's supplement, as the file gives it. The file shows no emphasis in the passage; the commentary's italics on "*inhibited*", "*mingling-in*" and "*consciousness and at once conscious*" (L13.10) are its own. The key-passage row says the method "is presented without its name"; the name stands in the sentence just before, "the method of the phenomenological reduction" (src L1590).

The passage is built on "only" (*nur*, *erst*), five times: "only when", "only when", "only then", "only then". It states conditions, not results. The reduction is introduced by what it makes possible, and the lecture promises the method itself for later ("as we shall show in detail later"). The promise points beyond this volume, to the theory of the reduction in *Erste Philosophie II*. What Hua VII gives is the method's place in the argument: the dispute about inner observation, in which "every attempt" at a convincing description "failed" (src L1590), cannot be settled until something is removed from what is observed.

What is removed is described twice, and the second description explains the first. The natural attitude "with all its givennesses" is "inhibited" (*unterbunden*); the "mixing-in" (*Einmengung*) of what is objectively experienced is "made quite impossible". *Unterbinden* means to stop, to tie off, to prevent something from continuing; it is a word for interrupting a process, as one ties off a flow. The attitude is not denied or doubted, it is stopped from operating. The second phrase says why: otherwise its contents get mixed into the description "unavoidably" (*unvermeidlich*). The parenthesis gives the equivalence on which the method rests: what is "objectively experienced" is "the same" as what is "transcendent to pure inwardness". The world is excluded not because it is doubtful but because it is not inner, and the descriptions of inner life failed because they described something that was not purely inner.

The rest of the passage turns from method to seeing. The verbs are verbs of appearing: "can be seen", "comes forth" (*tritt hervor*), "can and must be seen". The proviso marks what stands in the way: "provided that prejudices do not, in a kind of hypnosis, make us blind again to what is actually seen". The hypnosis image goes further than the commentary's account of method. The obstacle is not lack of skill in observation but a trance in which one sees and does not register what one sees. The reduction is the waking from it. What is then seen is stated in a formula whose two halves name both sides of the reflexive structure: inner life is "consciousness and at once conscious" (*Bewußtsein und zugleich Bewußtes*). It is consciousness of something, and it is itself something one is conscious of. Hence it is "to be described only as such": as intentional, and as given to a reflection that is itself consciousness. The next paragraph unfolds the second half against Locke's *tabula rasa*: reflection on reflection, "continua of consciousness of consciousness of consciousness" (src L1598–1611).

The word *unterbunden* is not the course's last word for the method, and the difference is instructive. The Seventeenth Lecture, the one place where the operation is named again in this volume, speaks of "change of attitude from the natural into the transcendental, by 'bracketing' (*„Einklammerung“*) of all objectivity posited beforehand or to be co-posited" (src L2269, Hua VII 126). Bracketing keeps what it excludes inside the brackets, available as a theme; inhibiting only stops a flow. *Erste Philosophie II* mostly uses a third verb, to "inhibit" (*inhibieren*), for suspending one's stance toward the world's existence, and it keeps *unterbunden* for the relapse: the putting out of play must be so effective "that every attempt to fall back into the naive performance of experience is inhibited" (*unterbunden ist*, Hua VIII c. 80, src L1578 of that file). In Hua VII it is the natural attitude that is stopped; in Hua VIII it is the return to it. The 1923 history, working on Locke's failure to describe inner life, uses the word for what had made the descriptions impure. The theory of the reduction uses it for what keeps the purity, which this passage named as the method's third task: "to carry with it scientific security that this purity is kept".

## Lecture 14, Hua VII 100–101: the tabula rasa and the reification of consciousness

Reads [Lecture 14, block 11](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#fourteenth-lecture-the-exemplariness-of-modern-natural-science-as-a-hindering-motive-for-the-formation-of-a-genuine-intuitionistic-science-of-consciousness).

Locke's naturalism, Husserl argues, lies in an image before it lies in any thesis. The soul is a blank tablet on which experience writes signs, and the signs are ideas. But marks on a tablet are things, and a tablet is a kind of space; the image makes the field of consciousness into a space for inner thing-like items, to be described and causally explained as physics treats outer things. Husserl calls this a "tendency to reification" (*Verdinglichung*). The understanding the passage builds is that a picture of consciousness, taken over without examination, settles in advance what consciousness can be found to contain.

> It grows out of this: that the realm of purely inner experience, this so-called realm of "ideas", is thought wholly on the analogy of the spatial world, as the realm of outer experience. Characteristic is the famous *tabula rasa* simile, which Locke takes up again from ancient tradition. The soul, when it awakens to consciousness, resembles an unwritten white paper on which experience writes signs. What appears there in the soul, or rather in the sphere of inner experience, are in succession ever new such signs, ever new ideas. In this simile there expresses itself the tendency to a reification that works itself out ever more sharply in the further development of Locke's philosophy. Signs on a writing tablet, chalk strokes or ink strokes, are thingly occurrences and also symbolize only what is thing-like. As space is the field of being of physical things, so the field of consciousness, the empty writing tablet, is a kind of space for inner-psychic thinglinesses. Just as natural science, first descriptive and then explanatory, deals with the things and processes of outer experience, those of outer space, describes them and explains them causally, so psychology has the analogous tasks in the field of consciousness for ideas and formations of ideas.

German:

> Sie erwächst daraus, daß das Reich der rein inneren Erfahrung, dieses sogenannte Reich von „Ideen“, ganz nach Analogie der Raumwelt, als des Reiches der äußeren Erfahrung, gedacht wird. Bezeichnend ist das berühmte *tabula-rasa*-Gleichnis, das Locke aus antiker Tradition wiederaufnimmt. Die Seele gleicht, wenn sie zu Bewußtsein erwacht, einem unbeschriebenen weißen Papier, auf das die Erfahrung Zeichen anschreibt. Was da in der Seele oder vielmehr in der inneren Erfahrungssphäre auftritt, sind in der Folge immer neue solche Zeichen, immer neue Ideen. In diesem Gleichnis drückt sich die Tendenz zu einer Verdinglichung aus, die in der Fortentwicklung der Locke'schen Philosophie immer schärfer sich auswirkt. Zeichen auf einer Schreibtafel, Kreidestriche oder Tintenstriche sind dingliche Vorkommnisse und symbolisieren auch nur Dingartiges. Wie der Raum das Seinsfeld der physischen Dinge ist, so ist das Bewußtseinsfeld, die leere Schreibtafel, eine Art Raum für innerseelische Dinglichkeiten. So wie die Naturwissenschaft, zunächst die beschreibende und dann die erklärende, von den Dingen und Vorgängen der äußeren Erfahrung, denen des äußeren Raumes handelt, sie beschreibt und kausal erklärt, so hat die Psychologie im Bewußtseinsfeld die analogen Aufgaben für die Ideen und Ideengebilde.

*Note on the text.* The file breaks "Verding- / lichung" at a line end and has a page break (Hua VII 100/101) inside the sentence on chalk and ink strokes, which reads on intact (src L1764–1769).

Husserl reads the simile by taking it literally, and the reading works because the simile is about writing. A white paper and a writing tablet are surfaces; signs are written on them; signs "symbolize". So far the image seems apt for ideas, which also refer. Husserl's objection is that the image leaves the reference out. "Chalk strokes or ink strokes are thingly occurrences and also symbolize only what is thing-like" (*sind dingliche Vorkommnisse und symbolisieren auch nur Dingartiges*). A chalk mark is a thing first and a sign only for someone who reads it. On the tablet, ideas become marks: things that happen to be there, whose meaning is an extra relation that the tablet itself does not contain. The intentional "of" that the Eighth Lecture demanded as the ABC of psychology (reading 9) has no place in the picture.

The paragraph moves from image to theory in a chain of analogies, each introduced by its connective. The realm of ideas is thought "wholly on the analogy of" (*ganz nach Analogie der*) the spatial world. "As space is" (*Wie der Raum*) the field of physical things, "so is" (*so ist*) the field of consciousness "a kind of space" (*eine Art Raum*) for inner things. "Just as" (*So wie*) natural science describes and explains, "so" (*so*) psychology has "the analogous tasks". Each step is an analogy, and the final one assigns psychology its method. "A kind of" is the only hedge, and it is Husserl's description of Locke, not Locke's of himself. The point is that nobody argued for the method: it follows from the picture. The phrase "so-called realm of 'ideas'" (*dieses sogenannte Reich von „Ideen“*) adds Husserl's distance to Locke's term with both "so-called" and quotation marks. Lockean "ideas" are not Platonic ideas, and the lectures on abstraction will hold that confusion against empiricism (reading 21).

"Reification" (*Verdinglichung*) is a "tendency" (*Tendenz*) that "works itself out ever more sharply" (*immer schärfer sich auswirkt*). The noun names a direction, not a completed doctrine, and the course follows it. In the next lecture the image yields Husserl's own coinage, the "tablet of consciousness" (*Bewußtseinstafel*), and its consequence for the self: Locke speaks of the mind "almost as if a human being stood before the tablet of consciousness and busied himself with the signs, which is palpably nonsense" (src L1821, Hua VII 103). The Seventeenth Lecture repeats the charge: naive naturalism sees in inner experience "only something like data of a tablet of consciousness" and then "unnoticed" sets "behind the tablet a whole human subject" (src L2186, Hua VII 120). The tablet makes the ego into a reader outside the page. In the Twenty-second Lecture Hume's psychology is "a *tabula rasa* psychology" (src L2720, Hua VII 157), and in the Twenty-third the image is exhausted: once Hume removes spiritual substance, "the soul can now not even be compared with a *tabula rasa*, or with a stage on which all kinds of fleeting psychic shapes appear. For to this tablet, this stage, nothing real corresponds" (src L2730, Hua VII 158). Reification ends by losing even the thing it reified. The stage image there anticipates Hume's "theatre" (reading 29).

At its place the passage explains why Locke's "fore-intimation" of a universal clarification of cognition, praised in the paragraph before, could not be worked out: "every way … was blocked by the naive naturalization of consciousness" (src L1748–1751). The next paragraph names the source of the picture. Natural science had become "the prototype of genuine science überhaupt", and so spatial-thingly reality was taken "as a matter of course" as the prototype of every reality, including the psychic (src L1775–1780). The tablet is the form in which that prototype entered the study of the mind. The image Locke "takes up again from ancient tradition" turns out to carry the modern prejudice of the Fourteenth Lecture's title: the exemplariness of natural science as a hindrance to a science of consciousness.

## Lecture 15, Hua VII 106: the intentional relation is not a relation between existents

Reads [Lecture 15, block 9](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#fifteenth-lecture-the-problem-of-immanence-and-of-the-synthetic-unity-in-consciousness).

The relation of an act to its object, Husserl insists, is not one of the relations we assert between things. An ordinary assertion of relation claims that two existing things stand in a relation; the relation of a perception to what it perceives holds whether or not the perceived exists. Its object is the "mere intentional object", what the act means, taken purely as meant, "without question and decision" about its real being. The passage takes over Brentano's name and the Scholastic background he invoked, and changes what the "immanent object" is. This is the conceptual centre of the critique of Locke: a consciousness made of thing-like signs on a tablet has no room for a relation of this kind.

> This relation, which after Brentano is designated as intentional (and according to which the experiences were also called "intentional experiences" by me), has an essentially different sense from the other relations that we ascribe, be it to objects among one another, be it to the I or to the respective consciousness toward some objects or other. The object of the intentional relation is the one that is enclosed purely in the act, in the intentional experiencing itself; it is the mere intentional object, the immanent one, as Brentano, following Scholasticism, also introduces it. It is what is meant in the act, purely as meant, without question and decision about whether it is in truth, whether it "really" is or not. Where we simply, in the normal and modified sense, make an assertion of relation, it raises, according to its own sense, the claim to set what exists into relation to what exists, and the relation itself is put forward, asserted as a relation between actually existing objects (no matter whether they are real or ideal ones).

German:

> Diese Beziehung, die nach Brentano als intentionale bezeichnet wird (und wonach auch die Erlebnisse von mir „intentionale Erlebnisse“ genannt wurden), hat einen wesentlich anderen Sinn als die sonstigen Beziehungen, die wir sei es Gegenständen untereinander, sei es dem Ich oder dem jeweiligen Bewußtsein zu irgendwelchen Gegenständen zuschreiben. Das Objekt der intentionalen Beziehung ist derjenige, der rein im Akte, im intentionalen Erleben selbst beschlossen ist, ist das bloße intentionale Objekt, das immanente, wie es Brentano im Anschluß an die Scholastik auch einführt. Es ist das im Akt Vermeinte rein als Vermeintes, ohne Frage und Entscheidung darüber, ob es in Wahrheit, ob es „wirklich“ sei oder nicht. Wo wir schlechthin, im normalen und modifizierten Sinn, eine Beziehungsaussage machen, da erhebt sie ihrem eigenen Sinn gemäß den Anspruch, Seiendes zu Seiendem in Beziehung zu setzen, und die Beziehung selbst ist hingestellt, behauptet als Beziehung zwischen wirklich seienden Gegenständen (gleichgültig ob es reale oder ideale sind).

*Note on the text.* "Das Objekt … ist derjenige, der" joins a neuter noun to a masculine pronoun, which suggests that the sentence once had *Gegenstand* (object) as its subject. It is quoted as the file has it. The file sets the passage line by line with words broken at line ends; the quotation joins them.

Husserl assigns the vocabulary with care, and the assignments are the first move of the argument. The relation "is designated as intentional after Brentano" (*nach Brentano*): the name is Brentano's in the sense that Husserl follows his usage. The phrase "intentional experiences", in quotation marks, is Husserl's own coinage (*von mir … genannt*), from the *Logical Investigations*. And the "mere intentional object, the immanent one", is introduced by Brentano "following Scholasticism". Brentano's text in the corpus shows the source of the last attribution. Every psychic phenomenon is characterized by "what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (also mental) inexistence of an object", and Brentano adds that the expression "immanently objective" is sometimes used in the same sense, with "immanent" meant to exclude the misunderstanding that a real existence outside the mind is meant. "Each contains something as object in itself" (*Jedes enthält etwas als Objekt in sich*; Brentano, *Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkt* I, c. 124–125, src L8441–8470 of that file, a scan-grade conversion).

Against that background, Husserl's next two sentences take Brentano's word and change its sense. His first description keeps the containment: the object is "the one that is enclosed (*beschlossen*) purely in the act". His second replaces it: the object "is what is meant in the act, purely as meant" (*das im Akt Vermeinte rein als Vermeintes*). Brentano's immanent object is in the act as a content is in a container. Husserl's is in the act as what the act means, and its being "in" consists in being meant. The phrase "without question and decision about whether it is in truth, whether it 'really' is or not" makes the shift explicit. The immanent object is not a second, mental object beside the real one. It is the object itself taken under a suspension of the question of its reality. The quotation marks around "really" (*„wirklich“*) mark the word as the one being set aside.

The contrast with assertions of relation fixes what the suspension excludes. An ordinary statement of relation "raises the claim, according to its own sense" (*ihrem eigenen Sinn gemäß*), to relate "what exists to what exists" (*Seiendes zu Seiendem*). The claim belongs to the assertion's sense, not to a theory about it. The intentional relation makes no such claim, so its holding does not depend on the existence of its second term. The paragraph after the passage shows this on an example. "I see this tree" says, in its normal sense, that the tree is there and that I see it, so it contains an intentional relation inside an assertion of relation. But if I put the tree's existence in question, or "abstain at will from every position-taking" toward it, "this changes nothing" about the perception's being "perception of 'this tree'", even if it should later prove an illusion (src L1959, Hua VII 107). Hence the conclusion: "a change of attitude" is needed, "a modification of sense", to make the meant object as such recognizable "under abstention from every position-taking" (src L1961). The abstention described in the example is the method the Thirteenth Lecture named (reading 16). Here it is derived from the structure of the intentional relation itself, not imposed on it.

The passage supplies what the Locke critique needs. If ideas are signs on a tablet (reading 17), then the relation of an idea to a thing is a relation between two existents, a sign and what it signifies, and must be established by inference or causal theory. The Sixteenth and Seventeenth Lectures attack exactly this: the image and sign theories of perception that the Twelfth Lecture had ruled out in advance (reading 15). The Fifteenth Lecture provides the reason they fail. The perceived tree is not a sign of a tree. It is the tree as meant, and whether the tree exists is a further question that the perception, as perception, leaves open. The next paragraph names the methodological result, "a purely immanent description", which is "overstepped and cancelled" as soon as what is meant is described otherwise than exactly as it is meant (src L1967–1971, Hua VII 108).

## Lecture 16, Hua VII 115: empathy and the one nature

Reads [Lecture 16, block 12](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#sixteenth-lecture-the-irreality-of-the-immanent-contents-of-the-consciousness-synthesis-in-their-iobject-polarization-and-the-problem-of-intersubjectivity-remarks-on-berkeleys-critique-of-locke).

Each subject, on Locke's premises, experiences nature only in its own images and can never get beyond them by inference. The one way out of one's own subjectivity is empathy (*Einfühlung*): the motivated positing of another subjectivity with images analogous to one's own. But empathy only multiplies image-systems, and so the question becomes sharper: why do we call what others see "the same things" that we see, and speak of "one" nature? The passage poses the problem of intersubjectivity as the question Locke "would have had to" ask. It does not solve it. The understanding it carries is that objectivity, if it is to be "one nature for all", is a problem of the relation between subjects before it is a problem of the relation between a subject and things.

> It must also be noted that each subject experiences nature purely in itself, that it therefore never goes beyond its so-called images. In connecting experiences with experiences, in living through the concordance of its images, in other words in convincing itself of the rightful existence of things, of nature überhaupt, it can obviously never find a motive to undertake those inferences into the transcendent that arose from misunderstanding and were invented. So much for the single I in its own direct experience. The only way to transcend one's own subjectivity and self-experienced nature is empathy into another subjectivity, the inwardly motivated positing of analogous mentality and analogous sensuous "images" like those experienced by oneself. But why then do we call these the same things as which *we* see them, why do we speak of *one* nature that we all see? So Locke would have had to consider and ask.

German:

> Es ist doch auch zu beachten, daß jedes Subjekt rein in sich selbst Natur erfährt, daß es also über seine sogenannten Bilder nie hinausgeht. Indem es Erfahrungen mit Erfahrungen verknüpft, das Zusammenstimmen seiner Bilder erlebt, m.a.W. sich von dem rechtmäßigen Dasein der Dinge, der Natur überhaupt überzeugt, kann es offenbar nie einen Anlaß finden, jene aus Mißverständnis entsprungenen und erfundenen Schlüsse ins Transzendente zu unternehmen. So als einzelnes Ich in eigener direkter Erfahrung. Die einzige Weise, die eigene Subjektivität und selbsterfahrene Natur zu transzendieren, ist die Einfühlung in eine andere Subjektivität, die innerlich motivierte Setzung von analoger Geistigkeit und analogen sinnlichen „Bildern“ wie den selbst erfahrenen. Aber warum nennen wir diese dann dieselben Dinge, als welche w i r sehen, warum sprechen wir von *einer* Natur, die wir alle sehen? So hätte Locke überlegen und fragen müssen.

*Note on the text.* "w i r" (we) is in spaced type in the file and "*einer*" (one) in italics; both are emphasis. "Dieselben Dinge" (the same things) has none; the commentary's italics on "*the same things*" (L16.12) are its own. "Als welche wir sehen" (as which we see) is the file's wording, where one might expect "die wir sehen" (that we see); the translation keeps it.

The passage is argued on Locke's premises, and its first sentence states them in his vocabulary with Husserl's distance: each subject never goes beyond "its so-called images" (*seine sogenannten Bilder*). Inside those premises, the inference from images to a transcendent nature has no motive. A subject who connects experiences and lives through "the concordance of its images" (*das Zusammenstimmen seiner Bilder*) is thereby convinced of the existence of things. Concordance is all the conviction there is, and the inferences "into the transcendent" are "invented" (*erfundenen*) and arose "from misunderstanding". This is Husserl's own view stated as a consequence of Locke's: the existence of the experienced world is constituted in concordant experience, not inferred from it. "So much for the single I" (*So als einzelnes Ich*) closes the case as far as one subject goes.

The second step is "the only way" (*die einzige Weise*) to transcend one's own subjectivity: empathy. The definition is careful. Empathy is a "positing" (*Setzung*) of another mind, "inwardly motivated" (*innerlich motiviert*), not an inference, and what it posits is "analogous": analogous mentality and analogous "images". The quotation marks around "images" carry the distance of "so-called" from the first sentence. Empathy does not reach the things; it reaches other image-systems like one's own. The commentary is right that intersubjectivity is raised here and not solved (L16.12), and the wording shows why. On these terms empathy can only add subjects. The sentence before the passage said as much in Berkeley's voice: positing an "original" nature behind the images merely increases "the number of subjects by one more" (src L2080).

The question then turns on two emphasized words. "Why do we call these the same things as which *we* see them, why do we speak of *one* nature that we all see?" The spaced "we" sets the self against the others whose images empathy posits; the italic "one" sets the single nature against the plurality of image-systems. "The same" (*dieselben*) is unemphasized, but it is the word the question is about: sameness across subjects is what neither concordance within one subject nor empathy as analogy can give. The final sentence returns the question to its addressee in the past conditional that the Twelfth Lecture used: "So Locke would have had to consider and ask" (*So hätte Locke überlegen und fragen müssen*). The form makes the problem of intersubjectivity part of what Locke's own question demanded (reading 15).

Within the course the problem is taken up twice and answered neither time. Berkeley meets it with God. In his monadological approach, "there are only I and the others", each with its own sensuous complexes, and "wonderfully, as the mutual understanding of the subjects teaches, the one and wholly identical nature is constituted in all"; "the creator of this wonderful intersubjective order or of the common nature is God", who must be inferred from just this fact (src L2696, Hua VII 153). The Twenty-sixth Lecture names what the rationalists left in the dark: the transcendental subjectivity "of the universal community of transcendental single subjects, connected transcendentally through possible mutual understanding, remained unseen, in the state of naive anonymity" (src L3039, Hua VII 184). Between Berkeley's God and the community left unseen, the 1923 history has only the question.

*Cartesian Meditations* states the answer as a title. Its §55 is "The communalization of monads and the first form of objectivity: intersubjective nature" (Hua I, src L3017 of that file). Intersubjective nature is "the first form of objectivity": the one nature of the 1923 question is constituted in the community of subjects that empathy, now worked out as "appresentation", makes accessible. The Sixteenth Lecture's "only way" became the route of the Fifth Meditation.

## Lecture 17, Hua VII 126: curable and incurable psychologism

Reads [Lecture 17, blocks 18–19](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#seventeenth-lecture-on-the-question-of-the-constitution-of-externality-the-cartesian-evidence-of-the-self-givenness-of-things-in-perception).

Psychologism and sensualism are both errors, but not errors of the same kind, and the medical pair "curable" (*heilbar*) and "incurable" (*unheilbar*) measures the difference. Psychologism is an error about the standing of analyses: it takes descriptions of consciousness to be facts about human minds and builds the theory of reason on them. Sensualism is an error in the analyses themselves: it never sees consciousness as consciousness of something, so it has no descriptions to misplace. The first can be cured by a change of attitude, because what it found survives the change. The second cannot, because there is nothing to carry over. Curability is a property of the findings, not of the theory that frames them.

> If it is to be able to begin as that at all, it must do so in the shape of a systematic and purely immanent analysis of consciousness: a psychological "phenomenology". Phenomenological elementary analyses and descriptions create its ABC. The investigation of this ABC and of the shapes to be formed from it *a priori*, or of the structural and genetic essential laws that belong to it, makes up (as only the present has shown) a whole science, and an apriori one at that. Of necessity it precedes every experiential psychology (a science of the facts of psychological empirical observation) "that is to be able to step forth as strict science". It is nothing other than the science of the very own essence of the psychic as such. A psychologism on the ground of such a phenomenological psychology (and even of one that had misjudged its apriori character) is curable. It would indeed have damaged the formation of a genuine theory of reason and philosophy, even made it impossible in principle, and yet it would have been a relatively easily corrected error, if only the analyses of consciousness were drawn from actual intuition and were actually intentional analyses. Through the change of attitude from the natural to the transcendental, through the "bracketing" of all objectivity posited in advance and to be co-posited, all immanent analyses would after all be preserved in their essential kernel and usable for the theory of cognition.
>
> On the other hand, a sensualistic psychologism is incurable. Its assertions about the psychic itself are from the outset no actual findings, none drawn from the very essence of conscious life itself. To uncover intentionality is to *understand* spiritually: to understand cognition and its formations, among them in particular the formations truth and right; that is, to understand intentional formations through the methodical uncovering of the intentional connections that constitute and form them. To do this scientifically and descriptively is to understand them scientifically. Where, by contrast, one has not yet even begun to see the kind of intentional implication, and so to create elements of an understanding, there is nothing understandable at all, and so nothing intelligible. But psychological genesis too is in its essence intelligible genesis, and so every naturalistic psychology necessarily offers only a pseudo-explanation of psychological genesis.

German:

> Soll sie als das überhaupt anfangen können, so muß sie es in der Gestalt einer systematischen und rein immanenten Bewußtseinsanalyse tun — einer psychologischen ,,Phänomenologie''. Phänomenologische Elementaranalysen und -deskriptionen schaffen für sie das ABC. Die Erforschung dieses ABC und der aus ihm *a priori* zu bildenden Gestalten bzw. der ihm zugehörigen strukturellen und genetischen Wesensgesetze macht — wie sich erst in der Gegenwart zeigte — eine ganze Wissenschaft aus, und dabei eine apriorische. In Notwendigkeit geht diese einer jeden Erfahrungspsychologie (einer Wissenschaft von den Tatsachen psychologischer Empirie) voraus — ,,die als strenge Wissenschaft soll auftreten können''. Sie ist eben nichts anderes als die Wissenschaft vom ureigenen Wesen des Psychischen als solchen. Ein Psychologismus auf dem Grunde einer solchen phänomenologischen Psychologie (und selbst einer solchen, die ihren apriorischen Charakter verkannt hätte) ist heilbar. Er hätte die Ausbildung einer echten Vernunfttheorie und Philosophie wohl geschädigt, ja im Prinzip unmöglich gemacht, und doch wäre er ein relativ leicht zu bessernder Irrtum gewesen — wenn nur die Bewußtseinsanalysen aus wirklicher Intuition geschöpft und wirklich intentionale Analysen waren. Durch Veränderung der Einstellung aus der natürlichen in die transzendentale, durch „Einklammerung“ aller vorangesetzten und mitzusetzenden Objektivität blieben doch alle immanenten Analysen in ihrem Wesenskern erhalten und erkenntnistheoretisch nutzbar.
>
> Andererseits ist ein sensualistischer Psychologismus unheilbar. Seine Aufstellungen über das Psychische selbst sind von vornherein keine wirklichen Feststellungen, keine aus dem eigenen Wesen des Bewußtseinslebens selbst geschöpften. Intentionalität enthüllen ist geistig *verstehen*, Erkenntnis und ihre Gebilde verstehen, darin im besonderen die Gebilde Wahrheit, Recht verstehen, das ist intentionale Gebilde durch methodische Enthüllung der intentionalen konstitutiv-bildenden Zusammenhänge verstehen. Es wissenschaftlich deskriptiv tun ist, sie wissenschaftlich verstehen. Wo hingegen man noch nicht einmal angefangen hat, die Art intentionaler Implikation zu sehen, also Elemente eines Verstehens zu schaffen, gibt es überhaupt nichts Verstehbares und somit Verständliches. Aber auch psychologische Genesis ist ihrem Wesen nach verständliche Genesis, und so bietet notwendig jede naturalistische Psychologie nur eine Scheinerklärung psychologischer Genesis.

*Note on the text.* The first paragraph opens a new page (Hua VII 126) and its "it" (*sie*) is psychology, named in the last sentence of p. 125. Only "*a priori*" and "*verstehen*" (understand) carry emphasis in the file. The commentary's italics on "systematic and purely immanent consciousness-analysis", "ABC", "curable", "incurable", "sensualistic", "if only", "intelligible" and "pseudo-explanation" (L17.18–19) are its own. The editor's footnote 10, "Cf. Beilage XIII, pp. 349 f.", has lost its marker in the body (src L2275); it belongs somewhere in this closing stretch of the Second Chapter.

The first paragraph builds its science in three steps, each marked by a word. Psychology must begin as a "psychological 'phenomenology'" (*psychologische ,,Phänomenologie''*), and the quotation marks hold the word at a distance: phenomenology in the full sense is transcendental, and a psychology of the same name is phenomenology on loan. Its elementary analyses give it an "ABC". The image is not new in the course. The Eighth Lecture asked how a psychology could get on the right path without "a systematic *elementary analysis of consciousness as consciousness of something*", "the ABC, as it were, of soul-life" (src L1136–1141, Hua VII 53). Here the alphabet has become a science, and an a priori one, which "precedes" every experiential psychology "of necessity". The phrase in quotation marks, "that is to be able to step forth as strict science", lends that empirical psychology the pathos of strict science only on condition of the a priori science that comes first.

The verdict on psychologism follows at once, and its grammar is that of a conditional concession. A psychologism "on the ground of" such a phenomenological psychology is curable, and the parenthesis widens the concession: even if the psychology "had misjudged its apriori character". Two misreadings are thus forgiven in advance, the transcendental one (taking the analyses to be about human minds) and the eidetic one (taking them to be empirical). Neither touches the analyses. The sentence that follows holds two judgements that look incompatible. Psychologism would have made a genuine theory of reason "impossible in principle" (*im Prinzip unmöglich*), and yet it would have been "a relatively easily corrected error". Both hold because the impossibility lies in the claim and the correctability in the material. The condition is "if only" (*wenn nur*) the analyses were drawn "from actual intuition" and were "actually intentional". The cure is then the reduction: "bracketing" (*„Einklammerung“*) the objectivity posited along with the analyses leaves them "preserved in their essential kernel" (*in ihrem Wesenskern erhalten*). The word "kernel" says what the cure acts on. It removes a shell of positing and leaves what was seen.

The second paragraph explains incurability through the one emphasized word, "*understand*". To uncover intentionality is to understand, and the sentence makes understanding the whole of the analysis: it runs from cognition to its formations, to "truth" and "right", and to the "constitutive" connections that form them. Where one has not even begun "to see the kind of intentional implication", nothing has been understood, so nothing remains for a change of attitude to preserve. Sensualism's assertions are "from the outset no actual findings" (*keine wirklichen Feststellungen*), and the bracketing that saves a psychologism's findings has nothing to work on here. The paragraph ends by drawing the consequence for genesis, and the inference is weaker than its connective. "But psychological genesis too is in its essence intelligible genesis" is asserted, not shown, and "and so" (*und so*) turns it into the verdict that "every" naturalistic psychology offers "necessarily" only a "pseudo-explanation" (*Scheinerklärung*). The pair of explanation and understanding is decided by definition: if genesis is intelligible by essence, a causal account that does not understand cannot explain it.

The passage closes the Second Section's Second Chapter, which had uncovered intentionality, the constitution of externality and synthesis beneath Locke's naturalized "ideas". The Third Chapter turns to the abstraction theory, where the "a priori" of this passage is the point at issue: empiricism denies the intuitive givenness of the general, so it cannot even conceive the eidetic science the passage requires. The pair curable and incurable does not return by name. Its substance does, and in a form that complicates the verdict. Hume's psychology is a "*tabula-rasa* psychology" that seeks its laws "in the sensualistically interpreted domain of the *ego cogito*", and yet, "at most", his *Treatise* is "the first draft of a pure phenomenology, but in the shape of a purely sensualistic and empirical phenomenology" (src L2720, Hua VII 157). The Twenty-sixth Lecture makes the task explicit: against the "immanent psychologism" the task was "to do justice to its content of genuineness" and "to make subjectivism true in a higher sense", which even Leibniz could not do with Locke's "sensualistic-empiricist intuitionism" (src L3043, Hua VII 185). Sensualism is incurable as psychology. As history it can still be made true: what is taken up from Hume is the problem his analyses posed, not the analyses.

Beilage XIII, which the editor's footnote cites and dates to about 1926, states the cure as Husserl's own history. "The evident kernel of truth of Locke's psychologism had to be held fast" (*Der offenbare Wahrheitskern des Locke'schen Psychologismus mußte festgehalten werden*), and the reduction of the empirical to the transcendental ego yielded "a reduction of Locke's purely psychic experience into a transcendental experience"; "to psychological description and genetic research there then corresponds a transcendental one" (src L5403, Hua VII 349). "Kernel of truth" answers the lecture's "essential kernel". The second draft of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* article (1927), printed in Hua IX, turns the concession into a theorem: every genuine pure psychology of cognition can be "turned" transcendentally, every genuine transcendental theory of cognition can be turned back into a psychology, "and on both sides this holds sentence for sentence" (*beiderseits gilt das Satz für Satz*; Hua IX file, src L4539–4549, c. Hua IX 266–267). The file has a seam just before this sentence (src L4535–4536), so the claim this sentence calls "the converse" (*das Umgekehrte*) is lost there, but the sentence itself is whole. The same draft calls Hume's *Treatise* "in a certain way the first attempt at a 'phenomenology'", with the quotation marks the lecture put on "psychological 'phenomenology'" (src L4535). The 1923 lecture states as a concession what 1927 states as a parallelism: a psychologism that saw something can be cured sentence for sentence, and a sensualism that saw nothing cannot be cured at all.

## Lecture 19, Hua VII 134: the general conjured away

Reads [Lecture 19, block 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#nineteenth-lecture-the-necessity-of-the-extension-of-the-idea-of-intuition).

Empiricism does not refute the consciousness of the general; it makes it disappear. The verb Husserl chooses, "conjures away" (*eskamotiert … weg*), is a conjurer's word, and the charge is precise: as in a trick, the thing is there the whole time, seen by everyone, and goes unnoticed because attention is led elsewhere. The empiricist points to the single intuition that does function in every intuitive thought about generalities and declares that beyond it there is "nothing further" than "mere representation". Husserl's reply turns the empiricist's own name against him: looked at in reflection, this "representation" is nothing other than the general intuiting it was meant to replace. The understanding the passage carries is that the empiricist theory of abstraction lives on what it denies.

> Quite otherwise the empiricist doctrine of abstraction since Locke. It conjures away the consciousness of generality and the general itself by pointing to the single intuitions that necessarily function in actually evident intuitive thinking, and then saying: beyond these there is "nothing further" than mere representation. For example, the seen or imagined triangle serves in geometrically intuitive thinking as representative for any triangle whatever. But what is this "mere representation"? Looked at exactly, if we keep to the insightful thinking in question itself, say in the example "a triangle überhaupt", to it itself as it is to be questioned in immediate reflection, this representation is nothing other at all than the denied general intuiting itself, however little apt the ambiguous word representation may be for it. But it is the naturalistic attitude that makes one theoretically blind to it. It is of course lived through by the empiricist too, and in a certain way seen, but it is not allowed to count. Everywhere, dazzled by the model of natural-scientific explanation, one wants to explain in the same way. For the problems of epistemological origin, those of the clarification of the consciousness constituting every kind of objectivity, one substitutes problems of a natural and causal explanation, by regarding the realm of inner experience as a closed field of things with natural laws that explain them. Instead of carrying out transcendentally pure self-knowledge, as retrospective consideration, analysis and immediate description of the contents that make the intentionality of consciousness and its accomplishment intelligible, one falls into psychological-causal constructions on the ground of a naturally misinterpreted inner experience; so here too for the realm of general givennesses and of the consciousness of generality. And yet all such constructions draw the semblance of explanation that clings to them from the theoretically disregarded consciousness, whose contents of sense mingle, shimmering, with the causally substructed ones.

German:

> Ganz anders die empiristische Abstraktionslehre seit Locke. Sie eskamotiert das Allgemeinheitsbewußtsein und das Allgemeine selbst dadurch weg, daß sie auf die bei wirklich evidentem anschaulichen Denken notwendig fungierenden Einzelanschauungen hinweist und dann sagt: darüber hinaus liege ,,nichts weiter" vor als bloße Repräsentation. Beispielsweise das gesehene oder fingierte Dreieck dient im geometrisch anschaulichen Denken als Repräsentant für jedes beliebige Dreieck. Aber was ist diese ,,bloße Repräsentation"? Genau besehen, wenn wir uns an das betreffende einsichtige Denken selbst, etwa im Beispiel ,,ein Dreieck überhaupt", halten, an es selbst, wie es in unmittelbarer Reflexion zu befragen ist, ist diese Repräsentation gar nichts anderes als das geleugnete allgemeine Anschauen selbst — mag dabei das vieldeutige Wort Repräsentation auch wenig passend sein. Die naturalistische Einstellung ist es aber, die dafür theoretisch blind macht. Es ist natürlich auch vom Empiristen erlebt und in gewisser Weise gesehen, aber es wird nicht gelten gelassen. Überall will man, geblendet von dem Vorbild naturwissenschaftlicher Erklärung, in gleicher Weise erklären. Den Problemen des erkenntnistheoretischen Ursprungs, denen der Aufklärung jede Art Objektivität konstituierender Bewußtseins, unterschiebt man Probleme einer naturalen und kausalen Erklärung, indem man das Reich der inneren Erfahrung wie ein geschlossenes Sachenfeld ansieht, mit zugehörigen erklärenden Naturgesetzen. Statt transzendental reine Selbsterkenntnis zu vollziehen, als rückschauende Betrachtung, Analyse und unmittelbare Beschreibung die Bewußtseinsintentionalität und ihre Leistung verständlichmachende Gehalte, verfällt man in psychologisch-kausale Konstruktionen auf dem Grunde einer natural mißdeuteten inneren Erfahrung; so auch hier für das Reich der allgemeinen Gegebenheiten und des Allgemeinheitsbewußtseins. Und dabei beziehen doch alle solchen Konstruktionen den Schein der Erklärung, der ihnen anhaftet, aus dem theoretisch mißachteten Bewußtsein, dessen Sinnesgehalte sich mit den kausal substrüierten schillernd mengen.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph opens Hua VII 134 and has no emphasis in the file; the commentary's italics on "conjures away", "nothing other than the denied general intuiting itself", "experienced", "seen", "let count", "origin", "transcendental pure self-knowledge" and "semblance of explanation" (L19.4–5) are its own. Two phrases are ungrammatical as the file has them: "jede Art Objektivität konstituierender Bewußtseins" lacks an article ("des … Bewußtseins", of the consciousness constituting every kind of objectivity), and "die … verständlichmachende Gehalte" needs a genitive ("der … Gehalte", of the contents that make … intelligible). The sense is clear in both, and the rendering follows it. "Fingierte" is "imagined" (made up in phantasy), not "fictionalized".

The trick has two moves, and the passage names both. The first is a gesture of pointing: the empiricist "points to" (*hinweist*) the single intuitions, which really are there and really do function. The second is a word: beyond them lies "'nothing further'" (*,,nichts weiter"*) than "mere representation" (*bloße Repräsentation*). The quotation marks set the empiricist's own phrase on display, and Husserl answers it with a phrase built the same way: this representation is "nothing other at all than the denied general intuiting itself" (*gar nichts anderes als das geleugnete allgemeine Anschauen selbst*). The deflationary "nothing further" becomes the identifying "nothing other". The general is not added to the single intuition from outside; it is what the word "representation" has been covering. "Denied" (*geleugnet*) names the move exactly, since what is denied must first have been there to deny.

The arbiter is reflection. Husserl keeps "to the insightful thinking in question itself", to "it itself as it is to be questioned in immediate reflection", and the doubled "itself" (*selbst … an es selbst*) carries the whole weight of the reply. Here the inference is thinner than its confidence. The empiricist who says that reflection shows only a single triangle used as a sign is not answered by a second appeal to reflection, and Husserl does not offer an argument that would settle it. He offers a diagnosis of why the other side does not see: "the naturalistic attitude … makes one theoretically blind". The next two sentences sharpen this into the conjurer's situation. The general intuiting "is of course lived through by the empiricist too, and in a certain way seen, but it is not allowed to count" (*es wird nicht gelten gelassen*). Blindness of this kind is not a failure of sight. It is a refusal of standing, and the word "representation", conceded to be "ambiguous" (*vieldeutig*), is the instrument of the refusal.

The second half of the paragraph says where attention was led instead: to explanation. "Dazzled" (*geblendet*) by the model of natural science, the empiricist substitutes causal problems for problems of origin, treating inner experience "as a closed field of things" (*wie ein geschlossenes Sachenfeld*). The last sentence completes the image of the trick. The causal constructions draw their "semblance of explanation" (*Schein der Erklärung*) from the very consciousness they disregard, whose sense-contents "mingle, shimmering" (*schillernd mengen*) with the causally substructed ones. The illusion succeeds because it borrows what it hides. This is the "pseudo-explanation" (*Scheinerklärung*) of the Seventeenth Lecture (src L2271, Hua VII 126) shown at work: there a naturalistic psychology was said to offer only a pseudo-explanation of genesis, and here the reason is given. Its apparent success is the unacknowledged success of understanding.

The passage stands at the turn of the Nineteenth Lecture. Before it, Husserl has laid out the field the empiricist conjures away: the different modes of generality in "the red", "some red", "every red", and the exact and the morphological universals of geometry and botany, a problematic "endlessly progressing … for one who has learned to grasp and describe consciousness as consciousness" (src L2365–2370, Hua VII 133). After it comes the consequence: the error of the theory of abstraction concerns "the whole realm of the Logos", and the *tabula-rasa* empiricism becomes "an extreme scepticism, though one that remains hidden from itself" (src L2378, Hua VII 135). The conjuring explains why the scepticism is hidden. One page on, the representation that was "mere" has become "the mythical representation" (*die mythische Repräsentation*), which cannot ground our right to assert general propositions such as "red is different from green" (src L2383). The trick began with Locke's own account in the Eighteenth Lecture, where the abstract idea is an individual moment that "the mind uses as representative or pattern" (*als Repräsentanten oder Musterbild*), so that general words become possible (src L2299, Hua VII 128).

The verb returns once, for Hume. His "extreme *nominalism*" is "completely blind to general seeing, and, as we already discussed in the critique of *Locke*, in this blindness it tries to conjure away all general thinking" (*alles allgemeine Denken wegzueskamotieren*), by substituting natural connections of singular items: "connections about which it of course makes general statements, about whose right one need only forget to ask" (src L2880, Hua VII 172). The file reads "durch Unterscheidung" (by distinguishing), but the textual notes record "durch Unterschiebung naturaler" (by substitution of natural), a C reading in place of "durch naturale" (apparatus to 172,32, src L7202); the OCR has turned *Unterschiebung* into *Unterscheidung*. "Need only forget to ask" (*nach deren Recht zu fragen man nur vergessen muß*) is the trick stated as a condition of its working.

The reply is older than the course. In the Second Logical Investigation, "the legitimate sense of general representation" (§27) concedes the word on one condition: if representation could be understood as the "new mode of consciousness" that is built on intuition and in which the consciousness of generality is characterized, "then all would be in order" (*dann wäre alles in Ordnung*), and the intuited image could be called a representative of the species. The same section names the "ambiguity" (*Vieldeutigkeit*) of the old talk of representative function (LU II/1, Hua XIX/1 178–179, A 172–173; src L3851–3853). In 1901 the identification of representation with general consciousness was offered as the one way to save the word. In 1923 it is the charge against those who use it. The theory is unchanged; what has changed is that the empiricist's word is now read as concealment, with the naturalistic attitude named as what does the concealing.

## Lecture 19, Hua VII 137–138: intuition measured to meaning

Reads [Lecture 19, block 12](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#nineteenth-lecture-the-necessity-of-the-extension-of-the-idea-of-intuition).

The lecture's title phrase, the "extension of the idea of intuition" (*Extension der Idee der Anschauung*), names a change in the measure of intuition, not only in its range. For the empiricist, what can be intuited is fixed in advance by the senses, and whatever is meant beyond that must be explained away. Husserl fixes the range from the other side. Experiencing is "consciousness of self-giving", and its scope is whatever can be meant, in whatever form of assertion, and then be given "exactly in this sense-forming". Intuition "measures itself" (*sich … anmißt*) to meaning. The *is* and the *not*, the *and* and the *if* become possible objects of intuition, because they are forms of what is meant.

> But of greater interest here still is the following: how does it stand with the entire grammatical forms of assertions, and already of individual assertions, with subject-form and predicate-form, with the Is and the Not, with the And and the Or, the If and the Then, and so on? We say in ordinary speech: I see that this house has a red roof, I hear that this tone is higher than that one; and we do not speak merely of seeing or hearing the house, the roof, the tone itself. In nature there are things, but in nature there are not, after all, these states of affairs with their subject- and predicate-forms, the relation of the Higher and on the other side, as another thing, the counter-relation of the Lower, which in each case is itself again a dependent moment in the intuited of the whole proposition, in the state of affairs. In fact, "experiencing" is not merely experiencing individual data; experiencing is consciousness of self-giving, of self-grasping, of self-having, and indeed of whatever can be meant at all and in any shapes of consciousness, meant in countless shapes of assertion with single forms, and then be given exactly in this forming of sense, itself seen and grasped as truly existing. Without this extension of the idea of intuition, which measures itself exactly to conscious meaning, possibly a wholly unintuitive meaning, in all its shapes, there can be no talk of a description of the situation of cognition or of an understanding about cognition and true being.

German:

> Aber von größerem Interesse ist hier noch folgendes: wie steht es denn mit den gesamten grammatischen Formen der Aussagen, und schon individueller Aussagen, mit der Subjektform und Prädikatform, mit dem Ist und dem Nicht, mit dem Und und dem Oder, dem Wenn und dem So usw.? Wir sagen in gewöhnlicher Rede: ich sehe, daß dieses Haus ein rotes Dach hat, ich höre, daß dieser Ton höher ist als jener; und wir sprechen nicht bloß vom Sehen bzw. Hören des Hauses, Daches, Tones selbst. In der Natur sind Dinge, aber in der Natur sind doch nicht diese Sachverhalte mit ihren Subjekt- und Prädikatformen, die Relation des Höher und andererseits als ein anderes Ding die Gegenrelation des Tiefer, die in jedem Falle selbst wieder unselbständiges Moment im Anschaulichen des ganzen Satzes, im Sachverhalt ist. In der Tat, „Erfahren“ ist nicht bloß individuelle Daten Erfahren, Erfahren ist Bewußtsein der Selbstgebung, des Selbsterfassens, Selbsthabens, und zwar von solchem, was überhaupt und in irgendwelchen Bewußtseinsgestalten gemeint, in unzähligen Aussagegestalten mit Einzelformen gemeint, und dann genau in dieser Sinnesformung eben gegeben, selbst erschaut und als wahrhaft seiend erfaßt sein kann. Ohne diese Extension der Idee der Anschauung, die sich dem bewußtseinsmäßigen Meinen, dem ev. ganz unanschaulichen, in allen Gestalten genau anmißt, ist von einer Beschreibung der Erkenntnislage und einer Verständigung über Erkenntnis und wahres Sein keine Rede.

*Note on the text.* The passage runs across the page break from Hua VII 137 to 138 (src L2468–2469, inside "Gegen-/relation"); the sentence runs on correctly. The file shows no emphasis anywhere in it, and it is set line by line as on the printed page, so the commentary's italics on "*grammatical forms*", "*is*", "*not*", "*see*", "*things*", "*states-of-affairs*" and "*extension of the idea of intuition*" (L19.12) cannot be confirmed; in a stretch transcribed line by line the converter may have lost them. Only the quotation marks around „Erfahren“ (experiencing) are certain. "Dem Wenn und dem So" is "the If and the Then": *so* answers *wenn* as the consequent of a conditional.

The question that opens the passage is put as a matter of grammar. Subject-form and predicate-form, "the Is and the Not", "the And and the Or, the If and the Then": Husserl capitalizes the particles into nouns (*dem Ist und dem Nicht*), and the nominalization is the claim in small. What grammar treats as connective words become objects one can ask about, with a "how does it stand with" of their own. The evidence offered is idiom: "We say in ordinary speech: I see that this house has a red roof." Here the inference is weaker than the wording. That we say "I see that" shows that the language of perception extends to states of affairs; it does not show that the extension is more than a manner of speaking. The Sixth Logical Investigation knew this. It grants that one can say "intelligibly and unambiguously" *ich sehe, daß dieses Papier weiß ist* (I see that this paper is white), but adds that "the meaning of this talk need not be that the meaning of the uttered sentence gives expression to a mere seeing" (Hua XIX/2 660, A 602–603; src L1561). In 1901 the idiom was the problem, and the solution was a founded act of a new kind. In 1923 the idiom is cited as support and the analysis is presupposed.

The sentence on nature carries the ontology. "In nature there are things, but in nature there are not, after all, these states of affairs": things (*Dinge*) and states of affairs (*Sachverhalte*) are set apart, and the relation "of the Higher" and its counter-relation "of the Lower" are said to be "dependent moments" (*unselbständiges Moment*) of the state of affairs, not parts of nature. The sentence trips once on its own vocabulary: the counter-relation is introduced "as another thing" (*als ein anderes Ding*). The phrase means only "as something else", but it lets *Ding* back in at the point where things were being excluded, and it shows how little the everyday word respects the distinction the sentence draws.

The definition then turns on the word in quotation marks. "'Experiencing' is not merely experiencing individual data" (*„Erfahren“ ist nicht bloß individuelle Daten Erfahren*): the first *Erfahren* is the word as the empiricist uses it, held at a distance, and the second is the empiricist's restriction, which the sentence refuses. In its place comes a triple: "consciousness of self-giving, of self-grasping, of self-having" (*der Selbstgebung, des Selbsterfassens, Selbsthabens*). The three compounds share the prefix "self", and the prefix carries the definition. Experience is not defined by its source in sensation but by the mode in which its object is there, itself. The object is then left wholly open: "whatever can be meant at all", "in countless shapes of assertion". The decisive phrase is "exactly in this forming of sense" (*genau in dieser Sinnesformung*): the state of affairs is given as it is meant, form included, or it is not given.

The last sentence states the extension and its measure in one clause. Intuition "measures itself exactly" to meaning, "possibly a wholly unintuitive" meaning (*dem ev. ganz unanschaulichen*). The measure of intuition is set by what can be emptily meant. The verb has a history inside the passage's own paragraph. A page earlier the concepts "tone" and "being-higher" occur in the assertion "in being measured to intuition" (*in Anmessung an die Anschauung*, src L2443); in the passage intuition measures itself to meaning; in the next paragraph fulfilment takes place "in 'measuring' to" the shape of self-giving (*in „Anmessung“ an solche Gestalt*, src L2496). The measuring runs both ways, and its reciprocity is what Husserl calls fulfilment. The Sixth Investigation used the same verb for the same relation: the expression "with its changing forms measures itself" (*sich … anmißt*) to the founded acts in which it finds fulfilment (src L1561).

In the lecture the passage moves the argument from abstraction to cognition as such. The block before it (L19.11) showed that empiricism cannot even account for a singular assertion, because "tone" and "higher" are general concepts. The passage widens the point from concepts to forms. The next paragraph draws the consequence: only if we "identify experience or intuition with evidence", and see that this widened "experience" (*erweiterte „Erfahrung“*) is "nothing other than self-grasping of the meant exactly as it is meant", can we understand how "logical objectivity" has "sense and exhibitable being" (src L2482–2493). The Third Section depends on this. Hume's nominalism is "completely blind to general seeing" (src L2880, Hua VII 172), and the charge presupposes the extended concept of seeing set out here.

The Sixth Investigation's chapter on sensuous and categorial intuition had drawn the extension as a consequence of the sameness of fulfilment. The "essential sameness of kind of the fulfilling function" makes it "unavoidable" to call every act that fulfils by confirming self-presentation a perception, "every fulfilling act at all an intuition", and its correlate an object; with the concept of perception, imagination too "must undergo a corresponding extension" (*eine entsprechende Extension erfahren muß*; §45, Hua XIX/2 672, A 614–615; src L1710–1712). The 1923 lecture keeps the word *Extension* and the argument from fulfilment. It drops the distinction of founded acts that the Investigation needed, and puts in its place the formula that later readings depend on: experience is self-giving, wherever meaning reaches.

## Lecture 19, Hua VII 139–140: first a priori, *then* the fact

Reads [Lecture 19, block 16](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#nineteenth-lecture-the-necessity-of-the-extension-of-the-idea-of-intuition).

Transcendental philosophy, Husserl says at the close of the Second Section, is "first, and with necessity, a priori philosophy, and *then* application to the fact". The claim is about order. The fact is not left out of transcendental philosophy; it comes second, as one case within a range of free possibilities that the eidetic science surveys first. The emphasized "then" (*dann*) carries the claim: it promises an application that the a priori makes possible, and it makes the fact depend on the essence rather than the other way round. The model is geometry, where a question about a particular natural thing is answered as a question about a possible shape.

> So there marks itself out here, from the critique of empiricism, the thought of a wholly other science of subjectivity out of purely "inner experience", an eidetic science of an I *überhaupt*, of possible pure consciousness überhaupt, of possible objects of consciousness überhaupt, in which all facticity is switched off and is included only within the range of free possibilities, as one of them. On deeper entering it even turns out that all transcendental questions to be put to individual objects and individually determined consciousness, say of these human beings and this world, can and may be treated only in the way that geometrical questions are treated concerning a determinate natural thing and determinate nature überhaupt. That means: the necessary method is that the single case is regarded as a single case of an a priori generality, and the problem is thus transposed from the fact into the realm of pure possibilities and their a priori. Transcendental philosophy is first, and with necessity, a priori philosophy, and *then* application to the fact.

German:

> So zeichnet sich also hier von der Kritik des Empirismus aus der Gedanke einer ganz anderen Wissenschaft von der Subjektivität aus rein „innerer Erfahrung“ ab, einer eidetischen Wissenschaft von einem Ich *überhaupt*, möglichem reinen Bewußtsein überhaupt, möglichen Bewußtseinsgegenständen überhaupt, wobei alle Faktizität ausgeschaltet und nur im Umfang freier Möglichkeiten als eine derselben mitbeschlossen ist. Bei tieferer Eingehen stellt es sich sogar heraus, daß alle transzendentalen Fragen, die an individuelle Gegenstände und individuell bestimmtes Bewußtsein, etwa dieser Menschen und dieser Welt, zu stellen sind, nur so behandelt werden können und dürfen, wie geometrische Fragen in Betreff eines bestimmten Naturdings und der bestimmten Natur überhaupt. Das sagt: die notwendige Methode ist die, daß der Einzelfall als Einzelfall apriorischer Allgemeinheit betrachtet und so das Problem aus dem Faktum in das Reich reiner Möglichkeiten und ihres Apriori versetzt wird. Transzendentale Philosophie ist zuerst, und mit Notwendigkeit, apriorische Philosophie, und *dann* Anwendung auf das Faktum.

*Note on the text.* The passage runs across the page break from Hua VII 139 to 140 (src L2549–2551). Only "*überhaupt*" after "Ich" and "*dann*" (then) carry emphasis in the file. The commentary italicizes the whole first clause, "*these*" humans, "*this*" world, and the whole last sentence (L19.16); the file supports none of these, so its stress falls on the order "first … *then*", not on the thesis as a whole. "Bei tieferer Eingehen" should read "tieferem" (on deeper entering), a slip in the print or the conversion; the sense is not in doubt.

The first sentence states where the thought comes from and what it looks like. It "marks itself out" (*zeichnet sich … ab*) "from the critique of empiricism", so it is presented as a result of the Second Section's critique, not as a doctrine brought to it. The science it marks out is "of subjectivity out of purely 'inner experience'", and the quotation marks around "inner experience" (*„innerer Erfahrung“*) hold the empiricist's phrase at a distance. The experience in question is the widened experience of the lecture's earlier paragraphs (reading 22): self-giving of whatever can be meant, essences included. Without that widening, a science of essences "out of" experience would be a contradiction. The triple "überhaupt" then sets the scope: an I *überhaupt*, possible pure consciousness überhaupt, possible objects of consciousness überhaupt. The emphasis falls on the first, the I in general. The main text's last thesis will speak of an "*ego* überhaupt" (src L3147, Hua VII 199).

The treatment of facticity borrows the vocabulary of the reduction. All facticity is "switched off" (*ausgeschaltet*), the word the course uses for the step Locke's successors were bound to take, purifying his intuitionism "under strict switching off of transcendent presuppositions" (*unter strenger Ausschaltung transzendenter Präsuppositionen*, src L2666, Hua VII 149), and is "included" (*mitbeschlossen*) "only within the range of free possibilities, as one of them". The two participles do opposite things, and the sentence needs both. The fact is excluded as a premise and kept as an instance. Switching it off does not lose it, because the actual is one of the possibles.

The second sentence states the method by analogy and claims more than the analogy shows. "On deeper entering it even turns out" (*stellt es sich sogar heraus*) that all transcendental questions about "these human beings and this world" "can and may" (*können und dürfen*) be treated "only" as geometrical questions about a determinate natural thing. The "even" (*sogar*) and the "only" (*nur*) make a strong claim from an inquiry that is not given here; the next paragraph admits as much: "how that is meant cannot yet be discussed here" (src L2554). The doubled modal joins a claim of possibility to a claim of right. Transcendental questions about the factual can be treated eidetically, and it is illegitimate to treat them otherwise. Geometry supplies the model because there the relation is already familiar: what holds of this table's edge holds of it as a case of a possible straight line, and the geometer does not first collect tables. "That means" (*Das sagt*) restates the analogy as method: the single case is regarded "as a single case of an a priori generality", and the problem is "transposed" (*versetzt*) "from the fact into the realm of pure possibilities".

The last sentence turns method into a thesis about the discipline. "First" (*zuerst*) and "with necessity" (*mit Notwendigkeit*) fix the a priori as the start, and the emphasized "*then*" names a second step that belongs to transcendental philosophy as well: "application to the fact" (*Anwendung auf das Faktum*). The word "application" leaves the fact a place, and the place is subordinate. Transcendental philosophy does not end in the eidetic; it reaches the fact by way of it.

The passage closes the Second Section, and the paragraph that follows names its result: "the idea of a universal science of the essence of pure subjectivity", "an eidetic science of the ego cogito" (*einer eidetischen Wissenschaft vom ego cogito*, src L2554, Hua VII 140). Before it, the lecture has argued that transcendental consciousness "can and must be considered in the method of *essence-intuition*", "in the purely grasped *Platonic method*" (src L2530–2534). The Third Section measures Berkeley and Hume by this standard. The title of the Twenty-fourth Lecture, "The necessary eidetics of the science of consciousness and the inductive-empirical objectivism in Hume" (src L2810), names the contrast. Hume's associative laws are treated as induced facts, where they should have been essence-insights, so in Hume the order of first and then is reversed. In the Twenty-seventh Lecture the course ends on a science of "the pure and absolutely necessary essence of an *ego* überhaupt" (src L3147). The "I *überhaupt*" of this passage has become the main text's last word.

*Cartesian Meditations* gives the method a section of its own and a technique. In §34 the eidetic method is "the principled shaping of the phenomenological method": one varies the perception of this table "in a completely free arbitrariness", and "we transform the fact of this perception … into a pure possibility", so that the type "perception", "lifted out of all facticity", becomes the eidos (Hua I, src L2199). §35 then works "in the frame of a purely eidetic phenomenology, in which the fact of the transcendental ego and the particular givens of its transcendental empirics have only the meaning of examples for pure possibilities" (src L2293, Hua I 107). The 1923 "included only … as one of them" becomes the example of the variation, and the geometrical analogy gives way to free phantasy. The Paris Lectures state the 1923 "then" in a sentence: "All rationality of the fact lies, after all, in the a priori" (*Alle Rationalität des Faktums liegt ja im Apriori*; src L1104, Hua I c. 38). In 1923 the order is stated as a thesis and the method is deferred. By 1929 the method has been worked out, and the thesis has become the reason for it.

## Lecture 20, Hua VII 148: overcoming as making true

Reads [Lecture 20, block 13](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#twentieth-lecture-the-positive-historical-significance-of-the-renewal-of-skepticism-through-locke-and-his-successors).

To overcome a radical scepticism, Husserl says, "*means*" to make it true in a good sense. The formula defines a kind of refutation that keeps what it refutes. A sceptical theory is overcome when the motive that drove it is given its right in a philosophy that can justify itself, so that the scepticism turns out to have been a distorted view of something true. The hyphenated "making-true" (*Wahr-machen*) then names this as work to be done: the critique can show the method only in outline, and the full making-true is the phenomenology that carries it out. The Third Section's reading of Berkeley and Hume as "pre-forms" (*Vorformen*) of phenomenology rests on this formula. Their scepticism is the shape in which a true thought first appeared.

> Radical critique of the empiricism of inner experience is therefore more than any other philosophical critique. It is a critique which, by showing what the empiricist really has before his eyes in absolute givenness and what on the other side he substructs, frees us from the objectivistic prejudices that make us blind to the specifically transcendental, to pure subjectivity and the life and accomplishing that takes place under the title of pure consciousness, in which all possible objectivity appropriates sense and being for a possible I. Precisely thereby the critique opens the way to that accomplishment of overcoming, as of all scepticism, so of the empiricist, which we indicated a little earlier with the words that to overcome a radical scepticism *means* to make it true in a good sense. The making-true itself, and in the fullest sense, of course means the actually executing work, whose method and horizons the critique could make visible only in general. But already the critique makes empiricism true insofar (and in a higher sense than, say, a critical transition from the Sophists to Socrates or Descartes makes these true) as it helps empirical intuitionism as intuitionism to its true right, and so defends empiricism, as it were, against itself, lets its true I and its leading idea, as it were, come to speech, or prepares out of the pseudo-empiricism the true and genuine empiricism.

German:

> Radikale Kritik des Empirismus der inneren Erfahrung ist also mehr als eine sonstige philosophische Kritik. Es ist eine Kritik, die, aufzeigend, was der Empirist wirklich in absoluter Gegebenheit vor Augen hat und was er andererseits substruiert, uns von den objektivistischen Vorurteilen befreit, die uns für das spezifisch Transzendentale blind machen, für die reine Subjektivität und das unter dem Titel reinen Bewußtseins sich vollziehende Leben und Leisten, in dem sich alle mögliche Objektivität Sinn und Sein für ein mögliches Ich zueignet. Eben damit eröffnet die Kritik den Weg zu derjenigen Leistung der Überwindung wie aller, so der empiristischen Skepsis, die wir vorhin mit den Worten andeuteten, einen radikalen Skeptizismus überwinden *heiße*, ihn in einem guten Sinne wahrmachen. Das Wahr-machen selbst und im vollsten Sinn, das sagt natürlich: die wirklich ausführende Arbeit, deren Methode und Horizonte die Kritik nur im allgemeinen sichtlich machen konnte. Aber schon die Kritik macht den Empirismus insofern wahr (und in höherem Sinn als etwa ein kritischer Übergang von der Sophistik zu Sokrates oder Descartes diese wahrmacht), als sie dem empirischen Intuitionismus als Intuitionismus zu seinem wahren Rechte verhilft und somit den Empirismus gleichsam gegen ihn selbst verteidigt, gleichsam sein wahres Ich und seine Leitidee zum Worte kommen läßt, oder aus dem Scheinempirismus den wahren und echten Empirismus herausräpariert.

*Note on the text.* "Herausräpariert" at the end is an OCR slip for "herauspräpariert" (prepares out, dissects out); the rendering follows the evident word. "*Heiße*" (means) is the only emphasis in the paragraph; the commentary's italics on "*more*", "*substructs*", "*transcendental*", "*make it true in a good sense*", "*empirical intuitionism as intuitionism*", "*against itself*" and "*pseudo-empiricism the true and genuine empiricism*" (L20.13) are its own. The formula "a little earlier" is at src L2581 (Hua VII 143), where the file prints "waHRZUmachen", a garbled rendering of spaced type, with stray margin line numbers (5, 10, 15 …) at the starts of lines. The word is *wahrzumachen*, emphasized.

The emphasis falls on the verb of definition, not on the definiens. "To overcome a radical scepticism *means* to make it true" (*überwinden heiße, … wahrmachen*): the stress on *heiße* claims an equation, and the subjunctive reports it as said "a little earlier". There, at the opening of the lecture, Locke's psychologism, "a scepticism that is, to be sure, veiled from itself", was said to set the new philosophy "the great demand to overcome it in the form that is the necessary one for every radical scepticism, namely to make it true in a higher sense" (src L2580–2581). The adjective changes between the two statements, from "higher" (*höheren*) to "good" (*guten*), and returns to "higher" in the comparison inside the passage. The variation shows that the qualifier does the work. Making scepticism true without qualification would be scepticism. Making it true "in a good sense" means that its claim is kept and its negation dropped.

The second sentence splits the making-true in two. "The making-true itself, and in the fullest sense" (*Das Wahr-machen selbst und im vollsten Sinn*) is the "actually executing work" (*die wirklich ausführende Arbeit*). The hyphen turns the verb into a term and lets the parts be heard: to make (*machen*) something true is a labour, not a verdict. The critique has only made the method "visible in general". "But already the critique" makes empiricism true "insofar as", and the "insofar" (*insofern*) limits the claim to what a critique can do: it gives "empirical intuitionism as intuitionism" its right. Here the passage moves its object without saying so. What is overcome is scepticism; what is made true is intuitionism. The sceptical element of empiricism, its denial that objective cognition is possible, is not made true at all. It is cut away. What survives is the principle that all cognition must go back to its intuitive sources. The formula sounds like a reconciliation with scepticism, and it works by separating the sceptic's principle from his conclusion.

The last clause gives empiricism a self. The critique "defends empiricism, as it were, against itself" (*gleichsam gegen ihn selbst*), lets "its true I and its leading idea" (*sein wahres Ich und seine Leitidee*) "come to speech", and "prepares out of the pseudo-empiricism the true and genuine empiricism" (*aus dem Scheinempirismus den wahren und echten Empirismus*). The double "as it were" (*gleichsam*) marks the figure as figure. A philosophy is treated as a subject with a will it fails to understand, and the critic speaks for its "leading idea" against its doctrines. "Pseudo-empiricism" belongs to the same family as the "pseudo-explanation" of the Seventeenth Lecture (src L2271, Hua VII 126, reading 20) and the "semblance of explanation" of the Nineteenth (src L2374, reading 21). The semblance there was what the theory borrowed from the consciousness it disregarded; here the genuine empiricism is what the critique recovers from it.

The paragraph before the passage says what the true empiricism is. Philosophy "drives toward an intuitionism", but the genuine one is "not that of Locke" nor of the immanent philosophy issuing from him, which "ends necessarily in scepticism and countersense"; it is "the intuitionism of transcendental phenomenology" (src L2652, Hua VII 147). The comparison inside the passage then ranks this making-true above the classical ones. A critical transition "from the Sophists to Socrates or Descartes" also made its sceptics true, but in a lower sense. The course had used the formula at its turn to the moderns: "the deepest sense of modern philosophy" is the task "to make the radical subjectivism of the sceptical tradition true in a higher sense", by overcoming the "paradoxical, playful, frivolous" subjectivism through "the transcendental subjectivism" (src L1226, Hua VII 61). The Twenty-sixth Lecture finds the task unfulfilled on the rationalist side: against the new "immanent psychologism" the task was "to make subjectivism true in a higher sense", and "even a Leibniz" could not draw out of Locke's "sensualistic-empiricist intuitionism" "the genuine idea of a transcendental intuitionism" (src L3043, Hua VII 185). Making-true is the measure by which the Third Section ranks its figures. Berkeley and Hume carry the sceptical consequence through; the rationalists refute without making true.

*Ideas I* had already claimed the empiricist's own word. In the section titled "Empiricism as Scepticism" (§20), Husserl writes: "If 'positivism' means as much as the absolutely unprejudiced grounding of all sciences on the 'positive', i.e. what is to be grasped originarily, then we are the genuine positivists" (*dann sind wir die echten Positivisten*; Hua III/1 c. 45, src L1236–1238 of the recheck file, confirmed against the CW2 translation, src L701). The lecture's "true and genuine empiricism" repeats that claim, and the Seventeenth Lecture's "positivism in a good sense" (src L2266) repeats it in the lecture's own words. In 1913 the claim was polemical: it took the word away from the empiricists. In 1923 it is historical: it presents empiricism as phenomenology that did not yet understand itself.

## Lecture 21, Hua VII 151: the identical thing and its adumbrations

Reads [Lecture 21, block 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#twenty-first-lecture-berkeleys-discovery-and--naturalistic-misinterpretation-of-the-problem-of-the-constitution-of-the-real-world).

Against Berkeley's sensualism Husserl sets one distinction, and the passage rests on it: the change of the modes of appearance against the appearing thing itself. The thing seen in continuous perception is given as identical, and what is evidently identical cannot be the constant change of sensuous data. The data adumbrate (*abschatten*) the thing; they present it from moment to moment in shifting aspects, and the thing is the unity that holds across them. Berkeley saw that perception gives the world itself and not a copy. He then took the world to be the data, because he had no concept of synthesis. The understanding the passage carries is that identity across appearances is itself given, evidently and within immanence, and that missing it turns the right discovery into a naturalism.

> On the other side, Berkeley remains caught in the naturalism of the *tabula-rasa* conception. Sensualistically he confuses the thing perceived at any moment, in its evidence of being perceived, with the respective complex of sensation-data, of visual, tactual, acoustic and other data, without becoming aware that the ***identical*** thing evidently given in the continuity of perceiving, precisely as evidently identical, cannot possibly be the constant change of the sensuous data. He, like all sensualists, indeed all psychologists of the naturalistic school, misses the evident distinction, to be grasped within immanence, between the change of the modes of appearance, of the steadily shifting aspects, which already concerns every single mark of the thing, and the appearing thing itself and its appearing marks, and indeed purely as appearing. If one lets these adumbrating appearances count as sensation-data, the Berkeleyan thesis therefore says that the experienced world of the cognizer is nothing other than his respective complexes of sensation, that he [substitutes] for the unity of the experienced thing the manifold of its adumbrating appearances; which is connected precisely with his being blind to the consciousness of a thing as consciousness of unity, and to the synthesis of consciousness that holds sway in continuous experiencing, and to the experienced thing itself as synthetic unity in the continuity of manifold meaning.

German:

> Andererseits bleibt Berkeley im Naturalismus der *tabula-rasa-* Auffassung befangen. Sensualistisch verwechselt er das jeweilig wahrgenommene Ding in seiner Evidenz der Wahrgenommenerheit mit dem jeweiligen Komplex von Empfindungsdaten, von visuellen, taktuellen, akustischen und sonstigen Daten, ohne dessen innezuwerden, daß das in der Kontinuität des Wahrnehmens evident gegebene ***identische*** Ding eben als evident identisches unmöglich der beständige Wandel der sinnglichen Daten sein kann. Ihm wie allen Sensualisten, ja allen Psychologisten der naturalistischen Schule entgeht die evidente und innerhalb der Immanenz zu erfassende Unterscheidung zwischen dem Wandel der Erscheinungsweisen, der sich stetig verschiebenden Aspekte, der schon jedes einzelne Dingmerkmal betrifft, und dem erscheinenden Ding selbst und seinen erscheinenden Merkmalen, und zwar rein als erscheinenden. Läßt man diese abschattenden Erscheinungen als Empfindungsdaten gelten, so besagt also die Berkeley'sche These, daß die erfahrene Welt des Erkennenden nichts anderes sei als seine jeweiligen Empfindungskomplexe, daß er der Einheit des erfahrenen Dinges unterscheidet die Mannigfaltigkeit seiner abschattenden Erscheinungen, was eben damit zusammenhängt, daß er blind ist für das Dingbewußtsein als Einheitsbewußtsein und für die im kontinuierlichen Erfahren waltende Bewußtseinssynthesis und für das erfahrene Ding selbst als synthetische Einheit in der Kontinuität der mannigfaltigen Meinung [^1].

*Note on the text.* "***identische***" (identical) is set in bold italic, the file's strongest emphasis, and it is the only emphasis in the paragraph besides "*tabula-rasa-*"; the commentary's italics on "*caught in the naturalism*", "*Sensualistically*", "*complex of sensation-data*", "*continuity*", "*be*", "*distinction between the change of the modes of appearance*", "*appearing thing itself*", "*manifold of its adumbrating appearances*", "*blind for the thing-consciousness as unity-consciousness*" and "*synthetic unity in the continuity of the manifold meaning*" (L21.6) are its own. "Sinnglichen" is a misprint for *sinnlichen* (sensuous). One clause is damaged: "daß er der Einheit des erfahrenen Dinges unterscheidet die Mannigfaltigkeit" cannot be construed, since *unterscheiden* does not take this dative. The sense and the construction fit *unterschiebt* (substitutes … for), the verb Husserl uses two lectures earlier for the same move ("unterschiebt man Probleme", src L2374), and the commentary reads it so. The rendering puts [substitutes] in brackets; the print must settle it. Footnote [^1] (src L2682) belongs to the end of the paragraph.

The first sentence locates the error exactly. Berkeley "confuses" (*verwechselt*) the perceived thing "in its evidence of being perceived" (*in seiner Evidenz der Wahrgenommenerheit*) with a complex of data. The coinage *Wahrgenommenerheit* (the state of being perceived) concedes Berkeley's own principle: the thing is taken only as perceived, inside immanence. Husserl does not object that there is a thing outside perception. He objects that within perception, as perceived, the thing is not a complex of data. The ground is given by the one word the file sets in its strongest emphasis: the thing is "***identical***", and "precisely as evidently identical" it "cannot possibly be" (*unmöglich … sein kann*) the constant change of data. The argument is short, and its whole weight lies on "evidently". If identity is given, it cannot be a sum of what changes. A sensualist can deny that identity is given and call it a habit of the imagination. Husserl's argument does not answer him; it states the evidence the sensualist would have to deny.

The second sentence names what is missed and where it is to be found. The distinction is "evident" and "to be grasped within immanence" (*innerhalb der Immanenz zu erfassende*). Both qualifiers turn Berkeley's own method against him: the pure inner attitude he adopted is enough to see it. On one side stand "the change of the modes of appearance" (*der Wandel der Erscheinungsweisen*) and "the steadily shifting aspects" (*der sich stetig verschiebenden Aspekte*), which already concern "every single mark of the thing". On the other stand "the appearing thing itself and its appearing marks, and indeed purely as appearing" (*rein als erscheinenden*). The last phrase guards the distinction against a realist reading. The thing that appears is not a thing in itself behind the appearances; it is the appearing thing, the one that the aspects are aspects of.

The third sentence introduces the term and states the error in its terms. "If one lets these adumbrating appearances count as sensation-data" (*Läßt man diese abschattenden Erscheinungen als Empfindungsdaten gelten*): the conditional concedes that the appearances may be called data, and the participle "adumbrating" (*abschattend*) says what they do. They present the thing in shades, perspectivally, from one side and then another. Berkeley's thesis that the world is "nothing other than" (*nichts anderes … als*) complexes of sensation then means that the manifold of adumbrations is put in the place of the unity. The final clause gives the reason in three phrases of increasing precision: blindness "to the consciousness of a thing as consciousness of unity" (*Einheitsbewußtsein*), to "the synthesis of consciousness" (*Bewußtseinssynthesis*) in continuous experience, and to the thing as "synthetic unity in the continuity of manifold meaning" (*in der Kontinuität der mannigfaltigen Meinung*). The thing is a unity of meaning across many meanings of it. The footnote then extends the point to the single property: Hobbes and Locke identify the perceived thing with a complex that "has" its data "as collective elements, but anything but as properties", whereas "already every single property" is "evident synthetic unity". The colour I see unchanged while I move my eyes is seen "as this identical one", while "the sensation-data that present this same colour from moment to moment (the adumbrations of the colour-perspective)" constantly change (src L2682).

The paragraph before the passage gives Berkeley his due: he "restitutes the right of natural experience"; "perception does not substruct, makes no inferences" (src L2675, Hua VII 151). The passage takes back the price of that right. The two halves together are the "discovery and naturalistic misinterpretation" of the lecture's title. The paragraph after it draws the sensualist consequence: what gives unity to the separate data of the senses is "mere association", and "the things are nothing other than associative complexes" (src L2680). Synthesis, missed, is replaced by association, and the Hume lectures inherit the substitution. The identity problem then moves from the thing to the I. For Hume the I is "nothing further than a bundle of different perceptions", and so "how does it come about that I take myself for an identical I … in this constant change of perceptions?" (src L2730, Hua VII 158). The question the passage answered for the thing, by appeal to evident synthetic unity, Hume answers for the self by fiction.

The term had appeared once before in the course, in the Fourteenth Lecture, in a passage that reads like a footnote set into the body (it begins mid-sentence at src L1788), which complains that the confusion of things with thing-phenomena, carried over to "the properties of things (against property-phenomena with their adumbrating sensation-data, etc.)", still runs through the literature "despite my proofs given long ago" (src L1798, Hua VII 101). The proofs were in print. *Ideas I* §41 states that "the same colour appears 'in' continuous manifolds of colour-adumbrations" (*Dieselbe Farbe erscheint „in“ kontinuierlichen Mannigfaltigkeiten von Farbenabschattungen*), and that "the one and same shape … appears continuously again and again 'in another way', in ever other shape-adumbrations" (Ideen I 74–75; Hua III/1 recheck file src L1906, confirmed against the CW2 translation, src L1145). The same section adds that the adumbration, "though called by the same name, is of essential necessity not of the same genus" as the adumbrated: the adumbrating is a lived experience, the adumbrated is spatial (CW2 src L1163). That is the distinction Berkeley lacks. The lecture's footnote gives the colour example in nearly the words of 1913; the lecture itself turns the doctrine of 1913 into a historical verdict. Berkeley is the first to pose the problem of constitution within immanence, and he fails at the first distinction the problem requires.

## Lecture 22, Hua VII 156–157: Hume's psychology as the first pure phenomenology

Reads [Lecture 22, block 11](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#twenty-second-lecture-berkeleys-monadological-approach-comparison-with-leibniz-transition-to-hume).

Hume called his work a science of man, and Husserl reads it as something else: a science of pure conscious givenness, carried out without presupposing the world, and therefore not a psychology at all in the ordinary sense. The verdict is given as a series of names, each stronger than the last and each qualified: "pure egology", "the first concrete and purely immanent theory of cognition", and, "at most", "the first draft of a pure phenomenology". The qualifications are not hedges of politeness. They say exactly what Hume lacked. The understanding the passage carries is that Hume's *Treatise* is phenomenology in its method and sensualism in its content, and that the method can be read against the author's own account of it.

> In truth this Humean psychology is the first systematic attempt at a science of the pure givennesses of consciousness; I would say it is the attempt at a pure egology, if Hume had not also set up the I as a mere fiction. It is a *tabula-rasa* psychology which, in radical abstinence, wants to use nothing but the immanent findings of the *tabula rasa*, therefore only the immediately evident stocks of consciousness, and now seeks in this domain (that is, in the sensualistically interpreted domain of the *ego cogito*) psychological laws by which psychological explanation is to be won. We can also say it is the first systematic and universal draft of the concrete constitutive problematic, the first concrete and purely immanent theory of cognition. At most we could also say that Hume's *Treatise* is the first draft of a pure phenomenology, but in the shape of a purely sensualistic and empirical phenomenology.

German:

> In Wahrheit ist diese *Hume*'sche Psychologie der erste systematische Versuch einer Wissenschaft von den reinen Bewußtseinsgegebenheiten, ich würde sagen, es ist der Versuch einer reinen Egologie, wenn nicht Hume auch das Ich als bloße Fiktion hingestellt hätte. Es ist eine *tabula-rasa*-Psychologie, die, in radikaler Enthaltsamkeit, nichts verwerten will als die immanenten Vorfindlichkeiten der *tabula rasa*, also nur die unmittelbar evidenten Bewußtseinsbestände, und nun in diesem Bereich (also im sensualistisch gedeuteten Bereich des *ego cogito*) psychologische Gesetze sucht, nach denen psychologische Erklärung zu gewinnen sei. Wir können auch sagen, es ist der erste systematische und universale Entwurf der konkreten konstitutiven Problematik, die erste konkrete und rein immanente Erkenntnistheorie. Allenfalls könnten wir auch sagen, Humes *Treatise* ist der erste Entwurf einer reinen Phänomenologie, aber in Gestalt einer rein sensualistischen und empirischen Phänomenologie.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph sits on Hua VII 157 (src L2719–2720) and ends the Third Section's First Chapter; the Second Chapter's heading follows at src L2724. Only "*Hume*", "*tabula-rasa*", "*tabula rasa*", "*ego cogito*" and "*Treatise*" are italic in the file, all names and titles. The commentary's italics on "*the first systematic attempt …*", "*pure egology*", "*I*", "*psychological laws*", "*first systematic and universal project …*", "*first concrete and purely immanent theory of cognition*", "*first project of a pure phenomenology*" and "*purely sensualistic and empirical phenomenology*" (L22.11) are its own. The commentary's "project" renders *Entwurf*, which is "draft" or "sketch".

The paragraph begins by replacing a description. "In truth" (*In Wahrheit*) answers the paragraph before, where the preface of the *Treatise*, read at its word, gave "a crass anthropologism that seems to leave room for no other interpretation" (src L2714, Hua VII 156). Husserl's rule for overriding it was stated there: "Hume's own speech is misleading", and "one must read the sense of his psychology out of his almost wholly consistent methodical procedure" (src L2714). The method presupposes "nothing further than occurrences in the sphere of perceptions", and the results declare "all objective world" a "fictive formation"; so the work is "anything but an ordinary psychology" (src L2716). A science that proves human beings to be fictions cannot be a science of human beings. "Such a nonsense cannot be imputed to a Hume." The passage's names follow from this argument, and they are only as strong as its premise: that a method is to be judged by what it actually presupposes, whatever its author says it is.

The names then climb in four steps, and the grammar of each step marks how far Husserl will go. The first is a plain assertion: Hume's psychology "is the first systematic attempt at a science of the pure givennesses of consciousness". The second is a personal and conditional one: "I would say (*ich würde sagen*) it is the attempt at a pure egology, if Hume had not also set up the I as a mere fiction." The counterfactual names the one thing missing from an egology, and it is the *ego*. The third is offered as an equivalent: "We can also say" (*Wir können auch sagen*) it is "the first systematic and universal draft of the concrete constitutive problematic". "Constitutive" is the course's word for what Berkeley first saw (reading 25), and "concrete" says that Hume carried it through for the whole world, not only for bodies. The fourth is the strongest and the most guarded: "At most we could also say" (*Allenfalls könnten wir auch sagen*), with the potential subjunctive, that the *Treatise* is "the first draft of a pure phenomenology". The highest name is the one Husserl is least willing to assert.

Between the second and third steps stands the description that holds the names together. Hume's is "a *tabula-rasa* psychology" which, "in radical abstinence" (*in radikaler Enthaltsamkeit*), uses "nothing but the immanent findings". Abstinence is the word for a reduction practised without a theory of it. Then the parenthesis says what the domain is: "the sensualistically interpreted domain of the *ego cogito*" (*im sensualistisch gedeuteten Bereich*). The participle "interpreted" (*gedeutet*) locates the flaw. The domain is right; the interpretation of it is wrong. And what Hume seeks there is "psychological laws by which psychological explanation is to be won". The aim of explanation is the sensualist's aim, and the Seventeenth Lecture had already said what it yields: "every naturalistic psychology necessarily offers only a pseudo-explanation" (src L2271, reading 20).

The final clause restricts the highest name at once: "but in the shape of a purely sensualistic and empirical phenomenology" (*aber in Gestalt einer rein sensualistischen und empirischen Phänomenologie*). The two adjectives name the two faults the course has charged. "Sensualistic" is the fault of the Seventeenth and Twenty-first Lectures, the blindness to intentionality and synthesis. "Empirical" is the fault of the Nineteenth, the missing eidetic science (reading 23). The word "pure" (*rein*) appears twice in the clause, in "pure phenomenology" and "purely sensualistic", and the doubling sums up the verdict. Hume is pure in what he excludes, and his purity is sensualistic. By the standard of the Seventeenth Lecture, a sensualistic psychologism is "incurable" (src L2271). The passage does not retract that verdict. What it praises is the domain and the method, and what is incurable is the content.

The Second Chapter's title states the double verdict as a programme: "Hume's positivism: the completion of scepticism and at the same time the decisive preparatory step to a transcendental ground-science" (src L2724). The chapter opens on the counterfactual of the passage. For Hume the I is "nothing further than a bundle of different perceptions", and the identity of the person becomes "a great problem" (src L2730, Hua VII 158). The Twenty-fifth Lecture ends the line: Hume's philosophy is "the open bankruptcy of every philosophy that would give scientific information about 'the' world through natural science or metaphysics" (src L3001, Hua VII 180). The second draft of the Britannica article, printed in Hua IX, keeps the verdict and drops its hedges: Hume's "ingenious *Treatise*" has "the shape of a structural investigation of the pure sphere of lived experience, bent on strict consistency", and is "in a certain way, therefore, the first attempt at a 'phenomenology'" (Hua IX file, src L4535, c. Hua IX 266). The scare quotes take over the work of the lecture's "at most".

*Crisis* §23 judges the same work under the title "David Hume's psychology as fictionalist theory of cognition: the 'bankruptcy' of philosophy and science" (Hua VI 88, src L1437 of that file). There "all categories of objectivity … are fictions", "Hume ends at bottom in a solipsism", and Hume is reproached for lacking "a correspondingly great philosophical ethos" (src L1445–1451, Hua VI 90–92). The 1936 text keeps the claim that a "hidden, inescapable truth" made itself felt in Hume (§24, src L1455, Hua VI 93), but no longer calls the *Treatise* a phenomenology. In 1923 the verdict leads with Hume's achievement and qualifies it; in 1936 it leads with the bankruptcy and saves a truth from it.

## Lecture 23, Hua VII 163: impression as a mere *Sache*

Reads [Lecture 23, block 12](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#twenty-third-lecture-humes-nominalistic-reduction-of-all-ideas-to-impressions-and-the-countersense-in-this-principle).

"Impression is a *Sache*." With this sentence Husserl names the category mistake at the root of Hume's principle. A *Sache* in this lecture is whatever merely is: an item with factual marks (*sachliche Merkmale*), such as intensity and liveliness, that occurs and recurs under factual laws. It is not a spatial thing (*Ding*). The lecture before had shown that the perceived *Ding* is a synthetic unity of manifold meaning (reading 25), so a thing in that sense is precisely not a mere *Sache*. A *Sache* means nothing and intends nothing. If impressions are *Sachen*, they cannot be experience *of* anything, and Hume's demand that every idea be traced back to an impression loses its sense, since the demand presupposes that impressions give their objects themselves. The understanding the passage carries is the opposition of being and meaning: what consciousness does cannot be found among the properties of what merely is.

> So *impression* is a thing [*Sache*] and as such distinguished by material marks, and nothing less vanishes than that it is experience of something experienced, of something self-given. It vanishes, and yet is presupposed, already by one's constantly claiming that these data are given in immediate evidence. But mere things are what they are in their material properties; as things they are, but they *signify* nothing, they *mean* nothing, they bear nothing of sense in themselves, nothing of differences between meaning and meant, of empty presentation or self-grasping, of a same that is repeatedly meant and given, or at once meant and given. To want to find all this in things or as material properties is countersense.

German:

> Also *Impression* ist eine Sache und als solche durch sachliche Merkmale ausgezeichnet — und nichts minderes verschwindet, als daß sie Erfahrung von Erfahrenem, von Selbstgegebenem ist. Es verschwindet, und wird doch vorausgesetzt, schon dadurch, daß man doch immerfort prätendiert, daß diese Daten in unmittelbarer Evidenz Gegebenes sind. Aber bloße Sachen sind, was sie sind, in ihren sachlichen Eigenschaften, als Sachen sind sie, aber *bedeuten* sie nichts, *meinen* sie nichts, tragen nichts in sich von Sinn, nichts von Unterschieden zwischen Meinung und Gemeintem, von Leervorstellung oder Selbsterfassung, von Selbigem, das wiederholt gemeint und gegeben oder zugleich gemeint und gegeben ist. All dergleichen in Sachen oder als sachliche Eigenschaften finden zu wollen, ist Widersinn.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph is on Hua VII 163 (src L2757–2766), not 160 as the key-passage list had it. "*Impression*", "*bedeuten*" (signify) and "*meinen*" (mean) are italic in the file. The commentary's italics on "*impression is a thing*", "*experience of experienced, of self-given*", "*presupposed*", "*given in immediate evidence*", "*are*", "*sense*" and "*countersense*" (L23.12) are its own, and its "nothing less vanishes" renders *nichts minderes* (nothing less) correctly. In the paragraph before, "vor allen zu instruierenden und erklärenden Theorien" is an OCR slip for *substruierenden* (theories to be substructed), as the commentary reads it (L23.11).

The first sentence draws a consequence ("So", *Also*) from the paragraph before, where everything "first and in the Cartesian sense most indubitably given", the "presenting-as-this-and-that" and the "*holding-for-this-and-that* as such, in short, consciousness, falls under the table, so to speak" (src L2761). The italic "*Impression*" sets Hume's term in the subject place, and the predicate names what Hume makes of it: "a *Sache* and as such distinguished by material marks" (*durch sachliche Merkmale ausgezeichnet*). The adjective repeats the noun. *Sache* and *sachlich* form one family through the paragraph (*sachlichen Eigenschaften*, "in Sachen oder als sachliche Eigenschaften"), and the family is held against a second, *bedeuten*, *meinen*, *Sinn*, *Meinung*, *Gemeintem*. The paragraph is built on the two families and on the claim that nothing from the second can be found in the first.

The second sentence turns the mistake into a contradiction. What vanishes, that the impression "is experience of something experienced, of something self-given", vanishes "and yet is presupposed" (*und wird doch vorausgesetzt*). The proof is in Hume's own procedure: "one constantly claims" (*prätendiert*) that these data are "given in immediate evidence". Givenness in evidence is a character of meaning, of how something is had; a *Sache* is not given to itself. The verb *prätendieren* (to lay claim, to pretend) carries a shade of the false claim. Hume's appeal to evidence uses what his theory of impressions has no room for.

The third sentence states the opposition at full length, and its syntax gives being and meaning one clause each. "As things they *are*" (*als Sachen sind sie*), "but they *signify* nothing, they *mean* nothing" (*aber bedeuten sie nichts, meinen sie nichts*). The italics fall on the two verbs of meaning, not on "are", and the unusual inversion after "aber" (*bedeuten sie*, where *aber sie bedeuten* would be normal) puts the verbs first. The list that follows names what meaning involves: the difference "between meaning and meant", "empty presentation or self-grasping", "a same that is repeatedly meant and given". These are the distinctions the Second Section worked out against Locke, and the list is a summary of what a *Sache* cannot hold. The last sentence calls the attempt to find them in things "countersense" (*Widersinn*), the course's word for a theory that denies what it presupposes, and the second sentence has already shown the presupposition.

The paragraph that follows limits the claim. Taken "exclusively as events" in "the universal form of immanent time", experiences do have "a kind of construction out of real parts and peculiarities that makes possible a kind of material description" (*eine Art sachlicher Beschreibung*, src L2767, Hua VII 164). So the *Sache* view is not empty. It is right about experiences as temporal events and wrong about them as consciousness. The lecture had prepared this from the start. Hume and the positivism that follows him "make the characters impression and idea into material marks"; consciousness is to be regarded "in 'theory-free', 'metaphysics-free' materiality" (*Sachlichkeit*) and "to count as a mere field of things" (*als bloßes Sachenfeld*), so that impression and idea differ only as fresh and faint, original and copy (src L2753, Hua VII 162). The phrase "field of things" had appeared in the Nineteenth Lecture, where empiricism regards inner experience "as a closed field of things with natural laws that explain them" (src L2374, reading 21). Two pages on, the opposition is turned against Hume's own principle. Impression is "in truth a title for consciousness of evidence überhaupt", and "a real thing, which as such merely is, confirms nothing" (*Eine reale Sache, die als solche bloß ist, bewährt nichts*). "Not the thing can confirm, but only the self-intuition of the thing" (src L2800, Hua VII 165). In that sentence *Sache* means the real object, and the self-intuition is *of* it. The word has kept its sense, what merely is, while changing its place: in Hume's theory the *Sache* stands where consciousness should be, and in Husserl's account it stands where the object of consciousness is. The same lecture rejects William James's remedy for the same reason: "fringes" and "overtones" are still "material characters, however unheard-of and unique" (src L2798, Hua VII 165).

*Ideas I* had given the material layer a name and a place. In §85, "sensuous hyle, intentional morphe", the sensuous contents are listed in the words the lecture gives Hume's data: "'contents of sensation' such as colour-data, touch-data, tone-data and the like" (*Farbendaten, Tastdaten, Tondaten u. dgl.*), which "we will no longer confuse with appearing thingly moments" and through which these "'present' themselves" (Hua III/1 c. 192, recheck file src L3849, confirmed against the CW2 translation, src L2257). The hyle is the stratum the lecture's "material description" can reach. What Hume lacks is not the data but the other half of the doublet, the intentional form that makes data present something. Read through §85, the passage does not say that Hume described nothing real. It says that he described the material layer of experience and took it for the whole of consciousness.

## Lecture 24, Hua VII 172: the *prōton pseudos* of bad intuitionism

Reads [Lecture 24, block 10](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#twenty-fourth-lecture-the-necessary-eidetics-of-the-science-of-consciousness-and-the-inductive-empirical-objectivism-in-hume).

The *prōton pseudos*, the first falsehood from which the rest follows, is a restriction of intuition. Empiricism is a "bad intuitionism" (*schlechter Intuitionismus*): it accepts that cognition must go back to self-giving, and then admits only individual and temporal singularities as self-given. Everything general must then be explained, and explained inductively. Husserl answers with a paradox he names as such: consciousness is a site of immediate essence-insights, and even the laws of association, the empiricist's model of an inductive law, would turn out on a correct, purely immanent grasp to be essence-insights. The understanding the passage carries is that Hume's scepticism does not begin in his conclusions but in his concept of intuition, and that correcting the concept reverses the status of his own laws.

> The *prōton pseudos* lies in the prejudice of empiricism, which as bad intuitionism knows only experience of individual or temporal singularities as self-giving, and is blind to the fact that the general, conceptual generalities and generalities of states of affairs, can be seen with immediate insight, and are also, so to speak, constantly seen. We need in fact only point out that consciousness is self-evidently a site of immediate essence-insights of pure generality and necessity; we would even venture to demonstrate this for the laws of association, which would only need a more correct, purely immanent formulation. That sounds very paradoxical even today, so accustomed has one been since **Hume** and *Mill*, and not merely on the empiricist side, to regard association as an empirical peculiarity of human soul-life and to parallel the laws of association of the soul's inwardness with the law of gravitation as the law of the inert masses of outer nature.

German:

> Das [*prōton pseudos*] liegt im Vorurteil des Empirismus, der als schlechter Intuitionismus nur Erfahrung von individuellen oder zeitlichen Einzelheiten als Selbstgebung kennt und daß blind ist, daß Allgemeines, begriffliche Allgemeinheiten und Sachverhaltsallgemeinheiten, sich unmittelbar einsichtig erschauen lassen; und auch sozusagen beständig erschaut werden. Wir brauchen in der Tat nur darauf hinzuweisen, daß das Bewußtsein selbstverständlich eine Stätte von unmittelbaren Wesenseinsichten von reiner Allgemeinheit und Notwendigkeit ist; wir würden uns sogar trauen, dies für die Assoziationsgesetze, die nur richtiger rein immanenter Fassung bedürften, nachzuweisen. Das klingt heutzutage noch sehr paradox, so sehr ist man seit **Hume** und *Mill* daran gewöhnt — und nicht etwa bloß auf empiristischer Seite — Assoziation als eine empirische Eigenheit menschlichen Seelenlebens zu betrachten und die Assoziationsgesetze der seelischen Innerlichkeit mit dem Gravitationsgesetz als Gesetz der trägen Massen der äußeren Natur zu parallelisieren.

*Note on the text.* The Greek is LaTeX garble in the file: `$\pi \rho\tilde{\omega}\tau\circ \nu \;\psi\tilde{\epsilon}\upsilon\delta \circ \zeta$`. The German above puts *prōton pseudos* in brackets in its place. The letters encode pi, rho, omega, tau, omicron, nu, psi, epsilon, upsilon, delta, omicron and zeta, with the circumflex on the epsilon and a zeta for the final sigma, so the phrase is *prōton pseudos* (first falsehood), but its exact form in the print cannot be checked here. "Und daß blind ist" is a slip for "und dafür blind ist" (and is blind to the fact); the rendering follows the sense. "**Hume**" is bold in the file, "*Mill*" italic; both are spaced-type names. The commentary's italics on "*prejudice of empiricism*", "*general*", "*site of immediate essence-insights …*" and "*association-laws*" (L24.10) are its own.

The term places the error at the start of a chain. A first falsehood is the false premise from which the later errors follow validly, and the phrase is the old logician's term for it (the corpus has no Aristotle to cite it from). Husserl had used it this way before. In the fourth of his reports on German writings on logic from the years 1895–99 he asks whether it must be said "what a *prōton pseudos* lies in a theory that undertakes to clarify the essence of apperception überhaupt through the genetic reduction of all materially determinate apperceptions to a single one among them", which grows up naturally under the factual biological conditions of human beings; "even if such a reduction had succeeded, the problem would still stand untouched" (Hua XXII 219–220, src L2301 of that file). The structure is the same in 1923. A factual, genetic account is offered where an essence is in question, and nothing it establishes can reach the question.

The prejudice is named by an oxymoron. Empiricism is an "intuitionism", the honorific of the whole Third Section, where "the sense of philosophy itself … drives toward an intuitionism" (src L2652, reading 24); and it is "bad" (*schlecht*), with the adjective the Twenty-sixth Lecture gives to the "bad subjectivism" that a positive critique must reshape into the one "necessarily demanded" (src L3043, Hua VII 185). Its badness is a restriction: it "knows only experience of individual or temporal singularities as self-giving". The verb "knows" (*kennt*) makes the restriction a limit of acquaintance, not a doctrine argued for. The empiricist is then "blind" to a fact that the sentence states twice, once as possibility and once as practice: the general "can be seen with immediate insight" (*sich unmittelbar einsichtig erschauen lassen*) "and is also, so to speak, constantly seen" (*und auch sozusagen beständig erschaut werden*). The second clause carries the charge. If the general is constantly seen, the empiricist sees it too and does not let it count, which is the conjurer's situation of the Nineteenth Lecture (reading 21).

The second sentence moves from assertion to bravado. "We need in fact only point out" (*Wir brauchen in der Tat nur darauf hinzuweisen*): the proof is a pointing, and "self-evidently" (*selbstverständlich*) claims that nothing more is required. The sentence then raises the stakes with a conditional of courage: "we would even venture" (*wir würden uns sogar trauen*) to demonstrate that the laws of association are essence-insights. The subjunctive is precise. The demonstration is not given, and the condition attached to it, that the laws "would only need a more correct, purely immanent formulation", admits that the laws as Hume states them are not essence-insights. What is claimed is that their correct form would be. Here the inference is weaker than the wording makes it look: the passage asserts a demonstrability it does not supply.

The third sentence names the paradox and its source. One is "accustomed since Hume and Mill" to parallel association with gravitation, the law of "the inert masses of outer nature" (*der trägen Massen*). The image of inertia returns in the next lecture, where Hume's imagination works by an "inertia" (*Trägheit*) of the soul (src L2997). The parenthesis "and not merely on the empiricist side" widens the target to the experimental psychology of the present. The next paragraph turns the parallel into a question of fact. For gravitation "we have the history of physics"; "where is the parallel for psychology?" It is missing, as it is for the logical and arithmetical axioms that "a Mill" also claimed as inductive, "because it is a matter of general essence-insights which are precisely not induced" (src L2880). The file then says they are drawn "from purely general induction" (*aus rein genereller Induktion*), which contradicts the clause before it; the sense requires *Intuition*, and the commentary reads it so (L24.11).

Before the passage, the lecture has shown that Hume's psychology shares one trait with objective psychology: it is "*inductive* psychology", and what is legitimate for an objective science of nature is "for a subjective, purely immanent psychology … in principle illegitimate, indeed countersensical", if it is to be the ground-science (src L2874, Hua VII 171). The passage gives the reason for that illegitimacy. A ground-science that rests on induction "stands wholly in the air" (src L2870), and it rests on induction only because the first falsehood has removed every other source of generality. The lecture's title, "The necessary eidetics of the science of consciousness and the inductive-empirical objectivism in Hume" (src L2810), states the alternative the passage forces. The eidetics is "necessary" because Hume's own laws demand it. In the Nineteenth Lecture transcendental philosophy was declared "first … a priori philosophy, and *then* application to the fact" (src L2552, reading 23); here the order is applied to the laws of the mind themselves. The empiricist begins with the fact and generalizes; the passage claims that even his most empirical-looking law is the fact-side of an essential law.

## Lecture 25, Hua VII 179: the theatre of reason and imagination

Reads [Lecture 25, blocks 14–15](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#twenty-fifth-lecture-the-constitution-problem-in-hume--but-its-ending-in-complete-skepticism).

Husserl's account of Hume's fictionism is told as a play. Human cognition is a "theatre" on which reason and imagination enter as actors and destroy one another. Reason holds a territory with a boundary stone inscribed "countersense"; imagination produces the experienced world by crossings of that boundary that it permits itself "in secret". Its principle is "inertia": the soul, set swinging by past experience, cannot stop and "shoots out beyond experience". The images carry the critique. In Hume's theory the world is produced by faculties that act like forces, and the world they produce is a fiction. The understanding the passage carries is that Hume explains the constitution of the world without any consciousness that constitutes it: a blind mechanism stands where intentional accomplishment should be, so its product can only be fiction.

> The sceptical art of Hume consists in treating human cognition like a theatre on which reason and imagination appear as actors and annihilate each other as irreconcilable enemies. Reason has its firmly bounded sphere of dominion; its boundary stone bears the inscription: countersense. Within this sphere of right there are only ideas and relations of ideas, but nothing of a real world. This belongs to the domain of another faculty, "imagination", which according to immanent-psychological laws, in particular (but not only) those of the association of ideas and of habit, brings forth experienced nature as a fictive creation, permitting itself in secret, in doing so, boundary-crossings that are without right, indeed countersensical. The process is always that the imagination, according to its blind lawfulness, first produces a countersense and then, to make this first countersense more palatable, adds a new countersense by fiction. The general principle of the imagination lies in a peculiar inertia belonging to the human soul, by virtue of which, having come into a habitual swing through previous experiences, it cannot stop and must shoot out beyond experience. Wherever some regularity of coexistence and succession had offered itself to it in actual experience, it must at once go on to prolong this regularity beyond previous experience, to project it into the future, to absolutize it as simply objectively subsisting. So it invents, on the ground of the approximate coexistence of data, abiding things as independent of consciousness; so it invents causal connections with supposed necessities, and so on. Reason here permits neither that the appearing world (the imaginative world of things that is substructed beneath the actual sensation-data) count as existing in any sense, nor that this world be regarded as the manifestation of a transcendent lying still further behind it. Something that is not to be found in our consciousness and exists for itself is, according to Hume, at most an empty possibility of thought. The only way to infer from the given to the not-given is that of association and habit, which, however, itself entitles to nothing.

German:

> Die skeptische Kunst Humes besteht darin, die menschliche Erkenntnis wie ein Theater zu behandeln, auf dem Vernunft und Einbildungskraft als Akteure auftreten und sich als unversöhnliche Feinde zunichte machen. Die Vernunft hat ihre fest umgrenzte Herrschaftssphäre, ihre Grenzmark trägt die Inschrift: Widersinn. Innerhalb dieser Rechtssphäre gibt es nur Ideen und Ideenrelationen, aber nichts von einer realen Welt. Diese gehört in den Bereich eines anderen Vermögens, der „Einbildungskraft“, welche nach immanent-psychologischen Gesetzen, insbesondere (aber nicht allein) denen der Ideenassoziation und Gewohnheit, die erfahrene Natur als fiktive Schöpfung hervorbringt, indem sie sich dabei im geheimen rechtlose, ja widersinnige Grenzüberschreitungen gestattet. Der Prozeß ist dabei immer der, daß die Imagination nach ihrer blinden Gesetzmäßigkeit zuerst einen Widersinn erzeugt und dann, diesen ersten Widersinn schmackhafter zu machen, einen neuen Widersinn hinzuzufichtet. Das allgemeine Prinzip der Imagination liegt in einer eigentümlichen zur menschlichen Seele gehörigen Trägheit, vermöge deren sie, durch bisherige Erfahrungen in einen gewohnheitsmäßigen Schwung gekommen, nicht halten kann und über die Erfahrung hinausschießen muß. Wo sich ihr irgendetwas von Regelmäßigkeit der Koexistenz und Folge in wirklicher Erfahrung dargeboten hatte, muß sie sofort dazu übergehen, diese Regelmäßigkeit über die bisherige Erfahrung hinaus zu verlängern, sie in die Zukunft zu projizieren, sie zu verabsolutieren als schlechthin objektiv bestehende. So erfindet sie auf Grund ungefährer Koexistenz von Daten bleibende Dinge als vom Bewußtsein unabhängige, so erfindet sie kausale Zusammenhänge mit vermeinten Notwendigkeiten usw. Die Vernunft gestattet hier weder, die erscheinende Welt (die imaginative Dingwelt, die den wirklichen Empfindungsdaten substruiert wird) in irgendeinem Sinn als seiend gelten zu lassen, noch gestattet sie, diese Welt als Bekundung eines noch weiter dahinter liegenden Transzendenten anzusehen. Ein in unserem Bewußtsein nicht Vorfindliches und für sich Seiendes ist nach Hume höchstens eine leere Denkmöglichkeit. Der einzige Weg, von Gegebenem auf Nicht-gegebenes zu schließen, ist der der Assoziation und Gewohnheit, die aber selbst zu nichts berechtigt.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph has no emphasis in the file; the commentary's italics on "*skeptical art*", "*theater*", "*reason*", "*imagination*", "*countersense*", "*fictive creation*", "*boundary-oversteppings*", "*fictionally adds a new countersense*", "*inertia*", "*swing*", "*shoot out beyond experience*" and others (L25.14–15) are its own. "Hinzuzufichtet" is garbled; the sense and the commentary's "fictionally adds" point to *hinzufingiert*, which the print must confirm. The page is Hua VII 179, not 180: the file's running head "180" stands before the paragraph, but the textual notes place its clauses at 179,11–14 and the next paragraph's first sentence at 180,3f. (src L7202). Those notes record three changes inside the passage. The quotation marks around „Einbildungskraft“ (imagination) were set in C (179,11). "Immanent-psychological and in particular (but not only) those" was inserted in C (179,12f.). For "brings forth" (*hervorbringt*) the editor printed his own word: the typescript B has *hervorzaubert* (conjures forth), the manuscript A *vollzieht* (carries out) (179,14).

The paragraph's first word for Hume's method is "art" (*Kunst*), and the theatre follows from it. Hume treats cognition "like a theatre" (*wie ein Theater*), and the comparison makes the faculties into persons. Reason and imagination "appear as actors" (*als Akteure auftreten*) and "annihilate each other as irreconcilable enemies". Whether the theatre is Hume's own figure the corpus cannot check, since it has no *Treatise*; in the lecture it is the frame of Husserl's paraphrase. The frame is a judgement: sceptical art stages a conflict between faculties, where a theory of cognition would describe the accomplishments of consciousness. The play has no spectator who understands it, and that is the point. No consciousness in the play means anything; the faculties only act.

Reason's part is told in the language of law and territory. It has a "sphere of dominion" (*Herrschaftssphäre*) with a "boundary stone" (*Grenzmark*) inscribed "countersense", and "within this sphere of right" (*Rechtssphäre*) there are only ideas and relations of ideas. The real world falls outside it, in the domain of imagination, which crosses the boundary "in secret" (*im geheimen*) and "without right" (*rechtlose*). The legal vocabulary turns Hume's theory into a question of title: the world is not in reason's sphere, so it has no right to exist, and imagination's production of it is a trespass. The C correction sets "imagination" in quotation marks, holding Hume's faculty at the distance at which the course holds his "ideas".

The verb for imagination's work is where the editions part. The print says it "brings forth" (*hervorbringt*) nature as a fictive creation, but the word is the editor's. The manuscript has the neutral "carries out" (*vollzieht*), and Landgrebe's typescript has "conjures forth" (*hervorzaubert*). The typescript's verb belongs to the passage's family of images, with the "art" and the "theatre", and with the "conjuring away" of the general in the Nineteenth Lecture (reading 21). There empiricism made the general vanish; here imagination makes the world appear. The editor's choice keeps the sense and drops the magic. It also hides that the typescript read Hume's fictionism as a trick of the same kind the course had charged against Locke.

The mechanism of imagination is told in the language of mechanics. Its principle is "inertia" (*Trägheit*); it has come into a "habitual swing" (*gewohnheitsmäßigen Schwung*), "cannot stop" (*nicht halten kann*) and "must shoot out beyond experience" (*über die Erfahrung hinausschießen muß*). The verbs are compulsions: it "must" prolong, project and absolutize. The Twenty-fourth Lecture had complained that since Hume one parallels the laws of association with "the law of gravitation as the law of the inert masses of outer nature" (src L2878, reading 28). The paraphrase here shows the parallel at work. The soul is an inert mass, and its fictions are the overshooting of a body in motion. The process has a fixed form: imagination "first produces a countersense and then, to make this first countersense more palatable" (*schmackhafter*), adds a second by fiction. "Palatable" is irony, and its point is that the second fiction answers to comfort, not to evidence.

The last sentences shut reason out of the result. Reason permits neither that the appearing world, "the imaginative world of things that is substructed beneath the actual sensation-data", count as existing, nor that it be read as the manifestation of something behind it. Something not found in consciousness and existing for itself is "at most an empty possibility of thought" (*höchstens eine leere Denkmöglichkeit*), and the only route from given to not-given, association and habit, "itself entitles to nothing" (*selbst zu nichts berechtigt*). Imagination makes the world and has no right to it; reason has the right and makes nothing. The play ends with both actors destroyed, as the first sentence announced.

The passage follows the separation of causal from "purely rational" necessity, by which causal inference is dissolved "into fiction" (src L2993, Hua VII 179). It leads to Hume's accommodation to "the ruling and ecclesiastically guarded" views (src L2999) and to the verdict that Hume's philosophy is "the open bankruptcy of every philosophy" that would give scientific information about "the" world, a "completed countersense", since that philosophy, "as universal psychology", is itself meant to be fact-science (src L3001, Hua VII 180). The fictions had been announced at the Second Chapter's threshold, where Hume's results declare "all objective world, with all the categorial forms belonging to it", a "fictive formation in the perceptional sphere" (src L2716, Hua VII 156). Beilage XIV, written for the Twenty-third Lecture, gives the same closure in Hume's voice, from the section on existence of the *Treatise*: one may advance with one's imagination "to the heavens or to the outermost limits of the universe", yet one "never gets a step beyond oneself". "That yields the universe of imagination" (*Das ergibt das Universum der Einbildungskraft*; src L5416, Hua VII 350). *Crisis* §23 states the result without the theatre: in the *Treatise* "the world überhaupt, nature, the universe of identical bodies, the world of identical persons", and objective science with them, are turned "into fiction", and the necessity of causal succession is "a fictive substitution" (*eine fiktive Unterschiebung*; Hua VI 90, src L1445). The 1923 lecture told how the fiction is produced; the 1936 text lists what it covers.

## Lecture 26, Hua VII 189–190: "transcendental method", regressive and progressive

Reads [Lecture 26, block 16](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-7-erste-philosophie-i-commentary.md#twenty-sixth-lecture-the-basic-features-of-the-positively-constructing-line-of-modern-rationalism-and-its-dogmatism).

Two methods share the name "transcendental". The one the nineteenth century took from its reading of Kant is regressive and constructive: it starts from valid objective cognition as a given and asks what must be assumed about the cognizing subject, its faculties and functions, for that cognition to be possible. Husserl's is progressive: it starts from what is insightfully given and moves forward from grounded to grounding-upon. The passage claims more than a difference of method. It gives the regressive method a genealogy. Its question has the form of the reconciliation metaphysics of the seventeenth century, which asked how the world must be laid out so that it can be God's work. The understanding the passage carries is that "conditions of possibility" names a construction from a result fixed in advance, and that philosophy, which may have no dogma, cannot proceed that way.

> When in the nineteenth century, starting from the interpretation of the *Kantian* critique of reason, one speaks of "transcendental method", one means by it, in contrast to what we have here so designated, a peculiarly regressive and constructing method, which pursues the "conditions of the possibility" of objectively valid cognition in the sense of the question: what must be presupposed for the cognizing subjectivity, how must its faculties of cognition, how must the functions of cognition, of intuiting and thinking, that are graded within it be assumed, how must they work to form cognition, so that cognition of a true objectivity in the form of truths and sciences valid in themselves is to become possible and intelligible? In a similar sense the reconciliation metaphysics is reconstructive from the outset, and it would seem to me that the mighty role that reconstruction has won for the theory of cognition has its original historical source in this reconstructing metaphysics.

German:

> Wenn man im 19. Jahrhundert, im Ausgang von der Interpretation der *Kant*’schen Vernunftkritik, von „transzendentaler Methode“ spricht, so meint man damit im Gegenzug zu dem, was wir hier so bezeichneten, eine eigentümlich regressive und konstruierende Methode, welche den „Bedingungen der Möglichkeit“ objektiver gültiger Erkenntnis im Sinn der Frage nachgeht: was muß für die kennende Subjektivität vorausgesetzt werden, wie müssen ihre Erkenntnisvermögen, wie die in ihr sich abstufenden Erkenntnisfunktionen des Anschauens und Denkens angenommen werden, wie müssen sie erkenntnisformend wirken, damit Erkenntnis einer wahren Objektivität in Form an sich gültiger Wahrheiten und Wissenschaften möglich und verständlich werden soll? In einem ähnlichen Sinn rekonstruktiv ist von vornherein die Versöhnungsmetaphysik, und es will mir scheinen, daß die gewaltige Rolle, welche die Rekonstruktion für die Erkenntnistheorie gewonnen hat, ihre ursprüngliche historische Quelle in dieser rekonstruierenden Metaphysik hat [^11].

*Note on the text.* "Kennende" is an OCR slip for *erkennende* (cognizing), and "objektiver gültiger" for *objektiv gültiger* (objectively valid); the rendering follows the sense. Only "*Kant*" is italic in the file. The passage begins on Hua VII 189 and ends on 190: the running head "190" stands before it, but the textual notes place "von vornherein" (from the outset) at 189,39, a C insertion, and "historische" (historical) at 190,1f., also a C insertion (src L7202). Both words were added in revision: the reconstructive character of reconciliation metaphysics "from the outset", and its "historical" source. The note marker [^11] answers the editor's note at src L3085, "Cf. Beilage XVIII, pp. 365 ff." The footnote tail set into the body earlier in the lecture (src L3069) lies before this passage and is not quoted.

The two senses are set against each other in the first clause: what "one" means by "transcendental method" is meant "in contrast to what we have here so designated" (*im Gegenzug zu dem, was wir hier so bezeichneten*). The quotation marks around „transzendentaler Methode“ hold the nineteenth-century use at a distance. The source of the use is put with care: not Kant, but "the interpretation of the *Kantian* critique of reason". The passage targets the neo-Kantian reading, and the next lecture deals with Kant himself. The method is "peculiarly regressive and constructing" (*eigentümlich regressive und konstruierende*), and the two adjectives name two features. It goes back from a result to its conditions, and it builds the conditions rather than finding them. "Conditions of the possibility" (*„Bedingungen der Möglichkeit“*) is quoted as a formula, the second set of quotation marks in the sentence.

The question the method asks is given in full, and its grammar carries the critique. It is a chain of "must" (*muß*, *müssen*) and "be assumed" (*angenommen werden*): what "must be presupposed" for the subject, how its faculties "must … be assumed", how they "must work", "so that" (*damit*) cognition of objectivity "is to become possible and intelligible". The result stands in the purpose clause and is fixed before the inquiry begins; the subject's faculties are whatever the result requires. "Be assumed" is the decisive verb. The faculties are posited as hypotheses to fit, not seen. The Second Section had used "substruct" (*substruieren*, src L1797, L1874) for the empiricist's invention of what is not given; here the rationalist counterpart is assumption in the service of a given result.

The genealogy follows, hedged and then strengthened. "In a similar sense the reconciliation metaphysics is reconstructive from the outset", and "it would seem to me" (*es will mir scheinen*) that reconstruction has its "original historical source" in it. The personal hedge marks the thesis as a historian's judgement, not a demonstration, and the C revisions made it bolder, adding "from the outset" and "historical". The evidence is not in this paragraph but in the next, which states reconciliation metaphysics' own question in the same grammatical form: metaphysics was pressed "onto the constructive way: how must we lay out for ourselves the natural world, the objective sciences, so that it can become a God-effected, a teleologically intelligible world?" (*wie müssen wir uns … zurechtlegen, damit sie eine gottgewirkte … Welt werden kann?*, src L3081, Hua VII 190). "How must …, so that … can" in both: the theological result in one, objective science in the other. The genealogy rests on the shared form of the question, and the shared form is all the passage shows. Whether the nineteenth century took its method from Malebranche and Leibniz, or reinvented the form, the lecture does not say.

The paragraph after the next draws the methodological consequence. What may be granted to theology "does not yet belong to philosophy. It may have no preceding dogma", and "its procedure can and may only be progressive" (*Ihr Verfahren kann und darf nur progressiv sein*, src L3083–3089). The doubled modal is the one the Nineteenth Lecture used for the eidetic method (*können und dürfen*, reading 23). A concession then limits the ban. "In a certain sense every scientific researcher proceeds constructively and regressively; he does so in his inventive trains of thought." Invention needs anticipation, a guiding idea of what is sought. "But with all this only a sketch is won (*nur ein Anschlag*) and a provisional probability"; the "actually accomplishing work" follows "progressively" from grounded to grounded, and the knowledge it yields is often "different from the original sketch" (src L3091). Regression is allowed as heuristic and refused as grounding. The neo-Kantian error, on this account, is to take the sketch for the proof.

The C revisions also show how carefully Husserl kept the rationalists from the transcendental. In the paragraph before the passage, reconciliation metaphysics is said to be co-determined by regard for "the motive, effective since Descartes, of the *res cogitans* closed in itself, of the spirit immediately conscious only of itself". The textual notes record that C put these words in place of "the transcendental subjectivity" (189,22–24, src L7202). The first version credited the rationalists with reckoning with transcendental subjectivity. The revision gives them only the Cartesian mind-substance, which is what Beilage XVIII, the text the footnote cites, says Leibniz took over: the Cartesian evidence "in the shape of the indubitable evidence of the mind of its own being as *mens sive animus*, as *substantia cogitans*" (src L5980, Hua VII 365). The Twenty-seventh Lecture applies the passage to Kant, whose "deductions" proceed by "a *constructive* thought-procedure" (src L3132). *Crisis* §30 states the charge against Kant in the passage's terms: Kant "forbids his readers to convert the results of his regressive procedure into intuitive concepts", and every attempt "at a progressive construction proceeding from original and purely evident intuitions" (Hua VI, src L1748, c. Hua VI 118–119). The section title calls the result Kant's "mythical constructions". In 1923 the regressive method has a theological ancestry; in 1936 it produces myth.

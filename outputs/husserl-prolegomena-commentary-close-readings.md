# Husserl, *Prolegomena zur reinen Logik*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserl-prolegomena-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md) works through the *Prolegomena to Pure Logic*, the first volume of the *Logical Investigations*, paragraph by paragraph. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding goes in the rest of the book and in Husserl's other works.

The text is quoted from the converted German file of Husserliana XVIII (edited by Elmar Holenstein) in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-18-logische-untersuchungen-erster-band-prolegomena-zur-reinen-logik.md`). Hua XVIII prints the second edition (B, 1913) and records the first (A, 1900) in its apparatus. Passages are cited by §, by A and B page, and by Hua XVIII page. The main commentary cites § numbers only, and unit references such as "§19 ¶3" name the commentary's block under its "Paragraph 3" heading of § 19. All English renderings of Husserl and of the apparatus are my working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows. Findlay's English translation is in the corpus and is cited, by name, only where it departs from the German in a way that matters.

The main commentary is titled "(1900)" and quotes several words in the spelling of A, but it records no difference between the editions. Where a reading turns on one, it quotes the B text that Hua prints and gives the A wording from the apparatus. Husserl's own foreword to B says that he did not rework the book radically, but that "from about the middle on" he made "many substantial improvements of the presentation" (*etwa von der Mitte ab viele erhebliche Verbesserungen der Darstellung*), so the later readings meet more variants than the earlier.

The file has limits that bear on more than one reading. It is on the corpus's list of files unsafe to quote unchecked. Its A/B page numbers come from markers placed at line level and can be one out; its Hua pages come from sparse running heads. Hua's spaced type has been rendered as italics or dropped, so emphasis is argued from only where the file keeps it, and its absence proves nothing. Many apparatus notes have lost their anchors in the body, and some note numbers are defined twice, so an A variant can be tied to its sentence only where Hua's corner brackets or a nearby anchor survive; the readings say when they cannot. Greek is set in LaTeX, partly garbled. The calculating-machine passage of § 22 is damaged by OCR. In four places the file carries text that is not Husserl's (a footnote filled with the converting model's own remarks, a stray end marker, undefined note anchors and an editorial aside); none of it is quoted.

## Preface, A VI–VII / B VI–VII, Hua 7: the unity of theory

Reads [Preface ¶5](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#preface-vorwort), fifth paragraph under the Preface.

The Preface places the failure of a psychological grounding of logic at one transition: from the psychological connections of thinking to "the logical unity of the thought-content", which Husserl glosses, in a parenthesis, as "the unity of theory". A theory holds together in a way that a train of thought does not, and the method Husserl had followed could not get from the one to the other. The Preface does not argue that the transition is impossible. It reports that it would not go through, and that this local failure raised a doubt of principle about how the objectivity of science could be compatible with a psychological grounding. The book's last chapter defines the unity of theory. The Preface states the problem in the very term the solution will use.

> Where it was a question of the origin of mathematical representations, or of the shaping of practical methods, which is indeed psychologically determined, the achievement of psychological analysis seemed to me clear and instructive. But as soon as a transition was made from the psychological connections of thinking to the logical unity of the thought-content (the unity of theory), no proper continuity and clarity would emerge. All the more, therefore, was I troubled by the doubt of principle how the objectivity of mathematics, and of all science whatever, was compatible with a psychological grounding of the logical. Since in this way my whole method, carried by the convictions of the reigning logic (to clarify given science logically through psychological analyses), was shaken, I saw myself driven to an ever increasing degree to general critical reflections on the essence of logic, and especially on the relation between the subjectivity of knowing and the objectivity of the content of knowledge. Left in the lurch by logic everywhere I hoped it would give me information on the definite questions I had to put to it, I was at last forced to set my philosophical-mathematical investigations wholly aside until I should have succeeded in reaching secure clarity in the basic questions of the theory of knowledge and in the critical understanding of logic as a science.

German:

> Wo es sich um die Frage nach dem Ursprung der mathematischen Vorstellungen oder um die in der Tat psychologisch bestimmte Ausgestaltung der praktischen Methoden handelte, schien mir die Leistung der psychologischen Analyse klar und lehrreich. Sowie aber ein Übergang von den psychologischen Zusammenhängen des Denkens zur logischen Einheit des Denkinhaltes (der Einheit der Theorie) vollzogen wurde, wollte sich keine rechte Kontinuität und Klarheit herausstellen lassen. Um so mehr beunruhigte mich daher auch der prinzipielle Zweifel, wie sich die Objektivität der Mathematik und aller Wissenschaft überhaupt mit einer psychologischen Begründung des Logischen vertrage. Da auf solche Weise meine ganze, von den Überzeugungen der herrschenden Logik getragene Methode — gegebene Wissenschaft durch psychologische Analysen logisch aufzuklären — ins Schwanken geriet, so sah ich mich in immer steigendem Maße zu allgemeinen kritischen Reflexionen über das Wesen der Logik und zumal über das Verhältnis zwischen der Subjektivität des Erkennens und der Objektivität des Erkenntnisinhaltes gedrängt. Von der Logik überall im Stiche gelassen, wo ich von ihr Aufschlüsse in Beziehung auf die bestimmten Fragen erhoffte, die ich an sie zu stellen hatte, ward ich endlich gezwungen, meine philosophisch-mathematischen Untersuchungen ganz zurückzustellen, bis es mir gelungen sei, in den Grundfragen der Erkenntnistheorie und in dem kritischen Verständnis der Logik als Wissenschaft zu sicherer Klarheit vorzudringen.

*Note on the text.* The file breaks two words across lines and prints Hua's margin line numbers ("5", "10", "15") at the start of its lines, and it sets the A/B page markers inside "Be-gründung". The quotation joins the broken words and leaves out the line numbers and markers; no wording is changed.

The passage first concedes a field to psychology. For the origin of mathematical representations, and for practical methods whose shaping is "indeed psychologically determined" (*in der Tat psychologisch bestimmte*), psychological analysis did its work. The concession is not withdrawn later. It returns as the book's verdict on the psychologists' argument in § 20, that psychology is "co-involved" (*mitbeteiligt*) in the foundation of normative logic, though it does not supply its essential foundation. The failure is therefore located exactly: not in psychology, but in a step beyond it.

The sentence that names the step has no agent. A transition "was made" (*vollzogen wurde*), and then no continuity and clarity "would emerge" (*wollte sich … herausstellen lassen*). The modal is the same one Husserl used a sentence earlier of his whole psychological foundation, which "would never quite satisfy me" (*wollte mir … nie recht genügen*). Both report a resistance met in the work, not a proof. The main commentary's block says that psychological analysis "does not and cannot yield" the ideal unity of logical truths. That is the conclusion of Chapters III to VIII, carried back into a paragraph that only narrates a failure.

What failed is measured by the method's own goal. The method was "to clarify (*aufklären*) given science logically through psychological analyses", and what would not emerge was "clarity" (*Klarheit*). The paragraph ends by deferring everything until "secure clarity" is reached. The word family holds the paragraph together: psychological analysis seemed "clear", the transition lacked "clarity", the method of "clarifying" was shaken. Findlay renders the decisive phrase "no true continuity and unity could be established". His "unity" repeats the word that has just named the goal, and it cuts the link between the failure and the method's aim.

The parenthesis "(the unity of theory)" carries the understanding. "Thought-content" (*Denkinhalt*) is a word of the logic Husserl is leaving; it occurs nowhere else in the *Prolegomena*. The gloss replaces it at once with the term the book will define. The next sentence changes the vocabulary again: the relation to be reflected on is between "the subjectivity of knowing" (*Subjektivität des Erkennens*) and "the objectivity of the content of knowledge" (*Objektivität des Erkenntnisinhaltes*). That second pair is the one the book keeps. Two clauses also widen the charge. The doubt concerns "all science whatever", not mathematics alone, and the method was "carried by the convictions of the reigning logic". The commentary hears in the Preface a public self-critique. The wording makes Husserl's psychologism the reigning logic's conviction, which he shared and which left him "in the lurch". The self-critique is also an indictment.

The passage closes the Preface's second line of motivation. The first (¶¶1–3) ran through formal arithmetic and the theory of manifolds to the form of knowledge as against its matter; it began with "the rational essence of deductive science with its formal unity". The second line arrives at the same unity from the side of psychology. The two lines meet in the unity of theory. The next paragraph explains the "independence" with which Husserl parts from "the reigning logical direction", and the Goethe quotation follows.

The book gives the unity of theory its content in Chapter XI. § 63, "Continuation. The unity of theory" (A/B 231–232, Hua 233–234), defines it: "the systematic unity of the ideally closed totality of laws that rest on a ground-lawfulness as on their last ground and spring from it by systematic deduction is the unity of the systematically completed theory" (*die Einheit der systematisch vollendeten Theorie*). A footnote to the same section excludes what the Preface's method had looked for: necessity, as an objective predicate of a truth, is "not a subjective, psychological character of the judgement, still less a feeling of being compelled". The unity of theory is a unity of grounds in laws. No connection of thinking can supply it, because connections of thinking are causal, as § 22 shows with the calculating machine. The Preface's second pair also returns. § 47 (A/B 174) asks the reader to attend to "the fundamental difference between the subjective-anthropological unity of knowledge and the objective-ideal unity of the content of knowledge", and §§ 65–66 divide the question of the possibility of theory into one "referred to actual knowledge" and one "referred to the content of knowledge". The Preface's single doubt becomes the book's architecture. In 1913 Husserl qualified its result. His foreword to B says that the *Prolegomena* had to keep "the concept of 'truth in itself', too one-sidedly oriented to the *vérités de raison*" (*allzu einseitig nach den vérités de raison orientierte*). The objectivity the Preface asked about was secured, on his later view, by too narrow a model.

Husserl's diary, which the editor of Husserliana XII quotes (Hua XII, editor's introduction, XXVI–XXVII), retells the same crisis. While working on the logic of mathematical calculation, "the incomprehensibly alien worlds tormented me: the world of the purely logical and the world of act-consciousness, as I would say today, of the phenomenological and also psychological. I did not know how to set them into one, and yet they had to have a relation to each other and form an inner unity." The diary keeps the Preface's two sides and changes the task. In the Preface the transition from thinking to content failed and had to be given up; in the diary the two worlds are to be related and joined in "an inner unity". The diary also renames the first side "act-consciousness" and calls it phenomenological. The *Prolegomena* separates the unity of theory from the connections of thinking. The second volume of the *Investigations* takes up the relation between them that the Preface had found impossible to make.

## §2, A/B 6, Hua 22: the confusion of domains as *metabasis*

Reads [§2 ¶5](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-necessity-of-a-renewed-discussion-of-the-questions-of-principle), fifth paragraph under § 2.

The charge the *Prolegomena* will bring against psychologism is set up here before psychologism is named. A science goes wrong worst not by drawing its domain too narrowly but by mixing heterogeneous things into a domain that only seems to be one. Husserl calls this a confusion of domains (*Gebietsvermengung*) and gives it Aristotle's name, a *metabasis eis allo genos* (a passing over into another kind). Its root is a misreading of the very objects the science is meant to study, and the error goes unnoticed. It can therefore be shown only by its effects, and the paragraph lists them. The understanding the reading follows is that list: it is a programme the critical chapters carry out item by item.

> Incomparably more dangerous, however, is another imperfection in the demarcation of the domain, namely the confusion of domains, the mixing of heterogeneous things into a supposed unity of domain, especially when it is grounded in a complete misreading of the objects whose investigation is to be the essential goal of the intended science. Such an unnoticed *metabasis eis allo genos* can bring the most harmful effects in its train: the fixing of invalid goals; the following of methods wrong in principle, because incommensurable with the true objects of the discipline; the jumbling of the logical strata, so that the truly foundational propositions and theories, often in the strangest disguises, are pushed along among wholly alien trains of thought as apparently subordinate moments or incidental consequences, and so on. It is precisely in the philosophical sciences that these dangers are considerable, and for that reason the question of range and limits has an incomparably greater importance for the fruitful development of these sciences than for the so highly favoured sciences of external nature, where the course of our experiences forces separations of domain upon us, within which at least a provisional establishment of successful research is possible.

German:

> Ungleich gefährlicher ist aber eine andere Unvollkommenheit in der Abgrenzung des Gebietes, nämlich die Gebietsvermengung, die Vormischung von Heterogenem zu einer vermeintlichen Gebietseinheit, zumal wenn sie gründet in einer völligen Mißdeutung der Objekte, deren Erforschung das wesentliche Ziel der intendierten Wissenschaft sein soll. Eine derart unbemerkte μεταβασις εις αλλο γενος kann die schädlichsten Wirkungen nach sich ziehen: Fixierung untriftiger Ziele; Befolgung prinzipiell verkehrter, weil mit den wahren Objekten der Disziplin inkommensurabler Methoden; Durcheinanderwerfung der logischen Schichten, derart, daß die wahrhaft grundlegenden Sätze und Theorien, oft in den sonderbarsten Verkleidungen, sich zwischen ganz fremdartigen Gedankenreihen als scheinbar nebensächliche Momente oder beiläufige Konsequenzen fortschieben usw. Gerade bei den philosophischen Wissenschaften sind diese Gefahren beträchtlich, und darum hat die Frage nach Umfang und Grenzen für die fruchtbare Fortbildung dieser Wissenschaften eine ungleich größere Bedeutung, als bei den so sehr begünstigten Wissenschaften von der äußeren Natur, wo der Verlauf unserer Erfahrungen uns Gebietsscheidungen aufdrängt, innerhalb deren wenigstens eine vorläufige Etablierung erfolgreicher Forschung möglich ist.

*Note on the text.* The file sets the Greek in LaTeX without accents and with one stray prime; it is given here in plain Greek letters as the file spells them, without accents. "Vormischung" is the file's reading; the sense, and Findlay's "mixture", require *Vermischung* (mixing). The reading does not depend on the word. The paragraph continues with Kant's dictum and the announcement that the logic of the day has succumbed to these dangers.

The paragraph turns on its comparative. The confusion of domains is "incomparably more dangerous" (*ungleich gefährlicher*) than the fault of § 2's previous paragraph, a domain grasped too narrowly. A narrow domain leaves the science's objects as they are and only stops short; a mixed domain changes what the objects are taken to be. The word "supposed" (*vermeintlich*) carries the contrast. A few sentences earlier § 2 had laid down that "the domain of a science is an objectively closed unity" and that "it is not up to our choice where and how we demarcate domains of truth". A confused domain is a unity only in the opinion of the researcher. The clause "especially when it is grounded in a complete misreading of the objects" names the root. The confusion is not an error in classification. It follows from mistaking what kind of thing the science deals with, and in the case of logic that will be the mistake of taking ideal laws for real ones.

"Unnoticed" (*unbemerkte*) explains the form of the argument to come. A *metabasis* that went unnoticed cannot be refuted by pointing at it, since its author does not see it. It must be brought to light through what it does. Husserl therefore lists effects, in an order that runs from goals through methods to the structure of the theory. The list is open: it ends with "and so on". Its middle term is the most precise. Methods are "wrong in principle" because "incommensurable" with the true objects. The word is mathematical, and it says that no common measure exists between the method and the object, not that the method is crude. The last effect is the most vivid. The "truly foundational propositions" do not disappear; they are "pushed along" (*fortschieben*) among alien trains of thought, "in the strangest disguises", as "apparently subordinate moments or incidental consequences". The foundations are present in the confused science but misdressed.

The comparison with the sciences of nature gives the paragraph its function in the Introduction. In physics or zoology "the course of our experiences" forces the separation of domains, and research can proceed before the boundaries are settled. In the philosophical sciences no experience separates the domains. Their boundaries must be argued, which is why § 2 is titled "the necessity of a renewed discussion of the questions of principle" and why the *Prolegomena* is a book of prolegomena at all.

The critical chapters can be read as the list carried out. The "methods incommensurable with the true objects" are the empiricist consequences of Chapter IV: § 21 shows that laws known by induction are only probable, while the principle of contradiction does not say that something is to be "surmised". The "strangest disguises" are found in § 39 (A/B 126, Hua 133), where Sigwart calls pure principles such as the principle of contradiction "functional laws" or "fundamental forms of movement of our thinking": the foundational proposition reappears as a fact about the mind. The "misreading of the objects" is named in Chapter VIII, whose second prejudice is answered in § 46 by the claim that the domain of pure logic, like that of pure mathematics, is ideal. The test the key-passage list proposes, whether the later diagnoses have the form of a *metabasis*, is passed in Husserl's own words. The Greek returns twice. In § 40 (A/B 145, Hua 150) Erdmann's claim that we cannot conceive other ways of thinking gains "a semblance of right" only "when we perform the *metabasis*, confuse the region of the psychological laws of thought with that of the purely logical ones, and then misinterpret the latter in a psychologistic sense". In § 46 (A/B 171, Hua 174) the term clinches the argument from arithmetic: numbers point back to acts of counting, and yet, "despite this 'psychological origin' of the arithmetical concepts, everyone recognizes it as a faulty *metabasis* that mathematical laws should be psychological". In both places the kinds between which the passage happens are the same: real laws of thinking and ideal laws of content. The § 46 sentence also settles the concession of the Preface. A concept may have a psychological origin, and its laws still belong to another kind.

Seven years later Husserl widened the charge. The summary of *The Idea of Phenomenology* (Hua II 6) says that a critique of knowledge "can make use of no natural science", and that "every such linkage signifies a faulty *metabasis*". It arises from a "shift of problem" (*Problemverschiebung*) "between the psychological, natural-scientific explanation of knowledge as a natural fact and the clarification of knowledge according to the essential possibilities of its accomplishment". In 1900 the *metabasis* ran from logic into psychology. In 1907 any recourse to a natural science, psychology included, is a passing over into another kind, because the critique of knowledge may presuppose none of the knowledge it examines. The *Prolegomena* separates the ideal from the real. *The Idea of Phenomenology* separates the clarification of knowledge from every science that takes knowledge as a fact. Both use the Aristotelian name for the step they forbid.

## §3, A/B 8, Hua 24: the path from technology to pure logic

Reads [§3 ¶3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-contested-questions-the-path-to-be-taken), third paragraph under § 3.

The *Prolegomena* does not take a side in the dispute it has just described. It takes the psychologists' starting point, logic as a technology (*Kunstlehre*, an art of correct thinking), and asks what that technology rests on. The answer is a science that is "new" and was nevertheless "intended" by Kant and the formal logicians, and Husserl's word for how it is reached is "singling out" (*Aussonderung*). Pure logic is not set against the technology. It is separated out from what the technology already presupposes, as its "most important" foundation, which leaves room for other foundations. The understanding the reading follows is this method of separation, and the superlative that limits it.

> Since what we are aiming at is not really a participation in these traditional disputes, but rather a clarification of the differences of principle that play in them and, ultimately, a [clarification] of the essential goals of a pure logic, we shall take the following path. We take as our starting point the determination of logic as a technology, which is at present almost universally accepted, and fix its sense and its justification. To this there naturally attaches the question of the theoretical foundations of this discipline, and in particular of its relation to psychology. In essence this question coincides, if not as a whole then in a main part, with the cardinal question of the theory of knowledge, the one concerning the objectivity of knowledge. The result of our investigation of it is the singling out of a new and purely theoretical science, which forms the most important foundation for every technology of scientific knowledge and possesses the character of an a priori and purely demonstrative science. It is the science that Kant and the other representatives of a "formal" or "pure" logic intended, but did not correctly grasp and determine in its content and range. As the final result of these reflections there emerges a clearly outlined idea of the essential content of the disputed discipline, with which a clear position on the disputed questions raised is given of itself.

German:

> Da wir es nicht eigentlich auf eine Beteiligung an diesen traditionellen Streitigkeiten, vielmehr auf eine Klärung der in ihnen spielenden prinzipiellen Differenzen und letztlich auf eine [B 8] rung der wesentlichen Ziele einer reinen Logik abgesehen haben, so wollen wir folgenden Weg einschlagen: Wir nehmen als Ausgangspunkt die gegenwärtig fast allgemein angenommene Bestimmung der Logik als einer Kunstlehre und fixieren ihren Sinn und ihre Berechtigung. Daran schließt sich naturgemäß die Frage nach den theoretischen Grundlagen dieser Disziplin und im besonderen nach ihrem Verhältnis zur Psychologie. Im wesentlichen deckt sich diese Frage, wenn auch nicht dem Ganzen, so doch einem Hauptteile nach, mit der Kardinalfrage der Erkenntnistheorie, die Objektivität der Erkenntnis betreffend. Das Ergebnis unserer diesbezüglichen Untersuchung ist die Aussonderung einer neuen und rein theoretischen Wissenschaft, welche das wichtigste Fundament für jede Kunstlehre von der wissenschaftlichen Erkenntnis bildet und den Charakter einer apriorischen und rein demonstrativen Wissenschaft besitzt. Sie ist es, die von Kant und den übrigen Vertretern einer ,,formalen“ oder ,,reinen“ Logik intendiert, aber nach ihrem Gehalt und Umfang nicht richtig erfaßt und bestimmt worden ist. Als letzter Erfolg dieser Überlegungen resultiert eine klar umrissene Idee von dem wesentlichen Gehalt der strittigen Disziplin, womit von selbst eine klare Position zu den aufgeworfenen Streitfragen gegeben ist.

*Note on the text.* A word is lost at the B 8 page marker: the file reads "auf eine [B 8] rung", the end of a word broken across the page. Findlay's "a clarification of the essential aims" suggests *Klärung* (clarification); the English supplies it in brackets. The reading does not turn on the word.

The paragraph sets two sentences of aim against two parties. § 3's previous paragraph had reduced the four disputed questions to "really only two" parties: logic is theoretical, independent, formal and demonstrative, or it is a technology dependent on psychology. Husserl declines "participation" (*Beteiligung*) in the dispute and aims at "a clarification of the differences of principle that play in them". He does not then split the difference. He grants the second party its premise, takes "the determination of logic as a technology" as his starting point, and promises to "fix its sense and its justification". The concession is real. Chapters I and II establish that logic is indeed a normative discipline and a technology, and only then ask what it rests on.

"Singling out" (*Aussonderung*) is the paragraph's key word. It is a word of separating one thing from a mass in which it is already contained, not of founding or inventing. The result of the inquiry is a science that the technology already carries within it, unrecognized. That is why the science can be "new" and also "intended" by Kant: new as a recognized discipline, old as a tendency. The word anticipates the thesis of § 14, that every normative discipline has a theoretical content "separable from the thought of norming", and § 46 says that pure logic is "to be detached as an independent theoretical discipline" (*als selbständige theoretische Disziplin abzulösende*). Findlay renders *Aussonderung* as "delineation", which loses the sense of extraction.

The superlative limits the claim. The new science "forms the most important foundation" (*das wichtigste Fundament*) for every technology of scientific knowledge. A most important foundation is one among several. The paragraph does not say that psychology contributes nothing to the technology; it reserves the question of the relation to psychology as the one that "coincides, if not as a whole then in a main part" with the question of objectivity. The double hedge is deliberate: the dispute about psychology is mostly, not entirely, a dispute about the objectivity of knowledge, which is the doubt of the Preface. Findlay's "the all-important foundation" turns the superlative into an absolute and erases the room it leaves. The main commentary's roadmap makes the same move when it lists, as its third step, that Husserl will "show that psychology cannot provide these foundations". § 20 shows only that psychology is not the essential foundation, and it grants that psychology is "co-involved" (*mitbeteiligt*).

The last clause, which the commentary's block leaves out, says how the dispute ends. The clear idea of pure logic brings with it "of itself" (*von selbst*) a clear position on the disputed questions. Husserl expects to settle the four questions without arguing them one by one: once pure logic is singled out, logic is theoretical in its foundation and practical in its application, independent of psychology in its pure part and dependent on it in its technical rules, formal and a priori in the one and empirical in the other. The two parties were each right about a different discipline.

The book follows the path in the order given. Chapter I fixes the sense of logic as a normative discipline and a technology. Chapter II states the thesis that normative disciplines rest on theoretical ones. Chapters III to IX ask whether the theoretical foundation is psychology and refute the psychologistic answer. Chapter XI gives "the idea of pure logic". The concession to the technology returns intact in Chapter VIII. § 46 (A/B ~173, Hua 177) grants that logic, "in the sense of the technology of scientific knowledge", deals "to a wide extent with psychic experiences", and denies only that this holds of its "purely logical parts". § 50 (A/B 183) grants that the logical technology must investigate the psychological conditions under which evidence "lights up" in judging. The Kant clause is redeemed in § 58 (A/B 215, Hua 218): "We agree with Kant in the main tendency, but we do not find that he clearly saw through the essence of the intended discipline (*das Wesen der intendierten Disziplin*) and brought it to presentation in its proper content." The same participle, "intended", frames the debt and its limit. Before that sentence Husserl sets aside Kant's faculties of understanding and reason as "mythical concepts" and rejects the logic of Jäsche's edition as "unspeakably meagre". He takes from Kant the direction, not the discipline.

## §5, A/B 11–12, Hua 27: theory of science

Reads [§5 ¶2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-theoretical-supplementation-of-the-individual-sciences-through-metaphysics-and-theory-of-science), second paragraph under § 5.

The theory of science (*Wissenschaftslehre*) enters as the second of two supplements that the individual sciences need. Metaphysics examines the presuppositions of the sciences of the real; the theory of science examines "what makes sciences in general into sciences", and so concerns every science alike, including those whose objects are "thought independently of real being or non-being". The term is defined by its scope, and the scope is fixed by the first appearance in the book of the line between the real and the ideal. The word is Bolzano's title, but the definition is not Bolzano's, and Husserl says so.

> But this metaphysical grounding does not suffice to reach the desired theoretical completion of the individual sciences. In any case it concerns only the sciences that deal with real actuality, and not all do that, certainly not the purely mathematical sciences, whose objects are numbers, manifolds and the like, which are thought independently of real being or non-being as mere bearers of purely ideal determinations. It is otherwise with a second class of investigations, whose theoretical settlement likewise forms an indispensable postulate of our striving for knowledge. They concern all sciences in the same way, because, in short, they are directed at what makes sciences in general into sciences. With this, however, the domain of a new discipline is designated, a complex one, as will soon be shown, whose peculiarity it is to be science of science, and which for that very reason would most pointedly be named theory of science.

German:

> Diese metaphysische Grundlegung reicht aber nicht aus, um die gewünschte theoretische Vollendung der Einzelwissenschaften zu erreichen; sie betrifft ohnehin bloß die Wissenschaften, welche es mit der realen Wirklichkeit zu tun haben, und das tun doch nicht alle, sicher nicht die rein mathematischen Wissenschaften, deren Gegenstände Zahlen, Mannigfaltigkeiten u. dgl. sind, die unabhängig von realem Sein oder Nichtsein als bloße Träger rein idealer Bestimmungen gedacht sind. The paragraph argues by a difference in scope. Metaphysical grounding "concerns only" (*betrifft ohnehin bloß*) the sciences of "real actuality". The particle *ohnehin* (in any case) marks this as a second objection, added to the first that metaphysics "does not suffice": even where it applies it would not complete the sciences, and it does not apply everywhere. The counter-case is mathematics. Its objects "are thought (*gedacht sind*) independently of real being or non-being as mere bearers of purely ideal determinations". This is the first time the book uses "ideal" in its own technical sense. The verb is guarded. Mathematical objects are said to be *thought* as independent of real being. Husserl does not yet assert that there are ideal objects; that thesis is argued in § 46, whose heading says that the research domain of pure logic, "like that of pure mathematics", is "an ideal one". Here mathematics supplies only the case that metaphysics cannot reach, and the case defines the scope of the new discipline.

The definition is formal and reflexive. The second class of investigations is directed at "what makes sciences in general into sciences" (*was Wissenschaften überhaupt zu Wissenschaften macht*). The doubled noun says that the subject matter is science as such, not any of its domains, and the next clause turns this into a name: "science of science", "for that very reason" (*eben darum*) called theory of science. The name follows from the definition, not from a tradition. The paragraph names no predecessor, and the conditional "would most pointedly be named" (*am prägnantesten … zu benennen wäre*) proposes the name as the apt one rather than taking it over. One parenthesis looks ahead: the discipline is "complex, as will soon be shown". The complexity is the argument of Chapters I and II: the theory of science will turn out to be a normative discipline, a technology, and a theoretical science underlying both.

The term is taken up at once. § 6 (A/B 16, Hua 32) equates it with logic: the need for groundings "makes possible and necessary not only sciences but, with the sciences, also a theory of science, a logic". § 8 rests its possibility on "the independence of form from the field of knowledge": without it there would be only separate logics, one for each science. § 11 (A 27 / B 26–27, Hua 41) makes it "a normative discipline", and its extension to the conditions within our power makes it a "technology of science". Bolzano is first named in § 12 (A/B ~29, Hua ~43). There Husserl finds "excellent thoughts on the demarcation of our discipline" in Bolzano's *Wissenschaftslehre*, but "more in the critical preliminary investigations than in the definition he himself prefers". That definition "sounds strange enough": theory of science (or logic) is "the science that instructs us how we should present the sciences in suitable textbooks". The main commentary says that § 5's term "deliberately echoes Bolzano". The *Prolegomena* gives the title Bolzano's book bore and rejects the definition Bolzano gave it. Its own definition, science of what makes sciences sciences, comes from the scope argument of § 5. The full credit to Bolzano, the Appendix to Chapter X (A 225–226 / B 226, Hua 227), is for the content of his first two volumes, the "elementary doctrine", not for the name or its definition.

The phrase "what makes sciences in general into sciences" returns in Chapter XI as a question of possibility. § 65 is titled "The question of the ideal conditions of the possibility of science, or of theory in general". The *Prolegomena* thus ends where § 5 began, with the difference that the conditions are now called "ideal" outright. Mathematics, which in § 5 marked the limit of metaphysics, returns in § 71 as the discipline in which "a considerable part" of the theories belonging to pure logic "has long since constituted itself as formal mathematics".

In the lectures of 1906/07, *Introduction to Logic and Theory of Knowledge* (Hua XXIV 6), Husserl repeats the identification and states what it presupposes. "A concept of logic can with secure right be so taken that it coincides with that of the science of science in general, the theory of science." The identification "makes only a slight presupposition, … namely that the logical shows no special forms outside science as against those that occur in the sciences". The *Prolegomena* had equated the theory of science with logic in passing, in § 6's apposition "a theory of science, a logic". Six years later the equation is "a concept of logic" among several, and it rests on an assumption about the logical outside science that the *Prolegomena* never stated.

## §6, A/B 12–13, Hua 28: evidence as luminous certainty

Reads [§6 ¶2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-possibility-and-justification-of-a-logic-as-theory-of-science), second paragraph under § 6.

Evidence (*Evidenz*) enters the *Prolegomena* as what turns a correct judgement into knowledge in the strict sense: "the luminous certainty that what we have acknowledged is, or that what we have rejected is not". The definition is given in the vocabulary of judgement as acknowledging and rejecting, and it is secured by a threat, not an analysis: we must distinguish this certainty from blind conviction "if we are not to founder on the rocks of extreme scepticism". The main commentary reads it as already saying that evidence "is not a psychological feeling". The passage leaves that open. What evidence is, a feeling that accompanies judgements or the experience of truth itself, is the question of Chapter VIII, and the B text answers it more firmly than A.

> In knowledge, however, we possess truth. In actual knowledge, to which we see ourselves referred back in the end, we possess it as the object of a correct judgement. But this alone does not suffice; for not every correct judgement, not every positing or rejecting of a state of affairs that agrees with the truth, is a knowledge of the being or non-being of this state of affairs. For that there belongs rather, if knowledge in the narrowest and strictest sense is to be spoken of, evidence, the luminous certainty that what we have acknowledged is, or that what we have rejected is not; a certainty that we must, in a well-known way, distinguish from blind conviction, from vague opinion, however firmly decided, if we are not to founder on the rocks of extreme scepticism.

German:

> Im Wissen aber besitzen wir die Wahrheit. Im aktuellen Wissen, worauf wir uns letztlich zurückgeführt sehen, besitzen wir sie als Objekt eines richtigen Urteils. Aber dies allein reicht nicht aus; denn nicht jedes richtige Urteil, jede mit der Wahrheit übereinstimmende Setzung oder Verwerfung eines Sachverhalts ist ein Wissen vom Sein oder Nichtsein dieses Sachverhalts. Dazu gehört vielmehr — soll von einem Wissen im engsten und strengsten Sinne die Rede sein — die Evidenz, die lichtvolle Gewißheit, daß ist, was wir anerkannt, oder nicht ist, was wir verworfen haben; eine Gewißheit, die wir in bekannter Weise scheiden müssen von der blinden Überzeugung, vom vagen und sei es noch so fest entschiedenen Meinen, wofern wir nicht an den Klippen des extremen Skeptizismus scheitern sollen.

*Note on the text.* The file prints Hua's margin line numbers and breaks words across lines ("überein-/stimmende", "verworfen/25 haben"); the quotation joins them. The A/B 13 markers fall inside the second sentence.

The definition moves from possession to qualification. "In knowledge we possess truth" states the goal. The next sentence says how: "as the object of a correct judgement". Correctness is then shown to fall short, because a judgement can agree with the truth by luck. Evidence is the extra that makes the agreement knowledge. The definition is phrased entirely in terms of the judgement's own content. Evidence is certainty "that what we have acknowledged is" (*daß ist, was wir anerkannt*). Its object is the being or non-being of the state of affairs, not the judging. The syntax keeps evidence on the side of what is judged: the clause after "certainty" names the state of affairs, and the verbs of the judging subject ("acknowledged", "rejected") stand in a subordinate relative clause.

The verbs are not Husserl's own. A judgement as a "positing or rejecting" (*Setzung oder Verwerfung*), and its two forms as acknowledging and rejecting (*anerkennen*, *verwerfen*), are the vocabulary of Brentano's theory of judgement, for which judging is a distinct mode of consciousness that acknowledges or rejects what is presented (*Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint*, Book II, ch. 7, § 5, in the corpus's German second volume). So is the contrast of evident and "blind" judgements. Husserl signals the borrowing only by "in a well-known way" (*in bekannter Weise*). In that school, evidence is a character that some judgements have. Nothing in § 6 excludes this. Evidence is "a certainty", a qualification of the act of judging, and the one image the definition offers, "luminous" (*lichtvoll*), describes how the certainty is had, not what it reaches.

The justification is dialectical. The distinction "must" be drawn "if we are not to founder on the rocks of extreme scepticism". Husserl does not show that it exists; he shows that denying it costs knowledge altogether. The same form returns, with the same words, in § 32 (A/B 111, Hua 119), where the noetic conditions of the possibility of theory are fixed. A judging person who could never "experience in himself" (*in sich zu erleben*) the distinction that justifies a judgement would lack "the evidence that distinguishes [judgements] from blind prejudices and gives him the luminous certainty not merely of holding for true but of holding the truth itself"; and a theory that denies every advantage of the evident over the blind judgement abolishes what distinguishes it from arbitrary assertion. § 6 defines evidence. § 32 makes its possibility a condition of theory, and adds the verb of experience.

Chapter VIII settles what § 6 leaves open, and the edition difference falls exactly here. § 49 reports the third prejudice: evidence is "a peculiar psychic character, well known to everyone from inner experience, a peculiar feeling that guarantees the truth of the judgement to which it is attached". In B this definition is reported speech, "so it is said" (*so sagt man*). In A it read "With this word we denote" (*Mit diesem Worte bezeichnen wir*), with the parenthesis "it is usually called a feeling". § 51 (A/B 190, Hua 193–194) gives Husserl's answer: "Evidence is no accessory feeling that attaches itself by chance or by natural law to certain judgements. … Evidence is rather nothing other than the 'experience' of truth." Truth is "an idea, whose individual case is actual experience in the evident judgement". This answer is the one the main commentary attributes to § 6. B then adds a sentence that A did not have: "The evident judgement, however, is a consciousness of original givenness", whose object is "given as itself present". Where B speaks of a "phenomenological" content of the judgement that would remain the same with or without the attached character, A had said "psychological". The vocabulary of givenness and self-presence is that of the period after 1900, not of § 6.

The light image of § 6 is the thread that runs through these places. § 50 grants that the logical technology must investigate the conditions under which evidence "lights up" (*aufleuchtet*) in judging. § 51 turns the image into an analogy: the analogy that joins all originally giving experiences leads one to call evidence "a seeing, an insight, a grasping" of the self-given state of affairs. A had written only of "the image of seeing" (*das Gleichnis vom Sehen*). The metaphor of § 6 is thus promoted, between the editions, from a figure of speech to a claim about a kind of experience. Read from B's § 51, § 6's "luminous certainty" is a first name for originary givenness. Read on its own, as A's reader met it, it is a Brentanian certainty with an image attached. The commentary's claim that evidence "is not a psychological feeling" is true of the book's result and is not yet stated at § 6.

## §7, A/B 18–19, Hua 33–34: the law of form

Reads [§7 ¶3–4](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#continuation-the-three-most-significant-peculiarities-of-groundings), third and fourth paragraphs under § 7.

The second peculiarity of groundings is that they have forms, and that a form stands in relation to a law that justifies every grounding of that form "at a stroke". Husserl calls the law "a priori" and the fact that no grounding stands isolated "the most remarkable fact". What the passage establishes is not yet that logical laws are ideal; it is that the connection between premises and conclusion is governed by a law of form and not by an arrangement of the mind. The alternative it rejects is stated first, as a possible thought: a caprice of nature might have built our mental constitution so that certain cognitions are tied to others without any rational law. That alternative is the psychologistic picture in its simplest form, and the passage answers it before psychologism is named.

> But that is not how things stand. It is not that a blind arbitrariness has swept together some heap of truths P1 P2 … S and then arranged the human mind so that it must inevitably (or under "normal" circumstances) attach the knowledge of S to the knowledge of P1 P2 …. In no single case is it so. Not arbitrariness and chance rule in the connections of grounding, but reason and order, and that means: regulating law. An example is hardly needed to make this clear. […] We notice at once that these groundings have something in common, a like inner constitution, which we express intelligibly in the "inferential form": every A is B, X is A, therefore X is B. But not only these two groundings have this same form, but countless others. And more still. The inferential form represents a class-concept under which falls the infinite manifold of connections of propositions of the constitution sharply marked in it. At the same time, however, there holds the a priori law that every purported grounding that proceeds in accordance with it is also actually a correct one, provided it proceeds from correct premises at all.
>
> And this holds generally. Wherever we ascend by grounding from given cognitions to new ones, there dwells in the path of grounding a certain form that it has in common with countless other groundings, and that stands in a certain relation to a general law which permits all these individual groundings to be justified at a stroke. No grounding stands, and this is the most remarkable fact, isolated. None connects cognitions to cognitions without there being stamped on it, whether in the external mode of connection, or in this and at once in the inner structure of the individual propositions, a definite type which, grasped in general concepts, leads over at once to a general law referring to an infinity of possible groundings.

German:

> Aber so steht die Sache nicht. Nicht hat eine blinde Willkür irgendeinen Haufen von Wahrheiten P1P2… S zusammengerafft und dann den menschlichen Geist so eingerichtet, daß er an die Erkenntnis der P1P2… unweigerlich (bzw. unter ,,normalen'' Umständen) die Erkenntnis von S anknüpfen muß. In keinem einzigen Falle verhält es sich so. Nicht Willkür und Zufall herrscht in den Begründungszusammenhängen, sondern Vernunft und Ordnung, und das heißt: regelndes Gesetz. Kaum bedarf es eines Beispiels zur Verdeutlichung. Zugleich besteht aber das apriorische Gesetz, daß jede vorgebliche Begründung, die ihr gemäß verläuft, auch wirklich eine richtige ist, sofern sie überhaupt von richtigen Prämissen ausgeht.

Keine Begründung steht, dies ist die höchst merkwürdige Tatsache, isoliert. Keine knüpft Erkenntnisse an Erkenntnisse, ohne daß, sei es in dem äußerlichen Modus der Verknüpfung, sei es in diesem und zugleich in dem inneren Bau der einzelnen Sätze, ein bestimmter Typus ausgeprägt wäre, der, in allgemeine Begriffe gefaßt, sofort zu einem allgemeinen, auf eine Unendlichkeit möglicher Begründungen bezüglichen Gesetze überleitet.

"also ist XB" is the file's reading, where the form requires "X is B"; it is quoted as it stands. The file breaks "Begründungswege" across a page head and "Beziehung" across the A 19 marker; the quotation joins them.

The passage is built on negations of a picture. "Not … a blind arbitrariness", "in no single case", "not arbitrariness and chance". What is denied is a story about the mind: a heap of truths "swept together" and a human mind "arranged" (*eingerichtet*) so that it "must" attach one cognition to others. The parenthesis "(or under 'normal' circumstances)" anticipates the psychologists' hedge and mocks it with quotation marks; the same scare-quoted "normal" had appeared in § 6's account of the "normal" person under "normal" conditions, and it returns whenever Husserl discusses the normal thinker of the empiricists. The positive term is given by apposition: "reason and order, and that means: regulating law" (*regelndes Gesetz*). The participle matters. A law that regulates prescribes what is correct; it does not, like the arrangement of the mind, bring about what happens. The difference between a law that regulates and a law that causes is the subject of § 22, and here it is already in the adjective.

The step to the a priori is made by comparison, and the wording hides how large it is. "An example is hardly needed"; "we notice at once" (*wir bemerken sofort*) that two groundings share a form. What is noticed is a likeness among cases. What is asserted next is "the a priori law" that every grounding of this form is correct. The connective is "at the same time, however" (*zugleich aber*), which places the law beside the class-concept without deriving it. The fourth paragraph repeats the hesitation: the form "stands in a certain relation" (*in gewisser Beziehung*) to the law. The passage does not say what the relation is. It does not identify the form with the law, nor claim that we know the law by comparing groundings. It claims only that every form leads over to a law, and the proviso "provided it proceeds from correct premises at all" marks the law as a law of consequence, not of truth. The commentary's block says that correctness is "guaranteed a priori … by the very nature of the form". That is the result, and the passage reaches it by juxtaposition.

The emphasis falls on isolation. "No grounding stands, and this is the most remarkable fact, isolated." The inserted clause interrupts the sentence to say that what is about to be stated is not obvious, although it is everyday. § 8 explains why: these peculiarities do not strike us "because we are all too little inclined to make the everyday into a problem". The law of form is the condition of method. Without it, § 8 continues, every advance would be chance, and we could learn nothing from one grounding for the next. Here the edition differs. Where B says that the typical form of a premise-system could "impose itself on us" in a new case and ease the gaining of new knowledge, A added "according to the laws of the association of ideas" (*nach den Gesetzen der Ideenassoziation*). The A text explained the use of form in actual thinking by association. B removes the psychological mechanism and leaves only the form's being typical. The passage itself is unchanged between the editions; the account of how we profit from it was cleansed.

Two later places say what kind of thing the form is. § 7 calls it "a class-concept" under which fall connections of propositions. § 47 (A/B ~175, Hua ~178) warns against reading such terms psychologically: in pure logic the names of forms of judgement are "not titles for classes of judgements, but titles for ideal forms of propositions", and "the same holds of the inferential forms". In § 7 the class-concept is already one of connections of propositions, not of acts. § 47 adds the word "ideal". The counterfactual of the "caprice of nature" also returns, inverted. §§ 36 and 40 consider beings whose mental constitution differs from ours, and Husserl argues that their laws of thought could differ as facts without touching the laws of form. § 7 had imagined a mind arranged without law; Chapter VII shows that even a differently arranged mind would stand under the same law of form.

The argument outlasted the book unchanged. In the lectures of 1906/07 (Hua XXIV 24) Husserl repeats § 8's conclusion almost word for word: "It is the regulated form that makes the existence of the sciences possible", and the independence of form from the field of science makes possible "a science of science, or a theory of science". The law of form was the first piece of the *Prolegomena* to be settled, and the one Husserl never revised.

## §11, A 27 / B 26–27, Hua 41: normative science and its ground-measure

Reads [§11 ¶2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#logic-or-theory-of-science-as-normative-discipline-and-as-technology), second paragraph under § 11.

A normative science is defined here by its relation to a "norming ground-measure" (*normierendes Grundmaß*): an idea or a supreme end against which objects are measured. Its propositions state which characteristics guarantee conformity to the measure, or are indispensable for it. Two features of the definition carry the rest of the book. A normative discipline gives only special criteria, never a universal one, and its propositions can be stated, "equivalently", as propositions about forms: every procedure of the form M is a correct one. The second feature is the seed of § 14's thesis that every normative discipline has a theoretical content.

> For this is the essence of normative science, that it grounds general propositions in which, with reference to a norming ground-measure, e.g. an idea or a supreme end, definite characteristics are stated whose possession guarantees conformity to the measure, or, conversely, provides an indispensable condition for this conformity; and likewise related propositions in which the case of non-conformity is taken into account, or the absence of such situations is expressed. Not that it would need to give general marks that say how an object in general should be constituted in order to correspond to the basic norm; as little as therapy gives universal symptoms, so little does any normative discipline give universal criteria. What the theory of science in particular gives us, and alone can give, are special criteria. In establishing that, in view of the supreme goal of the sciences and of the factual constitution of the human mind, and whatever else may come into consideration, the methods, say M1 M2 …, arise, it utters propositions of the form: Every group of mental activities of the kinds α β … that unfold in the complex form M1 (or M2 …) yields a case of correct method; or, what is equivalent: Every (purportedly) methodical procedure of the form M1 (or M2 …) is a correct one. If it succeeded in really setting up all the propositions of this and related kinds that are possible and valid in themselves, then indeed the normative discipline would contain the measuring rule for every purported method whatever, but even then only in the form of special criteria.

German:

> Denn das ist das Wesen der normativen Wissenschaft, daß sie allgemeine Sätze begründet, in welchen mit Beziehung auf ein normierendes Grundmaß — z. B. eine Idee oder einen obersten Zweck — bestimmte Merkmale angegeben sind, deren Besitz die Angemessenheit an das Maß verbürgt oder umgekehrt eine unerläßliche Bedingung für diese Angemessenheit beistellt; desgleichen auch verwandte Sätze, in welchen der Fall der Unangemessenheit berücksichtigt oder das Nichtvorhandensein solcher Sachlagen ausgesprochen ist. M2 …) sich abwickeln, liefert einen Fall richtiger Methode; oder was gleichwertig ist: Jedes (angeblich) methodische Verfahren der Form M1 (bzw. M2 …) ist ein richtiges. Gelänge es, alle an sich möglichen und gültigen Sätze dieser und verwandter Art wirklich aufzustellen, dann allerdings enthielte die normative Disziplin die messende Regel für jede angebliche Methode überhaupt, aber auch dann nur in Form von Spezialkriterien.

*Note on the text.* The file's "Nicht ob sie … brauchte" does not construe; the sense, confirmed by the following clause, is "Not that it would need to give …", and the English renders that sense without repairing the German. "die die Methoden" doubles the article. The file sets the symbols in LaTeX; they are given as plain letters and Greek. The A 27 page marker falls after "begründet".

The definition is relational from its first clause. A normative science does not study objects as they are; it states "characteristics" (*Merkmale*) of objects "with reference to" a measure. The measure is what gives the discipline its unity, and § 14's heading names it as such: "the ground-measure or principle that gives it unity". The examples, "an idea or a supreme end", leave the measure's nature open. It may be a value, as in ethics, or a goal, as in the technologies. For logic it is both at once: the "idea of science", which the first paragraph of § 11 calls what logic investigates "so that we can measure" the given sciences against it, and "the supreme goal of the sciences". The verb of the definition is measuring. "Conformity to the measure" (*Angemessenheit an das Maß*) and "the measuring rule" (*die messende Regel*) frame the paragraph.

The criteria are special because the measure does not say how to meet it. A universal criterion would say "how an object in general should be constituted in order to correspond to the basic norm". The therapy analogy explains why none exists. Medicine aims at health, but it diagnoses by the symptoms of particular diseases; there are no symptoms of illness as such. Logic aims at valid science, but it can state only that this or that form of method is correct. The point protects the book's later argument. If logic gave a universal criterion of truth, it would compete with evidence; since it gives special criteria, the question becomes what theoretical knowledge those criteria draw on.

The last sentence-pair answers that question in advance. The theory of science establishes its methods "in view of the supreme goal of the sciences and of the factual constitution of the human mind". The mind's factual constitution belongs to the grounds of the technology, which is the share that psychology keeps throughout the book. Then two propositions are declared "equivalent" (*gleichwertig*). The first speaks of "groups of mental activities of the kinds α β" that unfold in a form; the second speaks only of "procedures of the form M", and is about the form. The equivalence turns a statement about acts into a statement about a form of procedure. "Purportedly" (*angeblich*) in the second proposition repeats § 7's "purported grounding" (*vorgebliche Begründung*): the law of form decides whether what claims to be a method is one. The paragraph does not yet say that the second proposition is theoretical. It shows that the normative proposition can be written in a form from which the thought of a norm has dropped out.

That is what § 14 makes explicit. Its opening thesis (A/B 40, Hua 53) is that every normative discipline rests on theoretical ones, because its rules must possess "a theoretical content separable from the thought of norming (of the ought)". § 11's equivalence is the first instance of the separation, and § 14 generalizes it to the ought in all its forms. Chapter XI turns the measure into a principle of the unity of science. § 64 (A ~236, Hua ~238) names, as the last of the "extra-essential" principles, the unity that arises "from a unified valuing interest, and is thus objectively determined by a unified basic value (or by the unified basic norm), as we discussed at length in Chapter II, § 14". Normative unity is there set "in opposition" to material unity, the unity grounded in the things themselves. The ground-measure that defined normative science in § 11 is, in the end, the mark of a science whose unity is not theoretical; and normative sciences take from the theoretical ones "everything that makes up what is scientific in them".

The definition survived into Husserl's ethics, with one change of accent. A supplement to the ethics lectures of 1908/09 (Hua XXVIII 368) contrasts theoretical science, which investigates "what belongs to the objects of a domain, what they are", with what is demanded of objects "under the idea of a basic norm and a practical end, in conditional manner": "what they must be if they are to accord with norms and ends". The *Prolegomena*'s normative proposition stated a sufficient or necessary characteristic. The later text calls its demand conditional and the disciplines that make it "hypothetically" proceeding. What § 11 left implicit, that the ought holds only given the measure, has become the definition.

## §14, A/B 40, Hua 53: the separable theoretical content

Reads [§14 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-concept-of-normative-science-the-ground-measure-or-principle-that-gives-it-unity), first paragraph under § 14.

Every normative discipline, and every practical one, rests on one or more theoretical disciplines, because its rules must have a theoretical content that can be separated from the thought of norming. The thesis has two parts, and the book needs both. The first is separability: what a norm says, apart from its being a norm, is a theoretical proposition. The second is a plurality: a discipline rests on "one or more" theoretical disciplines, so that the question of which of them is its essential foundation stays open. The psychologist and Husserl agree on the first part. They part over the second.

> We begin with the fixing of a proposition that is of decisive importance for the further investigation, namely that every normative and likewise every practical discipline rests on one or more theoretical disciplines, inasmuch as its rules must possess a theoretical content separable from the thought of norming (of the ought), the scientific investigation of which is incumbent precisely on those theoretical disciplines.

German:

> Wir beginnen mit der Fixierung eines Satzes, der für die weitere Untersuchung von entscheidender Wichtigkeit ist, nämlich daß jede normative und desgleichen jede praktische Disziplin auf einer oder mehreren theoretischen Disziplinen beruht, sofern ihre Regeln einen von dem Gedanken der Normierung (des Sollens) abtrennbaren theoretischen Gehalt besitzen müssen, dessen wissenschaftliche Erforschung eben jenen theoretischen Disziplinen obliegt.

The sentence states the thesis before the analysis that proves it. "We begin with the fixing" (*Fixierung*) of a proposition; its proof comes two sections later, after the analysis of the ought in § 14 and of the technology in § 15. § 16 then says that it is "now easy to see". The order is that of a theorem stated and then demonstrated, and § 14's heading, "the concept of normative science", names the lemma on which the demonstration rests.

The connective carries the argument. The discipline rests on theoretical ones "inasmuch as" (*sofern*) its rules "must possess" a separable content. *Sofern* gives the ground and the limit at once: a normative discipline depends on theory exactly to the extent of that content, and no further. Its unity, as the rest of § 14 shows, comes from elsewhere, from the basic norm, which "is the correlate of the definition of the 'good'" and "does not, in the proper sense, represent a normative proposition". The "must" is not empirical. A rule that had no theoretical content would say nothing about its objects beyond the demand. The thought it would lack is named in a parenthesis: "the thought of norming (of the ought)". The ought is a thought added to a content, not a component of the content.

"Separable" (*abtrennbar*) is the key word, and § 16 repeats it as "detachable from all norming" (*von aller Normierung ablösbar*). The repetition is where the proof lies. Every normative proposition expresses "the thought of a measuring relation between norm and normed", and that relation, "if we abstract from the valuing interest", is "objectively a relation between condition and conditioned". "An A ought to be B" includes "Only an A that is B has the properties C", where C is the constitutive content of "good" in the given sphere. "The new proposition is a purely theoretical one; it contains nothing more of the thought of norming." The converse holds as well. If a theoretical proposition of that form is valid, and a valuation of C arises "as something new", the theoretical proposition "takes on the normative form". Normativity is added to a theoretical content by a valuation, and it can be taken away by abstraction. That is the sense of "separable".

The plural is the hinge to Chapter III. "One or more theoretical disciplines": § 16's last paragraph grades them. Theoretical sciences may share "in different measure" in grounding a normative discipline. Some are "decisive", others "useful and possibly very important, but only of secondary significance", so that their removal would restrict the discipline without abolishing it. The example is ethics: without the norms of ethical valuation there would be no ethics at all; without the propositions about practical realization there would only be no ethical technology. "With reference to such differences" Husserl then defines the "essential theoretical foundations" of a normative science. § 17 at once applies the definition to logic and asks which theoretical sciences supply "the essential foundations of the theory of science". Psychologism's answer is psychology. § 20 answers that psychology is co-involved, but that the essential foundation, "in the sense we defined (§ 16)", may lie in another science. The whole of Chapters III to X lives in the space between "one" and "more".

The thesis is turned back on logic in § 41 (A/B 156, Hua 160–161). The logical laws, "by their own nature, are not normative but theoretical truths", and like the truths of any discipline they can "serve for the norming of judging". Husserl then asks whether "the idea of regulation (of the ought)" must lie "in the content of the logical propositions themselves", or whether it can "ground in this content with evident necessity". § 14 separated the ought from every norm's content. § 41 asks why, in logic, the content calls for the ought. The answer, that logical laws have a "distinguished content of meaning" that gives them "a natural vocation for the regulation of thought", is the positive side of separability: the theoretical content is not only what remains when the ought is subtracted, it is what makes the ought fitting.

The commentary calls the thesis "the lever". The wording shows how far the lever reaches. It separates norm and theory in every normative discipline. It does not by itself separate logic from psychology. That needs the grading of § 16 and the critical chapters, since a psychologist can accept § 14 entire and reply that the separable content of logic's rules is psychological.

## §14, A/B 40–41, Hua 54: the widened ought

Reads [§14 ¶3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-concept-of-normative-science-the-ground-measure-or-principle-that-gives-it-unity), third paragraph under § 14.

The ought (*Sollen*) is detached from anyone's wishing or commanding and redefined as a condition on a value-predicate. "A warrior ought to be brave" means that only a brave warrior is a "good" warrior. The redefinition is what makes § 14's thesis provable, since a condition on a predicate is a theoretical relation. It is also why the right to demand comes last: a demand is right because the value-judgement holds, not the other way round.

> Too narrow, evidently, is the original sense of the ought, which has reference to a certain wishing or willing, to a demand or a command, e.g.: You shall obey me; X shall come to me. Just as in a wider sense we speak of a demand where there is no one who demands, and possibly also no one who is called upon, so we often speak of an ought independent of anyone's wishing or willing. If we say: "A warrior ought to be brave", this does not mean that we or anyone else wish or will, command or demand this. One might rather take the meaning to be that generally, i.e. in relation to every warrior, a corresponding wishing and demanding is justified; though even this is not quite right, since it is not strictly necessary that such an evaluation of a wish or a demand should actually take place here. "A warrior ought to be brave" means rather: only a brave warrior is a "good" warrior, and this implies, since the predicates good and bad divide the extension of the concept warrior between them, that a warrior who is not brave is a "bad" warrior. Because this value-judgement holds, everyone is right who demands of a warrior that he be brave; for the same reason it is also desirable, praiseworthy, and so on, that he should be. Likewise in other examples. […] In all these cases, then, we make our positive valuation, the attribution of a positive value-predicate, dependent on a condition to be fulfilled, whose non-fulfilment brings the corresponding negative predicate in its train. In general we may set as the same, or at least as equivalent, the forms: "An A ought to be B" and "An A that is not B is a bad A", or "Only an A that is B is a good A".

German:

> Zu enge ist offenbar der ursprüngliche Sinn des Sollens, welcher Beziehung hat zu einem gewissen Wünschen oder Wollen, zu einer Forderung oder einem Befehl, z.B.: Du sollst mir gehorchen; X soll zu mir kommen. Wie wir in einem weiteren Sinn von einer Forderung sprechen, wobei niemand da ist, der fordert, und evtl. auch niemand, der aufgefordert ist, so sprechen wir auch oft von einem Sollen, unabhängig von irgend jemandes Wünschen oder Wollen. Sagen wir: „Ein Krieger soll tapfer sein“, so heißt das nicht, daß wir oder jemand sonst dies wünschen oder wollen, befehlen oder fordern. Eher könnte man die Meinung dahin fassen, daß allgemein, d.h. in Beziehung auf jeden Krieger, ein entsprechendes Wünschen und Fordernd Berechtigung habe; obschon auch dies nicht ganz zutrifft, da es doch nicht geradezu nötig ist, daß hier solches eine Bewertung eines Wunsches oder einer Forderung wirklich Platz greife. „Ein Krieger soll tapfer sein“, das heißt vielmehr: nur ein tapferer Krieger ist ein „guter“ Krieger, und darin liegt, da die Prädikate gut und schlecht den Umfang des Begriffs Krieger unter sich teilen, da ein nicht tapferer ein „schlechter“ Krieger ist. Weil dieses Werturteil gilt, hat nun jedermann recht, der von einem Krieger fordert, daß er tapfer sei; aus demselben Grunde ist, daß er sei, auch wünschenswert, lobenswert usw. Ebenso in anderen Beispielen. […] In allen diesen Fällen machen wir also unsere positive Wertschätzung, die Zuerkennung eines positiven Wertprädikates, abhängig von einer zu erfüllenden Bedingung, deren Nichterfüllung das entsprechende negative Prädikat nach sich zieht. Überhaupt dürfen wir als gleich, zum mindesten als äquivalent setzen [^1] Formen: „Ein *A* soll *B* sein“ und „Ein *A*, welches nicht *B* ist, ist ein schlechtes *A*“, oder „Nur ein *A*, welches *B* ist, ist ein gutes *A*“.

*Note on the text.* The omission […] is the examples of neighbourly love and the drama. The file has several OCR slips that the reading does not depend on: "Fordernd" for *Fordern*, "solches eine Bewertung" (the construction is unclear), "da" twice for *daß* ("da ein nicht tapferer", the second clause), and an orphaned note anchor "[^1]" where an article (*die*) is missing before "Formen"; the definition of that note in the file ("A: 'den'") belongs to a different sentence. The A/B 41 markers fall inside "gehorchen". The emphasis on *A* and *B* is the file's.

The paragraph proceeds by three readings of one sentence. The first, the "original sense", ties the ought to someone who wishes or commands: "You shall obey me." German uses one modal, *sollen*, for the command and for the norm, and the example exploits that. "You shall obey me" has a speaker; "a warrior ought to be brave" has none. The analogy that bridges them is a demand "where there is no one who demands, and possibly also no one who is called upon". The second reading is tried and withdrawn: the ought as the justification of a possible demand, "in relation to every warrior". "Not quite right", because no evaluation of a wish need take place at all. The third reading removes the subject entirely: "only a brave warrior is a 'good' warrior". The ought has become a condition on a predicate. The order of the readings is the argument: each removes an act (commanding, then justifying a demand) until only a judgement about a class of objects remains.

The connective "because" reverses the usual order of ground. "Because this value-judgement holds, everyone is right who demands of a warrior that he be brave." The demand, which in the first reading was the ought, is now a consequence of it, and so are desirability and praise. The equivalence then fixes the form: "An A ought to be B" is "the same, or at least equivalent" to "Only an A that is B is a good A". The hedge "or at least equivalent" matters. Husserl does not claim that the two sentences mean the same, only that each holds when the other does, the same restraint § 50 will show when it denies that the evidence-equivalent of a logical law "says the same".

The quotation marks around "good" and "bad" mark the predicates as variables of the schema. The next paragraph says that "good" is used "in the widest sense of the valuable in any way", to be read in each case "in the particular sense of the valuations that underlie" the propositions: useful, beautiful, moral. The quotation marks keep the analysis neutral among values. § 14 later says what supplies the predicate: every normative proposition "presupposes a certain kind of valuation (approval, esteem)" through which the concept of a "good" A arises, and whether that valuation is "objectively valid" is not in question "in the mere establishing of the sense of ought-sentences". Here the editions differ. B says it suffices "that an intention is performed with the content that something is of value or good". A said only that it suffices that something "is held to be of value" (*für wert gehalten wird*), with an obscure continuation. B describes the valuation as an intentional act with a content, in the vocabulary of the Fifth Investigation; A had spoken only of holding something to be valuable. The main commentary keeps the A spelling *Werthhaltung* (valuation), but not the A text's thinner account of what a valuation is.

The analysis serves the thesis of the section's first paragraph. Once "ought" is a condition on a value-predicate, § 16 can separate the condition, "only an A that is B has the properties C", from the valuation of C. It also prepares the treatment of logic. § 19 sets Lipps's formula that logic "is physics of thinking or it is nothing at all" (A/B ~54, Hua 66) against the anti-psychologists' reply that logic concerns how we ought to think, "not a physics but an ethics of thinking" (A/B 55, Hua 67). § 14 has already shown that an ethics of thinking would rest on theoretical propositions, so the dispute cannot be settled by the word "ought". § 41 draws the conclusion for logic: its laws are "by their own nature … not normative but theoretical truths".

§ 14's formal treatment of value-predicates, with its table of "ought", "ought not", "need not" and "may", is neutral among ethics. It names the basic norms of two of them, "the categorical imperative" in Kant's and "the greatest possible happiness of the greatest possible number" in the utilitarians', and declines to choose. The basic norm "is the correlate of the definition" of the good, and "does not, in the proper sense, represent a normative proposition". So the widened ought of the warrior sentence is the same whichever good is adopted; what changes from one discipline to the next is only the content C that the valuation marks out, and that content is theoretical.

## §19, A/B 57–58, Hua 69–70: inferring according to rules and from rules

Reads [§19 ¶3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-usual-arguments-of-the-opposing-party-and-their-psychologistic-resolution), third paragraph under § 19.

The anti-psychologists charge that grounding logic on psychology is circular, since psychology, like every science, presupposes the validity of logic's rules. The psychologists answer by distinguishing two senses of "presuppose": a rule may be a premise of a science's groundings, or a rule according to which it must proceed. To infer according to logical rules is not to infer from them, and only the second would make a circle. The distinction is sound, and Husserl later keeps it. In § 19 it is spoken in the psychologists' voice, and § 43 shows that it cuts the other way: turned on pure logic itself, it yields a circle of a new kind.

> The other side will answer: that this argument cannot be right is clear already from the fact that the impossibility of logic in general would follow from it. Since logic as a science must itself proceed logically, it would indeed fall into the same circle; it would have at the same time to ground the validity of the rules it presupposes.
>
> But let us look more closely at what the circle is supposed to consist in. In that psychology presupposes the logical laws as valid? But mark the equivocation in the concept of presupposition. That a science presupposes the validity of certain rules can mean: they are premises for its groundings; but it can also mean: they are rules according to which the science must proceed in order to be science at all. The argument throws the two together; to infer according to logical rules and to infer from them count for it as the same; for only if one inferred from them would the circle exist. But just as many an artist creates fine works without knowing the least thing about aesthetics, so a researcher can construct proofs without ever having recourse to logic; so logical laws cannot have been their premises. And what holds of single proofs holds of whole sciences too.

German:

> Man wird von der Gegenseite antworten: Daß diese Argumentation nicht richtig sein kann, erhellt schon daraus, daß aus ihr die Unmöglichkeit der Logik überhaupt folgen würde. Da die Logik als Wissenschaft selbst logisch verfahren muß, so verfiele sie ja demselben Zirkel; die Triftigkeit der Regeln, die sie voraussetzt, müßte sie zugleich begründen.
>
> Aber sehen wir näher zu, worin der Zirkel eigentlich bestehen soll. Darin, daß die Psychologie die logischen Gesetze als gültig voraussetze? Aber man achte auf die Äquivokation im Begriff der Voraussetzung. Eine Wissenschaft setzt die Gültigkeit gewisser Regeln voraus, das kann heißen: sie sind Prämissen für ihre Begründungen; es kann aber auch heißen: sie sind Regeln, denen gemäß die Wissenschaft verfahren muß, um überhaupt Wissenschaft zu sein. Beides wirft das Argument zusammen; nach logischen Regeln schließen und aus ihnen schließen, gilt ihm als dasselbe; denn nur, wenn aus ihnen geschlossen würde, bestände der Zirkel. Aber wie so mancher Künstler schöne Werke schafft, ohne von Ästhetik das Geringste zu wissen, so kann ein Forscher Beweise aufbauen, ohne je auf die Logik zu rekurrieren; also können logische Gesetze nicht deren Prämissen gewesen sein. Und was von einzelnen Beweisen gilt, das gilt auch von ganzen Wissenschaften.

*Note on the text.* The file prints Hua's margin line numbers inside the text and sets the A/B 58 markers between the two paragraphs; the quotation leaves them out. In A the second paragraph read "the circle urged" (*der urgierte Zirkel*); B drops the participle. The second paragraph continues across a page of footnotes (Lotze, Natorp, Erdmann, Stumpf for the circle charge), which the quotation skips.

The voice of the passage is the first thing to settle. § 19's heading announces "the usual arguments of the opposing party and their psychologistic resolution", and the paragraph opens "The other side will answer" (*Man wird von der Gegenseite antworten*). The "we" of "let us look more closely" belongs to that answer: Husserl argues the psychologists' case for them, and better than they did. The main commentary says Husserl "acknowledges its force". He does, but in Chapter VIII, not here.

The reply has two steps, and the first is a reductio. If presupposing logical rules made a grounding circular, logic itself would be impossible, since it too "must proceed logically" and would have "to ground the validity of the rules it presupposes". The second step diagnoses the reductio. The anti-psychological argument trades on an "equivocation in the concept of presupposition". The distinction is carried by two prepositions: inferring "according to" (*nach*) logical rules and inferring "from" (*aus*) them. A premise enters the content of a grounding; a rule governs its course. The difference is the one § 7 drew between a grounding and the law of its form: the law justifies the grounding "at a stroke" without being one of its steps.

The proof of the distinction is the artist. "Many an artist creates fine works without knowing the least thing about aesthetics." The image is borrowed from the book's own beginning. § 4 (A/B 10, Hua 25) had used it to show that practical mastery seldom rests on theoretical knowledge: the artist "does not create according to principles", but by "the inner motion of his harmoniously formed powers" and a "finely trained artistic tact". There it served Husserl's argument that the individual sciences, too, proceed by a trained tact and are theoretically incomplete, which is why they need a theory of science. In § 19 the psychologists take the same image and draw from it that logic's laws "cannot have been" the premises of proofs. The inference is weaker than the image. That researchers do not consult logic shows that logical laws are not premises they cite; it does not show what kind of laws they are, still less that their foundation is psychological. The image concerns the researcher's knowledge, the conclusion concerns the logical status of the rules.

The commentary credits the distinction as "a genuine insight". The source shows where it leads. § 43 (A/B 165–167, Hua 169–170), the review of the idealist counter-arguments, finds "something right beside what is erroneous" in the circle argument. There is no circle in a psychological grounding of logic as a technology. But to derive the purely logical propositions from "the contingent content" of a factual science is "a crass incongruity", and in certain cases "a kind of circle". Deriving primitive principles such as the principle of contradiction or *modus ponens* would presuppose them "not in the manner of premises, but in that of principles of derivation, without whose validity the derivation would lose sense and validity". "In this respect one could speak of a *reflective circle*, as opposed to the ordinary or direct *circulus in demonstrando*, where premises and conclusions run into one another." This is the psychologists' own distinction, premise against rule of proceeding, turned against them. Inferring according to the principle of contradiction is harmless in psychology; it is fatal in a psychology that would ground the principle of contradiction. Pure logic alone escapes the reflective circle, because its premises are "homogeneous" with its conclusions in what they refer to, and because it sets the principles every deduction presupposes "at the head of all deductions as axioms". A footnote adds that "the impossibility of theoretical connections between heterogeneous domains, and the essence of the heterogeneity in question, has not been sufficiently investigated logically", the confusion of domains of § 2 named again as an open problem.

The passage therefore marks a pattern of the critical chapters. Husserl gives the psychologists their best argument against a weak anti-psychologism, accepts the distinction it rests on, and then shows that the distinction, applied to pure logic, refutes psychologism more sharply than the argument it answered. The commentary also omits the formula of Lipps that § 19 quotes as the psychologists' position, that logic "is physics of thinking or it is nothing at all", against which the anti-psychologists set "not a physics but an ethics of thinking". The circle argument is the anti-psychologists' second weapon, and the "according to"/"from" distinction disarms it only as long as logic is taken for a technology.

## §20, A 59–60 / B 59–60, Hua 70–71: psychology co-involved

Reads [§20 ¶2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#a-gap-in-the-psychologistic-line-of-argument), second paragraph under § 20.

The psychologists' argument, which § 18 set out and § 19 defended, proves less than it claims. It shows that psychology is "co-involved" (*mitbeteiligt*) in the foundation of normative logic, not that it is involved alone or even chiefly, and not that it supplies the essential foundation in the sense of § 16. That leaves a place, as a possibility, for a pure logic. The hinge of Chapters III and IV is a modal claim: something "remains open", a place "may be" there, a "conjecture" is to be confirmed. The rest of the critique turns the possibility into a result.

> Let us return to the question raised above, concerning the essential theoretical foundations of normative logic. Is it really settled by the argument of the psychologists? Here we notice at once a weak point. What the argument proves is only this, that psychology is co-involved in the foundation of logic, but not that it is involved in it alone, or even predominantly, not that it supplies it with the essential foundation in the sense we defined (§ 16). The possibility remains open that another science contributes to its foundation, and perhaps in an incomparably more significant way. And here may be the place for that "pure logic" which, according to the other party, is to lead its existence independent of all psychology, as a naturally delimited science closed in itself. We gladly concede that what the Kantians and Herbartians have worked out under this title does not quite correspond to the character it would have to have according to the conjecture we have put forward. For they speak everywhere of normative laws of thinking, in particular of concept-formation, of judgement-formation, and so on; proof enough, one might say, that the material is neither a theoretical one nor one foreign to psychology. But this misgiving would lose its force if, on closer investigation, the conjecture were confirmed that forced itself on us above (§ 13, p. 38): namely that those schools were not happy in the definition and construction of the intended discipline, but did come close to it inasmuch as they noticed in traditional logic a wealth of theoretically connected truths that cannot be placed in psychology or in other individual sciences, and so let a realm of truth of its own be divined.

German:

> Kehren wir zu der oben aufgeworfenen Frage nach den wesentlichen theoretischen Fundamenten der normativen Logik zurück. Ist sie durch die Argumentation der Psychologisten wirklich erledigt? Hier bemerken wir sofort einen schwachen Punkt. Erwiesen ist durch das Argument nur das eine, daß die Psychologie an der Fundierung der Logik mitbeteiligt ist, nicht aber, daß sie an ihr allein oder auch nur vorzugsweise beteiligt ist, nicht, daß sie ihr das wesentliche Fundament in dem von uns (§ 16) definierten Sinn beistellt. Die Möglichkeit bleibt offen, daß eine andere Wissenschaft und vielleicht in ungeleich bedeutsamerer Weise zu ihrer Fundierung beiträge. Und hier mag die Stelle sein für jene „reine Logik”, welche nach der anderen Partei ihr von aller Psychologie unabhängiges Dasein führen soll, als eine natürlich begrenzte, in sich geschlossene Wissenschaft. Wir gestehen gerne zu, es entspricht, was von den Kantianern und Herbartianern unter diesem Titel bearbeitet worden ist, nicht ganz dem Charakter, der ihr nach der angeregten Vermutung eignen müßte. Ist doch bei ihnen allerwege die Rede von normativen Gesetzen des Denkens, im besonderen der Begriffsbildung, der Urteilsbildung usw.; Beweis genug, möchte man sagen, daß der Stoff weder ein theoretischer, noch ein der Psychologie fremder ist. Aber dieses Bedenken verlöre seine Kraft, wenn sich bei näherer Untersuchung die Vermutung bestätigte, die sich uns oben (§ 13, S. 38) aufdrängte, nämlich, daß jene Schulen zwar in der Definition und im Aufbau der intendierten Disziplin nicht glücklich waren, aber ihr doch insofern nahe kamen, als sie eine Fülle theoretisch zusammengehöriger Wahrheiten in der traditionellen Logik bemerkten, die sich weder in die Psychologie, noch in andere Einzelwissenschaften einreihen und somit ein eigenes Reich der Wahrheit ahnen ließen.

*Note on the text.* The file has "ungeleich" for *ungleich* (incomparably) and "beiträge" for *beitrage*; both are quoted as they stand. It sets doubled note anchors at "bemerken" and "waren" whose notes are not in the file, and the A/B 60 markers inside "Definition | und … Aufbau |"; the quotation drops the anchors and markers. The paragraph ends with two more sentences in which Husserl hopes "to be able to show" that this is the true state of affairs.

The verb that carries the paragraph is a compound. Psychology is *mitbeteiligt*: involved along with something else. The prefix *mit-* (co-) does the work. It grants the psychologists their whole argument, since the argument showed that psychology has a share, and it denies their conclusion, since a share is not the foundation. The next clauses grade the share by the scale of § 16: not "alone", not "predominantly", not "the essential foundation in the sense we defined". The commentary quotes the A spelling *mitbetheiligt*, and paraphrases the paragraph faithfully up to this point.

Then the modality changes, and the commentary's block does not follow it. "The possibility remains open"; another science contributes "perhaps" (*vielleicht*) in an incomparably more significant way; "here may be the place" (*mag die Stelle sein*); the character pure logic "would have to have according to the conjecture we have put forward" (*nach der angeregten Vermutung*). Husserl has found a gap in a proof, not a proof of the opposite. The block's gloss, that the psychologistic argument "does not and cannot establish" that psychology is the essential contributor, again turns a "does not" into a "cannot". The paragraph claims only that the argument has not established it, and calls the alternative a conjecture to be confirmed "on closer investigation". The confirmation is Chapters IV to VIII.

The concession that follows, which the block drops, is what keeps the conjecture honest. The pure logic of "the Kantians and Herbartians" does "not quite" (*nicht ganz*) answer to the conjecture, since they speak "everywhere of normative laws of thinking". Husserl states the objection in its strongest form, "proof enough, one might say", that their material is neither theoretical nor foreign to psychology. His reply rests on a distinction between what the schools defined and what they noticed. They "were not happy in the definition and construction of the intended discipline", but they "noticed" a wealth of theoretically connected truths that fit no individual science, and so "let a realm of truth of its own be divined" (*ahnen ließen*). "Intended" repeats § 3's verdict on Kant, and "divined" places the Kantians' achievement in their sense for the matter, not in their theory. The comparative of the preceding paragraph prepares the judgement: "the more important part of the truth lies on the anti-psychologistic side", only "the decisive thoughts" are "not properly worked out".

The edition difference falls in that preceding paragraph, and it concerns the same balance. Husserl asks whether an unresolved remainder does not survive in the anti-psychologists' arguments, "with some errors or unclarities in detail, which offered the handles for the refutations". A had: "with many an error in detail, which the refutations undoubtedly bring to light" (*welche durch die Widerlegungen unzweifelhaft zutage tritt*). The key-passage list described the A wording as a qualification that B removes; it is rather reworded, and the rewording runs one way. In A the psychologists' refutations "undoubtedly" exposed real errors. In B the errors, now joined by mere "unclarities", only "offered the handles" by which the refutations took hold. B grants the refutations less: they gripped the weak points of the arguments, not their substance. The 1913 Husserl is more sure than the Husserl of 1900 that the anti-psychologists were mostly right.

The paragraph's "we shall show" is redeemed in Chapter VIII. § 41 says of the logical laws that "by their own nature they are not normative but theoretical truths", which is the "realm of truth of its own" the Kantians divined. § 46 names its domain as ideal. § 43 then gives the anti-psychologists' arguments their due, sentence by sentence, as the "something right beside what is erroneous" that § 20 had promised to find. The co-involvement of psychology is never withdrawn. § 46 grants that logic as a technology deals "to a wide extent with psychic experiences", and the Preface's concession to psychological analysis of origins stays in force. The gap in the psychologists' proof becomes the place of pure logic without psychology losing its share of the technology.

## §21, A/B 62–63, Hua 74–75: surmise and insight

Reads [§21 ¶3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#characterisation-of-two-empiricist-consequences-of-the-psychologistic-standpoint-and-their-refutation), the block headed "Paragraph 3 (Second consequence)" under § 21.

The second empiricist consequence turns on the difference between a law that is probable and a law that says something is probable. A natural law can be grounded only by induction, and induction justifies with insight only the probability of the law's validity. If the logical laws were natural laws of thinking, they would have the rank of mere probabilities. But the principle of contradiction "does not say that it is to be surmised" that one of two contradictory judgements is true. The logical laws are known by apodictic evidence, and what is seen is "not mere probabilities of their validity but their validity or truth itself". The opposition that carries the section is surmise against insight (*Vermutung*, *Einsicht*).

> Second. If someone, in order to escape the first objection, wanted to deny the thoroughgoing inexactness of psychological laws and to ground the norms of the class just singled out on supposedly exact natural laws of thinking, not much would yet be gained.
>
> No natural law is knowable *a priori*, is itself groundable with insight. The only way to ground and justify such a law is induction from single facts of experience. Induction, however, grounds not the validity of the law but only the more or less high probability of this validity; what is justified with insight is the probability, and not the law. Consequently the logical laws too, and without exception, would have to have the rank of mere probabilities. Against this nothing seems more manifest than that the "purely logical" laws are all valid *a priori*. Not by induction but by apodictic evidence do they find grounding and justification. What is justified with insight is not mere probabilities of their validity, but their validity or truth itself.
>
> The principle of contradiction does not say that it is to be surmised that of two contradictory judgements one is true and one false; the *modus Barbara* does not say that, if two propositions of the form "All *A* are *B*" and "all *B* are *C*" are true, it is to be surmised that a corresponding proposition of the form "All *A* are *C*" is true. And so everywhere, in the domain of the purely mathematical propositions too. Otherwise we should have to keep open the possibility that the surmise might not be confirmed when our always merely limited circle of experience is extended.

*Note on the text.* The key-passage list records "vermuten" (surmise) as emphasized in the source. The file prints it without italics, and the file has lost much of Hua's spaced type, so the reading does not argue from emphasis either way. The italics on *a priori*, *modus Barbara* and the letters are the file's. "Einwände" (for *Einwande*) is quoted as the file has it. In A the second paragraph's first sentence read "No natural law is *a priori*, that is, knowable with insight" (*a priori, d.h. einsichtig erkennbar*); B splits the one predicate into two, "knowable *a priori*, … itself groundable with insight".

The argument has the form of a dilemma, and this is its second horn. The first consequence assumed that psychological laws are vague, so that the exact logical laws could not rest on them. The opponent escapes by positing exact laws of thinking. Husserl concedes the exactness for the sake of argument and changes the ground of the objection: exact or not, a natural law is known only by induction, and induction gives probability. "Not much would yet be gained" (*noch nicht viel gewonnen*) is an understatement. Nothing is gained, because the defect lies in how any natural law is known, not in psychology's state of development.

The core sentence places the insight carefully. Induction does not lack insight. "What is justified with insight is the probability, and not the law" (*einsichtig gerechtfertigt ist die Wahrscheinlichkeit und nicht das Gesetz*). The difference between natural and logical laws is therefore not that one is seen and the other is not. It is a difference in what is seen: in the one case a probability of validity, in the other "validity or truth itself". The last sentence of the second paragraph repeats the construction with its object reversed. Evidence, defined in § 6 as the luminous certainty that what we acknowledge is, here becomes the mark of the a priori. The A text made the link a definition, "a priori, that is, knowable with insight". B no longer equates the two terms, but it keeps both.

The examples shift the argument from our knowledge to the law's content, and here the inference is weaker than the wording. "The principle of contradiction does not say that it is to be surmised" (*sagt nicht, es sei zu vermuten*). If logical laws were inductive, Husserl argues, their content would be a surmise. But a psychologist could answer that an exactly formulated law of thinking need not say "it is to be surmised"; it would say flatly that contradictory judgements are not both true, and only our warrant for it would be inductive. The step from "known by induction" to "says that something is to be surmised" needs a premise that the paragraph does not state: that the sense of a law is what is evident in it, so that a law known only as probable could only be meant as probable. The next sentences supply it indirectly. If the laws were inductive, "we should have to keep open the possibility" that they fail when experience widens; "perhaps our logical laws are then only 'approximations'". That possibility, which the meaning of the principle of contradiction excludes, is what an inductive law must leave open. The section then closes on the appeal it has been making all along: "the mere look at any one of the logical laws, at its proper meaning and the insight with which it is grasped as truth in itself, would have to put an end to the deception". "Probability cannot contend against truth, surmise not against insight."

The counter-case is physics. Newton's law of gravitation is recommended by the most comprehensive inductions and verifications, yet no scientist takes it as absolutely valid; Weber's law of electrical phenomena could serve as well within the limits of observational error, and "infinitely many" such factors are conceivable. "Therefore we know *a priori*" that infinitely many laws can do what Newton's does. The phrase is pointed: even the underdetermination of natural laws is known a priori. The list of logical laws in the first consequence has a pointed member too: "the Bernoullian inference from n to n + 1", mathematical induction, stands among the exact laws, set against the empirical induction that grounds natural laws. The word "induction" is used in both senses within two paragraphs, and the contrast is the argument in small.

The understanding returns at the end of the book, where probability itself is given a logic. The first consequence already counted "the principles of probability inferences" among the exact logical laws. § 72 (A 255–256, Hua 257) extends pure logic to "pure probability theory as pure theory of empirical knowledge". "All theory in the empirical sciences is merely supposed theory. It gives explanation not from basic laws certain with insight, but only from basic laws probable with insight" (*einsichtig wahrscheinlichen*). The phrase repeats § 21's "what is justified with insight is the probability". In § 21 that probability was the mark of natural laws, which disqualified them as foundations of logic. In § 72 it becomes the subject of ideal laws in its own right: the probability of empirical theories stands under laws that are not themselves probable. The surmise is excluded from the content of logic and readmitted as one of its objects.

## §22, A/B 67–68, Hua 79: the calculating machine

Reads [§22 ¶3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-laws-of-thought-as-supposed-natural-laws-that-in-isolated-efficacy-cause-rational-thinking), third paragraph under § 22.

A law can stand to a process in two ways: as the rule the process conforms to, or as the law that causes it. § 22 shows that the two can coincide in their results and still be different in kind. A calculating machine produces numerals in the order arithmetic requires, yet no one explains the machine by arithmetic; it runs by mechanics. A being whose thinking always conformed to logic would likewise owe that conformity to psychological laws, and those laws would not be the laws of logic. The distinction between normative and causal regulation is the one the whole book relies on, and the machine is its proof. The main commentary's block for § 22 states the distinction without the machine.

> We imagine an ideal man in whom all thinking proceeds as the logical laws require. Naturally the fact that it proceeds so must have its explanatory ground in certain psychological laws which regulate the course of this being's psychic experiences, starting from certain first "collocations", in a certain way. I now ask: would these natural laws and those logical laws be identical under the assumptions made? The answer must obviously be negative. Causal laws according to which thinking must run as it could be justified according to the ideal norms of logic, and these norms themselves: that is by no means the same. That a being is so constituted that it cannot pass contradictory judgements in any unified train of thought, or cannot carry out an inference that would offend against the syllogistic moods, in no way implies that the principle of contradiction, the *modus Barbara* and the like are natural laws that could explain such a constitution. The example of the calculating machine makes the difference entirely clear. This other thinking could belong just as well to the performance of the same thinking machine as of others, but ideal evaluation and causal explanation would still remain heterogeneous.

The argument is a thought experiment that grants the opponent everything. Husserl does not deny that thinking has causes, nor that a being might be built to think only correctly. He "imagines an ideal man" whose thinking always conforms, and adds "naturally" (*natürlich*) that this fact "must have its explanatory ground in certain psychological laws". Even the first "collocations" (*Kollokationen*), the initial arrangement of conditions from which a causal explanation must start, are granted, in quotation marks that mark the term as the empiricists' own. The question is then put as one of identity: "would these natural laws and those logical laws be identical?" The answer comes without argument: "obviously" no. The argument is the machine.

The machine works because its accord with arithmetic is perfect. "The arrangement and connection of the numerals that spring forth is regulated by natural law in the way the arithmetical propositions demand for their meanings." The sentence uses one verb for both sides and keeps them apart by their objects. The numerals are "regulated" by natural law; the arithmetical propositions "demand" (*fordern*) the arrangement "for their meanings". The physical order matches the ideal order exactly, and yet "no one" would explain the first by the second. The example removes the opponent's last resource. With human thinking one can always point to errors and say that the logical laws are causes sometimes disturbed. A machine that never errs shows that perfect conformity still does not make the norm a cause.

The next sentences block the obvious reply, that a machine does not think. Husserl concedes it, "to be sure", and then turns the concession around. The machine does not "understand itself" or "the meaning of its performances". But "our thinking machine" (*unsere Denkmaschine*) might work in the same way, except that its correct course would be "acknowledged as correct" by an insight occurring "in another thinking", possibly in the same thinking machine. Insight into logical lawfulness is then one more event in the mind. It evaluates the other events; it does not cause them, and it does not make the laws it sees into their causes. "Ideal evaluation and causal explanation would still remain heterogeneous." The word is § 2's: the confusion of domains mixed the heterogeneous into a supposed unity, and here the two domains are named, evaluation and explanation.

The paragraphs before the experiment name the confusions it corrects. The first confuses "the logical laws with the judgements … in which they may be known", the law as "judgement-content" with the judgement as a real event; the second confuses "the law as a member of causation and the law as rule of causation", the "mythical" talk of natural laws as ruling powers. The section ends by listing what the psychologistic logicians fail to see: the "essential and eternally unbridgeable differences between ideal law and real law, between norming regulation and causal regulation, between logical and real necessity, between logical ground and real ground". "No conceivable gradation can establish mediations between the ideal and the real." Sigwart is the named target: he could suppose that for an intellectually ideal being "logical necessity would at the same time be a real one that produces actual thinking". That is the very ideal man of the thought experiment, and Husserl's answer is that the coincidence of the two necessities in such a being would still not make them one.

B sharpened the section in small ways that point in one direction. Where B asks for "the descriptive and genetic analyses" that would entitle the psychologist to explain thinking by two classes of natural laws, A had asked only for "genetic analyses"; "descriptive and" is an addition of B. B also adds "in psychic connection" (*im seelischen Zusammenhang*) to the causal laws of thinking and "sceptical" to the absurdities of extreme probabilism. The additions bring in the descriptive standpoint of the second volume. The second volume, in turn, takes up the machine. In the Second Investigation (§ 27, A 174), Husserl rejects the view that thinking reduces to "unconscious associative mechanisms that make the particulars spring forth at their places like the numerals of a calculating machine": there is a conceptual presenting, "descriptively distinct" from intuitive presenting, "a meaning of fundamentally new kind". In the *Prolegomena* the machine showed that even a mechanism that worked exactly like correct thinking would not make logic causal. In the Second Investigation it is denied that thinking is such a mechanism at all, and the denial rests on description. The two uses are compatible, but they answer different opponents: the first the psychologist who confuses norm and cause, the second the nominalist who confuses meaning with the play of signs. The same "springing forth" of numerals serves both.

## §28, A/B 92–93, Hua 101–102: the double position of the principle of contradiction

Reads [§28 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-supposed-double-sidedness-of-the-principle-of-contradiction), first paragraph under § 28.

The view § 28 attacks gives the principle of contradiction a "double position" (*Doppelstellung*): as a natural law it is "a determining power of our actual judging", as a norm-law it is "the foundation of all logical rules". Its most attractive defender is F. A. Lange, whose *Logical Studies* aimed at a "new foundation of formal logic", not at a psychological logic in Mill's manner. Husserl classifies Lange's position as "a psychologism, only of another genus", the genus to which the doctrine of innate faculties belongs and, on the prevailing reading, Kant's formal idealism. The edition difference falls in the footnote that extends the charge from Lange to the Kantians, and it changes the target from a school to a tendency.

> Particularly noteworthy seem to us the attempts to procure for the principle of contradiction a double position, according to which, on the one hand, as a natural law, it is to be a determining power of our actual judging, and on the other hand, as a norm-law, the foundation of all logical rules. This view is advocated in a particularly appealing way by F. A. Lange in the *Logical Studies*, a spirited work which otherwise aims to be a contribution not to the advancement of a psychological logic in the style of Mill, but "to the new foundation of formal logic". To be sure, if one looks more closely at this new foundation and reads that the truths of logic, like those of mathematics, derive from spatial intuition, that the simple foundations of these sciences, "since they guarantee the strict correctness of all knowledge whatever", "are the foundations of our intellectual organization", and that therefore "the lawfulness we admire in them stems from ourselves … from the unconscious ground of ourselves", then one will hardly be able to avoid classifying Lange's position once again as a psychologism, only of another genus, under which Kant's formal idealism too, in the sense of its prevailing interpretation, and the other species of the doctrine of innate faculties of knowledge or "sources of knowledge" belong.

Footnote:

> That Kant's theory of knowledge has sides that strive beyond this psychologism of the faculties of the soul as sources of knowledge, and in fact reach beyond it, is well known. Here it suffices that it also has strongly prominent sides that reach into psychologism, which naturally does not exclude lively polemic against other forms of psychologistic grounding of knowledge. Incidentally, not only Lange but a good part of the *Kantianizing* philosophers belong in the sphere of psychologistic theory of knowledge, however little they will admit it. *Transcendental psychology* is psychology too.

*Note on the text.* The file sets the A/B 93 markers in a LaTeX block between "der" and "formalen Logik", and the note anchors after Lange's quotations; the quotation drops them. "kantanisierenden" is the file's spelling of *kantianisierenden*. For it, the apparatus gives the A reading "Neukantianer" (Neo-Kantians). Two later A variants in § 28 (notes 55 and 56) have lost their anchors and cannot be tied to their sentences.

The section's opening sentence states the doctrine in two parallel phrases, and the first of them is already the diagnosis. As natural law, the principle is to be "a determining power of our actual judging" (*eine bestimmende Macht unseres tatsächlichen Urteilens*). § 22 had named the second confusion behind psychologism as "the mythical talk of natural laws as ruling powers", and said that, once the law is confused with knowing it, "the law appears as a determining power of our course of thought" (*eine bestimmende Macht unseres Denkverlaufs*). The double position puts that confusion on one side of the principle and keeps the norm on the other. The two terms "natural law" and "norm-law" (*Normalgesetz*) are Lange's, and § 28 quotes him at length to show what the first means: "the point at which the natural laws of thinking touch the norm-laws".

Lange's own wording supplies the evidence for the classification. The psychological law of the incompatibility of immediate contradictions is "the sharp edge" by which untenable combinations of ideas are destroyed "in the course of experience", "like the progress of organisms", the many perishing and "the better surviving". It "is given immediately by our organization and works before all experience as condition of all experience". The last phrase is Kant's formula for the transcendental, applied to a psychological law that works, Lange adds, without needing "to be brought to consciousness". That is why Husserl speaks of "another genus". Mill's psychologism derives logic from experience; Lange's derives it from "our intellectual organization", from "the unconscious ground of ourselves", and so from conditions that precede experience. Both make the logical law depend on the constitution of the knower. The genus is named by its species: the doctrine of "innate faculties of knowledge or 'sources of knowledge'", and Kant's formal idealism "in the sense of its prevailing interpretation".

The footnote is guarded in two directions. It grants that Kant's epistemology has sides that "strive beyond" the psychologism of faculties "and in fact reach beyond it", and calls this "well known". It then says that the other sides suffice for the argument. The sentence that follows widens the charge to "a good part of the *Kantianizing* philosophers", and ends with an epigram: "*Transcendental psychology* is psychology too." Here the editions differ. A said "a good part of the Neo-Kantians" (*Neukantianer*). A names a school, the contemporary movement that claimed to have overcome psychologism by returning to Kant. B names a manner of philosophizing, and "Kantianizing" is less a description than a diagnosis: the philosophers in question imitate Kant where he is weakest. B's wording also no longer implies that the Neo-Kantians as such are psychologists. The key-passage list calls B's change a softening. It softens the reference to persons and sharpens the reference to doctrine.

Husserl's reply in the same section denies that there is a natural law here at all. "We do not deny the psychological facts" Lange describes, "but we miss everything that could justify speaking here of a natural law." The "natural law of contradiction" reduces to "a crude empirical generality" with an unfixable "sphere of indeterminacy", valid only for normal individuals. The logical law "does not speak of the struggle of contradictory judgements", which are temporal real acts, but "of the lawful incompatibility of timeless, ideal unities that we call contradictory propositions". The word "struggle" (*Kampf*) answers Lange's struggle for survival among ideas. To weld the vague generality and the exact law into one "supposedly two-sided law" (*zusammenzuschweißen*) is "downright absurd". The main commentary's block says that the two readings "yield different propositions altogether". The source's point is stronger: one of the two is not a law.

The footnote's epigram returns in the chapter on relativism. § 38 lists, among the forms of psychologism that are relativism, the "'transcendental psychology'" that passes as "formal idealism", with "consciousness in general" read as "generic reason". § 58 then sets out the points of contact with Kant, agreeing with his "main tendency" while rejecting the "mythical concepts" of understanding and reason as faculties. § 28's footnote is the first place where the double attitude to Kant is stated, and it already has both halves: Kant reaches beyond psychologism, and the Kantianizing philosophers do not.

## §31, A/B 105–108, Hua 113–116: Heymans's chemistry of judgements

Reads [§31 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#inferential-formulas-and-chemical-formulas), first paragraph under § 31.

The comparison of inferential formulas with chemical formulas is not Husserl's device. It is G. Heymans's, offered to make plausible the doctrine that inferential formulas express "empirical laws of thinking". Husserl quotes it, takes it seriously, and shows that it proves too much: if a syllogistic formula says only that two judgements produce a third "under suitable circumstances", then the formula of a fallacy says exactly the same, and the analogy fits the fallacy "just as well and as badly". What the comparison cannot accommodate is the difference between valid and invalid inference, and that difference is the whole content of logic. The understanding the reading follows is this turn of an opponent's analogy against him.

> The doctrine that the inferential formulas express "empirical laws of thinking" Heymans tries to make more plausible by comparison with the chemical formulas. "Just as in the chemical formula 2H2 + O2 = 2H2O there is expressed only the general fact that two volumes of hydrogen combine with one volume of oxygen under suitable circumstances to give two volumes of water, just so the logical formula MaX + MaY = YiX + XiY states only that two universal affirmative judgements with a common subject-concept produce in consciousness, under suitable circumstances, two new particular affirmative judgements …"
>
> […] What surprises us in this conception is the assertion that in the combinations excluded by logicians no production of new judgements takes place. With regard to every fallacy, e.g. of the form XeM + MeY = XeY, one will surely have to say that in general two judgements of the forms XeM and MeY do, "under suitable circumstances", yield a new judgement in consciousness. The analogy with the chemical formulas fits here just as well and as badly as in the other cases.

German:

> Die Lehre, daß die Schlußformeln „empirische Gesetze des Denkens“ ausdrücken, versucht Heymans durch den Vergleich mit den chemischen Formeln plausibler zu machen. „Genau so, wie in der chemischen Formel 2H2 + O2 = 2H2O nur die allgemeine Tatsache zum Ausdruck kommt, daß zwei Volumen Wasserstoff mit einem Volumen Sauerstoff sich unter geeigneten Umständen zu zwei Volumen Wasser verbinden, — genau so sagt die logische Formel MaX + MaY = YiX + XiY nur aus, daß zwei allgemein bejahende Urteile mit gemeinschaftlichem Subjektbegriff unter geeigneten Umständen im Bewußtsein zwei neue partikulär bejahende Urteile erzeugen …“
>

*Note on the text.* The formulas are LaTeX in the file and are given as plain letters. The first omission is the rest of Heymans's quotation, with its "experiments" on the "mechanism of thinking"; the second is Husserl's summary of the experimental method. The A/B 106 markers fall inside the Heymans quotation.

The section's first sentence fixes the authorship. "Heymans tries" (*versucht Heymans*) "to make more plausible" a doctrine already stated. The verb places the analogy as an attempt at persuasion, and the long quotation that follows is Heymans's own: "Just as in the chemical formula … just so the logical formula". The main commentary's block opens "Husserl introduces a vivid analogy" and calls it "one of Husserl's most memorable polemical devices". It is Heymans's device, and Husserl's contribution is to show what it implies. Heymans himself supplied the hostage. Why a new judgement arises from MaX + MaY but not from MeX + MeY "we know nothing at present"; and the "unshakeable necessity" that compels us to hold the conclusion true can be confirmed "by repetition of the experiments".

Husserl's objection takes the formula at its word. If the logical formula states only what judgements "produce in consciousness under suitable circumstances", then a fallacy is a formula of the same kind, since people do draw fallacious conclusions under some circumstances. The phrase in quotation marks, "under suitable circumstances", is Heymans's escape clause turned against him: it fits every combination of premises. The verdict is an idiom: the analogy fits "just as well and as badly" (*genau so recht und schlecht*). *Recht und schlecht* means "after a fashion", but its two words are "right" and "bad", and they name exactly the two classes of inference that the analogy cannot tell apart. The analogy fits valid and invalid inference alike; it fits "right and wrong" equally.

The next step closes the reply the empiricist might make. Why do we distinguish the two classes of formulas at all? "Because with regard to the one we have come to the insight that what they express are truths, and with regard to the others that it is falsehoods." The empiricist cannot give this answer, because on his reading the empirical propositions corresponding to fallacies "are valid in the same way". His appeal to the felt "unshakeable necessity" fails for the same reason: "all inferences, whether logically justified or not, take place with psychological necessity", and someone who holds to a fallacy against all criticism feels "the compulsion of not-being-able-otherwise" exactly as one who infers correctly. The felt necessity "must not be confused with genuine, logical necessity", which "says nothing other than the ideal-lawful validity of the inference, knowable with insight". The argument repeats § 21's opposition of surmise and insight in the case of inference, and it anticipates § 49's rejection of a "feeling of evidence": a feeling that accompanies fallacies as well as proofs cannot be what makes proofs valid.

Husserl then measures the analogy against chemistry itself, and it flatters psychology. In chemistry "we know the 'circumstances'" under which the syntheses occur; they are "to a considerable extent exactly determinable", which is why chemical formulas count among "the most valuable inductions of natural science". In psychology the circumstances reduce to "a certain 'tension of attention', a certain 'mental freshness', a certain 'previous training'". Heymans's "chemistry of judgements" is to be counted among the attempts "hopeless in Kant's sense". The section ends with the formula's "true meaning", expressed as "equivalent ideal incompatibilities": "it holds generally that two propositions of the forms 'all M are X' and 'no P is M' are not true without a proposition of the form 'some X are not P' also being true." Of consciousness, acts of judging and circumstances of judging, "there is no talk here". The ideal incompatibility repeats § 28's definition of the principle of contradiction as "the lawful incompatibility of timeless, ideal unities", and the transformation of a law into an equivalent statement of conditions of truth is the device § 50 will apply to the laws of evidence.

The commentary's account of the chemical analogy, that "chemical formulas describe causal processes in nature" while syllogistic formulas "express ideal relationships", is the conclusion Husserl draws. The section reaches it by a different road: it never contests the analogy's picture of chemistry, but shows that the analogy, granted entire, cannot distinguish a valid inference from a fallacy.

## §32, A/B 110–112, Hua 118–120: logical and noetic scepticism

Reads [§32 ¶2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-ideal-conditions-for-the-possibility-of-a-theory-in-general-the-strict-concept-of-scepticism), second paragraph under § 32.

Scepticism in the strict sense is defined by the content of a thesis, not by an attitude of doubt: a theory is sceptical if its theses "expressly state or analytically include" that the conditions of the possibility of any theory are false. The conditions are of two kinds, and so is scepticism. The objective-logical conditions are the laws grounded in the concepts of truth, proposition, object and the like; the subjective conditions are ideal too, rooted "in the form of subjectivity in general", and Husserl names them "noetic". The main commentary's block gives only the first kind. The second, a coinage of this section, is what Chapter VII uses against relativism and Chapter XI reworks.

> In a twofold respect one can speak here of evident "conditions of possibility" of every theory in general. First, in a subjective respect. Here it is a matter of the a priori conditions on which the possibility of immediate and mediate knowledge, and so the possibility of the rational justification of every theory, depends. […] One sees that by subjective conditions of possibility are here not to be understood real conditions, rooted in the individual judging subject or in the variable species of judging beings (e.g. the human), but ideal conditions, rooted in the form of subjectivity in general and in its relation to knowledge. For distinction we shall speak of them as noetic conditions.
>
> In an objective respect, talk of conditions of the possibility of every theory concerns not theory as a subjective unity of cognitions, but theory as an objective unity of truths or propositions connected by relations of ground and consequence. The conditions are here all the laws that are grounded purely in the concept of theory, more specifically, purely in the concepts of truth, proposition, object, property, relation and the like, in short in the concepts that essentially constitute the concept of theoretical unity. […] We therefore distinguish (naturally not with a classificatory intention): false, absurd, logically and noetically absurd, and finally sceptical theories; under the last title comprising all theories whose theses either expressly state or analytically include that the logical or noetic conditions for the possibility of a theory in general are false.
>
> With this a sharp concept has been won for the term scepticism, and at the same time a clear division into logical and noetic scepticism.

German:

> In doppelter Hinsicht kann hier von evidenten „Bedingungen der Möglichkeit“ jeder Theorie überhaupt sprechen. Fürs erste in subjektiver Hinsicht. Hier handelt es sich um die apriorischen Bedingungen, von denen die Möglichkeit der unmittelbaren und mittelbaren Erkenntnis und somit die Möglichkeit der vernünftigen Rechtfertigung jeder Theorie abhängig ist. […] Man sieht, daß unter subjektiven Bedingungen der Möglichkeit hier nicht etwa zu verstehen sind reale Bedingungen, die im einzelnen Urteilssubjekt oder in der wechselnden Spezies urteilender Wesen (z. B. der menschlichen) wurzeln, sondern ideale Bedingungen, die in der Form der Subjektivität überhaupt und in deren Beziehung zur Erkenntnis wurzeln. Zur Unterscheidung wollen wir von ihnen als von noetischen Bedingungen sprechen.
>
> In objektiver Hinsicht betrifft die Rede von Bedingungen der Möglichkeit jeder Theorie nicht die Theorie als subjektive Einheit von Erkenntnissen, sondern Theorie als eine objektive, durch Verhältnisse von Grund und Folge verknüpfte Einheit von Wahrheiten bzw. Sätzen. Die Bedingungen sind hier all die Gesetze, welche rein im Begriffe der Theorie gründen — spezieller gesprochen, die rein im Begriffe der Wahrheit, des Satzes, des Gegenstandes, der Beschaffenheit, der Beziehung u. dgl., kurz in den Begriffen gründen, welche den Begriff der theoretischen Einheit wesentlich konstituieren. […] Wir unterscheiden also (natürlich nicht in klassifikatorischer Absicht): falsche, absurde, logisch und noetisch absurde und endlich skeptische Theorien; unter dem letzteren Titel alle Theorien befassend, deren Thesen entweder ausdrücklich besagen oder analytisch in sich schließen, daß die logischen oder noetischen Bedingungen für die Möglichkeit einer Theorie überhaupt falsch sind.
>
> Hiermit ist für den Terminus Skeptizismus ein scharfer Begriff und zugleich eine klare Sonderung in logischen und noetischen Skeptizismus gewonnen.

*Note on the text.* "kann hier von … sprechen" lacks its subject (*man*) in the file and is quoted as it stands. The first omission is the example of evidence (see the reading of § 6); the second is the sentence on denying the logical laws and the paragraph on where logical offences can lie. The A/B 111 and 112 markers fall within the omitted and quoted text; the file sets them as comments. Note 71, attached to "knowledge", is not a variant but Husserl's own note, which the reading quotes.

The definition starts from a phrase in quotation marks: "conditions of possibility". The marks distance a formula that every reader of 1900 knew as Kant's, and § 65 later says so: "the historical echoes in the form of the question are of course intended", since the question generalizes Kant's question about the conditions of the possibility of experience. § 32 uses the formula for theory, not experience, and splits it at once "in a twofold respect".

The subjective side carries the coinage, and the sentence that introduces it is built to exclude a misreading. "One sees that by subjective conditions … are not to be understood real conditions, rooted in the individual judging subject or in the variable species of judging beings (e.g. the human), but ideal conditions, rooted in the form of subjectivity in general." The two rejected roots, the individual and the species, are the two relativisms § 34 will name, individual and specific. The positive root, "the form of subjectivity in general and its relation to knowledge", is a priori: a knower as such must be able to experience the justification of a judgement, or there is no knowledge. "For distinction" Husserl calls these conditions "noetic". The word is chosen to avoid "subjective", which in the debate meant psychological. A note on the same page warns that "knowledge" in this work is "not understood in the widespread restriction to the real": the noetic conditions hold for knowing mathematical objects as much as natural ones.

The objective side is defined by a list of concepts: truth, proposition, object, property, relation, "in short the concepts that essentially constitute the concept of theoretical unity". The list anticipates Chapter XI's "pure categories of meaning" and "object-categories" (§ 67). Denying the laws grounded in these concepts "is equivalent" to asserting that the terms lack "a consistent sense", which is why a theory that denies them "abolishes itself".

The classification is graded, not classificatory, and Husserl says so in a parenthesis: false, absurd, logically and noetically absurd, sceptical. Every sceptical theory is absurd, but not every absurd theory is sceptical. Scepticism is the extreme case in which the offence lies in the thesis itself. "That it belongs to the concept of the sceptical theory to be absurd is clear from the definition without further ado." The ancient theses, "there is no truth", "there is no knowledge", are examples; so is empiricism, "the moderate no less than the extreme", by the arguments of Chapters IV and V. The definition makes the refutation analytic. It does not show that scepticism is false by evidence against it, but that its thesis contradicts what any thesis presupposes.

The noetic half does the work in the next sections. § 36 argues that specific relativism is absurd because "the same judgement-content" cannot be true for one species and false for another, an objective-logical point; but § 32's own example is noetic: a theory that "denies every advantage of the evident over the blind judgement" abolishes what distinguishes it from arbitrary assertion. § 33 then removes metaphysical scepticism, the denial of a knowable external world, from scepticism in the strict sense, so that "the confusion between metaphysical and logical-noetic scepticism" is met at once.

Chapter XI revises the pair, and says so. § 65 (A/B 236–238, Hua 239–240) asks again for "the conditions of the possibility of science in general" and divides the ideal conditions of knowledge into noetic ones, which "ground in the idea of knowledge as such, a priori, without regard to the empirical peculiarity of human knowing", and purely logical ones, which "ground purely in the 'content' of knowledge". A footnote looks back: in § 32, where the subtle distinction did not matter for fixing the concept of scepticism, "I merely contrasted noetic conditions of theoretical knowledge and objective-logical conditions of theory itself"; in § 65 the logical conditions are first treated as conditions of knowledge too, and only then related to the objective theory. The same note says that this draws on "the critical considerations on the evidence-theory of logic", since "evidence is indeed nothing other than the character of knowledge as such". The noetic conditions of § 32 were defined through evidence; § 65 makes the link explicit.

The word "noetic" itself changed sense between the editions. In 1900 it named the ideal conditions on the side of the knower. The Foreword to B (B XIV–XV, Hua 14) speaks instead of "the difference and parallelism of the 'noetic' and the 'noematic'", whose fundamental role "only the *Ideas* bring to full clarity", and regrets that the First Investigation emphasizes "one-sidedly" the "noetic concept of meaning". In 1913 "noetic" names one side of a correlation whose other side is the noema. The *Prolegomena*'s "noetic scepticism", printed unchanged in B, keeps the older sense, and a reader of B should not hear the *Ideas* in it.

## §36, A/B 117–118, Hua 124–125: truth for a species

Reads [§36 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#critique-of-specific-relativism-and-in-particular-of-anthropologism), first paragraph under § 36.

Specific relativism holds that what is true for each species of judging beings is what must count as true according to its constitution and its laws of thought. Husserl calls the doctrine "absurd" (*widersinnig*), not false, and the proof is one line long: the doctrine implies that the same judgement-content can be true for one species and false for another, and "the same judgement-content cannot be both, true and false". The absurdity lies in the words "true" and "false". Anthropologism, the case where the species is man, is the variety the section is after, because it is the one that "the newer and newest philosophy" holds.

> 1. Specific relativism makes the assertion: true for each species of judging beings is what, according to its constitution, according to its laws of thought, has to count as true. This doctrine is absurd. For it lies in its sense that the same judgement-content (proposition) can be true for one, namely for a subject of the species *homo*, and false for another, namely for a subject of a differently constituted species. But the same judgement-content cannot be both, true and false. This lies in the mere sense of the words true and false. If the relativist uses these words with the sense that belongs to them, his thesis says what is contrary to its own sense.
>
> The evasion that the wording of the principle of contradiction, by which we unfolded the sense of the words true and false, is incomplete, that it speaks precisely of humanly true and humanly false, is obviously void. […] Absurd is the possibility, kept open, that the same judgement-content (we say, in a dangerous equivocation: the same judgement) should be both true and false according to who judges. Correspondingly the answer for specific relativism will now run: "truth for this or that species", e.g. for the human, is, in the way it is meant here, an absurd expression. One can, to be sure, also use it in a good sense; but then it means something totally different, namely the range of truths that are accessible, knowable to man as such. What is true is absolutely, is "in itself" true; truth is identically one, whether men or non-men, angels or gods grasp it in judging.

German:

> 1. Der spezifische Relativismus stellt die Behauptung auf: Wahr ist für jede Spezies urteilender Wesen, was nach ihrer Konstitution, nach ihren Denkgesetzen als wahr zu gelten habe. Diese Lehre ist widersinnig. Denn es liegt in ihrem Sinne, daß derselbe Urteilsinhalt (Satz) für den einen, nämlich für ein Subjekt der Spezies *homo*, wahr, für einen anderen, nämlich für ein Subjekt einer anders konstituierten Spezies, falsch sein kann. Aber derselbe Urteilsinhalt kann nicht beides, wahr und falsch, sein. Dies liegt in dem bloßen Sinne der Worte wahr und falsch. Gebraucht der Relativist diese Worte mit ihrem zugehörigen Sinn, so sagt seine These, was ihrem eigenen Sinn zuwider ist.
>
> Die Ausflucht, es sei der Wortlaut des herangezogenen Satzes vom Widerspruch, durch den wir den Sinn der Worte wahr und falsch entfalteten, unvollständig, es sei in ihm eben von menschlich wahr und menschlich falsch die Rede, ist offenbar nichtig. […] Widersinnig ist die offengehaltene Möglichkeit, daß derselbe Urteilsinhalt (wir sagen in gefährlicher Äquivokation: dasselbe Urteil) je nach dem Urteilenden beides, wahr und falsch, sei. Entsprechend wird nun auch die Antwort für den spezifischen Relativismus lauten: „Wahrheit für die oder jene Spezies”, z. B. für die menschliche, das ist — so wie hier gemeint ist — eine widersinnige Rede. Man kann sie allerdings auch in gutem Sinne gebrauchen; aber dann meint sie etwas total Verschiedenes, nämlich den Umkreis von Wahrheiten, die dem Menschen als solchem zugänglich, erkennbar sind. Was wahr ist, ist absolut, ist „an sich” wahr; die Wahrheit ist identisch eine, ob Menschen oder Unmenschen, Engel oder Götter urteilend erfassen.

*Note on the text.* In the file, the passage from "in gefährlicher Äquivokation" to the end of the paragraph stands inside an HTML comment that opens with the running head of Hua 125 and is never closed on that line; the text is Husserl's, but the published file would hide it. It is quoted here from the file's source text. The omission is the analogous reply to individual subjectivism. The A/B 118 markers follow the paragraph. For "Äquivokation" the apparatus gives the A reading "Laxheit" (laxity).

The definition is the relativist's own, and its modal betrays it. True for each species is what "has to count as true" (*als wahr zu gelten habe*) according to its constitution. The relativist cannot state his thesis without a "has to": a normative claim about what each species must accept. Husserl does not exploit the modal; he exploits the noun phrase it governs. The thesis speaks of "the same judgement-content (proposition)" (*derselbe Urteilsinhalt (Satz)*). The parenthesis equates judgement-content with proposition, the ideal unit, not the act. Once the thesis is stated about the same content, it says that one content is true and false, and the principle of contradiction, which "lies in the mere sense of the words true and false", excludes that. The relativist who uses the words "with the sense that belongs to them" contradicts his thesis by its own sense. That is § 32's definition of a sceptical theory in its first application: the thesis denies a condition of the possibility of any thesis.

The parenthesis in the second paragraph names the confusion that keeps the doctrine alive. One says "the same judgement" where one means "the same judgement-content", and B calls this "a dangerous equivocation" (*gefährlicher Äquivokation*), where A had "a dangerous laxity" (*Laxheit*). A blamed carelessness of speech; B names a double meaning of the word "judgement", act and content. The same section's third point makes the double meaning the core of the reply: "one must not confuse the judgement as judgement-content, i.e. as the ideal unity, with the single real act of judging", and the judgement "2 × 2 is 4" "is the same, whoever passes it". B's word places the error where the book's argument places it.

"Truth for a species" is given a "good sense" as well as an absurd one, and the good sense is what keeps the argument from proving too much. Truths may well be relative to a species in the sense of "the range of truths accessible, knowable, to man as such". What is relative is access, not truth. The formula that follows, "truth is identically one, whether men or non-men, angels or gods grasp it", is the book's strongest statement of truth in itself, and the list of judges is chosen to be indifferent to constitution. The second point of the section repeats the reply against beings "not bound by these principles". Either they mean by "true" and "false" what we mean, and then the principles hold for them; or they mean something else, and "the whole dispute is a dispute about words": if they "call trees what we call propositions", our principles do not hold of their "trees". If they mean only that their judging is not psychologically regulated by the principles, "we find nothing strange in that, for we ourselves are such beings". § 22's distinction between norm and cause carries the second horn.

The main commentary's block treats "specific relativism" and "anthropologism" as names of one doctrine ("Specific relativism (*Anthropologismus*)"). The heading says "specific relativism, and in particular anthropologism", and § 34 defines anthropologism as the species of specific relativism in which the species is man. The difference matters for the target: § 36 refutes the genus, so that the refutation holds whatever the species, and then applies it to man, because that is where the philosophy of the day stands.

The argument is carried to its targets in the next sections. § 38 declares that psychologism "in all its varieties" is relativism, so that the argument of § 36 reaches every psychologistic logic; §§ 39 and 40 apply it to Sigwart and Erdmann, and Erdmann's defence of the possibility of other ways of thinking is answered with the § 36 dilemma. The "in itself" of § 36 returns in Chapter XI, where § 62 makes "truth in itself" the correlate of "being in itself". In the Foreword to B Husserl named this concept as one of the "very essential inadequacies" he had to keep, "the concept of 'truth in itself', too one-sidedly oriented to the *vérités de raison*". § 36 shows the orientation: its examples are the principles of logic and "2 × 2 is 4", and its truth is one for angels and gods. Truths of fact, which § 36's third point mentions only to say that their temporal determination concerns the fact and not the truth, are not its model.

## §38, A/B 123–124, Hua 130–131: psychologism is relativism

Reads [§38 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#psychologism-in-all-its-forms-is-a-relativism), first paragraph under § 38.

"We have fought relativism; we meant, of course, psychologism." § 38 transfers the refutation of specific relativism to every psychologism, the empiricist kind and the aprioristic kind alike. Its means is a set of quotation marks and one inserted word. The aprioristic formulas, "original forms" of the understanding, "consciousness in general", "generic reason", are quoted, and the adjective "human" is placed in parentheses inside them. Read in the "natural sense" that relates them to the human species, the formulas make logical laws depend on the constitution of a species, and the argument of § 36 applies. The charge is conditional on that reading, and Husserl says so.

> We have fought relativism; we meant, of course, psychologism. In fact psychologism, in all its varieties and individual developments, is nothing other than relativism, only not always recognized and expressly admitted. It makes no difference whether it relies on "transcendental psychology" and, as formal idealism, believes it can rescue the objectivity of knowledge, or whether it relies on empirical psychology and takes relativism upon itself as unavoidable fate.
>
> Every doctrine that either, in the manner of the empiricists, takes the purely logical laws as empirical-psychological laws, or, in the manner of the apriorists, traces them back more or less mythically to certain "original forms" or "modes of functioning" of the (human) understanding, to "consciousness in general" as (human) "generic reason", to the "psychophysical constitution" of man, to the *intellectus ipse* which, as an innate (generally human) disposition, precedes factual thinking and all experience, and the like, is *eo ipso* relativistic, and indeed of the kind of specific relativism. All the objections we have raised against it apply to them too. Of course, one must take the partly iridescent catchwords of apriorism, e.g. understanding, reason, consciousness, in that natural sense which gives them an essential relation to the human species. It is the curse of the theories in question that they lay under them now this real and now an ideal meaning, and so weave together an intolerable tangle of partly correct, partly false propositions.

> Den Relativismus haben wir bekämpft, den Psychologismus haben wir natürlich gemeint. In der Tat ist der Psychologismus in allen seinen Abarten und individuellen Ausgestaltungen nichts anderes als Relativismus, nur nicht immer erkannter und ausdrücklich zugestandener. Es ist dabei ganz gleich, ob er sich auf ,,Transzendentalpsychologie“ stützt und als formaler Idealismus die Objektivität der Erkenntnis zu retten glaubt, oder ob er sich auf empirische Psychologie stützt und den Relativismus als unvermeidliches Fatum auf sich nimmt.
>
> Jede Lehre, welche die rein logischen Gesetze entweder nach Art der Empiristen als empirisch-psychologische Gesetze faßt oder sie nach Art der Aprioristen mehr oder minder mythisch zurückführt auf gewisse ,,ursprüngliche Formen“ oder ,,Funktionsweisen“ des (menschlichen) Verstandes, auf das ,,Bewußtsein überhaupt“ als (menschliche) ,,Gattungsvernunft“, auf die ,,psychophysische Konstitution“ des Menschen, auf den *intellectus ipse*, der als angeborene (allgemein menschliche) Anlage dem faktischen Denken und aller Erfahrung vorhergeht, u. dgl. — ist *eo ipso* relativistisch, und zwar von der Art des spezifischen Relativismus. Alle Einwände, die wir gegen ihn erhoben haben, treffen auch sie. Selbstverständlich muß man aber die zum Teil schillernden Schlagwörter des Apriorismus, z.B. Verstand, Vernunft, Bewußtsein, in jenem natürlichen Sinne nehmen, der ihnen eine wesentliche Beziehung zur menschlichen Spezies verleiht. *Note on the text.* The file sets the A/B 124 markers as note anchors between "u. dgl. —" and "ist", and a note anchor at "ihnen" whose note is not in the file; the quotation drops them. The italics on *intellectus ipse* and *eo ipso* are the file's.

The first sentence is a confession of method. Chapter VII spent four sections on relativism, a doctrine few would admit to, so that its results could now be applied to a doctrine many hold. "Of course" (*natürlich*) says that the reader should have seen the target all along. The second sentence states the thesis with the qualifier that does the work: psychologism is relativism, "only not always recognized and expressly admitted". The argument does not need the psychologist's admission. It needs only that his thesis, by its content, makes truth depend on a species. That is § 32's definition of a sceptical theory, which judges theses by what they "expressly state or analytically include".

The two branches of psychologism are set in parallel and distinguished by what they think they achieve. The transcendental branch "believes it can rescue the objectivity of knowledge" (*zu retten glaubt*); the empirical branch "takes relativism upon itself as unavoidable fate" (*als unvermeidliches Fatum auf sich nimmt*). The verbs carry the judgement: the one deceives itself, the other resigns. Both end in the same place. The main commentary's block has the empirical branch "openly embrace subjectivism"; the source says it accepts relativism, and resignation is not embrace.

The second paragraph does its work with punctuation. The aprioristic formulas are quoted, so that they stand as the apriorists' words: "original forms", "modes of functioning", "consciousness in general", "generic reason", "psychophysical constitution". Into three of them Husserl inserts a parenthesis: forms of the "(human)" understanding, consciousness in general as "(human)" generic reason, the *intellectus ipse* as an "innate (generally human) disposition". The parentheses supply what the formulas leave unsaid and the theories, on Husserl's reading, mean. "Consciousness in general" (*Bewußtsein überhaupt*) is the Kantian phrase that most resists the charge, since it is meant to name no particular consciousness. The parenthesis reads it as generic reason, the reason of a genus, and so of a species of judging beings. Chapter V had prepared the move: the principle of contradiction in the form that contradictory judgements cannot coexist in one consciousness excludes nothing, "unless one interprets 'consciousness' as 'consciousness in general', as supratemporal normal consciousness", and a primitive logical principle cannot presuppose the concept of the normal (§ 27, A/B 88–89).

The condition follows, and it limits the charge. One "must" take the catchwords "in that natural sense which gives them an essential relation to the human species". The catchwords are "iridescent" (*schillernd*), shifting colour with the light, and "the curse" of the theories is that they give them "now this real and now an ideal meaning". On the ideal meaning, the apriorist would not be a relativist, but then his "understanding" would be the domain of pure logic itself, and his talk of faculties would explain nothing. § 58 makes this point against Kant with an image: to explain logic by the faculty of understanding is like explaining the art of dancing by the faculty of dancing. The "more or less mythical" of § 38 anticipates § 58's "mythical concepts that Kant so loves", as § 22 had called mythical the talk of natural laws as ruling powers. In both places the myth is the same: a rule taken for a power.

The paragraph after the quotation narrows the target and widens the result. Some "Kantianizing researchers" exempt a few logical principles as principles of "analytic judgements", and their relativism is restricted to mathematical and natural knowledge. They do not escape "the sceptical absurdities", since they still derive "the ideal from the real, more specifically: the necessity of the laws from the contingency of facts". The verdict reaches Kant's synthetic a priori without naming it. § 28's footnote had said that "transcendental psychology is psychology too"; § 38 adds that it is relativism too. § 58 then states what remains of Kant once the faculties are gone: agreement "in the main tendency", the separation of pure and applied logic, and the judgement that Kant did not "clearly see through the essence of the intended discipline". The *Prolegomena*'s anti-Kantianism is aimed at a reading of Kant on which consciousness in general is a human consciousness, and § 38's parentheses are where the reading is made explicit.

## §40, A/B 137–143, Hua 143–148: Erdmann's presumption and the Archimedean point

Reads [§40 ¶1–2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#anthropologism-in-b-erdmanns-logic), first and second paragraphs under § 40.

Erdmann is the one psychologistic logician who draws the relativistic consequence and defends it. He argues "decidedly for relativism", and he appeals to the possible change of the laws of thought against a "presumption" (*Vermessenheit*): the presumption "that thinks it can leap over the limits of our thinking at this point and gain a standpoint for us outside ourselves". Husserl answers that phrase with its counter-image. The insight in which the logical laws are seen is a point that one must "either accept as the Archimedean one", from which "the world of unreason and doubt" is lifted off its hinges, "or give up", and with it all reason and knowledge. The standpoint outside ourselves that Erdmann forbids is the point Archimedes asked for, and Husserl claims to stand on it.

> It is quite otherwise with Erdmann. With instructive consistency he argues decidedly for relativism in a longer discussion, and by pointing to the possibility of a change in the laws of thought he believes he must counter the "presumption" "that thinks it can leap over the limits of our thinking at this point and gain a standpoint for us outside ourselves". It will be useful to go into his doctrine more closely.

Later in the section (Hua 148):

> I emphasized just now *every* judgement, to draw attention to the fact that the sense of this strict universality excludes *eo ipso* every restriction, hence also that to human or other kinds of judging beings. I cannot force anyone to see what I see. But I myself cannot doubt; I see again that every doubt here, where I have insight, i.e. grasp the truth itself, would be perverse; and so I find myself at all events at the point that I either accept as the Archimedean one, in order to lift from here the world of unreason and doubt off its hinges, or that I give up, in order thereby to give up all reason and knowledge.

German:

> Ganz anders liegt die Sache bei Erdmann. In lehrreicher Folgerichtigkeit tritt er in einer längeren Ausführung für den Relativismus entschieden ein, und durch Hinweis auf die Möglichkeit der Änderung der Denkgesetze glaubt er der „Vermessenheit“ begegnen zu müssen, „die da meint, an diesem Punkte die Grenzen unseres Denkens überspringen, einen Standort für uns außerhalb unserer selbst gewinnen zu können“. Es wird nützlich sein auf seine Lehre näher einzugehen.

> Ich betone soeben *jedes* Urteil, um aufmerksam zu machen, daß der Sinn dieser strengen Allgemeinheit jede Einschränkung, also auch die auf menschliche oder andersartige Gattungen urteilender Wesen *eo ipso* ausschließt. Ich kann niemanden zwingen, einzusehen, was ich einsehe. Aber ich selbst kann nicht zweifeln, ich sehe ja abermals ein, daß jeder Zweifel hier, wo ich Einsicht habe, d.i. die Wahrheit selbst erfasse, verkehrt wäre; und so finde ich mich überhaupt an dem Punkte, den ich entweder als den archimedischen gelten lasse, um von hier aus die Welt der Unvernunft und des Zweifels aus den Angeln zu heben, oder den ich preisegebe, um damit alle Vernunft und Erkenntnis preiszugeben.

*Note on the text.* "preisegebe" is the file's reading for *preisgebe* (give up) and is quoted as it stands. The emphasis on *jedes* is the file's, and the text says it is Husserl's ("I emphasized"). The Erdmann quotation is from his *Logik* I, § 60, no. 370, as the file's footnote gives it. The page numbers are approximate: the first passage follows the running head of Hua 143, the second that of Hua 148, and the file's A/B markers in the stretch are sparse.

The first sentence corrects a picture of Erdmann as a reluctant relativist. He argues "with instructive consistency" (*in lehrreicher Folgerichtigkeit*), and he "decidedly" (*entschieden*) takes the side of relativism. The main commentary's block has him "attempt to defuse" the relativistic consequences and treat alien logics as "a merely theoretical possibility with no practical significance". The source has the opposite: the possibility of changed laws of thought is Erdmann's weapon, used against the "presumption" of the absolutist. The quotation marks around *Vermessenheit* give the word to Erdmann. It is a moral word: to presume is to claim more than one is entitled to, and Erdmann's charge is that the absolutist claims a standpoint that no finite thinker can occupy, "outside ourselves". Husserl's adjective "instructive" says why he spends a long section on him: Erdmann shows where psychologism leads when it does not hedge.

Husserl's reply works first on Erdmann's argument and then on his image. The argument infers, from the fact that the denial of the logical principles cannot be carried out, that the principles express "the essence of our representing and thinking". Husserl separates two concepts that Erdmann identifies: the "unperformability" (*Unvollziehbarkeit*) of the denial as an act, and the "impossibility" of the negative proposition. "We logical absolutists" deny the first and maintain the second. "Not the denial as act is impossible, … but the negative proposition that forms its content is impossible, and indeed, as an ideal one, in an ideal sense; this means: it is absurd and so evidently false." The act of denying is quite possible, and the history of philosophy shows it: "great philosophers like Epicurus and Hegel have denied the principle of contradiction". Here the editions differ. B says that the ideal impossibility of the negative proposition "does not conflict at all with the real possibility of the negating act"; A had "real impossibility". A granted Erdmann, for argument's sake, that the act might be impossible and still separated the two; B asserts that the act is possible, as the next paragraph's examples show. The sentence's point, that ideal and real modality are independent, holds either way; B states it in the stronger form.

The image is answered by a figure that accepts its terms. Erdmann forbade a standpoint "outside ourselves"; Husserl names the point Archimedes asked for, outside the world, from which the world could be moved. The sentence is built as a dilemma between acceptance and surrender: "either accept … or give up". Its first-person verbs do the work. "I cannot force anyone to see what I see. But I myself cannot doubt; I see again that every doubt here … would be perverse." The insight that the laws hold for every judgement is self-confirming at the second order: I see that doubting what I see would be wrong. This is not an argument that compels the relativist. Husserl concedes as much ("I cannot force anyone"). It locates the absolutist's standpoint in evidence, the luminous certainty of § 6, and says that whoever gives it up gives up "all reason and knowledge", including the right to call anything presumptuous. Erdmann's "outside ourselves" is answered not by a standpoint outside the mind but by the content of an insight that holds "for every judgement", whoever judges.

The surrounding argument returns to the forms the earlier chapters prepared. The laws are "laws of thought" only "in the sense of laws called to play a role in the norming of thinking", a "practical function" and not "something lying in their content", which is § 41 in advance. The "binding" of judging beings by the laws is "not in the psychological sense of a compulsion of thought, but in the ideal sense of the norm", which is § 22. Erdmann's definition of truth, quoted later in the section, makes "objective truth" into "universal validity", that is, "validity for all", resting on "the general agreement of those judging". The relativism Husserl found in the phrase about presumption is built into the definition. The *metabasis* of § 2 is named again in the same section: only when we confuse the region of psychological laws of thought with that of the purely logical does Erdmann's claim gain "a semblance of right". § 40 is where the book's opposition of insight and the limits of our thinking is stated in its most personal form.

## §49, A/B 180–181, Hua 183–184: the feeling of evidence, reported

Reads [§49 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#third-prejudice-logic-as-the-theory-of-evidence), first paragraph under § 49.

The third prejudice makes logic a theory of evidence, and evidence a psychic character, "a peculiar feeling" that guarantees the truth of the judgement it attaches to. If that were so, the logical laws would be propositions of psychology about the conditions on which the "feeling of evidence" depends. § 49 states the prejudice and documents it in Mill, Sigwart, Wundt and Höfler; its refutation is §§ 50–51. The reading follows a narrow point, because the editions differ on it: who is speaking when evidence is defined as a feeling. In B the definition is the opponent's, marked as hearsay. In A it was given in the first person plural, with the word "feeling" only reported.

> We formulate a third prejudice in the following sentences. All truth lies in the judgement. But we recognize a judgement as true only in the case of its evidence. This word denotes, so it is said, a peculiar psychic character, well known to everyone from his inner experience, a peculiar feeling that guarantees the truth of the judgement to which it is attached. If logic, now, is the technology that seeks to further us in the knowledge of truth, then the logical laws are, as a matter of course, propositions of psychology. They are, namely, propositions that enlighten us about the psychic conditions on which the presence or absence of that "feeling of evidence" depends.

German (B, as Hua prints it):

> Wir formulieren ein drittes Vorurteil in folgenden Sätzen: Alle Wahrheit liegt im Urteil. Aber als wahr erkennen wir ein Urteil nur im Falle seiner Evidenz. Dieses Wort bezeichnet — so sagt man — einen eigentümlichen und jedem aus seiner inneren Erfahrung wohlbekannten psychischen Charakter, ein eigenartiges Gefühl, welches die Wahrheit des Urteils, dem es angeknüpft ist, verbürgt. Ist nun die Logik die Kunstlehre, welche uns in der Erkenntnis der Wahrheit fördern will, so sind die logischen Gesetze selbstverständlich Sätze der Psychologie. Es sind nämlich Sätze, die uns über die psychischen Bedingungen aufklären, von denen das Dasein oder Fehlen jenes „Evidenz-gefühls“ abhängig ist.

The A text of the third sentence, from the apparatus:

> Mit diesem Worte bezeichnen wir einen eigentümlichen und jedem aus seiner inneren Erfahrung wohlbekannten psychischen Charakter (er wird gewöhnlich als Gefühl bezeichnet), welcher die Wahrheit des Urteils, dem er angeknüpft ist, verbürgt.

English (A):

> With this word we denote a peculiar psychic character, well known to everyone from his inner experience (it is usually called a feeling), which guarantees the truth of the judgement to which it is attached.

*Note on the text.* The A sentence is assembled from the file's notes 114 and 115, which give A's wording for the two stretches in Hua's corner brackets; the rest of the sentence is common to both editions. Note 114 carries the converter's aside "(Note in source text appears to have nested footnote markers)", which is not Husserl's and is left out. A further note says that "the quotation marks are lacking in A"; its anchor is not secure, but the only quotation marks in the stretch are those around "Evidenz-gefühls". The file's note anchors inside the B text are dropped.

The paragraph is spoken in two voices from its first words. "We formulate a third prejudice": the "we" is the author stating a view he will reject. The next two sentences, "All truth lies in the judgement. But we recognize a judgement as true only in the case of its evidence", are theses Husserl himself can accept in some sense, and they are given without distancing. The third sentence, the definition, is where B intervenes. "So it is said" (*so sagt man*), set between dashes, takes the definition out of the author's mouth. The apposition "a peculiar feeling" then names what is said. In A the sentence ran on in the "we" of the formulation, "with this word we denote", and the feeling appeared only in a parenthesis that reported usage, "it is usually called a feeling". A's reader met a definition of evidence as "a psychic character … well known to everyone from inner experience", stated by the author, and was told only that others call that character a feeling.

B's change does two things at once. It removes the first-person definition, so that the psychic-character view of evidence is no longer even provisionally Husserl's. And it moves "feeling" from a remark about usage into the content of the prejudice. In A one could hold that evidence is a psychic character and reject only the word "feeling". In B the prejudice is the whole package, character and feeling together, and § 51's reply, "evidence is no accessory feeling", and "not a psychic character at all that could simply be attached", meets it as a whole. The quotation marks around "feeling of evidence", lacking in A on the likeliest reading of the apparatus, make the same distancing visible in the noun.

The edition difference has a reason in Husserl's own development, which the B text shows elsewhere. § 6, read as A's reader met it, defined evidence as "luminous certainty" in the vocabulary of acknowledging and rejecting, and said nothing against its being a character of the judging act. § 51's B text adds that "the evident judgement is a consciousness of original givenness", where A had spoken only of "the image of seeing". By 1913 Husserl's account of evidence had become a theory of givenness, and a first-person sentence that called evidence a psychic character "well known from inner experience" could no longer stand. B's § 49 makes the prejudice wholly other; B's § 51 gives the positive account that makes the distance possible.

One other variant in the section dates the debate. B says that the interpretation of logic as a practically turned psychology of evidence gained ground "towards the end of the last century"; A said "of the last decade" (*des letzten Jahrzehnts*). A was written inside the decade it describes; B keeps the date and changes only the reference, in line with the Foreword's insistence that the *Prolegomena* be read in relation to the time of its writing. The main commentary's block for § 49 gives Husserl's reply, that the laws of evidence are "ideal laws about the objective relationships among propositions", and says that evidence "is indeed a psychological experience". The reply belongs to §§ 50–51, and the concession misstates it: § 51 makes evidence the "experience" of truth, in quotation marks, "in no other sense than that in which an ideal can be experience in a real act".

## §50, A/B 184–185, Hua 187: equivalent, but not the same

Reads [§50 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-equivalent-transformation-of-logical-propositions-into-propositions-about-ideal-conditions-of-judgement-evidence), first paragraph under § 50.

Every purely logical law can be turned into a proposition about evidence, because "A is true" is evidently equivalent to "it is possible that someone judges with evidence that A". The theory of evidence that psychologism wants is therefore available, as a transformation of logic. But the equivalent "does not in itself say the same". "A is true" says nothing of anyone's judging, as "a + b = b + a" says nothing of anyone's counting. Equivalence without sameness of sense is the understanding § 50 rests on, and the evidence-propositions it yields remain a priori: the possibility they state is ideal, not psychological.

> Again, the *modus Barbara* is undoubtedly equivalent to the proposition: the evidence of the necessary truth of a proposition of the form "all *A* are *C*" (or, more exactly expressed, of its truth as one that follows necessarily) can occur in an inferring act whose premises have the forms "all *A* are *B*" and "all *B* are *C*". And similarly with every purely logical proposition. Entirely understandable, since there evidently holds the general equivalence between the propositions "*A* is true" and "it is possible that someone or other judges with evidence that *A* is". […]
>
> But insight into this connection gives us at the same time the handle for refuting the attempt to make pure logic dissolve into a psychology of evidence. In itself the proposition "*A* is true" surely does not say the same as its equivalent "it is possible that someone or other judges that *A* is". The first does not speak of anyone's judgements, not even of anyone's in quite general terms. It is here exactly as with the purely mathematical propositions. The statement that *a* + *b* = *b* + *a* says that the numerical value of the sum of two numbers is independent of their position in the combination, but *it says* nothing of anyone's counting and summing. Such a thing comes in only through an evident and equivalent transformation. *In concreto* there *is*, after all (and this is fixed *a priori*), no number without counting, no sum without summing.

German:

> Wieder ist der *modus barbara* zweifellos äquivalent dem Satze: die Evidenz der notwendigen Wahrheit eines Satzes der Form „alle *A* sind *C*“ (oder genauer ausgedrückt: seiner Wahrheit als einer notwendig erfolgenden) kann auftreten in einem schließenden Akte, dessen Prämissen die Formen haben „alle *A* sind *B*“ und „alle *B* sind *C*“. Und so ähnlich bei jedem rein logischen Satze. Völlig begreiflich, da evidentermaßen die allgemeine Äquivalenz besteht zwischen den Sätzen „*A* ist wahr“ und „es ist möglich, daß irgend jemand mit Evidenz urteilt, es sei *A*“. […]
>
> Aber die Einsicht in diesen Zusammenhang bietet uns zugleich die Handhabe zur Widerlegung des Versuches, reine Logik in Psychologie der Evidenz aufgehen zu lassen. An sich besagt doch der Satz „*A* ist wahr“ nicht dasselbe wie sein Äquivalent „es ist möglich, daß irgend jemand urteile, es sei *A*“. Der erstere spricht nicht von Urteilen irgend jemandes, auch nicht irgend jemandes ganz im allgemeinen. Es verhält sich hier ganz so wie bei den rein mathematischen Sätzen. Die Aussage, daß a + b = b + a ist, besagt, daß der Zahlenwert der Summe zweier Zahlen von ihrer Stellung in der Verknüpfung unabhängig ist, aber *sie sagt* nichts vom Zählen und Summieren irgend jemandes. Dergleichen kommt erst durch eine evidente und äquivalente Umformung hinein. *In concreto* *ist* ja (und dies steht *a priori* fest) keine Zahl ohne Zählen, keine Summe ohne Summieren.

*Note on the text.* The omission is the sentence that draws the general conclusion about propositions that state what lies in the concept of truth. The file's italics are kept; the variable letters are LaTeX in the file and are given as plain letters. The file sets a page marker ("||") inside the first and last paragraphs; the quotation drops them. For "*sie sagt*" A has "Er spricht" (it speaks), and for the last sentence A has "gibt es ja (und dies steht a priori fest) keine Zahl ohne Zählen, keine Summe ohne Summieren" (there is, after all, and this is fixed a priori, no number without counting, no sum without summing). The worked example of the principle of contradiction precedes the quotation, on the page before.

The section opens with concessions, and the concessions measure how close Husserl lets the opponent come. He grants that knowing truth "presupposes" insight, that the logical technology must investigate the conditions under which evidence "lights up", and, "a further step towards" the disputed view, that even the purely logical propositions have "a certain relation" to the datum of evidence and in a certain sense supply conditions of it. The qualification follows at once: the relation is "purely ideal and indirect", obtained "by way of application or conversion" (*Anwendung resp. Umwendung*), as any law grounded purely in concepts can be applied to the empirical cases of its concepts. The evidence-propositions that result "keep their a priori character", and the conditions they state are "anything but psychological, i.e. real".

The worked examples show the conversion at work. The principles of contradiction and excluded middle are "equivalent" to: evidence can occur in one, but only in one, of a pair of contradictory judgements. The *modus Barbara* is equivalent to: the evidence of the necessary truth of "all A are C" "can occur" in an inference whose premises are "all A are B" and "all B are C". The modal is the key. The evidence-propositions say what *can* occur. They do not say that evidence will occur, under what circumstances, or in whom; they state ideal possibilities and incompatibilities, as § 31 had stated the true sense of the syllogistic formulas as "ideal incompatibilities". The general equivalence follows: "A is true" holds exactly when "it is possible that someone or other judges with evidence that A is".

Then the distinction that carries the refutation: equivalence is not sameness of saying. "In itself the proposition 'A is true' surely does not say the same (*besagt … nicht dasselbe*) as its equivalent." The verb *besagen* (to say, to mean) is the one § 14 used for the ought and § 21 for the principle of contradiction, which "does not say that it is to be surmised". Two propositions can be necessarily equivalent and differ in what they are about. The first "does not speak of anyone's judgements, not even of anyone's in quite general terms"; the phrase forestalls the reply that "someone or other" is so general as to be harmless. Generality about judgers is still about judgers.

The arithmetical parallel and its edition difference show what is at stake. "a + b = b + a" says that the value of a sum is independent of the order of its terms, and "it says nothing of anyone's counting and summing". Talk of counting comes in "only through an evident and equivalent transformation". The last sentence then states the truth in the psychologist's favour, and here B changed it. A said that "there is, after all, no number without counting, no sum without summing", fixed a priori. Read strictly, A conceded that numbers depend on counting, which is close to the "psychological origin" of arithmetical concepts that § 46 grants and the *Philosophy of Arithmetic* had explored. B restricts the claim with "in concreto" and emphasizes the verb: *in concreto* there *is* no number without counting. The dependence holds of numbers as given in concrete acts, not of numbers as the ideal objects arithmetic is about. The B sentence is the one that fits the paragraph's argument, which needs the equivalence to hold a priori between an ideal proposition and a proposition about possible acts, without the ideal object depending on the acts.

B also adds a reservation at the head of the section. The opponent's premise "that all truth lies in judgement" is now called "a commonplace in circulation, but very much in need of clarification". In A it passed without remark; B's Husserl no longer lets the commonplace go unqualified, though § 50 does not say what clarification it needs. The main commentary's block is right about the point of § 50, and its example (contradictory propositions are not simultaneously judged with evidence) is a fair paraphrase of the first worked case. It omits the claim that carries the refutation, that the equivalent "does not say the same", and the parallel with arithmetic that proves it.

## §56, A/B 206–209, Hua 209–212: the *hysteron proteron* of thought-economy

Reads [§56 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#continuation-the-absurdity-of-a-thought-economic-grounding-of-the-purely-logical), first paragraph under § 56.

The heading of § 56 names a fallacy: "the *hysteron proteron* of a thought-economic grounding of the purely logical", the later put first. Thought-economy, the principle of Mach and Avenarius that thinking tends to the greatest result with the least expenditure, can be spoken of only by comparing actual thinking with an ideal of rationality, and the ideal is therefore "prior by nature" (*proteron tēi physei*). To ground the ideal on the economy is to put what comes second first. The section turns on that order, which the main commentary's rendering of the heading, "The Absurdity of …", does not keep.

> The semblance that we are dealing, in the principle of parsimony, with a principle either epistemological or psychological lies, for the main part, in the confusion of the factually given with the logical ideal that is unnoticeably substituted for it. We recognize with insight as the highest goal and as the ideally justified tendency of all explanation that goes beyond mere description, that it orders the facts, in themselves "blind", under laws as general as possible, and in this sense comprehends them as rationally as possible. […]
>
> Certainly the talk of economy in thinking, of thought-economic "comprehension" of facts by general propositions, of lower generalities by higher, and the like, is well justified. But it gains its justification only through comparison of actual thinking with the ideal norm recognized with insight, which accordingly is the *proteron tēi physei*. The ideal validity of the norm is the presupposition of all meaningful talk of thought-economy, hence it is no possible result of explanation by the doctrine of this economy. […]
>
> One therefore recognizes the *hysteron proteron*. Before all thought-economics we must already know the ideal, we must know what science strives for *idealiter*, what lawful connections, what basic laws and derived laws and the like are and achieve *idealiter*, before we can discuss and estimate the thought-economic function of knowing them.

The section opens by naming the confusion that produces the semblance: "the factually given" and "the logical ideal that is unnoticeably substituted for it" (*unvermerkt supponiert*). The participle explains why the economists do not see what they do. When they speak of thinking as tending to comprehend facts under the most general laws, they describe actual thinking in terms borrowed from the ideal of rationality, and then credit the tendency to biology. Husserl accepts the description of the ideal. Explanation aims to order "blind" facts, the quotation marks marking the word as relative to the ideal, "under laws as general as possible". He denies only that the ideal is a fact. The next paragraph says so outright: the principle of greatest rationality "is no biological and merely thought-economic principle, but rather a purely ideal and, into the bargain, a normative principle".

The Greek carries the argument's form. "Prior by nature" (*proteron tēi physei*) is Aristotle's phrase for what comes first in the order of being or of grounds, as against what comes first for us. Husserl assigns it to "the ideal norm recognized with insight". Talk of economy "gains its justification only through comparison" of actual thinking with the norm, so the norm is presupposed by the talk and cannot be its result: "the ideal validity of the norm is the presupposition of all meaningful talk of thought-economy, hence it is no possible result of explanation by the doctrine of this economy". The *hysteron proteron* follows as a conclusion, "one therefore recognizes" it: the economist puts first, as ground, the economy that is second, and second, as explained, the ideal that is first. A concession prevents the argument from proving too much. We have "vague concepts of these ideas" before pure logic is worked out, so that talk of thought-economy may precede the science; "but the essential situation is not changed thereby: in itself pure logic precedes all thought-economics". The phrase "in itself" (*an sich*) restates Aristotle's distinction between the order in itself and the order for us.

A small edition difference runs the same way. B says that a smallest set of general laws must exist "from which all other laws can be derived in pure deduction" (*aus welchen sich … ableiten*). A said "to which all other laws can be reduced" (*auf welche sich … zurückführen*). A described the order of discovery, from the derived back to the basic; B describes the order of grounds, from the basic forward. The change is slight, but it is the change from the order for us to the order in itself that § 56 makes its subject.

Two coinages in the section sharpen the opponent. The thought-economist is a *Denkökonom* "(*sit venia verbo*)", forgive the word, the same apology Husserl made in § 40 for calling himself a "logical absolutist". And "natural theories", the economists' name for pre-scientific thinking that yields results as if logically guided, is accepted only with a warning: we call such processes theories because their results are "as if they sprang from logically insightful thinking", and in naming them so we read "the properly theoretical into them". § 56 ends Chapter IX, and its final paragraph ends the critical half of the book: what is needed is a "most profound analysis of the fundamentally different directions of thinking in the spheres of the real and the ideal". The main commentary's block reduces the section to a contrast of usefulness and validity, with examples (an economical but invalid rule, a valid but burdensome one) that the section does not give. The section's argument is about order: the ideal is presupposed by the very talk that claims to explain it.

## §60, A/B 220–222, Hua 223–224: Leibniz's arguments in form

Reads [§60 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#points-of-contact-with-leibniz), first paragraph under § 60.

Husserl's claim that Leibniz stands "relatively nearest" to the pure logic of the *Prolegomena* rests on one passage of the *Nouveaux Essais*, quoted in French in a footnote. The theory of syllogistic forms, widened into a theory of "arguments in form" (*argumens en forme*), is "a kind of universal mathematics, whose importance is not sufficiently known". An argument in form is "every reasoning that concludes by the force of the form", and that includes a well-drawn account, an algebraic calculation and an analysis of infinitesimals, "since their form of reasoning has been demonstrated beforehand". Husserl hears in this his own law of form, and he extends Leibniz's concept, with a "would have to", beyond what Leibniz said.

> Leibniz too was led to efforts towards a reformed logic. But with more insight than his predecessors, he takes the scholastic logic, instead of disparaging it as empty formula-work, as a valuable preliminary stage of true logic, which despite its imperfection could offer thinking real help. Its development into a discipline of mathematical form and rigour, into a universal mathematics in a highest and most comprehensive sense, is a goal to which he sacrifices ever new efforts.

Footnote:

> I follow here the indications in the *Nouveaux Essais*, Book IV, ch. XVII. Cf. e.g. § 4, … where the theory of the syllogistic forms, widened into the wholly general theory of the "arguments in form", is designated as "a kind of universal mathematics, whose importance is not sufficiently known". "It must be known", it says there, "that by arguments in form I understand not only that scholastic manner of arguing that is used in the colleges, but every reasoning that concludes by the force of the form, and where one need not supply any article; so that a sorites, another tissue of syllogism that avoids repetition, even a well-drawn account, a calculation of algebra, an analysis of infinitesimals will be to me nearly arguments in form, since their form of reasoning has been demonstrated beforehand, so that one is sure not to be deceived in it." The sphere of the *Mathématique universelle* conceived here would thus be very much wider than the sphere of the logical calculus with whose construction Leibniz took great pains without quite succeeding. Strictly, Leibniz would have to include under this universal mathematics the whole *Mathesis universalis* in the usual quantitative sense …

German:

> […] auch bei Leibniz zu […] Bemühungen um eine reformierte Logik. Aber einsichtiger als seine Vorgänger, faßt er die scholastische Logik, statt sie als hohlen Formelkram zu verunglimpfen, als eine wertvolle Vorstufe der wahren Logik, welche trotz ihrer Unvollkommenheit dem Denken wahre Hilfen zu bieten vermöchte. Ihre Fortbildung zu einer Disziplin von mathematischer Form und Strenge, zu einer universellen Mathematik in einem höchsten und umfassendsten Sinne, ist ein Ziel, dem er immer neue Anstrengungen opfert.

Footnote (German, with Leibniz's French as Husserl quotes it):

> Ich folge hier den Andeutungen in den *Nouveaux Essais*, L. IV, ch. XVII. Vgl. z. B. § 4, *Opp. phil.*, Erdm. 395ᵃ, wo die Lehre von den syllogistischen Formen, erweitert zur ganz allgemeinen Lehre von den „*argumens en forme*“, bezeichnet wird als „*une espèce de Mathématique universelle, dont l'importance n'est pas assez connue.*“ „*Il faut savoir*“, heißt es dort, „*que par les argumens en forme je n'entends pas seulement cette manière scolastique d'argumenter, dont on se sert dans les collèges, mais tout raisonnement qui conclut par la force de la forme, et où l'on n'a besoin de suppléer aucun article; desorte qu'un sorites, un autre tissu de syllogisme, qui évite la répétition, même un compte bien dressé, un calcul d'Algèbre, une analyse des infinitésimales me seront à peu près des argumens en forme, puisque leur forme de raisonner a été prédémontrée, en sorte qu'on est sûr de ne s'y point tromper.*“ Die Sphäre der hier konzipierten *Mathématique universelle* wäre also sehr viel weiter as die Sphäre des logischen Kalküls, mit dessen Konstruktion sich Leibniz viel mühte, ohne damit ganz zu Rande zu kommen. Eigentlich müßte Leibniz unter diese allgemeine Mathematik die ganze *Mathesis universalis* im gewöhnlichen quantitativen Sinne mitbefassen …

*Note on the text.* Two words of the first German sentence are missing in the file where note anchors stand: the main verb before "auch bei Leibniz" (A: *führt*, leads; the B word is lost) and an adjective before "Bemühungen" (A: *unermüdlichen*, untiring); the English supplies "was led to". The file splits the footnote across a page break with a stray note about Lotze between its parts, breaks "à peu | près" at the B 221 marker, and has "as" for *als*; the quotation joins the footnote, drops the marker and quotes "as" as it stands. The French is Leibniz's as Husserl quotes it, in his spelling ("argumens", "desorte"), and the reading does not check it against a Leibniz edition, none being in the corpus.

The main text praises an attitude before a doctrine. Leibniz took scholastic logic, "instead of disparaging it as empty formula-work, as a valuable preliminary stage of true logic". The goal was its development "into a discipline of mathematical form and rigour, into a universal mathematics in a highest and most comprehensive sense". The superlatives answer the narrowness of the scholastic forms: Leibniz's universal mathematics is not the syllogistic writ large, and the footnote's French says why. The arguments in form include "not only that scholastic manner of arguing", but "every reasoning that concludes by the force of the form", where "one need not supply any article". An account, an algebraic calculation, an infinitesimal analysis are "nearly" (*à peu près*) arguments in form, "since their form of reasoning has been demonstrated beforehand (*prédémontrée*), so that one is sure not to be deceived".

The French phrases map onto the *Prolegomena*'s own law of form. "Concludes by the force of the form" is § 7's claim that every grounding of a given form is correct provided its premises are, and "demonstrated beforehand" is its consequence: the form is justified once, "at a stroke", for all its instances, so that the instance can be trusted without inspecting its matter. The parallel is close, and the difference is in what "form" covers. For Leibniz an algebraic calculation is "nearly" an argument in form; for Husserl calculation is mathematics, and mathematics and logic are two branches of one formal science. The footnote turns Leibniz in that direction by a modal. "Strictly, Leibniz would have to include (*müßte … mitbefassen*) under this universal mathematics the whole *Mathesis universalis* in the usual quantitative sense", and the *Ars combinatoria* as well, which Leibniz defines as the "general science of quality" over against the general science of quantity. Husserl does not say that Leibniz did this; he says what Leibniz's concept requires. The next paragraph speaks of foresight, not achievement: Leibniz "foresaw in brilliant intuitions" the probability calculus and the mathematical analysis of inference, and is "the spiritual father of the pure theory of manifolds" (§§ 69–70).

The distinction between quality and quantity recalls the Preface. There Husserl reported that the generalization of formal arithmetic beyond the quantitative domain awoke the insight that "the quantitative does not belong at all to the most general essence of the mathematical or 'formal'", and that "mathematizing logic" was a mathematics without quantity. Leibniz's *Ars combinatoria* as *scientia generalis de qualitate* is the historical form of that insight. The second note of § 60 closes the circle on the other side: for Leibniz the *Mathesis universalis* in the narrowest sense coincides with the *Logica Mathematicorum*, the art of judging and inventing "concerning quantities". In Leibniz the narrowest universal mathematics is quantitative and the widest is a theory of form; Husserl takes the widest.

§ 60's last paragraph states the claim the key-passage list asks to test: "with all this Leibniz stands on the ground of that idea of pure logic for which we argue here", since "nothing is further from him than the thought that the essential foundations of a fruitful art of knowledge might lie in psychology"; they are "wholly a priori". The test passes for the a priori and for form. It does not pass for the separation of a pure, theoretical logic from the art of knowledge: Leibniz's universal mathematics has "the vocation for the practical regulation of knowledge" built in, as arithmetic does, and the *Prolegomena*'s distinction of theory and technology is Husserl's, not Leibniz's.

In the *Prolegomena* "*Mathesis universalis*" is Leibniz's term, used only in § 60 and its notes; the book's own names are pure logic and the theory of manifolds. In the lectures of 1906/07 the term has become Husserl's. A rational theory in mechanics or optics, he says there, "has its mathematical form and stands under the types and laws of the theory of theories, the formal *mathesis universalis*" (Hua XXIV 119). The name § 60 borrowed for a predecessor's project is, six years later, the name of Chapter XI's third task.

## Appendix to Chapter X, A 225–226 / B 225–226, Hua 227: Bolzano de facto

Reads [Appendix.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#appendix-references-to-f-a-lange-and-b-bolzano), second block under the Appendix on Lange and Bolzano.

The Appendix credits Bolzano with having carried out pure logic in fact without having defined it. He "did not expressly discuss and advocate the independent demarcation of a pure logic in our sense", but "de facto" his first two volumes present its content with such purity and rigour that he "must count as one of the greatest logicians of all time". The credit is split by a contrast of intention and achievement, and the Appendix sets it against Lange, who advocated pure formal logic warmly and "had no idea" that it had long been realized. The 1913 text then moves Bolzano a little further from Leibniz.

> While Lange argued so warmly for the idea of a purely formal logic, he had no idea that it had long since been realized to a relatively high degree. I do not, of course, mean the many presentations of formal logic that grew up especially in the schools of Kant and Herbart, and that corresponded only too little to the claims they raised; but rather Bernhard Bolzano's *Theory of Science* of the year 1837, a work that in matters of the logical "elementary doctrine" leaves far behind everything that world literature offers in systematic drafts of logic. Bolzano, it is true, did not expressly discuss and advocate the independent demarcation of a pure logic in our sense; but *de facto* he presented it, in the first two volumes of his work, as the foundation of a theory of science in the sense of his conception, with such purity and scientific rigour, and furnished it with such a wealth of original, scientifically secured and in any case fruitful thoughts, that *he* must on that account count as one of the greatest logicians of all time. *Historically* he is to be set in fairly close relation to Leibniz, with whom he shares important thoughts and basic conceptions, and to whom he stands nearest philosophically in other respects too. To be sure, he too did not fully exhaust the wealth of Leibniz's logical intuitions, especially not with regard to mathematical syllogistic and the *mathesis universalis*. But at that time too little was known of Leibniz's literary remains, and the "formal" theory of manifolds was lacking as the key to understanding.

German:

> Während Lange für die Idee einer rein formalen Logik so warm eintrat, hatte er keine Ahnung, daß sie längst schon in relativ hohem Maße realisiert war. Ich mache natürlich nicht die vielen Darstellungen der formalen Logik, welche zumal in den Schulen Kants und Herbarts erwuchsen, und welche den Ansprüchen, die sie erhoben, nur zu wenig entsprachen; wohl aber Bernhard Bolzanos Wissenschaftslehre aus dem Jahre 1837, ein Werk, das in Sachen der logischen „Elementarlehre“ alles weit zurückläßt, was die Weltliteratur an systematischen Entwürfen der Logik darbietet. Zwar hat Bolzano die selbständige Abgrenzung einer reinen Logik in unserem Sinne nicht ausdrücklich erörtert und befürwortet; aber *de facto* hat er in den beiden ersten Bänden seines Werkes, nämlich als Unterlage einer Wissenschaftslehre im Sinne seiner Auffassung, in einer Reinheit und wissenschaftlichen Strenge dargestellt und mit einer solchen Fülle von originellen, wissenschaftlich gesicherten und jedenfalls fruchtbaren Gedanken ausgestattet, daß *er* um dessentwillen als einer der größten Logiker aller Zeiten werden gelten müssen. *Historisch* ist er in ziemlich nahe Beziehung zu Leibniz zu setzen, mit dem er wichtige Gedanken und Grundauffassungen teilt, und dem er philosophisch auch sonst zunächst steht. Freilich hat auch er den Reichtum der logischen Intuitionen Leibnizens nicht ganz ausgeschöpft, zumal nicht in Hinsicht auf die mathematische Syllogistik und die *mathesis universalis*. Doch war vom Nachlaß Leibnizens damals noch zu wenig bekannt, und es fehlte die „formale“ Mannigfaltigkeitslehre als der Schlüssel des Verständnisses.

*Note on the text.* "Ich mache natürlich nicht …" lacks its participle (presumably a verb of meaning, such as *meine*); the English supplies "mean". "werden gelten müssen" is the file's word order. The file's corner brackets and the A 226 marker are dropped; the italics on *de facto*, *er* and *Historisch* are the file's, and on the last two they may render Hua's markers of a variant passage rather than emphasis, so the reading does not argue from them. Two A variants are given by notes: for "den Ansprüchen … entsprachen" A had "die Ansprüche … befriedigten" (satisfied the claims), and for "Historisch ist er in ziemlich nahe Beziehung zu" A had "Seiner Stellung nach ist er dicht neben" (by his position he is to be set close beside).

The passage begins by setting Bolzano against Lange, and the main commentary's block misreads the pairing. It says that Lange "had already raised objections to the psychologistic grounding of logic that anticipate some of Husserl's own arguments". The Appendix praises Lange for one thing: that "in a time of prevailing underestimation of pure logic" he argued "with decisiveness" that science could expect much from "a separate treatment of the purely formal elements of logic". It begins "however wide the distance that separates my conception of logic from F. A. Lange's", and § 28 had classified Lange's position as a psychologism "of another genus". Lange is credited with the programme, and denied its execution and its grounding. Bolzano is credited with the execution and denied the programme. The two halves of pure logic, its idea and its content, are distributed between them.

The key word for Bolzano is *de facto*. He "did not expressly discuss and advocate" the demarcation of pure logic "in our sense"; but he presented its content "as the foundation of a theory of science in the sense of his conception". The double qualification ("in our sense", "in the sense of his conception") keeps the two projects apart. Bolzano's theory of science is not Husserl's, as § 12 had already said in calling Bolzano's definition of it "strange enough". What Husserl takes from Bolzano is the "elementary doctrine" of the first two volumes, and the reason he gives is a mathematician's virtue: Bolzano brings into logic "the same spirit of scientific rigour" that he was the first to bring into the foundations of analysis, free of the "profound ambiguity of system-philosophy". The praise ends with a limit: Bolzano's achievement is not "final", and its deficiencies are "particularly sensitive in the epistemological direction", the very task the second volume of the *Investigations* takes up.

The relation to Leibniz is where the editions differ. B says that "historically" Bolzano "is to be set in fairly close relation to Leibniz". A said that "by his position he is to be set close beside" him (*seiner Stellung nach … dicht neben*). A placed Bolzano next to Leibniz in the order of doctrine; B places him in a fairly close historical relation, and adds nothing that would undo the next sentence's limit, that Bolzano did not exhaust Leibniz's logical intuitions, "especially not with regard to mathematical syllogistic and the *mathesis universalis*", lacking "the 'formal' theory of manifolds as the key". The main commentary turns the passage into a lineage "that runs from Leibniz through Bolzano". The source, in both editions, gives a relation with a gap in it, and B loosens the relation.

The commentary also says that Husserl "regrets that he did not discover Bolzano's work earlier". The Appendix has no such statement, and the key-passage list traced the nearest wording to the Preface, where Husserl mentions, without names, researchers "whose writings I was formerly unable to value at their worth". The corpus has a closer source, written in 1913. In the second fragment of the draft preface to the second edition (Hua XX/1, Text Nr. 5, 298–299), Husserl tells how it "dawned" on him "all at once" that the first two volumes of the *Theory of Science*, under the titles of a theory of presentations in themselves and propositions in themselves, gave "a closed presentation of the domain of purely ideal doctrines", "a complete draft of a 'pure' logic"; and how, reworking his lectures with Bolzano's help, he recognized its imperfection: "it lacked the idea of a purely formal mathematics or 'theory of manifolds'". A variant of the same passage, printed in the volume's apparatus, adds that he had earlier taken Bolzano's "original thoughts about presentations, propositions, truths 'in themselves'" for "metaphysical abstrusities". If the commentary's regret has a source in the corpus, it is this draft of 1913, not the *Prolegomena*. The draft repeats the Appendix's two judgements, the pure logic de facto and the missing theory of manifolds, and adds a third the Appendix does not make: that the "Platonic" interpretation Husserl confirmed on Bolzano's text "was wholly foreign to Bolzano himself".

## §62, A/B 228–229, Hua 230–231: the connection of things and the connection of truths

Reads [§62 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-unity-of-science-the-connection-among-things-and-the-connection-among-truths), first paragraph under § 62.

What makes a science a science is not the real connection of its acts of thinking but an ideal connection, and that connection can be taken in two ways: as the connection of the things the acts refer to, or as the connection of the truths in which the unity of the things "comes to objective validity". The two are "given a priori together and inseparable from each other", because "truth in itself" is "the necessary correlate of being in itself". But the inseparability "is not identity". The positive half of the *Prolegomena* opens with this correlation, and B changed the verb that states it: where A said that in the truths "the validity of the things … is constituted", B says that in them "the actual subsistence of the things … is expressed".

> But greater determinacy and clarity are needed here. By the objective connection that ideally runs through scientific thinking and gives it, and so science as such, "unity", something twofold can be understood: the connection of the things to which the experiences of thinking (actual or possible) are intentionally related, and on the other side the connection of the truths in which the material unity comes, as what it is, to objective validity. The one and the other are given *a priori* together and are inseparable from each other. Nothing can be without being determined thus or thus; and that it is, and is determined thus or thus, is precisely the truth in itself that forms the necessary correlate of being in itself. Evidently the same that holds of single truths or states of affairs holds also of connections of truths or of states of affairs. This evident inseparability, however, is not identity. In the truths or connections of truths in question, the actual subsistence of the things and material connections is expressed. But the connections of truths are other than the connections of the things that are "truly" in them; this shows itself at once in that the truths that hold of truths do not coincide with the truths that hold of things that are posited in those truths.

German:

> Doch es bedarf hier größerer Bestimmtheit und Klarheit. Unter dem objektiven Zusammenhang, der das wissenschaftliche Denken ideell durchzieht, ihm und so der Wissenschaft als solcher „Einheit“ gibt, kann Doppeltes verstanden werden: Der Zusammenhang der Sachen, auf welche sich die Denkerlebnisse (die wirklichen oder möglichen) intentional beziehen, und auf der anderen Seite der Zusammenhang der Wahrheiten, in dem die sachliche Einheit als das, was sie ist, zur objektiven Geltung kommt. Eins und das andere ist *a priori* miteinander gegeben und voneinander unablöslich. Es kann nichts sein, ohne so oder so bestimmt zu sein; und daß es ist und so oder so bestimmt ist, dies ist eben die Wahrheit an sich, welche das notwendige Korrelat des Seins an sich bildet. Offenbar gilt dasselbe, was von einzelnen Wahrheiten bzw. Sachverhalten gilt, auch von Zusammenhängen von Wahrheiten bzw. von Sachverhalten. Diese evidente Unabtrennbarkeit ist aber nicht Identität. In den bezüglichen Wahrheiten oder Wahrheitszusammenhängen prägt sich das wirkliche Bestehen der Sachen und sachlichen Zusammenhänge aus. Aber die Wahrheitszusammenhänge sind andere als die Zusammenhänge der Sachen, die in jenen „wahrhaft“ sind; dies zeigt sich sofort darin, daß die Wahrheiten, die von Wahrheiten gelten, nicht zusammenfallen mit den Wahrheiten, die von Sachen gelten, welche in jenen Wahrheiten gesetzt sind.

*Note on the text.* The file's corner bracket and note anchors are dropped, and the A 229 marker after "gegeben". The apparatus gives A's readings: for "prägt sich das wirkliche Bestehen der Sachen und sachlichen Zusammenhänge aus" A had "konstituiert sich die Geltung der Sachen und sachlichen Zusammenhänge" (the validity of the things and material connections is constituted), and for „wahrhaft“ A had "wahr (wahrhaft)" (true, truly) without quotation marks.

The paragraph moves from correlation to difference in four steps. First the "objective connection" is doubled: the connection of things to which thinking is "intentionally" related, and the connection of truths in which "the material unity comes, as what it is, to objective validity". The phrase "as what it is" says that the truths add nothing to the things; they let the things' unity count as valid. Second, the two are "given a priori together and inseparable". The ground is a sentence of two clauses that ties being to determinacy and determinacy to truth: "nothing can be without being determined thus or thus; and that it is, and is determined thus or thus, is precisely the truth in itself". Truth is the "that" of being-so, said of the thing. Third, the correlation is extended from single truths to connections. Fourth comes the restriction: "this evident inseparability, however, is not identity". The proof is a reflexive asymmetry. There are truths about truths, about their forms, their grounding and their theory, that are not truths about the things; and these are the domain of pure logic.

The B verb puts the relation in the right direction for that conclusion. In the truths, "the actual subsistence (*das wirkliche Bestehen*) of the things … is expressed" (*prägt sich … aus*): the things subsist, and the truths bear their imprint. A had said that in the truths "the validity (*Geltung*) of the things … is constituted" (*konstituiert sich*). Two words changed, and both matter. "Validity of things" applied to things the predicate that belongs to propositions; B gives things "subsistence" and keeps "validity" for truths, as the earlier sentence already does ("comes to objective validity"). And "is constituted" said that the truths make the things' standing, which, read strictly, inverts the correlation just stated, in which truth in itself is the correlate of being in itself. B's "expressed" makes the truths depend on the being they state, without making them identical to it. The quotation marks that B puts around "truly" (*„wahrhaft“*) do similar work: the things "are" in the truths only in the sense in which a state of affairs is in the proposition that states it.

The main commentary's block reverses the order of dependence. It says that "the truths of a science form a unity because the objects they concern form a domain". The paragraph says that the two unities are given together and that neither is the other, and the next section asks "what determines the unity of science and thereby also the unity of the domain". Its answer, in § 63, is the unity of theory: in the abstract sciences the domain is unified by the explanatory unity of the laws. § 62 itself, at its end, calls the domain "a unified objectivity" to which the truths are "materially connected", and notes that the expression is taken in a wider sense than usual, referring forward to § 64. The domain does not ground the unity of the truths. The unity of theory determines the domain.

The term "object" is widened in the next paragraph, and the widening carries the correlation into pure logic. "Object, thing" are used "in the widest sense": an object "can be a real as well as an ideal, a thing or a process as well as a species or a mathematical relation, a being as well as an ought (*Seinsollen*)". The ought of § 14 and the ideal objects of § 46 are objects in this sense, so the correlation holds for the theory of norms and for mathematics as for natural science. The two unities are then "given to us in judgement, or more exactly in knowledge", and Husserl introduces a phrase he will keep: in performing an act of knowledge we "live in it". Chapter XI's positive programme starts from the knower's act and turns at once to the correlation of its objects and its truths.

The Foreword to B names the concept of "truth in itself" as one of the "very essential inadequacies" it had to keep, "too one-sidedly oriented to the *vérités de raison*". § 62's sentence is where the concept is defined, and the definition shows the orientation in reverse. "That it is, and is determined thus or thus" holds of any being whatever, facts included; but the truths that § 62 sets apart from the truths about things, the truths about truths, are of reason. The edition difference in the verb is Husserl's 1913 repair at the one place where the 1900 text had let the truths make the things.

## §64, A 233–235 / B 233–235, Hua 236–237: unity of explanation

Reads [§64 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-essential-and-extra-essential-principles-that-give-science-its-unity-abstract-concrete-and-normative-sciences), first paragraph under § 64.

The truths of a science are "essentially one" when their connection rests on what makes science science, knowledge from grounds, and so "essential unity of the truths of a science is unity of explanation", which means theoretical unity, "homogeneous unity of the explanatory principles". Every other principle of unity is extra-essential: the unity of one individual object or one empirical genus, which defines the concrete sciences (geography, history, anatomy), and the unity of a basic value, which defines the normative ones. The abstract or nomological sciences are the "basic sciences" from which the others draw what is theoretical in them. One sentence of A, deleted in B, said why: "for it belongs, after all, to the essence of every science as such to explain."

> The truths of a science are essentially one when their connection rests on what above all makes science science; and this is, as we know, knowledge from the ground, hence explanation or grounding (in the pregnant sense). Essential unity of the truths of a science is unity of explanation. But all explanation points to a theory and finds its conclusion in the knowledge of the basic laws, the principles of explanation. Unity of explanation thus means theoretical unity, that is, according to what was set out above, homogeneous unity of the grounding lawfulness, ultimately homogeneous unity of the explanatory principles.
>

The definition is reached by three identifications in a row. What makes science science is "knowledge from the ground", that is, "explanation or grounding (in the pregnant sense)", so essential unity is "unity of explanation"; explanation "finds its conclusion" in basic laws, so it is "theoretical unity"; and theoretical unity is, by § 63, "homogeneous unity" of grounding lawfulness. "Homogeneous" recalls § 43's ground for pure logic's escape from the reflective circle, that its premises are homogeneous with its conclusions, and § 2's warning against mixing the heterogeneous. A science is essentially one when one kind of law explains all its truths.

The names that follow are weighed as names. "Abstract" is "not very apt"; "theoretical" would be most characteristic, but the book has used it against "practical and normative"; v. B keeps the qualification and deletes its ground.

The deletion changes the standing of the concrete sciences only slightly, and the text shows how. The concrete sciences are united by "one and the same individual objectivity" or "one and the same empirical genus", and "one also calls them descriptive". B still says, a page later, that the name must not be taken "as if descriptive sciences aimed at mere description, which contradicts the concept of science authoritative for us" (*dem für uns maßgebenden Begriff von Wissenschaft*). Their unity is extra-essential because explanation guided by an empirical unity can lead into "widely separated or even heterogeneous theories". In A the requirement of explanation was stated twice, once as the essence of "every science as such", once as the concept of science "authoritative for us". B keeps only the second, which is relative to the concept the *Prolegomena* works with. The file cannot say why Husserl struck the first. The Foreword to B gives one context: it contrasts the "psychological description" carried out in inner experience with "phenomenological description", in which all transcending interpretations of the immanent data are excluded. A science whose method is description would, on A's sentence, not be a science at all. B no longer says so.

The extra-essential principles are what the main commentary's block misstates. It gives the essential principles as "the logical laws and fundamental concepts that define its domain" and the extra-essential ones as "auxiliary methods, conventions, etc.". In the source the essential principle is the unity of explanation, the extra-essential principles are the unity of an individual object or empirical genus and, last, the unity of "a unified basic value (or the unified basic norm), as we discussed at length in Chapter II, § 14". Methods and conventions are not principles of unity at all in § 64. The section's result serves Chapter XI's question: if essential unity is theoretical unity, then the ideal conditions of the possibility of science are those of theory in general, and § 65 asks for them.

## §67, A 243–245 / B 243–245, Hua 245–246: categories of meaning and their correlates

Reads [§67 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#the-task-of-pure-logic-first-the-fixing-of-the-pure-categories-of-meaning-the-pure-object-categories-and-their-lawful-combinations), first paragraph under § 67.

The first task of pure logic is to fix the concepts that "make possible" theoretical connection: concepts of the second level (concept, proposition, truth), the forms of connecting propositions and of building simple propositions, and the laws of their complication. These are the pure categories of meaning (*Bedeutungskategorien*). "In close, ideal-lawful connection" with them stand concepts "correlative" to them: object, state of affairs, unity, plurality, number, relation, connection, the pure or formal object-categories. The understanding the reading follows is that correlation. Its edition history shows B pressing the correlation further than A, toward the correlates of acts.

> In close, ideal-lawful connection with the concepts mentioned so far, the categories of meaning, stand others that are *correlative* to them, such as object, state of affairs, unity, plurality, number, relation, connection, and so on. These are the pure or formal objective categories. They too must therefore be taken into consideration. On both sides it is throughout a matter of concepts that, as their very function makes clear, are independent of the particularity of any matter of knowledge, and under which all concepts and objects, propositions and states of affairs and so on, that occur specially in thinking must be ordered; hence they can arise only *in regard to* the various "functions of thought", i.e. can have their concrete basis only in possible acts of thinking as such, or in the correlates graspable in them.

German:

> In nahem, ideal gesetzlichem Zusammenhang mit den bisher erwähnten Begriffen, den Bedeutungskategorien, stehen andere, zu ihnen *korrelative* Begriffe, wie Gegenstand, Sachverhalt, Einheit, Vielheit, Anzahl, Beziehung, Verknüpfung usf. Es sind die reinen oder formalen gegenständlichen Kategorien. Auch diese müssen also in Betracht gezogen werden. Beiderseits handelt es sich durchgehends um Begriffe, die, wie es schon ihre Funktion klarmacht, von der Besonderheit irgendwelcher Erkenntnismaterie unabhängig sind, und unter welche sich alle im Denken speziell auftretenden Begriffe und Gegenstände, Sätze und Sachverhalte usw. ordnen müssen; daher sie nur *im Hinblick* auf die verschiedenen „Denkfunktionen“ entspringen, d.h. in möglichen Denkakten als solchen oder den in ihnen erfaßbaren Korrelaten ihre konkrete Grundlage haben können.

The categories of meaning are defined by their role in theory. They are the concepts "that constitute the idea of theoretical unity", and they appear "constitutively" as "concepts of the second level, namely concepts of concepts and of other ideal unities". The paragraph says how they arise: a given theory is a deductive connection of given propositions, which are connections of given concepts, and "the idea of the corresponding 'form' of the theory arises by substitution of something indeterminate for those givens". Formalization by variables, which § 7 had used without naming it ("every A is B, X is A"), is here the method by which the categories of meaning are reached. The list is short: concept, proposition, truth; conjunctive, disjunctive and hypothetical connection; subject forms, predicate forms, plural form; and "fixed laws" that regulate the stepwise complication of new forms from primitive ones. The main commentary's block adds "falsity", "negation", "universality", "particularity"; the source's list does not name them.

The object-categories are introduced by their relation to the meaning-categories, not by a definition of their own. They stand "in close, ideal-lawful connection" with them and are "correlative" to them. The correlation is § 62's in categorial form. There the connection of things and the connection of truths were given together and were not identical; here the formal concepts of the things (object, state of affairs, unity, plurality, number, relation) answer to the formal concepts of the truths (concept, proposition, connection). A said "correlate" (*korrelat*), B "correlative" (*korrelative*), and the change is grammatical before it is philosophical: B makes the relation an attribute of the concepts. Both sides are independent of "any matter of knowledge", and everything that occurs in thinking "must be ordered" under them. Number among the object-categories is what joins formal logic to arithmetic, the junction the Preface had made its question.

The last clause, on the origin of the categories, is where B rewrote. A said that the categories arise "only through reflection on" (*durch Reflexion auf*) the various "functions of thought". That is the language of an empiricist theory of the origin of concepts, in which logical concepts come from reflection on mental operations. B says "only in regard to" (*im Hinblick auf*), and adds that their concrete basis lies in possible acts of thinking "or in the correlates graspable in them". The next paragraph makes the same correction in one word. The task is to investigate the "origin" of these concepts, "not as if the psychological question of the genesis of the conceptual presentations had the least interest"; it is a matter of their "phenomenological origin", where A had said "logical origin", or better, of "insight into the essence of the concepts in question". B moves the origin of the categories from reflection on acts to the acts' correlates, and names it phenomenological. The correlation of meaning-categories and object-categories, in B, has its basis on both sides of the act.

The pairing is carried through the rest of the chapter. § 68 assigns pure logic the laws and theories grounded in both groups of categories, and §§ 69–70 rise to the theory of possible forms of theories and its correlate, the theory of manifolds. In the draft preface of 1913, Husserl names the pair with the terms of his later work. Bolzano's draft of a pure logic, he writes, lacked the idea of a purely formal mathematics, and "in connection with this lacked any approach to a discussion of the relation between formal-ontological and formal meaning-theoretical consideration" (Hua XX/1, Text Nr. 5, 298). § 67 is where the *Prolegomena* states that relation without these names: object-categories and meaning-categories, correlative and inseparable, studied together by one discipline.

## §§69–70, A/B 247–250, Hua 248–251: the theory of manifolds

Reads [§69 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#third-the-theory-of-possible-theory-forms-or-the-pure-theory-of-manifolds) and [§70 ¶1](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-prolegomena-commentary.md#elaboration-of-the-idea-of-the-pure-theory-of-manifolds), the first paragraphs under § 69 and § 70.

The third and highest task of pure logic is a science of the possible forms of theories and their lawful relations. Its objective correlate is the theory of manifolds (*Mannigfaltigkeitslehre*): a manifold is a domain "determined solely by the fact that it is subject to a theory of such form", its objects left wholly undetermined in their matter. Every actual theory is a "specialization or singularization" of a theory-form, and every theoretically worked domain is "a single manifold". The *Prolegomena* ends its positive programme where the Preface began, with the discipline whose logical investigation first drove Husserl to logic. B adds to § 70 a parenthesis that names the relation between the two sciences, "in correlative conversion".

> These indications will perhaps seem somewhat obscure. That they are not vague fantasies but conceptions of firm content is proved by "formal mathematics" in the most general sense, or the theory of manifolds, this highest flowering of modern mathematics. In fact it is nothing other than (in correlative conversion) a partial realization of the ideal just sketched […]
>
> The objective correlate of the concept of the possible theory determined only by its form is the concept of a possible domain of knowledge in general, to be governed by a theory of such form. Such a domain, however, the mathematician (in his circle) calls a manifold. It is therefore a domain that is determined solely by the fact that it is subject to a theory of such form, or that for its objects certain connections are possible that stand under certain basic laws of this or that determinate form (here the only determining thing). In their matter the objects remain wholly undetermined; the mathematician, to indicate this, likes to speak of "objects of thought". […] In the theory of manifolds, for example, + is not the sign of the addition of numbers, but of a connection in general for which laws of the form a + b = b + a etc. hold. […]
>
> The most general idea of a theory of manifolds is to be a science that determinately works out the essential types of possible theories (or domains) and investigates their lawful relations to one another. All actual theories are then specializations or singularizations of corresponding theory-forms, just as all theoretically worked domains of knowledge are single manifolds. If in the theory of manifolds the theory-form in question is really carried out, then all deductive theoretical work for the construction of all actual theories of the same form is thereby done.

§ 69 builds the discipline in two steps. The science of the conditions of the possibility of theory "points beyond itself" to a complementary one, which treats "a priori of the essential kinds (forms) of theories and the associated laws of relation". From "purely categorial concepts" one can work out "manifold concepts of possible theories, pure 'forms' of theories"; the forms are "not without relation to one another"; there will be an "order of procedure" by which they are constructed and transformed "by variation of determining basic factors". This is "a last and highest goal of a theoretical science of theory in general". The sentence on method adds what the theory is good for: a problem within a theory can be solved by "going back to the categorial type, or (what is the same) the form of the theory", and from there "to a more comprehensive form".

§ 70 then proves that these "indications" are not "vague fantasies" by pointing to mathematics. The theory of manifolds, "this highest flowering of modern mathematics", is "a partial realization of the ideal". The qualifications are exact. It is "partial", and it is a realization only "in correlative conversion" (*in korrelativer Umwendung*), the phrase B adds. The ideal of § 69 was a theory of theory-forms. Mathematics works out domain-forms, manifolds. The two are correlates, as § 62's connection of truths and connection of things were correlates and § 67's meaning-categories and object-categories. The next sentence states the correlation without the parenthesis: "the objective correlate of the concept of the possible theory determined only by its form is the concept of a possible domain of knowledge in general, to be governed by a theory of such form". B's parenthesis applies the sentence back to the claim that mathematics realizes the ideal. A had said that mathematics simply was a partial realization of the theory of theories; B says it is one when converted into its correlate.

The definition of a manifold is negative on the side of matter. A manifold is a domain "determined solely" by the form of the theory that governs it, or by the forms of the connections possible among its objects. "In their matter the objects remain wholly undetermined", and the mathematician calls them "objects of thought" (*Denkobjekte*) to say so. The scare quotes on the mathematician's term mark it as a façon de parler: the objects are not thoughts; they are objects determined only as bearers of formal relations. The sign + "is not the sign of the addition of numbers, but of a connection in general". This is § 50's a + b = b + a raised one level. There the law said nothing of anyone's counting; here it says nothing of numbers. § 7's law of form and § 67's substitution of the indeterminate for the given find their completion: the forms are themselves objects of a science, and all theories of a form are dealt with when the form is.

The last paragraph restates the idea with "(or domains)" in a parenthesis that the file cannot securely assign to A or B. With or without it, the sentence that follows carries the correlation: "all actual theories are specializations or singularizations of corresponding theory-forms, just as all theoretically worked domains of knowledge are single manifolds". "Specialization" is the relation of species to genus, "singularization" of an individual case to its form, and both are said of theories and of domains alike. The consequence is a division of labour that § 71 draws: the mathematician is "not the pure theorist but only the ingenious technician" who builds theories "like a technical work of art", and the philosopher's task is insight into the essence of theory. The theory of manifolds is the summit of pure logic in content and the work of mathematicians in fact.

The understanding reaches back to the book's first page. The Preface named the logical investigation of "formal arithmetic and the theory of manifolds" as the source of its difficulties, and the generalization of formal arithmetic beyond the quantitative as the insight that the quantitative does not belong to the essence of the formal. § 60 made Leibniz, through his *Combinatoria*, "the spiritual father of the pure theory of manifolds"; the Appendix said that Bolzano lacked "the 'formal' theory of manifolds as the key". In the draft preface of 1913 Husserl tells the same story about himself: Bolzano's draft of a pure logic "lacked the idea of a purely formal mathematics or 'theory of manifolds', which I had formed for myself by material and historical studies in a purity that was at that time by no means familiar to mathematicians as it is now", and with it "any inkling of the inner unity of formal logic with pure arithmetic, pure ordinal theory, pure theory of magnitude, and so on, finally with the pure theory of manifolds and of theories" (Hua XX/1, Text Nr. 5, 298). The *Prolegomena* presents the theory of manifolds as mathematics' partial realization of an ideal. The draft of 1913 claims it as Husserl's own idea, worked out before the mathematicians had it in that purity. B's "correlative conversion" stands between the two: the mathematicians' manifolds are the correlate of the philosopher's theory-forms.

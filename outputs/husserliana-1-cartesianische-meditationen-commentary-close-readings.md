# Husserl, *Cartesianische Meditationen*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the block of the [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md) that it extends. The German is quoted from the Husserliana edition (Hua I, ed. S. Strasser) in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-1-cartesianische-meditationen-und-pariser-vortrage.md`). That file holds the whole volume, and the *Paris Lectures* (*Pariser Vorträge*) that precede the Meditations share much of their wording. Every passage here is quoted from the Meditations; where a reading compares the lecture version, it says so. The commentary cites § numbers only. Headings here add the Hua I page, taken from the page markers in the file. Odd pages are usually numbered there and even pages often are not, so a page can be off by one.

All English renderings of Husserl, and of the Latin and Greek he quotes, are working renderings. They stay close to the German so that its structure shows, and they are not taken from a published translation.

The file needs three cautions that affect more than one reading. First, italics and bold are present and can be argued from, but inside emphasis the conversion often runs words together ("*Enthüllungmeinerselbst*"). Where that happens the quotation gives what the file has, and no reading rests on a word boundary inside emphasis. Second, Husserl's spaced type survives only in fragments, so the absence of spaced emphasis is no evidence. Third, text has dropped out at a few page breaks: most of Hua I 84 and all of 86 in §20, a run-together sentence in §29 (Hua I 97), and Hua I 176 in §62. Elsewhere the page breaks that look like gaps are not: the file sets some paragraphs as single very long lines, and the text reads on across the break. Each reading says where a gap touches its passage, and no gap is filled from memory.

## §1, Hua I 44: the prototype of every beginning

Reads [§1, Paragraph 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#descartes-meditations-as-the-prototype-of-philosophical-self-reflection).

The Meditations claim Descartes' book as a "prototype" (*Urbild*): the original pattern of the meditations that every beginning philosopher must carry out for himself. The word does two jobs at once. An *Urbild* comes first in time, as the original of which later images are copies, and it is the norm those copies are measured by. Husserl's sentence claims the second through the first: a form of thinking that turned up once, in 1641, is declared binding on everyone who would philosophize. What makes it binding is not in the sentence but in the two before it. Philosophy is "a wholly personal affair" that its subject must be able to justify "at every step from his absolute insights". Insight cannot be inherited, so each philosopher must begin again, and the Meditations are the record of such a beginning. What the prototype hands on is a form of self-responsibility, not a doctrine.

> The Cartesian Meditations, then, do not mean to be a merely private affair of the philosopher Descartes, let alone a merely impressive literary form for a presentation of first philosophical groundings. Rather, they sketch the prototype of the necessary meditations of every beginning philosopher, from which alone a philosophy can originally grow.
>
> [Marginal note:] Insertion. [The text of the insertion reads:] ad 4. If it is objected that science, philosophy, grows in the collaboration of the scientific community of those who philosophize, and gains its perfection at each stage only there, Descartes' answer would presumably be: I, who philosophize alone or singly, may owe much to the others; but what counts as true for them, what they offer me as supposedly grounded by them with insight, is for me at first only an imposition. If I am to take it over, I must justify it from my own perfect insight. In this consists my theoretical autonomy — mine and that of every genuine scientist.

German:

> Die Cartesianischen Meditationen wollen also nicht eine bloße private Angelegenheit des Philosophen Descartes sein, geschweige denn eine bloße eindrucksvolle literarische Form für eine Darstellung erster philosophischer Begründungen. Sie zeichnen vielmehr das Urbild der notwendigen Meditationen eines jeden anfangenden Philosophen, aus denen allein eine Philosophie ursprünglich erwachsen kann [^2] [^2].
>
> &lt;Randbemerkung:&gt; Einlage. &lt;Der Text der Einlage lautet:&gt; ad 4. Wendet man ein, daß doch Wissenschaft, Philosophie, in Zusammenarbeit der wissenschaftlichen Gemeinschaft der Philosophierenden erwächst und in jeder Stufe da allein ihre Vollkommenheit gewinnt, so wäre darauf wohl Descartes' Antwort: den anderen mag ich, der einsam oder einzeln Philosophierende, vieles verdanken, aber was ihnen als wahr gilt, was sie mir als angeblich von ihnen einsichtig begründet darbieten, ist für mich zunächst nur eine Zumutung. Soll ich es übernehmen, so muß ich es aus eigener vollkommener Einsicht rechtfertigen. Darin besteht meine theoretische Autonomie — meine und eines jeden echten Wissenschaftlers.

*Note on the text.* The file doubles the note marker after "erwachsen kann" ("[^2] [^2]") and gives three notes numbered 1 and 2 nearby: the reference to Descartes' *Lettre de l'auteur* (numbered 1, with no marker in the text), the insertion quoted above, and two editorial notes on the English translation and on the typescript. Which note the edition attaches to this sentence cannot be settled from the file. The insertion is quoted here because it is Husserl's own and answers the sentence directly.

The sentence is built on two refusals and a replacement. The Meditations do "not" want to be a private affair, "let alone" (*geschweige denn*) a literary form. Both refusals deny that the first-person form is accidental: neither Descartes' biography nor his rhetoric explains why a first philosophy should be written as one man's six days. The replacement answers with a picture. The Meditations "sketch" (*zeichnen*) the prototype, and the verb belongs with the noun: they draw an original image. The *Paris Lectures* had the claim without the verb, in a bare apposition ("not a private affair of the philosopher Descartes, but the prototype of the necessary meditations of every philosopher beginning anew", src L408). The Meditations add the drawing and the final clause.

"Necessary" (*notwendigen*) qualifies the meditations, not the prototype. What is necessary is that every beginner meditate. That Descartes' meditations are the pattern for doing so is what *Urbild* asserts, and the inference is weaker than the "then" (*also*) that opens the sentence makes it look. The preceding sentences show that philosophy must begin in each subject's own insight. They do not show that the beginning must take the Cartesian form of overthrow and reconstruction. The word carries what the argument leaves open.

The final clause gives the reason for the word "alone" (*allein*): only from such meditations can a philosophy "originally grow" (*ursprünglich erwachsen*). The verb is organic, and it is chosen against the picture Husserl attacks two pages later. At §3 (Hua I 48–49) Descartes' "fateful prejudice" is that universal science must have "the form of a deductive system" resting on an "axiomatic foundation". A philosophy that grows from meditation is not built down from an axiom. The *Urbild* that §1 praises is thus already set against the doctrine that §3 rejects: Husserl keeps the form of the Meditations and drops their architecture.

The insertion tests the clause on the obvious objection, that science grows (*erwächst*, the same verb) in a community. Husserl answers in Descartes' name, but hedged: the answer "would presumably be" (*wäre … wohl*). The reply grants debt and denies validity. The solitary thinker "may owe much to the others", but what they offer is "at first only an imposition" (*zunächst nur eine Zumutung*), a claim on his assent that he must redeem from his own insight. The prototype is therefore a rule of justification, not of origin. Nobody is asked to forget what he learned, only to hold none of it as valid until he has seen it himself. That distinction keeps *Urbild* from being a merely historical claim. It also explains the change from the lectures' "every philosopher beginning anew" (*jedes neuanfangenden Philosophen*) to the Meditations' "every beginning philosopher" (*eines jeden anfangenden Philosophen*). One does not begin philosophy once and then possess it: whoever justifies his claims is, in this respect, always a beginner.

The work keeps the word "beginning" and changes its subject. §2 asks to "renew the radicalism of the beginning philosopher" (Hua I 47). §3 opens "We begin anew, each for himself and in himself" (*jeder für sich und in sich*, Hua I 48). §13 tells the reader not to be frightened by solipsism "as beginning philosophers" (Hua I 69). At §64 the adjective has passed from the philosopher to the philosophy: what remains is to branch out transcendental phenomenology "as it grows as beginning philosophy" (*wie sie als anfangende Philosophie erwächst*, Hua I 178–179), with §1's verb. The same paragraph turns the title into a common noun: "a radical and universal continuation of Cartesian meditations, or, what is the same, of a universal self-knowledge, is philosophy itself" (Hua I 182–183). The *Urbild* has become the form of philosophy. But §64 also corrects the insertion's "alone or singly". Self-knowledge is "first monadic, and then intermonadic". The solitary meditator whom the insertion defends turns out, after the Fifth Meditation, to be a first stage. The solipsism objection of §42 is the price of §1's prototype, and §64 states the prototype once more in a form that has paid it.

One earlier text in the corpus shows how long Husserl had read the Cartesian demand as universal. In the lectures *Erste Philosophie* (1923/24), the meditations begin with "that overthrow which Descartes demanded 'once in life' of everyone" (*den Descartes „einmal im Leben“ von allen forderte*, Hua VIII 23), with Descartes' Latin about those who seriously strive for a sound mind. (The file is on the corpus's list of texts to check before quoting, and gives the Latin as "*qui serie student*".) The quotation marks around "once in life" there, as in §1 of the Meditations (src L1156, where the *Paris Lectures* had none), mark the phrase as Descartes' own. The prototype claim inherits his demand for one overthrow in each life and turns it into a demand on each insight.

## §2, Hua I 46: the eternal sense of a tendency

Reads [§2, Paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#the-necessity-of-a-radical-new-beginning-in-philosophy).

The "eternal sense" (*Ewigkeitssinn*) of §2 is not a timeless content in Descartes' thoughts. It belongs to a historical movement: the turn from naive objectivism to transcendental subjectivism, which since Descartes has been striving toward a final form it has not reached. To say that this tendency carries an eternal sense is to say that it is a task, set by history and binding on whoever inherits it. The passage thus grounds the renewal of the Meditations not in their doctrine, which §1 has already given up, but in a direction of history that Husserl claims to read.

> On the other hand, it weighs heavily that the Meditations made epoch in philosophy in a wholly unique sense, and precisely through their return to the pure *ego cogito*. Indeed, Descartes inaugurates a completely new kind of philosophy: changing its entire style, it takes a radical turn from naive objectivism to transcendental subjectivism, which in ever new and yet ever inadequate attempts seems to strive toward a necessary final form. Should this ongoing tendency, then, not carry an eternal sense within it, for us a great task imposed on us by history itself, in which we are all called to collaborate?

German:

> Andererseits wiegt es doch schwer, daß die Meditationen in einem ganz einzigen Sinne in der Philosophie Epoche gemacht haben, und zwar gerade durch ihren Rückgang auf das reine *ego cogito*. In der Tat, Descartes inauguriert eine völlig neuartige Philosophie: ihren gesamten Stil ändernd, nimmt sie eine radikale Wendung vom naiven Objektivismus zum transzendentalen Subjektivismus, der in immer neuen und doch immer unzulänglichen Versuchen auf eine notwendige Endgestalt hinzustreben scheint. Sollte also diese fortgehende Tendenz nicht einen Ewigkeitssinn in sich tragen, für uns eine große, von der Geschichte selbst uns auferlegte Aufgabe, an der mitzuarbeiten wir alle berufen sind?

Three compounds with "eternity" (*Ewigkeit-*) frame the passage, and they are not interchangeable. §1 spoke of the Cartesian "motifs" that have an "eternal significance" (*Ewigkeitsbedeutung*, Hua I 43), and §2 opens by asking whether it is worth "tracing an eternal significance of these thoughts" (Hua I 45–46). Significance attaches to thoughts, as something one finds in them. Here the compound changes to "eternal sense" (*Ewigkeitssinn*), and its bearer changes with it: not the thoughts but "this ongoing tendency" (*diese fortgehende Tendenz*). At the end of §2 the renaissance is to disclose "the eternal values" (*Ewigkeitswerte*) that "spring" from the return to the ego (Hua I 48). The sequence runs from what Descartes thought, to the movement his thinking started, to what is still to come from it. The commentary renders the first two as "eternal significance" and "eternal meaning" without marking the shift. The shift is the passage's point: what lasts is not a content but a direction.

The syntax shows where the direction lies. The relative clause "which … seems to strive" (*der … hinzustreben scheint*) is masculine, so its subject is "transcendental subjectivism", not the new philosophy. The striving belongs to the end point of the turn. Subjectivism has been realized "in ever new and yet ever inadequate attempts" (*in immer neuen und doch immer unzulänglichen Versuchen*), so every attempt so far has failed, and the failures are taken as evidence of a goal. That is the weakest step, and the wording hedges it twice. The striving only "seems" (*scheint*) to aim at a final form. And the conclusion is put as a question: "Should this tendency, then, not carry an eternal sense?" The "then" (*also*) claims an inference that the question form declines to assert. What Husserl has shown is a series of attempts; the "necessary final form" (*notwendige Endgestalt*) they are said to seek is read into them from the end that phenomenology itself proposes.

The phrase "made epoch" (*Epoche gemacht*) is the ordinary German idiom for marking an era. The *Paris Lectures* had written of "the well-known epoch-making return to the ego" (*der bekannte epoche-machende Rückgang*, src L410). The Meditations write the method in Greek letters, ἐποχή, from §8 on, and keep the idiom in German, so the file gives the two words different scripts. Whether Husserl hears the method in the idiom cannot be settled from the text, and nothing in the argument depends on it. What does depend on the wording is "precisely through their return to the pure *ego cogito*" (*gerade durch ihren Rückgang*): the Meditations are epochal through one move, the return, and not through what Descartes built on it. §1 has already given up "nearly the entire known doctrinal content" (Hua I 43). The eternal sense has to be found somewhere other than in doctrine, and §2 places it in the tendency.

The last clause turns the tendency into a duty. The eternal sense is "for us a great task" (*Aufgabe*), "imposed on us by history itself", "in which we are all called to collaborate" (*an der mitzuarbeiten wir alle berufen sind*). Collaboration stands oddly beside §1, whose insertion defended the philosopher who meditates "alone or singly" against the claim that science grows in community. The tension is real, and it is the work's own. The beginner must justify everything from his own insight, yet the task he takes up is collective and historical. §2 goes straight on to the "fragmentation" of present philosophy, where philosophers meet at congresses but their philosophies do not, and they lack "the unity of a spiritual space in which they could be for one another" (Hua I 47). The collaboration that history imposes does not yet exist. The Fifth Meditation's community of monads is, among other things, the transcendental account of the space this sentence finds missing.

The passage sets the measure for the end of the work. The "final form" toward which subjectivism strives is named at §64: the idea of universal philosophy is realized "not as a universal system of deductive theory", but as "a system of phenomenological disciplines" on the ground "not of the axiom *ego cogito*, but of a universal self-reflection" (Hua I 182). The "naive objectivism" of §2 returns there as the naivety of daily life and of positive science, which are "naiveties of a higher level" (Hua I 180). And the eternal significance returns in a new form: "The Delphic word γνῶθι σεαυτόν [know thyself] has gained a new meaning" (*eine neue Bedeutung*, Hua I 183). The old word, like Descartes' Meditations, lasts by changing what it means. That is the model of eternity §2 has in view: not a doctrine kept intact, but a sense that each renewal must recover and alter.

The *Crisis* makes explicit what §2 leaves as a question, and names its risk. There Husserl calls his reading of history "teleological", in quotation marks, and says that only the later presentations are to "prove the right" of this view of history and of its methodical function; he calls its result "a radical transcendental subjectivism" and welcomes the reader's scepticism (*Krisis* §26, Hua VI 101). Later he says that the thoughts of earlier times were brought to a "resonance" in which their "orientation, as germinal forms, toward a final form" (*Ausgerichtetheit als Keimgestalten auf eine Endgestalt*) became evident, and he adds at once that "we ourselves" belong to the same unity of historicity (*Krisis* §56, Hua VI 196). §2 of the Meditations already uses the vocabulary of the final form, but it covers the teleology with "seems" and a question. The *Crisis* states it, and says what it owes.

## §3, Hua I 48–49: the fateful prejudice

Reads [§3, Paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#the-cartesian-overthrow-and-the-guiding-teleological-idea-of-a-grounding-of-science).

Husserl's first correction of Descartes concerns not a doctrine but a shape. Descartes took over, before his overthrow began, an ideal of science drawn from geometry: universal science is a deductive system resting on axioms, and the self-certainty of the ego is its deepest axiom. Husserl calls this a "fateful prejudice" (*verhängnisvolles Vorurteil*). It is a prejudice in the strict sense of a judgment made before the inquiry that should have decided it, and it is fateful because it shaped the centuries after Descartes as well as his own Meditations. The beginner who repeats the Meditations must therefore not adopt it. He keeps the goal of an absolutely grounded science but holds no ideal of what such a science must look like. That separation of goal from form is what the passage establishes, and the work's last section depends on it.

> Descartes himself had in advance an ideal of science, that of geometry, or of mathematical natural science. As a fateful prejudice it determines the centuries, and it determines too, critically unexamined, the Meditations themselves. It was for Descartes a matter of course from the outset that universal science has the form of a deductive system, in which the whole edifice must rest on an axiomatic foundation that grounds the deduction. A role similar to that of the geometrical axioms in geometry is played for Descartes, with regard to universal science, by the axiom of the absolute self-certainty of the ego, together with the axiomatic principles innate in this ego — except that this axiomatic foundation lies still deeper than that of geometry and is called to take part in the ultimate grounding of geometry too.
>
> None of this may determine us. As beginners we have as yet no normative ideal of science in force, and only insofar as we create it anew for ourselves can we have it.

German:

> Descartes selbst hatte im voraus ein Wissenschaftsideal, das der Geometrie, bzw. der mathematischen Naturwissenschaft. Es bestimmt als ein verhängnisvolles Vorurteil die Jahrhunderte und bestimmt auch, kritisch unerwogen, die Meditationen selbst. Es war für Descartes vorweg eine Selbstverständlichkeit, daß die universale Wissenschaft die Gestalt eines deduktiven Systems habe, bei dem der ganze Bau auf einem die Deduktion begründenden axiomatischen Fundament ruhen muß. Eine ähnliche Rolle wie in der Geometrie die geometrischen Axiome hat für Descartes in Hinsicht auf die Universalwissenschaft das Axiom der absoluten Selbstgewißheit des ego mit den diesem ego eingeborenen axiomatischen Prinzipien — nur daß dieses axiomatische Fundament noch tiefer liegt als das der Geometrie und dazu berufen ist, auch an ihrer letzten Begründung mitzuwirken.
>
> Das alles darf uns nicht bestimmen. Wir haben als Anfangende noch kein normatives Wissenschaftsideal in Geltung und nur soweit wir es uns neu schaffen, können wir es haben.

*Note on the text.* A page break (Hua I 48/49) falls between "verhängnisvolles" and "Vorurteil", but the sentence is complete in the file. Earlier in the same paragraph the edition itself notes that "a word seems to be missing" after "echter" (genuine), where Husserl says we have no given science as an example "of such genuine [science]". That gap does not touch the passage.

The charge is carried by the vocabulary of "before". Descartes had the ideal "in advance" (*im voraus*). It was a matter of course "from the outset" (*vorweg*). It determines the Meditations "critically unexamined" (*kritisch unerwogen*). Each phrase says the same thing from a different side: the ideal was in place before the overthrow, so the overthrow could not reach it. Descartes doubted every science but not the form science must take. Hence the double verb. The prejudice "determines the centuries and determines too … the Meditations themselves" (*bestimmt … die Jahrhunderte und bestimmt auch … die Meditationen selbst*): the history that §2 called epoch-making is here the history of a prejudice that the epoch-making book itself obeyed. Husserl then answers with the same verb, negated: "None of this may determine us" (*Das alles darf uns nicht bestimmen*). The correction is phrased as a refusal to be determined, not as a counter-thesis.

The axiom analogy is set out with care. Descartes' ego plays "a role similar to" (*eine ähnliche Rolle wie*) that of the axioms in geometry, "except that" (*nur daß*) it lies deeper and is meant to ground geometry too. The exception is what makes the prejudice fateful. If the ego is the axiom of all axioms, then the return to the ego, which §2 praised as the one epoch-making move, is from the start bent into a premise for deduction. What Descartes found and what he did with it are tied together by the form of science he assumed. Husserl's whole strategy in the First Meditation is to cut that tie: to keep the return and drop the axiom.

The second paragraph states what the beginner may have instead, and its syntax is cautious. "As beginners we have as yet no normative ideal of science in force" (*noch kein normatives Wissenschaftsideal in Geltung*). "In force" (*in Geltung*) is the vocabulary the reduction will use for what is suspended: the ideal is not refuted but put out of validity, like the sciences in §3's first sentence. And the beginner can have an ideal "only insofar as we create it anew for ourselves" (*nur soweit wir es uns neu schaffen*). The passage then asserts, against its own radicalism, that the goal survives: "But the general aim of an absolute grounding of science we do not therefore give up" (Hua I 49). The goal is kept in the form of a "presumption" (*Präsumption*) to which the meditator "tentatively" yields. Whether absolute grounding can be separated from the deductive form, so that one can doubt the form and keep the goal, is not shown here. It is the wager of the whole work, and §64 claims to have won it.

The prejudice returns twice before the end, and the second return changes its subject. In §10 Husserl calls it the prejudice "deriving from admiration for mathematical natural science and determining us ourselves as an old inheritance" (*uns selbst als alte Erbschaft bestimmende Vorurteil*, Hua I 63). §3 said it "may not determine us". §10 concedes that it does, as an inheritance, and turns the prohibition into a task of keeping it "at arm's length" (*vom Leibe halten*). The prejudice now has a precise object: the belief that under the title *ego cogito* we have "an apodictic axiom" that is to supply, with other hypotheses, "the foundation for a deductively explanatory science of the world", a science *ordine geometrico* [in the geometrical order]. The third return is §64's reversal. Universal philosophy is realized "quite otherwise than Descartes and his age, guided by the new natural science, thought it": "not as a universal system of deductive theory, as though everything that is stood in the unity of a calculation", but as a system of phenomenological disciplines on the ground "not of the axiom *ego cogito*, but of a universal self-reflection" (Hua I 182). The three key words of §3 come back in order: deductive system, the axiom, the ego. The goal of §3, a universal science from absolute grounding, is kept, and its form is replaced.

The *Crisis* widens the target. There Husserl describes the "physicalistic (or mathematizing) idea of philosophy, according to which the world in its full concretion bears within itself an objectively true being in the form of the *ordo geometricus*" (*Krisis* §56, Hua VI 195). In the Meditations the geometrical order is a prejudice about the form of science; in the *Crisis* it has become a prejudice about the being of the world. The Meditations' correction is methodical, and it leaves the world's mathematical form for the Fourth and Fifth Meditations to treat as a constituted sense. The *Crisis* names it as the root error of modern rationalism. §3 is the narrower and earlier form of a charge Husserl later carried into ontology.

## §6, Hua I 55–56: apodicticity as the inconceivability of non-being

Reads [§6, Paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#differentiations-of-evidence-the-philosophical-demand-for-an-apodictic-and-intrinsically-first-evidence).

Apodicticity, in §6, is not a higher degree of certainty. Every evidence is already certain: it grasps its object as "it itself" and so excludes doubt. Apodicticity is a different kind of perfection, and it is not found in the evidence alone. It is what a critical reflection on the evidence discloses, namely that the non-being of what is evident cannot be thought through at all. Because it is defined by that reflection and not by the fullness of what is given, apodicticity can belong to evidence that is inadequate, that is, to evidence that leaves much of its object unseen. That split between apodicticity and adequacy is the instrument of the next three sections, and §63 returns to the reflection that defines it.

> Although this idea [of adequate evidence] also constantly guides the scientist's aim, yet for him (as we grasp by living our way into it) a different kind of perfection of evidence has a higher dignity, namely that of *apodicticity*; it can occur, as the case may be, even in inadequate evidences. It is an absolute indubitability in a wholly definite and peculiar sense, the one that the scientist demands of all *principles*, and whose superior value shows itself in his effort to ground once again, and at a higher level, groundings that are in and of themselves already evident, by going back to principles, and so to procure for them the highest dignity of apodicticity. Its basic character is to be characterized as follows:
>
> Every evidence is a self-grasping of something that is, or is so, in the mode "it itself", in complete certainty of this being, a certainty that therefore excludes every doubt. It does not exclude the possibility that the evident might later become doubtful, that the being might turn out to be semblance, of which sensory experience gives us examples. This open possibility of becoming doubtful, or of possible non-being *in spite of the evidence*, can also be recognized in advance at any time by a critical reflection on what the evidence achieves. An apodictic evidence, however, has the distinctive property that it is not merely certainty of the being of the matters or states of affairs evident in it, but at the same time discloses itself, through a critical reflection, as the sheer inconceivability of their non-being; that it therefore excludes in advance every imaginable doubt as objectless. Here the evidence of that critical reflection, and so also the evidence of the being of this inconceivability of the non-being of what lies before us in evident certainty, is again of this apodictic dignity, and so in every critical reflection of a higher level.

German:

> Obschon diese Idee das Absehen des Wissenschaftlers auch beständig leitet, so hat für ihn doch (wie wir durch jenes Einleben in dasselbe erfassen) eine andersartige Vollkommenheit der Evidenz eine höhere Dignität, nämlich die der *Apodiktizität* ; sie kann eventuell auch an inadäquaten Evidenzen auftreten. Es ist eine absolute Zweifellosigkeit in einem ganz bestimmten und eigentümlichen Sinn, diejenige, die der Wissenschaftler allen *Prinzipien* zumutet und deren überlegener Wert sich in seinem Bestreben bekundet, an und für sich schon evidente Begründungen durch Rückgang auf Prinzipien nochmals und in höherer Stufe zu begründen und ihnen dadurch die höchste Dignität der Apodiktizität zu verschaffen. Der Grundcharakter derselben ist so zu charakterisieren:
>
> Jede Evidenz ist Selbsterfassung eines Seienden oder Soseienden in dem Modus „es selbst” in völliger Gewißheit dieses Seins, die also jeden Zweifel ausschließt. Nicht schließt sie aus die Möglichkeit, daß das Evidente nachher zweifelhaft werden könnte, das Sein als Schein sich herausstellen könnte, wofür ja die sinnliche Erfahrung uns Beispiele liefert. Diese offene Möglichkeit des Zweifelhaftwerdens bzw. des möglichen Nichtseins *trotzder Evidenz* ist durch eine kritische Reflexion auf ihre Leistung auch jederzeit im voraus zu erkennen. Eine apodiktische Evidenz aber hat die ausgezeichnete Eigenheit, daß sie nicht bloß überhaupt Seinsgewißheit der in ihr evidenten Sachen oder Sachverhalte ist, sondern sich durch eine kritische Reflexion zugleich als schlechthinige Unausdenkbarkeit des Nichtseins derselben enthüllt; daß sie also im voraus jeden vorstellbaren Zweifel als gegenstandslos ausschließt. Dabei ist die Evidenz jener kritischen Reflexion, also auch die vom Sein dieser Unausdenkbarkeit des Nichtseins &lt;des&gt; in evidenter Gewißheit Vorliegenden, abermals von dieser apodiktischen Dignität, und so in jeder kritischen Reflexion höherer Stufe.

*Note on the text.* The file runs "trotz der" together inside the italics ("*trotzder Evidenz*"); the angle brackets around "des" mark an editorial supplement. Neither affects the reading.

The definition turns on two verbs of exclusion that look alike and are not. Every evidence "excludes every doubt" (*jeden Zweifel ausschließt*). The apodictic evidence "excludes in advance every imaginable doubt as objectless" (*im voraus jeden vorstellbaren Zweifel als gegenstandslos ausschließt*). Ordinary evidence excludes the doubts that are actually there: while I see the house, I do not doubt it. Apodictic evidence excludes the doubts that could be imagined, and it excludes them "in advance", before any arises, by showing that they would have nothing to be about. "In advance" (*im voraus*) was the mark of Descartes' prejudice in §3. Here it marks the one kind of evidence that may legitimately run ahead of experience. The word that separates them is "objectless" (*gegenstandslos*): a doubt about the apodictically evident has no object to bite on, because the non-being it would have to entertain cannot be thought.

That is what "sheer inconceivability" (*schlechthinige Unausdenkbarkeit*) says. *Ausdenken* is to think something out to the end, to imagine it through. The non-being of the apodictically evident cannot be thought through: one can say the words "the ego is not", but one cannot carry out the thought. The criterion is a test of conceiving, and the commentary's "inconceivability" is right as far as it goes. The German adds the image of an attempt that breaks off before it is finished, which is how §9 will use the criterion on the "I am".

The syntax places apodicticity in the reflection, not in the evidence. The apodictic evidence "discloses itself, through a critical reflection" (*sich durch eine kritische Reflexion … enthüllt*). The same critical reflection, applied to ordinary evidence, shows "in advance at any time" that it might be semblance. So the reflection does the sorting, and apodicticity is the result of a test that every evidence must pass. That is why it can occur in inadequate evidence. Adequacy is measured by fullness of content, and the reflection asks only whether non-being can be thought. The last sentence then turns the test on itself: the evidence of the reflection is "again of this apodictic dignity, and so in every critical reflection of a higher level" (*abermals … und so*). This is asserted, not shown. If the apodicticity of an evidence is disclosed only by a reflection, the apodicticity of that reflection would seem to need a further one. "Again" and "and so" cover the step. The sentence reads as a regress that is declared harmless, not as a ground.

The wording of the definition is not Husserl's alone. The edition's text-critical apparatus records that the lines from "An apodictic evidence, however" to the end of the paragraph (Hua I 56, lines 14–24) "rest on improvements by Eugen Fink", and it prints Husserl's first version: an apodictic evidence has the distinctive property "that the certainty of being of what is seen in it discloses itself through a critical reflection at the same time as sheer inconceivability of non-being, and so also of being-able-to-be-doubtful" (*daß die Seinsgewißheit des in ihr Erschauten sich durch eine kritische Reflexion zugleich als schlechthinnige Unausdenkbarkeit des Nichtseins, also auch des Zweifelhaft-seinkönnens enthüllt*). Two phrases this reading has leaned on are Fink's: the contrast "not merely certainty of being … but", and the clause that excludes "in advance every imaginable doubt as objectless". In Husserl's version the certainty itself discloses its inconceivable opposite; Fink set apodicticity against ordinary certainty and added the exclusion in advance. The iteration was Husserl's own, and wider: the reflection keeps its apodictic dignity "at every critical level of reflection, as also with regard to apodictic reflections that are always possible". The regress §63 has to defuse was in the first draft. Fink sharpened the contrast with ordinary evidence, not the reflexive structure.

The surrounding words keep the scientist in view. Apodicticity has a "higher dignity" (*Dignität*), a rank and not a quantity. The scientist "demands" it of all principles (*zumutet*), the verb whose noun, an "imposition" (*Zumutung*), §1's insertion used for what others offer the solitary thinker. And the account is won "by living our way into" the scientist's aim (*durch jenes Einleben*), the method of §4, which the commentary's block leaves out. The definition is thus offered as the explication of what science already wants, not as a new stipulation. It is the hinge of the First Meditation. §6 closes by asking whether there are evidences that are "in themselves first" and apodictic, and adds that if they are inadequate, they must at least have "a recognizable apodictic content" (Hua I 57). §7 then denies apodicticity to the evidence of the world (Hua I 57–58). §9 tests the "I am" and finds what §6 allowed for, an apodictic core in an inadequate evidence (Hua I 62).

The work redeems the iteration of the last sentence only at its end. §63 admits that the Meditations "lost sight of" the demand for apodictic knowledge without giving it up, and that the first phenomenology is still burdened with a "naivety" of its own, "apodictic naivety" (*der apodiktischen Naivität*). Its last task is a "self-critique" that would determine the "scope and limits, but also modes, of apodicticity". Then comes the answer to §6's "and so": "with the essential self-reference of phenomenology, this critique too demands a critique. But in this respect there are no infinite regresses burdened with any difficulties or even countersenses, despite the evident possibility of iterable transcendental reflections and critiques" (Hua I 178). §6 asserted that each higher reflection keeps the dignity of the lower. §63 concedes that the iteration is real and denies that it is vicious, and still does not show why. The definition of apodicticity has a reflexive structure that the work names twice, at its beginning and at its end, and does not settle.

## §7, Hua I 57–58: the world as a validity-phenomenon

Reads [§7, Paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#the-evidence-for-the-existence-of-the-world-is-not-apodictic-its-inclusion-in-the-cartesian-overthrow).

At the end of §7 the being of the world stops being "a self-evident fact" and becomes "only a validity-phenomenon" (*nur ein Geltungsphänomen*). The world is not denied, and nothing is said about whether it exists. What changes is its standing for the meditator: it is now something that counts as existing, a claim whose validity can be looked at, instead of the ground on which every look is taken. The overthrow, which §3 applied to the sciences, here reaches what the sciences stand on. The passage earns this extension by less than its section title promises. It does not show that the evidence of the world is not apodictic. It shows only that the meditator may not take it as apodictic without a critique he has not yet carried out.

> As regards the first point, the universal sensory experience in which the world is constantly pregiven to us is obviously not to be claimed, just like that, as an apodictic evidence, one that would therefore absolutely exclude the possibility of its becoming doubtful whether the world really is, or the possibility of its non-being. Not only can something singly experienced suffer devaluation as sensory semblance; the whole coherence of experience that can be surveyed as a unity at any time can also prove to be semblance, under the title *coherent dream*.
>
> We need not already claim this reference to possible and actual reversals of evidence as a sufficient critique of the evidence, and see in it a full proof of the conceivability of a non-being of the world in spite of its constantly being experienced. We retain only this much: that for the purposes of a radical grounding of science the evidence of world-experience would in any case first need a critique of its validity and scope, and that we may therefore not claim it, unquestioned, as immediately apodictic. Accordingly it is not enough to put all the sciences pregiven to us out of validity, to treat them as pre-judgments inadmissible for us. Their universal ground too, that of the world of experience, we must deprive of its naive validity. The being of the world on the basis of natural experiential evidence may no longer be for us a self-evident fact, but itself only a validity-phenomenon.

German:

> Was das eine anbelangt, so ist die universale sinnliche Erfahrung, in der uns die Welt beständig vorgegeben ist, offenbar nicht so ohne weiteres als eine apodiktische Evidenz in Anspruch zu nehmen, die also die Möglichkeit eines Zweifelhaftwerdens, ob die Welt wirklich sei, bzw. die ihres Nichtseins absolut ausschließen würde. Nicht nur daß Einzelerfahrenes die Entwertung als Sinnenschein erleiden kann, auch der jeweils ganze, einheitlich überschaubare Erfahrungszusammenhang kann sich als Schein erweisen, unter dem Titel *zusammenhängender Traum*.
>
> Den Hinweis auf diese möglichen und vorkommenden Umschläge der Evidenz brauchen wir nicht schon als eine hinreichende Kritik der Evidenz in Anspruch zu nehmen und darin einen vollen Beweis für die Denkmöglichkeit eines Nichtseins der Welt trotz ihres beständigen Erfahrenseins zu sehen. Wir behalten nur so viel, daß die Evidenz der Welterfahrung zu Zwecken einer radikalen Wissenschaftsbegründung jedenfalls erst einer Kritik ihrer Gültigkeit und Tragweite bedürfte, daß wir sie also nicht fraglos als unmittelbar apodiktisch in Anspruch nehmen dürfen. Es genügt demgemäß nicht, alle uns vorgegebenen Wissenschaften außer Geltung zu setzen, sie als für uns unzulässige Vor-Urteile zu behandeln. Auch ihren universalen Boden, den der Erfahrungswelt, müssen wir der naiven Geltung berauben. Das Sein der Welt auf Grund der natürlichen Erfahrungsevidenz darf nicht mehr für uns selbstverständliche Tatsache sein, sondern selbst nur ein Geltungsphänomen.

The passage concludes more than its premises carry, and its wording records the gap. The section's title states a result: "The evidence for the existence of the world not apodictic". The text claims less. The first paragraph says the world's evidence is not to be claimed as apodictic "just like that" (*nicht so ohne weiteres*). The second paragraph withdraws even the dream argument as proof: the reference to reversals of evidence need not be taken "as a sufficient critique" or "a full proof of the conceivability of a non-being of the world". "We retain only this much" (*Wir behalten nur so viel*): the evidence "would … first need a critique" (*erst einer Kritik … bedürfte*, a subjunctive), so we "may not claim it, unquestioned, as immediately apodictic". The ground of the suspension is not a demonstrated weakness of world-experience but a rule of method: whatever has not passed §6's test may not serve as a first evidence. "Conceivability" (*Denkmöglichkeit*) is the exact counterpart of §6's "inconceivability" (*Unausdenkbarkeit*). Husserl declines to prove that the world's non-being can be thought, and suspends the world anyway. This is weaker than Descartes' dream argument and also safer. Husserl never needs to show that the world might not exist, only that its existence has not yet been certified.

The suspension is then described in a single word family. The sciences are "put out of validity" (*außer Geltung zu setzen*). The ground is "deprived of its naive validity" (*der naiven Geltung berauben*). What remains is a "validity-phenomenon" (*Geltungsphänomen*). Validity (*Geltung*) is the term that carries the whole operation, and it is chosen because it lets being be suspended without being denied. A judgment that is put out of validity has not been refuted. It is still there, but it no longer counts. The commentary renders the first phrase "set out of force" and drops the second object. The source treats the sciences "as pre-judgments inadmissible for us" (*als für uns unzulässige Vor-Urteile*), and the hyphen matters. It splits the everyday "prejudice" (*Vorurteil*) into "pre-" and "judgment". A science is not called biased. It is called a judgment made before the inquiry that must decide it, which is the sense §3 gave Descartes' "fateful prejudice". Every science, however well founded on its own terms, is in this sense a judgment in advance for a meditator who has not yet laid the ground. The hyphen marks the shift from a moral word to a temporal one.

"Only" (*nur*) in the last sentence must not be read as a demotion. The world's being "may no longer be for us a self-evident fact, but itself only a validity-phenomenon". The sentence contrasts two ways of being given, not two degrees of reality. A self-evident fact (*selbstverständliche Tatsache*) is one that no one would think to state, as §7's first paragraph had said of the world. A validity-phenomenon is something that appears as valid, so its validity can become a theme. "Itself" (*selbst*) adds that the ground is now treated as the sciences were.

§8 converts the negative result into a positive one, and its wording completes this passage. The world is now "instead of existing, only a phenomenon of being" (*statt seiend nur Seinsphänomen*), but this phenomenon "as my phenomenon is not nothing" (Hua I 59). What is suspended is retained "in the validity-modification *mere phenomena*" (*in der Geltungsmodifikation bloße Phänomene*, Hua I 60). And the decisive sentence equates the two words of §7's conclusion: "Everything worldly … is for me — that is, is valid for me" (*ist für mich — das heißt gilt für mich*, Hua I 60). To be, for the meditator, is to be valid for him. The validity-phenomenon of §7 is therefore not a substitute for the world's being. It is what that being has always been for the one who experiences it. §8 then calls the natural ground of being "secondary in its validity of being" (*in seiner Seinsgeltung sekundär*, Hua I 61). The word that first suspended the world becomes the word that orders it beneath the ego. This is how the overthrow of §7 prepares §41's "transcendental idealism": the world's being is not reduced to consciousness by an argument about existence, but redescribed, from the start, in the vocabulary of validity.

## §8, Hua I 60–61: the names of the epochē

Reads [§8, Paragraphs 5 and 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#the-ego-cogito-as-transcendental-subjectivity).

The *epochē* (ἐποχή, abstention) and the transcendental-phenomenological reduction are one method under two names. The *epochē* names it by what the meditator does: he withholds every position-taking toward the world, so that the world counts for him only as a phenomenon. The reduction names it by where the method leads: back to the ego and its *cogitationes* as the ground on which the world's validity rests. §8 introduces the first name through a chain of synonyms and derives the second from the first with a "therefore". The order matters. The abstention comes first and is described as something one does in freedom. The ground it leads to is named only after the meditator has seen what remains.

> This universal putting out of validity ("inhibiting", "putting out of play") of all position-takings toward the pregiven objective world, and so first of all of the position-takings on being (concerning being, semblance, possibly-being, presumably-, probably-being and the like) — or, as is also commonly said, this *phenomenological ἐποχή* or this *bracketing* of the objective world — therefore does not set us before a nothing. What rather becomes our own, and precisely through it, or more distinctly: what becomes mine, the meditator's, through it, is my pure life with all its pure experiences and all its pure "Gemeinheiten", the universe of *phenomena* in the sense of phenomenology. The *ἐποχή* is, as one can also say, the radical and universal method by which I grasp myself purely as I, and with my own pure life of consciousness, in which and through which the whole objective world is for me, and is just as it is for me. Everything worldly, all spatio-temporal being, is for me — that is, is valid for me — and that through my experiencing it, perceiving it, remembering it, thinking of it in any way, judging it, valuing it, desiring it, and so on. All this Descartes, as is well known, designates under the title *cogito*. The world is for me nothing at all other than the world that is conscious and valid for me in such a *cogito*. Its whole sense, universal and special, and its validity of being it has exclusively from such *cogitationes*. In them my whole world-life runs its course, to which my scientifically inquiring and grounding life also belongs. I cannot live, experience, think, value and act my way into any world other than the one that has sense and validity in me and from me myself. If I place myself above this whole life and abstain from every performance of any belief in being that takes *the world* straightforwardly as existing — if I direct my gaze exclusively at this life itself, as consciousness of *the world* — then I win myself as the pure ego with the pure stream of my *cogitationes*.
>
> So in fact the being of the pure ego and its *cogitationes*, as a being prior in itself, precedes the natural being of the world, the world of which I ever speak and can speak. The natural ground of being is secondary in its validity of being; it constantly presupposes the transcendental. The phenomenological fundamental method of the transcendental ἐποχή, insofar as it leads back to this ground, is therefore called transcendental-phenomenological reduction.

Was uns vielmehr, und gerade dadurch, zueigen wird, oder deutlicher: was mir, dem Meditierenden, dadurch zueigen wird, ist mein reines Leben mit all seinen reinen Erlebnissen und all seinen reinen Gemeinheiten, das Universum der *Phänomene* im Sinn der Phänomenologie. Die *ἐποχή* ist, so kann auch gesagt werden, die radikale und universale Methode, wodurch ich mich als Ich rein fasse, und mit dem eigenen reinen Bewußtseinsleben, in dem und durch das die gesamte objektive Welt für mich ist, und so, wie sie eben für mich ist. *Note on the text.* The file reads "all seinen reinen Gemeinheiten". In current German *Gemeinheiten* means "meannesses"; the commentary renders it "pure generalities", which the context supports, but the file cannot confirm whether the print has *Allgemeinheiten*. The rendering above leaves the word in German, and nothing below rests on it. The file also places the note markers of Ingarden's objections (`[^5]`) after "cogitationes", "Geltung hat" and "Reduktion"; they are omitted from the quotation. The sentence "Stelle ich mich … *cogitationes*" runs across the page break to Hua I 61 without loss.

The term is introduced as the last of four names, and each name carries a different picture. "Putting out of validity" (*Außergeltungsetzen*) is Husserl's own coinage and the plain description: a validity is suspended. The two words in quotation marks, "inhibiting" (*Inhibieren*) and "putting out of play" (*Außerspielsetzen*), are set off as borrowed labels, one physiological, one from games. Then comes "as is also commonly said" (*wie auch gesagt zu werden pflegt*), which introduces the Greek and the "bracketing" (*Einklammern*) as the established names. They are established because Husserl established them. *Ideas I* had called the operation "putting out of action", "switching off" and "bracketing", all in quotation marks, and had taken up the Greek word for "a certain abstention from judgment that is compatible with an unshaken conviction of the truth" (*Ideen I* §§31–32, Hua III/1 63–65). The Meditations speak of Husserl's own terminology as a usage, so the Greek word enters without a definition and is defined by the synonyms that come before it. *Ideas I* had also said that the image of bracketing "fits the sphere of objects better", and the talk of putting out of action fits "the sphere of acts" (Hua III/1 64). §8 keeps that division exactly: the putting out of validity concerns "all position-takings", the bracketing concerns "the objective world".

One change from *Ideas I* is decisive. There Husserl had written, "with good reason we limit the universality of this ἐποχή" (Hua III/1 65), since an unlimited abstention would leave no ground even for science. §8 opens with "this universal putting out of validity". The limit has gone, and the worry it answered is met with the passage's first claim: the *epochē* "does not set us before a nothing". What fills the place of the limit is the gain announced in the next sentence. The universal abstention gives the meditator "his own" (*zueigen*) pure life. The correction from "us" to "me" ("or more distinctly: what becomes mine, the meditator's") is the first time the Meditations take back the plural of §3's "we begin anew". §8.2 had said we may no longer properly speak "in the communicative plural". Here the grammar complies.

The derivation of the second name has a precise form. "The phenomenological fundamental method of the transcendental ἐποχή, insofar as it leads back to this ground, is therefore called (*heißt daher*) transcendental-phenomenological reduction." The subject of the sentence is the *epochē*. "Reduction" is predicated of it under a condition, "insofar as it leads back" (*sofern sie … zurückleitet*), which revives the Latin sense of *reducere*, to lead back. The reduction is therefore not a second operation added to the abstention. It is the abstention described by its result. In the course of one paragraph the *epochē* has also changed its adjective. At its entry it was "phenomenological"; in the naming sentence it is "transcendental". What happened in between is the argument that the natural ground of being "constantly presupposes the transcendental". The *epochē* becomes transcendental by disclosing the ground it leads to, and the "therefore" claims that this disclosure has taken place.

The disclosure rests on one equation and one impossibility. The equation is "is for me — that is, is valid for me" (*ist für mich — das heißt gilt für mich*). Its "that is" turns being-for-me into validity-for-me without argument; §7's "validity-phenomenon" prepared it. The impossibility is "I cannot live … my way into any world other than the one that has sense and validity in me and from me myself." The verbs repeat the prefix "into" (*hinein-*): living, experiencing, thinking, valuing and acting into the world. §64 takes up the same row of verbs to describe the naive life of everyday practice, "an experiencing, thinking, valuing, acting into the pregiven world" (Hua I 180). The prefix that here marks the meditator's confinement to his own sphere of sense marks there the naivety of a life that does not know the confinement.

The two names then go their separate ways through the work. "Reduction" acquires qualifiers: §15's reflection is carried out under it, and §44 introduces a "reduction to the sphere of ownness" within it (Hua I 124). The *epochē* stays the name of the abstention and returns at the close in the formula of §64, "One must first lose the world by ἐποχή in order to regain it in universal self-reflection" (Hua I 183). There, too, the *epochē* is the act and not the ground: the loss is the meditator's deed, the regaining is the work of reflection that the reduction makes possible.

## §9, Hua I 63: the apodictic core and its presumptive horizon

Reads [§9, Paragraphs 3 and 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#the-range-of-apodictic-evidence-of-the-i-am).

§9 restricts what §8 seemed to win. The transcendental ego is given apodictically, but only a core of it is given adequately: the "living self-presence" that the first-person present of *ego cogito* expresses. Everything else that makes the ego this ego, its past, its capacities, its habits, lies in a horizon that is meant along with the core but not itself experienced. That the ego is stands absolutely firm; what it is does not, and how far the certainty reaches into the what is an open question. The passage applies §6's split between apodicticity and adequacy to the one case that matters, and it leaves the First Meditation with a ground whose extent is not yet known.

> We recall here the earlier remark that *adequation* and *apodicticity* of an evidence need not go hand in hand. Perhaps this remark was coined precisely for the case of transcendental self-experience. In it the ego is originally accessible to itself. But this experience offers, at any time, only a core of what is properly experienced adequately: namely the living self-presence that the grammatical sense of the proposition *ego cogito* expresses, while beyond it there reaches only an indeterminately general, presumptive horizon, a horizon of what is properly not experienced but necessarily co-meant. To it belong the mostly wholly dark self-past, but also the transcendental capacities belonging to the I and its habitual peculiarities at any time. External perception, too (which, to be sure, is not apodictic), is a self-experience of the thing — it itself stands there — but in this standing-there-itself it has for the one experiencing an open, endless, indeterminately general horizon of what is properly not itself perceived, and has it as a horizon to be opened up as a presumption — by possible experience. Similarly, then, the apodictic certainty of transcendental experience concerns my transcendental *I am* in the indeterminate generality of an open horizon that adheres to it. The actual being of the ground of cognition that is first in itself accordingly stands absolutely firm, but not, just like that, what determines its being more closely and what, during the living evidence of the *I am*, is not yet itself given but only presumed. This presumption, implied in the apodictic evidence, is therefore subject, as regards the possibilities of its fulfilment, to the critique of its scope, which may perhaps be delimited apodictically. How far can the transcendental I deceive itself about itself, and how far do the absolutely indubitable holdings reach in spite of this possible deception? With the positing of the transcendental *ego* we stand altogether at a dangerous point, even if we leave aside for now the difficult questions of apodicticity.

> Wir erinnern uns hier an früherer Bemerkung, daß A d ä - *quation* und *Apodiktizität* einer Evidenz nicht Hand in Hand gehen müssen — vielleicht war diese Bemerkung gerade auf den Fall der transzendentalen Selbsterfahrung gemünzt. In ihr ist das ego sich selbst ursprünglich zugänglich. Aber nur einen Kern von eigentlich adäquat Erfahrenem bietet jeweils diese Erfahrung: nämlich die lebendige Selbstgegenwart, die der grammatische Sinn des Satzes *ego cogito* ausdrückt, während darüber hinaus nur ein unbestimmt allgemeiner, präsumptiver Horizont reicht, ein Horizont von eigentlich Nicht-Erfahrenem, aber notwendig Mitgemeintem. Ihm gehört zu die zumeist völlig dunkle Selbstvergangenheit, aber auch die dem Ich zugehörigen transzendentalen Vermögen und die jeweiligen habituellen Eigenheiten. Auch die äußere Wahrnehmung (die allerdings nicht apodiktisch ist) ist zwar Selbsterfahrung des Dinges — es selbst steht da — aber in diesem Selbstdastehen hat es für den Erfahrenden einen offenen endlosen, unbestimmt allgemeinen Horizont von eigentlich Nicht-selbst-Wahrgenommenem, und zwar als einen das darin als Präsumption — durch mögliche Erfahrung aufzuschließenden. Ähnlich also betrifft die apodiktische Gewißheit der transzendentalen Erfahrung mein transzendentales *Ich bin* in der ihm anhaftenden unbestimmten Allgemeinheit eines offenen Horizontes. Das Wirklichsein des an sich ersten Erkenntnisbodens steht demnach zwar absolut fest, nicht aber ohne weiteres das, was sein Sein näher bestimmt und was während der lebendigen Evidenz des *Ich bin* noch nicht selbst gegeben sondern nur präsumiert ist. Diese in der apodiktischen Evidenz mit implizierte Präsumption untersteht also hinsichtlich der Möglichkeiten ihrer Erfüllung der Kritik ihrer ev. apodiktisch zu begrenzenden Tragweite. Wie weit kann das transzendentale Ich sich über sich selbst täuschen und wie weit reichen die absolut zweifellosen Bestände trotz dieser möglichen Täuschung? Mit der Statuierung des transzendentalen *ego* stehen wir überhaupt an einem gefährlichen Punkt, selbst wenn wir die schwierigen Fragen der Apodiktizität zunächst außer Betracht lassen.

*Note on the text.* "Adäquation" survives in the file as a fragment of spaced type joined to italics ("A d ä - *quation*"), and "*Apodiktizität*" is italic: both terms are emphasized. The sentence about external perception is garbled after "und zwar als einen" ("einen das darin als Präsumption — durch mögliche Erfahrung aufzuschließenden"); the rendering gives the evident sense, that the horizon is presumed and to be opened up by possible experience, and nothing below rests on the wording of that clause. The passage is complete in the file; it does not break off at the page.

The decisive phrase is the appeal to grammar: the core is "the living self-presence that the grammatical sense of the proposition *ego cogito* expresses". What the proposition says, by its grammar, is first person and present tense: I, now, think. That is all that is adequately given. The commentary reports the phrase without asking why Husserl reaches for grammar here. The answer is that grammar draws the line he needs. "I think" does not say "I thought", or "I can", or "I am the sort of man who". The past, the capacities and the habits that §9 assigns to the horizon are exactly what the present indicative leaves out. So the appeal to grammar delimits the core by what the sentence does not say. It is also a concession. The most the meditator can claim adequately is what Descartes' sentence already contained, and the transcendental ego, which §8 introduced as the ground of the whole world's validity, is at this point no richer than its grammatical subject.

The analogy that follows does more work than its parenthesis admits. External perception "(which, to be sure, is not apodictic)" is also "self-experience" (*Selbsterfahrung*) of its object, which "itself stands there", and it too has an "open, endless" horizon of what is not perceived. "Similarly, then" (*Ähnlich also*), the certainty of the "I am" concerns it "in the indeterminate generality of an open horizon". The analogy treats self-experience as perception of a peculiar object, with a front side given and a back side meant. The parenthesis marks the one difference, that the thing's existence is not apodictic while the ego's is. Everything else is shared. The ego is thus given as things are given, in a core and a horizon, and the First Meditation's certainty attaches only to the existence of the core.

The wording divides that and what with care. "The actual being (*Wirklichsein*) of the ground … stands absolutely firm, but not, just like that (*nicht aber ohne weiteres*), what determines its being more closely." The qualifier "just like that" is the same one §7 used of the world's evidence: it does not deny that the horizon could be certain, it denies that it is certain without a critique. The presumption is "implied" (*mit impliziert*) in the apodictic evidence, so the certainty of the core carries an uncertainty inside it, and the critique of its "scope" (*Tragweite*) is to be "perhaps" (*ev.*) delimited apodictically. The questions that follow are real ones. "How far can the transcendental I deceive itself about itself?" assumes that it can. The "dangerous point" (*gefährlichen Punkt*) of the last sentence then turns from the problem of scope to a second danger, which §10 names: the temptation to take the "positing" (*Statuierung*) of the ego as Descartes did, as the rescue of "a little tail-end of the world" (Hua I 63). The legal ring of *Statuierung*, an establishing by decree, fits that danger. The ego has been set up as a ground before its extent is known.

The work answers §9 twice, and the answers differ. In §13 Husserl suggests that "perhaps" the absolute evidence of the *ego sum* "reaches into" the manifold of self-experience after all, not as certainty about any single content but as "a universal apodictic structure of experience of the I (e.g. the immanent time-form of the stream of experiences)" (Hua I 67). The horizon is not made adequate, but a form running through it is made apodictic. At the same time §13 sets the whole problem of scope aside for the length of the work, as the task of a "critique" to come. That critique reappears only in §63, where "our earlier indications" are said to show how, "for example through critique of transcendental recollection, an apodictic content of it is brought out" (Hua I 178). The memory that §9 asked about ("can an apodictic evidence be claimed for it?") is the example §63 chooses. Between the two the Meditations proceed on the core and its horizon without having delimited the horizon's certainty, as §9 said they would have to. The structure of the passage, an apodictic core in a presumptive horizon, is also the structure of the work: a certain beginning carried forward by presumptions it has not yet criticized.

## §10, Hua I 63–64: a little tail-end of the world

Reads [§10, Paragraphs 1 and 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#excursus-descartes-failure-to-make-the-transcendental-turn).

Descartes' failure, as §10 diagnoses it, is a single misreading of what the doubt had left him. He took the indubitable ego for a remnant of the world: a small piece that survived the overthrow, from which the rest of the world could be won back by inference. Once the ego is a piece of the world, it is a substance among substances, a human mind, and the first link in a causal chain. The transcendental ego that §8 found is not a piece of the world at all, and §11 will say so in as many words. The excursus names the error in one diminutive, "a little tail-end" (*ein kleines Endchen*), and attaches to it a charge whose proof it postpones: that the turn made Descartes "the father of countersensical transcendental realism".

> It seems so easy, following Descartes, to grasp the pure I and its cogitationes. And yet it is as if we were on a steep rock ridge, where to advance calmly and surely decides over philosophical life and philosophical death. Descartes had the earnest will to radical freedom from prejudice. But we know through recent research, and in particular the fine and profound research of Messrs Gilson and Koyré, how much scholasticism is hidden in Descartes' Meditations as an unclarified prejudice. But not only that: first of all we must keep at arm's length the prejudice already mentioned above, which derives from admiration for mathematical natural science and determines us ourselves as an old inheritance — as though under the title *ego cogito* we were dealing with an apodictic axiom that, together with others still to be exhibited and in addition with hypotheses perhaps grounded inductively, had to provide the foundation for a deductively explanatory science of the world, a nomological science, a science *ordine geometrico* [in the geometrical order], just like mathematical natural science. In connection with this, it must by no means count as a matter of course either that in our apodictic pure *ego* we had rescued a little **tail-end** of the world, as the one thing about the world that is unquestionable for the philosophizing I, and that what now matters is to infer the rest of the world by rightly conducted inferences according to principles innate in the *ego*.
>
> Unfortunately this is how it goes with Descartes, with the inconspicuous but fateful turn that makes the *ego* into a *substantia cogitans* [thinking substance], into the separated human *mens sive animus* [mind or soul], and into the initial link for inferences according to the principle of causality; in short, the turn by which he became the father of the (as cannot yet become visible here) countersensical transcendental realism. All this stays far from us if we remain true to the radicalism of self-reflection, and so to the principle of pure intuition or evidence, and therefore let nothing count here except what we actually and at first quite immediately have given on the field of the *ego cogito* opened to us by the ἐποχή, and therefore bring to statement nothing that we do not ourselves *see*. In this Descartes went wrong, and so it comes about that he stands before the greatest of all discoveries, has in a certain way already made it, and yet does not grasp its proper sense, the sense of transcendental subjectivity, and so does not pass through the entrance gate that leads into genuine transcendental philosophy.

German:

> Es scheint so leicht, Descartes folgend, das reine Ich und seine cogitationes zu fassen. Und doch ist es, als wären wir auf einem steilen Felsgrat, auf dem ruhig und sicher fortzuschreiten über philosophisches Leben und philosophischen Tod entscheidet. Descartes hatte den ernsten Willen zu radikaler Vorurteilslosigkeit. Aber wir wissen durch neuere Forschungen, und insbesondere die schönen und tiefgründigen der Herren Gilson und Koyré, wie viel Scholastik im verborgenen und als ungeklärtes Vorurteil in Descartes' Meditationen steckt. Aber nicht das allein; zunächst schon jenes oben bereits erwähnte, aus der Bewunderung der mathematischen Naturwissenschaft herstammende und uns selbst als alte Erbschaft bestimmende Vorurteil müssen wir uns vom Leibe halten, als ob es sich unter dem Titel *ego cogito* um ein apodiktisches Axiom handle, das im Verein mit aufzuweisenden anderen und dazu ev. induktiv begründeten Hypothesen das Fundament für eine deduktiv erklärende Weltwissenschaft abzugeben habe, eine nomologische Wissenschaft, eine Wissenschaft *ordine geometrico*, eben ähnlich wie die mathematische Naturwissenschaft. Im Zusammenhang damit darf es auch keineswegs als selbstverständlich gelten, als ob wir in unserem apodiktischen reinen *ego* ein kleines **Endchen** der Welt gerettet hätten, als das für das philosophierende Ich einzig Unfragliche von der Welt, und daß es nun darankomme, durch recht geleitete Schlußfolgerungen nach dem *ego* eingeborenen Prinzipien die übrige Welt hinzuzuschließen.
>
> Leider geht es so bei Descartes, mit der unscheinbaren, aber verhängnisvollen Wendung, die das *ego* zur *substantia cogitans*, zur abgetrennten menschlichen *mens sive animus* macht und zum Ausgangsglied für Schlüsse nach dem Kausalprinzip, kurzum der Wendung, durch die er zum Vater des (wie hier noch nicht sichtbar werden kann) widersinnigen transzendentalen Realismus geworden ist. All das bleibt uns fern, wenn wir dem Radikalismus der Selbstbesinnung und somit dem Prinzip reiner Intuition oder Evidenz getreu bleiben, also hier nichts gelten lassen, als was wir auf dem uns durch die ἐποχή eröffneten Felde des *ego cogito* wirklich und zunächst ganz unmittelbar gegeben haben, also nichts zur Aussage bringen, was wir nicht selbst *sehen*. Darin hat Descartes gefehlt, und so kommt es, daß er vor der größten aller Entdeckungen steht, sie in gewisser Weise schon gemacht hat, und doch ihren eigentlichen Sinn nicht erfaßt, also den Sinn der transzendentalen Subjektivität, und so das Eingangstor nicht überschreitet, das in die echte Transzendentalphilosophie hineinleitet.

*Note on the text.* The file sets "**Endchen**" in bold, which in this file may stand for the print's spaced type; either way the word is emphasized. The phrase "im Verein mit aufzuweisenden anderen und dazu ev. induktiv begründeten Hypothesen" can be read as "other [axioms] still to be exhibited, and hypotheses besides" or as "other, and perhaps inductively grounded, hypotheses"; the rendering keeps the first, as the commentary does.

*Endchen* is the diminutive of "end" (*Ende*): a scrap, a stub, the last bit of something larger. The commentary's "a small corner of the world" gives the size and loses the shape. A corner is part of a whole that is still there. A tail-end is what is left when the rest has gone, and it is still of the same stuff as what has gone. That is the error the word pins down. If the ego is the last bit of the world to survive the doubt, then it is worldly, and the world can be rebuilt from it by adding back what was lost. The infinitive that follows says so: the rest of the world is "to be inferred on" (*hinzuzuschließen*), concluded to and added to the remnant. The prefix "on to" (*hinzu-*) makes the inference an act of supplementing. The emphasis on the diminutive marks it as the point where the misreading becomes visible.

The second paragraph lists the stages of the "turn" (*Wendung*) as a series of nouns, each closer to the world. The ego becomes "*substantia cogitans*", a thing that thinks. Then "the separated human *mens sive animus*", which makes the thinking thing a human mind cut off from its body. Then the "initial link" (*Ausgangsglied*) in causal inference, which makes it the first member of a chain whose further members are worldly. Each step takes the ego further into the world it was supposed to ground. The turn is "inconspicuous but fateful" (*unscheinbar, aber verhängnisvoll*), and "fateful" repeats §3's adjective for the geometrical prejudice. The two failures are one. Because Descartes wanted an axiom from which a science *ordine geometrico* could be deduced, he needed the ego to be a premise in the same domain as its conclusions, and so a piece of the world.

The *Paris Lectures* had nearly the same paragraph, and the changes sharpen it (src L526–553). The lectures said Descartes had "the purest will" (*den reinsten Willen*) to freedom from prejudice; the Meditations say "the earnest will" (*den ernsten Willen*), which keeps his seriousness and drops the praise of purity that the scholasticism contradicts. The lectures spoke of plural prejudices "barely noticeable to ourselves"; the Meditations make it the one prejudice "already mentioned above", which refers back to §3, and call it "an old inheritance" that "determines us ourselves". The lectures had the ego as a "basic axiom" with other axioms "to be derived from it"; the Meditations add hypotheses "perhaps grounded inductively" and a "deductively explanatory", "nomological" science, which brings the target forward from Descartes' geometry to modern hypothetico-deductive science. And the lectures called Descartes plainly the father of countersensical transcendental realism. The Meditations insert a parenthesis: "(as cannot yet become visible here)".

The parenthesis is the honest part of the charge. The paternity claim is stated as a verdict in §10, but its ground lies thirty sections ahead. "Countersensical" (*widersinnig*) is a strong word in Husserl's vocabulary. It does not mean false but self-cancelling, a sense that contradicts itself. Nothing in the First Meditation yet shows that transcendental realism is that. §41 redeems the debt in the same words. The problem of how the ego gets out of its "island of consciousness" (*Bewußtseinsinsel*) to the world is "countersensical", "a countersense into which Descartes himself had to fall, because he missed the genuine sense of his transcendental ἐποχή and of the reduction to the pure *ego*" (Hua I 117). The cause given there is the one given here: Descartes "stands before the greatest of all discoveries, has in a certain way already made it", but does not grasp its sense. §41 adds what the parenthesis held back. The countersense lies in asking, as a man in the world, how one gets out to the world: the question presupposes the world it asks about.

The excursus also fixes the rule that keeps the meditator away from the tail-end: "bring to statement nothing that we do not ourselves *see*." The italic on "see" (*sehen*) is the whole method in one word. Descartes' turn was an inference from what he saw to what he did not. §11 draws the consequence in positive form. The ego that remains after the ἐποχή "is not a piece of the world" (*nicht ein Stück der Welt*), and when it says "I am, *ego cogito*", that "no longer means: I, this human being, am" (Hua I 64). "Not a piece" answers "a little tail-end". The ego is not the last bit of the world but the place where the world's validity is constituted, and the "entrance gate" of §10's last sentence is the step from the one to the other.

## §11, Hua I 65: transcendence as irreal containedness

Reads [§11, Paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#the-psychological-and-the-transcendental-ego-the-transcendence-of-the-world).

"Transcendental" and "transcendent" are defined in §11 as a pair, each by the other. The world is transcendent because it is not a component of my conscious life: no thing is found in consciousness as a part of it, as a sensation or an act is. Yet the world is contained in that life in another way, as the sense and validity that my experiencing gives it. Husserl calls this "irreal containedness" (*irreelles Beschlossensein*). The ego that carries the world in this way, and that the world's sense presupposes, is "called transcendental". The definition rests on the difference between two kinds of containing, as a component part (*reell*) and as a meant sense (*irreell*), and English "real" and "unreal" cannot mark it.

> This concept of the transcendental and its correlate-concept, that of the transcendent, must be drawn exclusively from our philosophically meditating situation. Here it is to be noted: just as the reduced I is not a piece of the world, so, conversely, the world and every worldly object is not a piece of my I, not to be found in my conscious life as its really inherent part, as a complex of sensation-data or acts. To the proper sense of everything worldly belongs this *transcendence*, although it gains, and can gain, the whole sense that determines it, together with its validity of being, only from my experiencing, my representing, thinking, valuing, doing at any time — including the sense of a being that is perhaps evidently valid, precisely from my own evidences, from my grounding acts. If this *transcendence* of irreal containedness belongs to the proper sense of the world, then the I itself, which carries the world in itself as a valid sense and is in turn necessarily presupposed by it, is called, in the phenomenological sense, *transcendental*; and the philosophical problems arising from this correlation are accordingly called transcendental-philosophical.

German:

> Dieser Begriff des Transzendentalen und sein Korrelatbegriff, der des Transzendenten, muß ausschließlich aus unserer philosophisch meditierenden Situation geschöpft werden. Es ist hierbei zu beachten: So wie das reduzierte Ich kein Stück der Welt ist, so ist umgekehrt die Welt und jedes weltliche Objekt nicht Stück meines Ich, nicht in meinem Bewußtseinsleben als dessen reeller Teil, als Komplex von Empfindungsdaten oder Akten reell vorfindlich. Zum eigenen Sinn alles Weltlichen gehört diese *Transzendenz*, obschon es den gesamten es bestimmenden Sinn, und mit seiner Seinsgeltung, nur aus meinem Erfahren, meinem jeweiligen Vorstellen, Denken, Werten, Tun gewinnt und gewinnen kann — auch den eines ev. evident gültigen Seins, eben aus meinen eigenen Evidenzen, aus meinen begründenden Akten. Gehört zum eigenen Sinn der Welt diese *Transzendenz* irreellen Beschlossenseins, so heißt dann das Ich selbst, das sie als geltenden Sinn in sich trägt und von diesem seinerseits notwendig vorausgesetzt ist, im phänomenologischen Sinne *transzendental*; die aus dieser Korrelation erwachsenden philosophischen Probleme heißen dementsprechend transzendental-philosophische.

Husserl has two German words where English has one. *Real* (with its noun *Realität*) means real in the sense of the world: spatio-temporal, causal, existing in nature. *Reell* means really inherent in consciousness, present in a lived experience as one of its components, as a sensation-datum or an act is. The world is *real* and not *reell*: it exists, and it is not a part of anyone's stream of consciousness. The passage uses only the second word. The world is not found in my conscious life "as its really inherent part" (*als dessen reeller Teil*), and its transcendence is a transcendence "of irreal containedness" (*irreellen Beschlossenseins*). "Irreal" here does not mean unreal. It means not contained as a component. The commentary renders the phrase "unreal containedness", which in English suggests that the world's containment, or the world, lacks reality. The sense is the opposite. The world is fully real and, as real, contained in the ego only as meant sense. Translated by "real/unreal", the definition reads as a denial of the world's reality, which is the idealist misreading Husserl spends §41 refuting.

The first half of the passage is built as a chiasmus. "Just as the reduced I is not a piece of the world, so, conversely, the world … is not a piece of my I" (*So wie … kein Stück der Welt … so ist umgekehrt … nicht Stück meines Ich*). The noun "piece" (*Stück*) answers §10's "little tail-end": Descartes made the ego a piece of the world, and the naive idealist makes the world a piece of the ego. §11 refuses both in one sentence. Neither is a part of the other, and that is what makes them correlates and not whole and part. The concession follows in "although" (*obschon*): the world gains its "whole" sense and its validity "only" from my experiencing. The *Transzendenz* is italic both times, and the italics hold it up against the concession. What the world owes to me entirely is its sense; what it does not owe to me is a place among my components.

"Containedness" (*Beschlossensein*) is the passage's own word for the relation, and it is ambiguous on purpose. *Beschließen* means to enclose or include; in the passive it is the usual word for "implied" (*darin beschlossen*, "included therein", as §11 has just said of psychology within anthropology). The world is included in the ego in this logical sense, the way a consequence is included in a premise or a meaning in an act, not the way a part is included in a whole. "Irreal" fixes which inclusion is meant.

The definition of "transcendental" then follows by "then" (*dann*) and the defining verb "is called" (*heißt*): "then the I itself … is called, in the phenomenological sense, *transcendental*". The I is transcendental because of a two-way dependence: it "carries" the world "as a valid sense" and is "necessarily presupposed" by that sense. The qualifier "in the phenomenological sense" marks off other uses without naming them, and the first sentence says where the concept may be drawn from: "exclusively from our philosophically meditating situation". The verb is "drawn" (*geschöpft*), the verb the previous paragraph used for the world, which "draws its whole sense and its validity of being" from me. Concept and world are drawn from the same source. No received concept of the transcendental, Kant's or anyone else's, may be brought into the meditation, and the word is defined, like everything else in it, from what the meditator sees.

The definition sets up the Third and Fourth Meditations. §18 states the same contrast with the same verb. The cube that appears is "in" the flowing consciousness, but this being-in is "not being-in as a really inherent component" (*nicht Darinsein als reelles Bestandstück*), rather "an appearing being-ideally-in", or "being-in as immanent *objective sense*"; the object "lies included in it as sense" (*liegt in ihm selbst als Sinn beschlossen*, Hua I 80). There the irreal containedness of §11 receives its positive name: the object is in consciousness ideally, as sense. §47 calls the primordial world an "immanent transcendence", which applies §11's formula within the sphere of ownness. And §41's "transcendental idealism" depends on the reading defended here: an idealism in which the world is not made into a complex of mental components, since §11 has already ruled that out. The *Crisis* changes where the concept comes from. There the word "transcendental" is used "in a widest sense" for the motive that Descartes set going in all modern philosophy, the reflection of the knower on himself as the last source of all knowledge. That concept "is not one that can be documented", but is gained "by deepening into the unified historicity of the whole of modern philosophy" (*Krisis* §26, Hua VI 101). The Meditations draw the concept from the situation of one meditator; the *Crisis* draws it from a history. The content is close; the method of acquiring it has moved from the first person to the history that §2 of the Meditations had invoked only as a question.

## §13, Hua I 69: transcendental solipsism as a substage

Reads [§13, Paragraphs 3 and 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#the-necessity-of-initially-excluding-problems-of-the-range-of-transcendental-cognition).

The reduction begins as "pure egology", and so seems to condemn the meditator to a "transcendental solipsism". §13 does not deny the appearance. It reclassifies it. Transcendental solipsism is "only a philosophical substage" (*nur eine philosophische Unterstufe*): a level of the inquiry that must be marked off on purpose, so that intersubjectivity can enter later as a problem built on it. The claim is a promise, made under several hedges, and the whole Fifth Meditation is written to keep it. Read closely, the promise is also narrower than it sounds. It does not say that solipsism will be overcome, only that it will be put in its place, as a lower storey that stays standing.

> Now, however, what is at issue is a so to speak absolutely subjective science, a science whose object in its being is independent of the decision about the non-being or being of the world. But more still. It seems as though its first, its only object is, and can only be, my transcendental ego, the ego of the one who philosophizes. Certainly it lies in the sense of the transcendental reduction that at the beginning it can posit as existing nothing other than the ego and what is included in it itself, and that with a horizon of indeterminate determinability. Certainly, then, it begins as pure egology, and as a science that, as it seems, condemns us to a solipsism, albeit a transcendental one. It is not yet at all foreseeable how, in the attitude of the reduction, other egos — not as mere worldly phenomena, but as other transcendental egos — are to become positable as existing, and so become co-entitled themes of a phenomenological egology.
>
> As beginning philosophers we must not let ourselves be frightened by such misgivings. Perhaps the reduction [to] the transcendental ego only carries with it the semblance of a permanently solipsistic science, while its consistent execution according to its own sense leads over to a phenomenology of transcendental intersubjectivity and, unfolding by means of this, to a transcendental philosophy as such. In fact it will turn out that a transcendental solipsism is only a philosophical substage and must be marked off as such with methodical intent, in order that the problematic of transcendental intersubjectivity, as a founded and therefore higher-level one, can be brought into play in the right way. But nothing definite can be made out about this at the present point of our meditations, as indeed the indications given can show their full significance only as they are carried further.

German:

> Jetzt aber handelt es sich um eine sozusagen absolut subjektive Wissenschaft, eine Wissenschaft, deren Gegenstand in seinem Sein von der Entscheidung über Nichtsein oder Sein der Welt unabhängig ist. Aber noch mehr. Es scheint so wie ihr erster, ihr einziger Gegenstand mein, des Philosophierenden, transzendentales ego zu sein und nur sein zu können. Sicherlich liegt im Sinne der transzendentalen Reduktion, daß sie zu Anfang als seiend nichts anderes setzen kann als das ego und was in ihm selbst beschlossen ist, und zwar mit einem Horizont unbestimmter Bestimmbarkeit. Sicherlich fängt sie also als reine Egologie an, und als eine Wissenschaft, die uns, wie es scheint, zu einem, obschon transzendentalen, Solipsismus verurteilt. Es ist ja noch gar nicht abzusehen, wie in der Einstellung der Reduktion andere ego — nicht als bloß weltliche Phänomene, sondern als andere transzendentale ego — als seiend sollen setzbar werden können, und damit zu mitberechtigten Themen einer phänomenologischen Egologie.
>
> Wir dürfen uns durch solche Bedenken als anfangende Philosophen nicht schrecken lassen. Vielleicht, daß die Reduktion das transzendentale ego nur den Schein einer bleibend solipsistischen Wissenschaft mit sich führt, während ihre konsequente Durchführung gemäß ihrem eigenen Sinne zu einer Phänomenologie der transzendentalen Intersubjektivität überleitet, und mittelst ihrer sich entfaltend zu einer Transzendentalphilosophie überhaupt. In der Tat wird sich zeigen, daß ein transzendentaler Solipsismus nur eine philosophische Unterstufe ist und als solche in methodischer Absicht abgegrenzt werden muß, um die Problematik der transzendentalen Intersubjektivität als eine fundierte, also höherstufige in rechter Weise ins Spiel setzen zu können. Doch darüber ist an der jetzigen Stelle unserer Meditationen nichts Bestimmtes auszumachen, wie denn überhaupt die gegebenen Vordeutungen erst in ihrer Fortführung ihre volle Bedeutung erweisen können.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "die Reduktion das transzendentale ego", without the preposition the sense requires ("the reduction [to] the transcendental ego"). The rendering marks the missing word; the reading does not depend on it.

The passage is built from two kinds of adverb, and the argument lies in their alternation. The first paragraph concedes with "certainly" (*Sicherlich*, twice): the reduction can posit at first only the ego, and it "therefore" begins as egology. The concessions are the passage's facts. Everything that tells against solipsism is hedged with "seems". The object only "seems" (*Es scheint so*) to be my ego alone; egology condemns us to solipsism "as it seems" (*wie es scheint*). The second paragraph turns these appearances into a "semblance" (*Schein*), and its own claims are introduced by "perhaps" (*Vielleicht*) and by a future, "it will turn out" (*wird sich zeigen*). The certainties are about the beginning; the hopes are about the end. What would make solipsism a mere semblance is that the reduction, carried out "consistently according to its own sense", "leads over" (*überleitet*) to intersubjectivity. The reduction is supposed to correct its own first result. The passage asserts that it can, and adds at once that "nothing definite" (*nichts Bestimmtes*) can be made out about it yet.

"Condemns" (*verurteilt*) is a judicial verb. The reduction seems to pass sentence on the meditator, and "albeit a transcendental one" (*obschon transzendentalen*) is placed inside the phrase as a mitigation of the sentence: a transcendental solipsism is not the psychological solipsism of a man shut in his own mind, since the ego in question is not a man. But the mitigation does not acquit. The problem is stated exactly in the last sentence of the first paragraph. Other egos would have to become "positable as existing" (*als seiend … setzbar*) "not as mere worldly phenomena", since those the reduction already has, but "as other transcendental egos", and so become "co-entitled themes" (*mitberechtigten Themen*) of an egology. An egology with many egos as co-entitled themes is the paradox the Fifth Meditation has to make sense of.

The key word, "substage" (*Unterstufe*), is a term of construction. It is the lower storey of a building, and the next clause names the upper one: intersubjectivity is "a founded, therefore higher-level" (*fundierte, also höherstufige*) problem. The "therefore" (*also*) runs from founding to height: what is founded on something stands above it. That gives solipsism a lasting role. A substage is not an error to be left behind but a level that must be "marked off with methodical intent" (*in methodischer Absicht abgegrenzt*), because the level above rests on it. The commentary calls the claim a "promissory restriction"; the wording adds that the restriction is permanent. Solipsism is not to be refuted but stratified.

The work keeps this promise in exactly that form. §42 states the objection again, now as the threat that phenomenology be "branded" as transcendental solipsism (Hua I 121). §44 then carries out the marking off that §13 required, as a second reduction "to the sphere of ownness", which delimits within the transcendental ego what is its own before any sense of the other enters (Hua I 124). §62 declares that the objection "dissolves in its groundlessness" (Hua I 175). And §64 places the substage in the system of philosophy: "In order, the first in itself of the philosophical disciplines would be the solipsistically restricted egology, that of the primordially reduced ego; only then would come the intersubjective phenomenology founded in it" (Hua I 181). The "solipsistically restricted egology" is still there at the end, first in order, as §13's "substage" said it would be. What §13 called a semblance is the appearance that solipsism is the last word. What survives is the first word.

The paragraph that follows §13.4 turns the passage's "nothing definite" (*nichts Bestimmtes*) into its opposite: "What is definitely (*Bestimmt*) marked out, in any case, is an essential departure from the Cartesian course" (Hua I 70). The open question about others is set beside a settled decision about method. Descartes' evidence "remains without fruit", because he did not see that the ego can lay itself out "into infinity and systematically". The decision to explore the ego's own field, and not to infer a world from it, is what makes solipsism a stage. Descartes, who argued from the ego to God and then to others, had no substage. Husserl, who refuses that argument, has to pass through one.

## §14, Hua I 71–72: intentionality as the third member of the *ego cogito*

Reads [§14, Paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#the-stream-of-cogitationes-cogito-and-cogitatum).

Intentionality enters the Meditations as an amendment to Descartes' formula. The "transcendental title *ego cogito*" must be "expanded by one member": every *cogito* means something and carries what it means, its *cogitatum*, within itself. Intentionality is the name for this property, and the passage restricts the name to it: the word "means nothing other than" being consciousness of something. Two claims are packed into the amendment. The relation to the object survives the *epochē* untouched, because it lies in the experience itself and not between the experience and a world. And the object is always carried in a particular way, as perceived, remembered, imagined or judged. The Second Meditation's whole programme of description is the working out of those two claims.

> It must not be overlooked that the ἐποχή with regard to all worldly being changes nothing about the fact that the manifold *cogitationes* that relate to something worldly bear this relation in themselves, that, for example, the perception of this table is, as before, precisely perception of it. So, in general, every experience of consciousness is in itself consciousness of this or that, however it may stand with the legitimate validity of this object as actual, and however I, as transcendentally attuned, may abstain from this as from every one of my natural validities. The transcendental title *ego cogito* must therefore be expanded by one member: every *cogito*, every experience of consciousness, as we also say, means something and bears in itself, in this manner of the meant, its *cogitatum* at any time, and each does so in its own manner. The perception of a house means a house, more exactly as this individual house, and means it in the manner of perception; a memory of a house in the manner of memory; a phantasy of a house in the manner of phantasy; a predicative judging about the house, which perhaps *stands there* perceptually, means it precisely in the manner of judging, and again in a new manner a valuing that is added, and so on. Experiences of consciousness are also called *intentional*, where, however, the word intentionality then means nothing other than this universal basic property of consciousness, to be consciousness of something, to bear as *cogito* its *cogitatum* in itself.

German:

> Es darf nicht übersehen werden, daß die ἐποχή hinsichtlich alles weltlichen Seins daran nichts ändert, daß die mannigfaltigen *cogitationes*, die sich auf Weltliches beziehen, in sich selbst diese Beziehung tragen, daß z.B. die Wahrnehmung von diesem Tisch nach wie vor eben Wahrnehmung von ihm ist. So ist überhaupt jedes Bewußtseinserlebnis in sich selbst Bewußtsein von dem und dem, wie immer es mit der rechtmäßigen Wirklichkeitsgeltung dieses Gegenständlichen stehen mag und wie immer ich als transzendental Eingestellter dieser wie jeder meiner natürlichen Geltungen mich enthalten mag. Der transzendentale Titel *ego cogito* muß also um ein Glied erweitert werden: jedes *cogito*, jedes Bewußtseinserlebnis, so sagen wir auch, meint irgend etwas und trägt in dieser Weise des Gemeinten in sich selbst sein jeweiliges *cogitatum*, und jedes tut das in seiner Weise. Die Hauswahrnehmung meint ein Haus, genauer als dieses individuelle Haus, und meint es in der Weise der Wahrnehmung, eine Hauserinnerung in der Weise der Erinnerung, eine Hausphantasie in der Weise der Phantasie; ein prädikatives Urteilen über das Haus, das etwa wahrnehmungsmäßig *dasteht*, meint es eben in der Weise des Urteilens, wieder in neuer Weise ein hinzutretendes Werten usw. Bewußtseinserlebnisse nennt man auch *intentionale*, wobei aber das Wort Intentionalität dann nichts anderes als diese allgemeine Grundeigenschaft des Bewußtseins, Bewußtsein von etwas zu sein, als *cogito* sein *cogitatum* in sich zu tragen, bedeutet.

"In itself" (*in sich selbst*, *in sich*) occurs three times, and it carries the first claim. The *cogitationes* "bear this relation in themselves"; every experience is "in itself consciousness of this or that"; the word intentionality means bearing the *cogitatum* "in itself". If the relation to the table were a link between two existing terms, the *epochē*, which suspends the table's existence, would cut it. Because the relation lies on one side, in the experience, the suspension "changes nothing" (*nichts ändert*). The perception of this table remains "as before, precisely perception of it" (*nach wie vor eben Wahrnehmung von ihm*). The phrase "as before" is the whole argument: what the reduction keeps is not a residue but everything consciousness had, with only the world's validity set aside. The verb is "bear" or "carry" (*tragen*), the verb §11 used for the ego that "carries" the world in itself as valid sense. The *cogito* carries its *cogitatum* as the ego carries its world.

The "member" (*Glied*) belongs to the vocabulary of formulas. Descartes' title had two members, *ego* and *cogito*; it now has three. The extension is presented as a correction inside the Cartesian frame, not as a new doctrine brought in from outside, and the formula keeps its Latin. From §15 on Husserl speaks of the "double title *cogito-cogitatum (qua cogitatum)*" (Hua I 74), and §16 makes "the separation between *cogito* and *cogitatum qua cogitatum*" "the first universal of description" (Hua I 77). The added Latin *qua cogitatum*, "as thought", states what the passage leaves implicit. The *cogitatum* is the object exactly as it is meant, not the object as it is in the world.

The second claim is carried by the word "manner" (*Weise*), which the passage repeats until it becomes the point: "in this manner of the meant", "each in its own manner", "in the manner of perception", "of memory", "of phantasy", "of judging", "again in a new manner". The same house is the *cogitatum* of five experiences, and each has it differently. This is why intentionality cannot be a bare relation of "aboutness". The *cogitatum* is always the object-in-a-manner, and the manners can be described. The example of judging adds a detail that matters later: the house of the judgment "perhaps *stands there* perceptually" (*dasteht*, italic). One experience founds another, the judgment rests on the perception, and the "valuing that is added" rests on both. The layering of acts that §§17–18 call synthesis is already in the sequence.

The last sentence takes over a word and narrows it. "Experiences of consciousness are also called (*nennt man auch*) intentional": the name is current usage, and the "one" who uses it is not named. "But" (*aber*) the word "then means nothing other than" (*dann nichts anderes als … bedeutet*) the property just described. The restriction keeps the word from bringing anything with it: no psychological theory of mental acts, no relation of a mind to things. The one who gave the word its modern sense is named three sections later. Only the clarification of synthesis, §17 says, makes fruitful "the significant discovery of Franz Brentano, that intentionality is the descriptive basic character of *psychic phenomena*" (Hua I 79). It is the only place the Meditations name Brentano. The order is telling. §14 defines intentionality as a transcendental title, purified by the *epochē*. §17 credits Brentano with a discovery about "psychic phenomena", the domain of psychology that the reduction has set aside, and makes its fruitfulness depend on Husserl's own analysis of synthesis. The word is Brentano's; the sense in which the Meditations use it is drawn, like "transcendental" in §11, from the meditating situation.

## §15, Hua I 73: the disinterested spectator

Reads [§15, Paragraph 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#natural-and-transcendental-reflection).

The phenomenologist is a "disinterested spectator" (*uninteressierter Zuschauer*), and the adjective is defined by its opposite. The natural I is "interested" in the world: it lives into the world, takes positions on it, holds things to exist, to be good, to be useful. The spectator is the same I with one difference: it does not join in those position-takings. Nothing else is taken away from it. It still sees everything the natural I lived through, including the natural I's belief in the world, and it keeps one interest of its own, "to see and to describe adequately". The split of the I (*Ichspaltung*) that results is not a division into two subjects but the establishment of a second attitude above the first, and the second attitude can always be reflected on in turn.

> We can also describe what is present here as follows. If we call the I that naturally experiences its way into the *world*, and otherwise lives its way into it, *interested* in the world, then the phenomenologically altered attitude, held constantly fixed as such, consists in this: that a splitting of the I takes place, in which the phenomenological I establishes itself above the naively interested I as *disinterested spectator*. That this takes place is then itself accessible through a new reflection, which, as transcendental, demands once again the performance of precisely this attitude of *disinterested* looking-on — with the only interest that remains to it, to see and to describe adequately.

German:

> Wir können, was hier vorliegt, auch so beschreiben: Nennen wir das natürlich in die *Welt* hineinerfahrende und sonstwie hin-einlebende Ich an der Welt *interessiert*, so besteht die phänomenologisch geänderte und beständig so festgehaltene Einstellung darin, daß sich eine Ichspaltung vollzieht, in dem sich über dem naiv interessierten Ich das phänomenologische als *uninteressierter Zuschauer* etabliert. Daß dies statt hat, ist dann selbst durch eine neue Reflexion zugänglich, die als transzendentale abermals den Vollzug eben dieser Haltung des *uninteressierten* Zuschauens fordert — mit dem ihm einzig verbleibenden Interesse, zu sehen und adäquat zu beschreiben.

*Note on the text.* The file hyphenates "hin-einlebende" mid-line, a remnant of a line break; the word is *hineinlebende*.

The definition is conditional. "If we call" (*Nennen wir*) the natural I "interested", then the phenomenological attitude is a split. The whole figure hangs on a name the passage gives, and the name is chosen for its negation. "Interested" (*interessiert*) is italic, and so are "disinterested spectator" and the second "disinterested" (*uninteressierten*). The emphasis falls on the pair, not on the spectator. Interest, as the passage uses it, is not curiosity or concern. It is the natural I's participation in the world's validity: to experience "into" the world (*hineinerfahrende*) and to live "into" it (*hineinlebende*), with the prefix §8 used for the life that cannot reach any world but its own. To be disinterested is to stop living into the world while still looking at that life.

The preceding sentences of §15 say exactly what is withheld. The reflecting I does not "join in" (*Nicht-mitmachen*) the position-taking of the straightforward perception of the house. But the perception's belief "is not missing", nor is the house's character of "simple *existence*" (*Dasein*). "The *not-joining-in*, the abstaining of the phenomenologically attuned I, is its affair, and not that of the perceiving it looks at reflectively" (Hua I 73). The split follows from that sentence. If the abstention belonged to the perception, the perception would be altered and the spectator would describe a doctored experience. Because the abstention belongs to a second I, the first I's belief is seen intact, as a belief. Husserl concedes that every reflection alters its experience, since it turns into an object what was lived through without being one. He limits the concession to that: the reflection's task is "not to repeat the original experience, but to look at it" (Hua I 73).

The spatial preposition matters. The spectator "establishes itself above" (*sich über … etabliert*) the interested I. "Above" places the two I's on levels, as §13's "substage" placed solipsism beneath intersubjectivity, and as §15 itself has just called reflections "grasping acts of a new level" (*einer neuen Stufe*). "Establishes itself" (*etabliert sich*) makes the split an act of the I on itself, held "constantly fixed" (*beständig so festgehalten*). The attitude has to be maintained; it is not a state one falls into.

The last sentence repeats the structure of §6. "That this takes place" is accessible "through a new reflection, which, as transcendental, demands once again (*abermals*) the performance of precisely this attitude". §6 said that the evidence of the critical reflection "is again (*abermals*) of this apodictic dignity, and so in every critical reflection of a higher level". Here the iterated reflection does not inherit a dignity but repeats an attitude: the spectator who watches the spectator is again disinterested. That is why the split never yields a third subject. Each new level is the same spectator, and §63 will say the same of critique applied to critique: the regress is real and harmless (Hua I 178).

The closing clause qualifies the adjective it has just italicized. The spectator is disinterested "with the only interest that remains to it, to see and to describe adequately". The disinterest is therefore not total. One interest survives, and it is theoretical. That clause holds the passage together. What the *epochē* suspends is every interest in the world's being; what it leaves is an interest in the world's appearing, which the next paragraph says can now be described "purely, free of all co-meanings and pre-meanings of the observer" (Hua I 74). "Adequately" is a demanding word after §9, which allowed adequacy only to the living present. The spectator's remaining interest is an aim, not a possession.

The *Crisis* keeps the figure and adds quotation marks. §69's heading speaks of "the establishment of the 'disinterested spectator'", and the text speaks of the change "from life in the natural interest in the world into that of the 'disinterested' observer" (*Krisis* §52, Hua VI 179). A few pages later he says that this attitude is "extremely hard to carry through radically, since it is constantly threatened by misunderstandings" (Hua VI 186). The quotation marks distance the adjective that the Meditations had only italicized. By then the word "disinterested" had to be guarded against being heard as indifference, which the Meditations' closing clause had already ruled out.

## §16, Hua I 77: mute experience and its first expression

Reads [§16, Paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-1-cartesianische-meditationen-commentary.md#excursus-the-necessary-beginning-of-transcendental-as-well-as-purely-psychological-reflection-with-the-ego-cogito).

Description, for Husserl, does not begin with elements and build experience out of them. It begins with experience as it is lived, which is "so to speak still mute", and its first task is to give that experience voice: to bring it "to the pure expression of its own sense". The first such expression is Descartes' *ego cogito* in its ordinary form, "I perceive — this house", "I remember — a certain street scene". Sensation-data and Gestalt qualities, with which the psychology of his day began, are not the material of experience. They are results that description may or may not reach later, and at the start they are only prejudices. The passage reverses the order of analysis, and the metaphor of a voice given to mute experience carries the reversal.

> Obviously, for a transcendental-descriptive egology as for a *pure inner psychology* (necessarily to be carried out as the fundamental psychological discipline), drawn descriptively (and really quite exclusively) from inner experience, there is no other beginning than the one with the *ego cogito*. Given the failure of all modern attempts to distinguish psychological from philosophical theory of consciousness, this remark is of the greatest importance. It means blocking one's access to both if, misled by the still all-dominant tradition of sensualism, one makes the beginning with a theory of sensations. This implies: one interprets conscious life in advance, as a supposed matter of course, as a complex of data of *outer* and (at best) also of *inner* sensibility, for whose connection into wholes one then lets the Gestalt qualities provide. To dispose of *atomism*, one adds the doctrine that the forms are necessarily founded in these data, so that the wholes are prior in themselves to the parts. But the radically beginning descriptive theory of consciousness does not have such data and wholes before it, except as prejudices. The beginning is the pure and, so to speak, still mute experience, which must now first be brought to the pure expression of its own sense. The truly first expression, however, is the Cartesian one of the *ego cogito*, for example: I perceive — this house; I remember — a certain street scene, and so on; and the first universal of description is the separation between *cogito* and *cogitatum qua cogitatum*. In which cases and in which different meanings sensation-data can then perhaps legitimately be exhibited as components is a special product of an uncovering and descriptive work, from which traditional theory of consciousness has entirely exempted itself, to its harm. Owing to its unclarity about what is principled in method, it lost entirely the immense thematic of the description of the *cogitata qua cogitata*, but also the proper sense and the particular tasks of the *cogitationes* themselves as modes of consciousness.

German:

> Offenbar ist, wie für eine transzendental-deskriptive Egologie, so für eine (als psychologische Fundamentaldisziplin notwendige durchzuführende) *reine Innenpsychologie*, deskriptiv (und wirklich ganz ausschließlich) aus innerer Erfahrung geschöpft, kein anderer Anfang als der mit dem *ego cogito*. Bei dem Versagen aller neuzeitlichen Versuche, psychologische und philosophische Bewußtseinslehre zu unterscheiden, ist diese Bemerkung von größter Wichtigkeit. Es heißt sich also den Zugang zu beiden versperren, wenn man, von der noch allherrschenden Tradition des Sensualismus mißleitet, den Anfang mit einer Empfindungslehre macht. Darin liegt: Man deutet vorweg das Bewußtseinsleben in vermeintlicher Selbstverständlichkeit als eine Komplexion von Daten *äußerer* und (günstigstenfalls) auch *innerer* Sinnlichkeit, für deren Verbindung zu Ganzheiten man dann die Gestaltqualitäten sorgen läßt. Um den *Atomismus* abzutun, fügt man noch die Lehre bei, daß in diesen Daten die Gestalten notwendig fundiert, also die Ganzen den Teilen gegenüber das an sich frühere sind. Aber die radikal anfangende deskriptive Bewußtseinslehre hat nicht solche Daten und Ganze vor sich, es sei denn als Vorurteile. Der Anfang ist die reine und sozusagen noch stumme Erfahrung, die nun erst zur reinen Aussprache ihres eigenen Sinnes zu bringen ist. Die wirklich erste Aussprache ist aber die Cartesianische des *ego cogito*, z.B.: Ich nehme wahr dieses Haus wahr, ich erinnere mich — eines gewissen Straßenauflaufs usw.; und das erste Allgemeine der Beschreibung ist die Scheidung zwischen *cogito* und *cogitatum qua cogitatum*. In welchen Fällen und in welchen unterschiedenen Bedeutungen dann eventuell Empfindungsdaten rechtmäßig als Bestandstücke aufzuweisen sind, das ist ein spezielles Produkt einer enthüllenden und deskriptiven Arbeit — deren sich die traditionelle Bewußtseinslehre zu ihrem Schaden völlig enthoben hat. Vermöge ihrer Unklarheit über das Prinzipielle der Methode ging ihr die ungeheure Thematik der Deskription der *cogitata qua cogitata* ganz verloren, aber auch der eigentliche Sinn und die besonderen Aufgaben der *cogitationes* selbst als Bewußtseinsweisen.

*Note on the text.* The first example is garbled in the file: "Ich nehme wahr dieses Haus wahr" ("I perceive this house perceive"), with the verb's separable particle doubled and no dash. The second example, "ich erinnere mich — eines gewissen Straßenauflaufs", has the dash, and the commentary gives the first as "I perceive — this house", which fits the parallel. The rendering follows the commentary's form, but the reading below rests on the second example, which the file gives intact. The edition's apparatus records that "psych." was later inserted after "stumme" (note to Hua I 77, line 7): in the revised text the beginning is "still mute *psychic* experience". Who made the insertion the note does not say.

The metaphor has three parts, and each does work. Experience is "mute" (*stumm*): it has a sense, but does not state it. It is "still" (*noch*) mute: the muteness is a stage, and speech is due to it. And the speech it is brought to is "the pure expression of its *own* sense" (*ihres eigenen Sinnes*). Description adds no sense from outside; it states what the experience already means. "So to speak" (*sozusagen*) hedges the figure, as Husserl hedges most of his figures in the Meditations, but the figure carries the claim. The sensualist, by contrast, "interprets in advance" (*deutet vorweg*), and interpretation is the opposite of letting experience speak. "In advance" (*vorweg*) and "supposed matter of course" (*vermeintlicher Selbstverständlichkeit*) are §3's terms for Descartes' geometrical prejudice, and the passage ends the sentence with the same noun: data and wholes are before the beginner only "as prejudices".

The examples are built to show what the first expression contains. "I remember — a certain street scene": the dash separates the act from what it is of. The grammar of the plainest report already holds the "separation between *cogito* and *cogitatum qua cogitatum*" that the next clause calls "the first universal of description". That is why the Cartesian expression is "the truly first". It does not report sensations and it does not report a whole built from them. It reports an I, an act, and an object meant in a manner. The commentary keeps the dash in both examples and does not say what it is for. The dash is the place where muteness first becomes articulation: the first cut experience makes in speaking of itself is the one between its doing and its object.

The target is named precisely, and the polemic follows the history of the doctrine. First "sensualism", the "still all-dominant tradition" that starts from sensations. Then its repair: Gestalt qualities are made to "provide for" the connection of data into wholes (*sorgen läßt*, a verb that treats them as stopgaps). Then the repair of the repair, "to dispose of atomism": the doctrine that wholes are founded in the data and yet "prior in themselves" to the parts. Husserl lists the three stages in one breath and answers them with one sentence. They differ about how data and wholes relate; they agree that consciousness consists of data and wholes. That agreement is the prejudice. The sensation-data are not denied. Whether, and "in which different meanings", they can be shown as components is "a special product" of description, which comes at the end of the work, not at its beginning.

The passage also binds psychology to the same beginning. "Pure inner psychology", which §14 had distinguished from transcendental phenomenology, must also begin with the *ego cogito*, and the failure to distinguish the two disciplines has made "access to both" impossible. The later insertion of "psych." after "mute" fits this double claim: the mute experience is the psychologist's as well as the phenomenologist's. The *Paris Lectures* had stated the same programme more plainly, and their apparatus records a later addition in different ink: the object is to be described "exactly as it lies intuitively before the first mute glance of reflection, and then it is the task to let it express itself" (note to *Pariser Vorträge* 13,35, src L3994). There the muteness belongs to reflection's first glance, and the object is let speak. In the Meditations the muteness belongs to experience itself, and the first word it is given is Descartes'. §17 draws the consequence: only the elucidation of synthesis makes Brentano's discovery, intentionality as "the descriptive basic character of psychic phenomena", fruitful (Hua I 79). The beginning with the *ego cogito* that §16 requires is where the description of that synthesis starts.

# Michel Henry, *L'essence de la manifestation*, Section II, read against Heidegger's *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* (GA 3)

## Section II. Repetition of the elucidation of the concept of phenomenon: transcendence and immanence

Henry is cited by section and paragraph (R §23 ¶4), counting the typographical paragraphs of each section, since the edition used carries no page numbers. Heidegger's Kantbuch is cited by GA 3 section and page, read from the running heads of the German text. Henry quotes the book from the 1953 French translation by Alphonse de Waelhens and Walter Biemel, which this commentary does not have. Each of his references ("K" and a page) is located once by its German wording and then given in GA 3 form. His French is checked against the German only; where it diverges, the divergence is laid to the translators unless Henry's argument rests on it. All translations are working renderings.

The pair has its own traps. GA 3 speaks in three voices: Kant quoted, Kant paraphrased under Heidegger's interpretation, and Heidegger's own fundamental ontology. Henry cites all three as "Heidegger says", so each quotation has to be assigned before his use of it can be judged. Henry's categories (ontological monism, the essence that objectivates itself, the milieu of exteriority, receptivity as the essence's reception of itself) are his, never GA 3's. The 1953 French renders letting-stand-over-against (*Gegenstehenlassen*) as "objectivation" and representing (*Vorstellen*) as "représentation"; both turn a letting into a producing, and Henry builds on both, so they count as his moves wherever he leans on them. Heidegger's pure self-affection (§34) forms the movement from itself toward and back to itself that first makes a self. It is not the affecting of a self already there, nor what Henry calls auto-affection, and Henry's verdict on it has to be tested against §34 rather than against his restatement. Taking-in (*Hinnahme*), which defines finitude in GA 3, is not empirical reception: Heidegger makes pure intuition a taking-in that gives itself what it takes, and warns against the narrow concept (§32, 173–174). Finally, chronology. Henry read the second edition in translation and could not know the marginalia printed in GA 3, the 1973 preface, the Davos disputation (first printed in 1973), or the Kant lectures of 1927–28 (GA 25). And his §§17–21 read the essay on Hegel's concept of experience (*Holzwege*, written 1942–43), a later Heidegger than the Kantbuch's, whom Henry treats as one "philosophy of being".

### § 17. The originary character of the manifestation of being and the problem of natural consciousness

**¶1.** Henry splits two senses of "being must be able to show itself": a possibility realized only when phenomenology completes its work of elucidation, or a manifestation already effective as the condition of that work and of every relation to beings. He takes the second and calls it the absolute. No GA 3 passage is cited; the first sense echoes the definition of phenomenology in *Sein und Zeit* §7, where what phenomenology must bring to view is what initially does not show itself. Against the Kantbuch the split is less new than it looks. GA 3 is built on the same "already": knowledge of beings is possible only on the ground of a prior knowledge of their constitution of being (§7, 38), and transcendental inquiry thematizes an understanding of being already at work (§3, 16). It also has the "at last": the laying of the ground unveils its origin by a regress (§§6–8). Henry's departure lies in the word "absolute". In GA 3 the prior understanding of being is "the innermost ground" of human finitude (§41, 228); for Henry the prior manifestation is absolute and complete. He leaves Heidegger's "already" standing and changes its rank.

**¶2.** Henry turns on a sentence he quoted at §16 from the Kantbuch (K 279 / GA 3 §40, 223): to understand the determination of beings by being, "the determining element itself must show itself with a certain clarity". He reads it as deferring being's manifestation to a future elucidation, and answers that the determination of beings by being, hence the manifestation of being, precedes every philosophical understanding of it. The German does not say what the French says. Heidegger writes that the determining must be "sufficiently graspable" and that being "must first be grasped" (*begriffen*): the sentence concerns the order of concept-formation within the question of being, not the moment at which being shows itself. Henry's objection rests on "se montre". At §16 he enlisted the sentence for his own requirement that the essence show itself; here he corrects it; both times he reads a claim about the concept as a claim about manifestation. Three pages on, Heidegger states Henry's thesis himself: "we understand being and yet lack the concept", and we understand it "at all times and in the whole field of the manifestness of beings" (§41, 226–227), so that the question of being "springs from the pre-conceptual understanding of being" (§40, 226). Heidegger's "already" and "at last" are understanding and concept, kept apart exactly as Henry wants them kept apart. The disagreement lies elsewhere, in the status of the pre-conceptual. Heidegger calls the being so understood indeterminate, familiar yet unknown, and questionless (227); Henry calls its manifestation originary and effective. Henry has misread his chosen sentence, but the misreading isolates the question Section II presses: whether the "already" is an understanding at all.

**¶3.** Henry restates the determination of beings by being as the dependence of what appears on appearing, and insists that the presence of being in which natural consciousness lives is not a presupposition reached by reflection but present in itself: "the reality of the relation is its manifestation". From Heidegger's Hegel essay (*Holzwege* 126) he takes the term Parousia, the presence of the absolute that science reaches "at its first step". Two departures from GA 3 open here. The first is of method. GA 3 reaches the understanding of being as the ground of the possibility of relating to beings, by a regress to conditions (§§3, 6–8). Henry demands that the condition itself be manifest, which no condition of possibility is merely by being one; the demand anticipates his charge that the philosophy of transcendence lacks a phenomenology of the ground (R §§28–29). Heidegger has a partial answer, since in GA 3 the understanding of being is not inferred but lived, in every statement and every mood (§41, 227). What he does not ask is how the understanding of being is manifest to the one who understands. "The reality of the relation is its manifestation" will become that question, addressed to transcendence. The second departure is of rank. By passing to "the absolute" through the Hegel essay, Henry gives being's manifestation a title GA 3 refuses: there, "something like being is given only where finitude has come to exist" (§41, 228).

**¶4.** Henry draws the consequence that consciousness is itself the manifestation of being, so that there is no introduction to phenomenology and the phenomenology of spirit is consciousness itself. Both quotations (*Holzwege* 190, 189) are Heidegger's exegesis of Hegel: natural consciousness cannot be led to where it already is, and it stands in the Parousia "according to the habit of natural consciousness". Henry turns exegesis into thesis, and adopts a word GA 3 avoids. The Kantbuch locates the understanding of being not in consciousness but in "the Dasein in the human being" (§41, 229), and at Davos in 1929 Heidegger said outright that to call Dasein consciousness "is precisely what I rejected" (GA 3, 289; retrospective). Henry does not ignore the refusal so much as dispute its ground. Section I argued that the philosophy of being and the philosophy of consciousness share one concept of manifestation, so the name of its bearer decides nothing. What GA 3 calls the understanding of being in Dasein, Henry calls consciousness, because for him both are defined by their manifestation.

**¶5.** Henry glosses the habit of natural consciousness as its immediate condition: the essence of consciousness is the Parousia, "presence in its presence", being-present as such being present. On that ground he contests Hegel's claim that the immediate manifestation of truth abstracts from its being-present (PhE I 198). Being-present, the absolute concept, is not abstracted from the manifestation of beings; it constitutes it. Nothing from GA 3 is cited. What the unit takes from Heidegger without question is the equation of being with presence, which GA 3 treats as a problem. Heidegger's §44 argues that the ancient understanding of being as *ousia* means "constancy in presence" and asks whether it does not betray a projection of being upon time (§44, 240). Henry's "presence of presence" keeps the presence and drops the question of its time-character. The omission is a commitment: at R §24 Henry will argue that what makes time the ground of manifestation in GA 3 is its intuitive, not its temporal, character.

**¶6.** Henry concludes that being shows itself "originarily": what is first present is not a being but being-present itself, and the origin is at once ontological (it is being) and phenomenological (it shows itself). Hegel's remark that the early religious community confused the origin with the immediate existence of the first manifestation (PhE II 271–272) lets Henry place the concept in the origin as "the first act of showing". GA 3 is the silent foil. Its procedure is a regress to the origin, the "source-ground" of ontological knowledge (§§6, 8), and Heidegger concludes that the laying of the ground does not lead to "the sun-clear absolute evidence of a first proposition and principle" but "goes and points consciously into the unknown" (§6, 37). Henry will quote that sentence at R §25 as proof that the philosophy of transcendence rests truth on an older untruth. Here he sets the counter-thesis in advance: the origin is what shows itself first, and the unknown cannot be original. The two concepts of origin do different work. Heidegger's origin is measured by what it makes possible and is reached by unveiling; Henry's is measured by its own appearing and needs no unveiling.

**¶7.** Consciousness cannot forget the being that is its essence. To say it lives in forgetfulness of being means only that it holds to beings and counts being, the appearing of what appears, as nothing; being never stops showing itself while it is "forgotten". The citations (*Holzwege* 163, 144) aim at the Hegel essay and, through it, at the later doctrine of the forgetting of being. Against the Kantbuch the paragraph lands differently. GA 3 already has forgetting: "the finitude of Dasein, the understanding of being, lies in forgottenness", which "forms itself necessarily and constantly" (§42, 233). What is forgotten there is not being's disclosure. The understanding of being pervades human existence "unknown in its breadth, constancy, indeterminacy and questionlessness" (§41, 228), and being is understood so self-evidently "that it looks as though there were no such thing" (227). Henry's gloss on forgetting restates that description. Against GA 3, then, his correction corrects nothing, since Heidegger's forgetting already presupposes that being never stops being understood. The disagreement concerns the remedy. For Heidegger the forgotten must be wrested out by a re-membering that lets it "come toward us again in its innermost possibility" (§42, 233–234). Henry's next paragraphs deny that any such turn adds to manifestation.

**¶8.** The manifestation of being requires no radical change in the life of consciousness. Such a change occurs only when consciousness turns from beings to the appearing by which they appear: the reversal (*Umkehrung*), in which consciousness represents "the appearing as appearing" (*Holzwege* 178, 175) and appearing reaches representation. Henry is reporting Heidegger's Hegel, whose vocabulary is representation. GA 3 has no reversal but has its function: the explicit grasp of the understanding of being in fundamental-ontological "construction" (§42, 232–233). Henry gives the turn the status of representation, manifestation become an object for consciousness. That decision prepares his reading of the Kantbuch, where representing is everywhere and where he will find transcendence itself to be representation (R §§18, 23). It leaves out what GA 3 says of the turn. Construction there is a projecting, because understanding is "not only a kind of knowing but primarily a basic moment of existing as such" (§42, 233); it does not set the understanding of being before consciousness as an object.

**¶9.** In representing manifestation, the reversal accomplishes the appearing of appearing: consciousness represents itself in its appearing (*Holzwege* 181), the absolute is present to itself, and "the manifestation of the absolute to itself in the Parousia demands the reversal". The last sentence states the position Henry is about to reject and should not be read as his. Its GA 3 counterpart is not a reversal but self-affection. In §34 time as pure self-affection forms "the essence of anything like concerning-oneself", so that the finite self "can be something like self-consciousness" (189–190). There the self's relation to itself precedes and enables any representation of itself. Henry here fixes the alternative he will hold Heidegger to: either self-manifestation is produced by a turn that represents, or it belongs to the essence. GA 3's self-affection fits neither side cleanly, which is why Henry spends two sections on it (R §§24, 31).

**¶10.** Representing appearing rather than what appears makes philosophical knowledge true knowledge, knowledge of truth and not of beings, and the Parousia is the deed of true knowledge. Henry still reports Hegel through Heidegger. GA 3 draws the same distinction without the reversal. Ontological knowledge "is no knowledge, if knowledge means grasping beings", yet it is rightly called knowledge because it is "the original truth" Kant calls transcendental truth (§25, 123); Henry will quote the passage at R §23. For Heidegger this truth is not reached by a philosopher's turn. Ontological knowledge "forms" transcendence by keeping open the horizon in which the being of beings is seen in advance (§25, 123), and natural experience already lives in it. Henry is one step from saying so himself.

**¶11.** In representing the appearing of what appears, true knowledge represents the knowledge of beings itself; it is the self-knowledge of that knowledge, which Fichte and Schelling called transcendental knowledge. Henry takes "transcendental" in the post-Kantian sense of knowledge of knowledge, a sense GA 3 sets aside on purpose. Heidegger glosses Kant's definition thus: transcendental knowledge "investigates not beings themselves but the possibility of the prior understanding of being, that is, at the same time, the constitution of being of beings", and concerns reason's passing over (transcendence) to beings (§3, 16). For the Kantbuch the transcendental is ontological, not reflexive, and the *Critique* is no theory of knowledge. Henry's Fichtean gloss is not a misreading of GA 3, which is not yet his target, but it installs the reading of the transcendental he will bring to the Kantbuch: transcendence as a representation the essence gives itself. The shared word hides a difference of function.

**¶12.** Henry reverses the Hegelian order. Transcendental knowledge is not true knowledge in the sense of the reversal, because it is the knowledge natural consciousness already has; since the manifestation of beings is as such the manifestation of being, "natural consciousness is transcendental consciousness". Here Henry and the Kantbuch converge. In GA 3 transcendence is the constitution of finite knowing as such (§3, 16), and being is understood "in the whole field of the manifestness of beings" (§41, 226–227); natural consciousness, in Heidegger's terms, is transcending Dasein. Because the contest will be fought on this common ground, two differences should be fixed now. Henry identifies the two manifestations: the manifestation of a being "is, as such, the manifestation of being". GA 3 keeps them apart: "manifestness of beings (ontic truth) turns on the unveiledness of the constitution of being of beings (ontological truth)" (§2, 13), and its account of truth depends on the difference. And what Henry calls transcendental is the manifestation of being, whereas what GA 3 calls transcendental is transcendence, the passing-over to beings. Section II will argue that these are not the same.

**¶13.** Being is pure manifestation, and its manifestation is the self-manifestation of pure manifestation; this belongs to the essence and is not the doing of the reversal or of philosophical knowledge, which presuppose it. "Self-manifestation is the essence of manifestation." The thesis is Henry's own, the formal demand for the autonomy of the essence set at the close of Section I (R §16), and nothing in GA 3 corresponds to it. In the Kantbuch being is unveiled in a forming: ontological knowledge "forms" transcendence (§25, 123), and imagination forms the look of the horizon (§19, 90). Unveiling is something finite Dasein accomplishes in projecting, and what is unveiled is held open, not self-manifest. Henry's thesis is the exact counter-position. GA 3 has one place, though, where a self-relation is put at the ground: time as pure self-affection (§34). Henry's reading of the Kantbuch will turn on whether that self-affection is the self-manifestation he here requires or a counterfeit of it.

### § 18. The concept of representation: ontological structure and existential understanding

**¶1.** Henry denounces the ambiguity of "representation". In its first sense representation is a determinate mode of conscious life, an act aimed at a thematic object with its unreal noematic correlate. Natural consciousness represents beings in this sense, philosophical knowledge represents being in this sense, and the self-manifestation of being presupposes both and "does not occur in representation". True knowledge is therefore a particular mode of life and has nothing to do with absolute knowledge. GA 3 is not cited, but the word is the Kantbuch's: representing (*Vorstellen*) runs through it, from knowledge in general (§4) to the pure representing at the core of transcendence (§16). Henry's first concept is Husserl's act, not Kant's genus. Heidegger starts from the genus, taking Kant's "representation in general" in "the wide formal sense in which something indicates, announces, presents another" (§4, 22), and then shows that the pure representing at the root of knowledge is unthematic. Henry cuts the thematic act loose from the essence before he admits the genus at all; GA 3 never separates them in that order, because for Heidegger thematic and unthematic representing are both ways of letting something stand over against a finite knower.

**¶2.** In a second sense, "in conformity with the fundamental presuppositions of ontological monism", representation names the essence of manifestation, being's self-manifestation, and its object is being itself. Natural consciousness represents being in this sense without making it a theme; philosophical consciousness represents being thematically, as an object in the same sense as natural consciousness's beings. Henry's second sense reconstructs GA 3's pure representing, which Heidegger calls letting-stand-over-against (*Gegenstehenlassen*) and makes "the original concept" and original act of the understanding (§16, 74), and whose look stands "unobjectively and moreover unthematically in a fore-view" (§9, 47). The departure is in the subject of the verb. In GA 3 finite reason represents: pure representing is a turning-toward by which a finite being lets objectivity stand against it. In Henry's sentence being represents itself. Calling the identification of the two "monist" already contains the charge he will bring against the Kantbuch: the representing GA 3 describes is the only form of self-manifestation monism knows. On philosophy's objectifying of being, Heidegger's 1927 lectures speak in the same terms, calling ontology an "objectification of being" (GA 24 §22b; retrospective).

**¶3.** To the two representations correspond two objects: the determinate object given to an act, and the object of universal consciousness, which is objectivity, "being itself in the form of a horizon", the condition of every object; "it belongs to the essence of the horizon not to let itself be thematized". The paragraph rests, unacknowledged, on GA 3 §25: "the horizon must be unthematic, but nonetheless in view. Only so can it push what is encountered in it into the theme", and the X, the "object in general", means not a being but "the horizon of a standing-against" (123). Henry will quote that sentence at R §23 (K 180). His reconstruction is faithful; the departure is that he calls this horizon being itself. In GA 3 objectivity is the being of beings as appearances for a finite knower, Kant's horizon, since "only for finite knowledge is there anything like an object" (§5, 31). Henry lifts Heidegger's Kant into the definition of being for the whole philosophy of being. Retrospectively, Heidegger's own note on the Kantbuch from the 1930s, printed in the 1973 preface, pairs "being: beingness, objectness, and 'time'" as the book's frame and says its own path was thereby "blocked and made open to misinterpretation" (GA 3, XIII). Henry's equation names a limit Heidegger later conceded in the book, though not one he conceded in being. Henry's note 13 adds a seed: philosophy may represent only a "representation" of being, and the essence may look obscure only to thematic representation. That is the later charge, announced in advance, that the Kantbuch's "unknown" root (§6, 37) is an artefact of its method.

**¶4.** Heidegger says natural knowledge contains a contradiction: "in its representing of beings it does not heed being and yet must heed it" (*Holzwege* 136). Henry splits the two heedings. One is the representation that is the essence of consciousness, where being is the transcendental horizon; the other is an act that grasps being. Natural knowledge is therefore not merely "the concept of knowledge" but knowledge in an absolute sense, and the opposition between natural and true knowledge is inessential. Henry's target is the Hegel essay, but his remedy is the Kantbuch's own distinction between understanding and concept (§41, 226–227), together with its programme for the analysis of everydayness, which is to show "that and how all dealings with beings, for which it looks as though there were only beings, are already grounded in the transcendence of Dasein" (§43, 235). Against the 1943 formulation Henry restores the 1929 position. He goes beyond both only in the predicate "absolute", where GA 3 says the same understanding is "the most finite in the finite" (§41, 229).

**¶5.** Henry fixes a distinction he will use throughout. Heeding and not heeding being are existential determinations, modes of conscious life; the heeding inherent in the essence is an ontological structure, at once indifferent and immanent to every determination. Natural knowledge therefore *is* ontological, and Heidegger's "natural consciousness is and is not ... ontological" (*Holzwege* 163) is improper. The vocabulary is Heidegger's from *Sein und Zeit* §4, where existentiell understanding is Dasein's self-understanding in its factical possibilities and the existential concerns the structures of existence, and where Dasein is "ontologically" distinguished in being pre-ontological (SZ 12). Henry's correction of the Hegel essay thus agrees with the earlier Heidegger and with GA 3 §41. The departure lies in the relation he sets between structure and determination. "Indifferent and immanent" means that the essence prescribes none of its modes and can be read off none of them. For Heidegger the structures of existence are reached only through its possibilities: "the roots of the existential analytic are ultimately existentiell, that is, ontic" (SZ 13), and GA 3 begins the analytic from everydayness (§43, 234). Henry cuts that root. The cut is the condition of his method, which must describe the essence without depending on any mode of existence, and it is the first point at which he and the Kantbuch part on how the ground is reached.

**¶6.** One says improperly that natural consciousness is not ontological because one qualifies a consciousness by the sense of its object, as one calls a consciousness mathematical; noesis and noema form one structure, so "an existential determination is a determination of the life of consciousness and, together with it, of its transcendent signification". Note 16 separates typical essences, such as perception, from the essence in the absolute sense. GA 3 is not engaged, but the definition reshapes a Heideggerian term. Henry now defines "existential" by Husserl's intentional correlation: an existential determination is an act together with its sense. In GA 3 existence means "dependence on beings as such in being delivered over to the being so dependent" (§41, 228), and the analytic of Dasein is kept from "an anthropological-psychological description of its 'experiences' and 'faculties'" (§43, 234). What Henry calls existential is close to what Heidegger excludes. The point bears on what follows: when Henry later measures the Kantbuch's transcendence as "representation", he measures it with the act-concept defined here.

**¶7.** To live in the presence of a signification is to understand, so every existential determination is an existential understanding, and an implicit self-understanding, regulated by the explicit understanding of the object, always accompanies it. Both presuppose existence itself and the transcendental horizon of being, and these are one: being's self-manifestation. "Existential understanding finds its ground in the ontological understanding of being", which is "the original manifestation of being to itself in consciousness" and precedes every act. Two Heideggerian doctrines are taken up. That existence implicitly understands itself from its objects restates Heidegger's thesis that Dasein tends to understand its own being from the beings it deals with, from the "world" (SZ §5, 15). The "ontological understanding of being" is the Kantbuch's understanding of being (*Seinsverständnis*), and here the reinscription is decisive. In GA 3 that understanding is a projecting: "in order to let beings be what and how they are, the existing being must always already have projected what it encounters upon its being a being" (§41, 228), and existence "is in itself finitude". Henry turns the projecting understanding into the manifestation of being to itself. The subject passes from Dasein to being and the projection drops out. Nothing in GA 3 supports the change, and Henry offers it as a definition rather than a reading. It is the first place where a central concept of the Kantbuch is taken over in Henry's sense, and every later charge against the philosophy of transcendence depends on keeping both senses in view.

**¶8.** Absolute knowledge resides in the ontological understanding of being. Philosophical knowledge is an existentiel or, better, existential (*existentiale*) understanding of that ontological understanding, and the implicit self-understanding of existence is always, implicitly, such an understanding. Philosophy merely thematizes it with the conceptual material required. Henry uses the pair as *Sein und Zeit* defines it; Commentary R inverts the two terms when glossing this paragraph. The thesis has its GA 3 counterpart: the question of being "springs from the pre-conceptual understanding of being" (§40, 226), and fundamental-ontological construction brings out what Dasein already understands (§42). Henry reconstructs it closely, with one omission. For Heidegger thematization is not neutral elaboration: what it takes up must be "wrested from forgottenness" against Dasein's own tendency (§42, 233), and a marginal note calls the philosophical question a "conditioned necessity" (GA 3, 225; retrospective). In Henry thematization is conceptual work on an implicit understanding, and nothing in existence resists it.

**¶9.** The ontological understanding of being is radically independent of every existential understanding: whether existence understands itself truly or falsely, "authentically" or "inauthentically", changes nothing in its original nature. Henry draws from Marx the absolute opposition between what existence is in itself and how it represents itself, reads Marx's "consciousness" as that self-representation, and declares that his inquiry concerns only the pure ontological element prior to any understanding of it. The barb is aimed at Heidegger's authenticity. In *Sein und Zeit*, and in GA 3's summary of its course through conscience, guilt and death to temporality (§44, 242), the existential analytic reaches the ground only by way of authentic existence. Henry denies that any existential modification bears on the ontological element. Heidegger is less far away than the barb suggests: he too holds that inauthentic everydayness already rests on transcendence (§43, 235), so that the structure is present in every mode, as Henry says. The difference is one of access. For Heidegger the structures are disclosed through a mode of existence; for Henry existence's understanding of itself has no bearing on what existence is. The appeal to Marx turns that difference into a principle of method.

### § 19. Being-for-itself from the ontological point of view and from the existential point of view: consciousness and truth

**¶1.** The distinction between ontological structure and existential self-understanding removes an equivocation on the for-itself. The term names both the essence of existence, which is self-manifestation, and a particular determination in which existence understands itself, thematically or implicitly, authentically or not. Ontology concerns existence as it is in itself, not as it understands itself; that self-understanding is existence's "experience", valid only "for it", as Hegel's unhappy consciousness of Judaism understands itself as an existence that leaves the essence outside it. The paragraph closes on a question: how can existence that is the for-itself fail to be "for itself"? Henry is reading Hegel, and "experience" comes from Heidegger's Hegel essay. The GA 3 parallel lies ahead, in §34. There Heidegger also separates an ontological self-relation from every act of self-relating. It is not that a mind exists and "among other things also relates something to itself for itself and performs self-positings"; the movement "from itself toward ... and back to itself" first constitutes the mind as a finite self (191), so that the self "can be something like self-consciousness" (190). Henry's ontological for-itself and Heidegger's pure self-affection occupy the same place, a self-relation prior to every act. At R §31 Henry will contest whether Heidegger's candidate is entitled to it. Here he fixes, in Hegelian terms, the place the contest is about.

**¶2.** Existence is the for-itself because its structure is the self-manifestation of the essence; it fails to be "for itself" only in that it does not grasp its essence in an act. The ontological for-itself is radically independent of the existential for-itself. A separation of essence and existence in representation is unreal, not because it is false but because it occurs in representation, as a signification aimed at in a mode of conscious life; and the unity of the two understood in true knowledge is just as unreal. Note 19 points forward to the problem of the real and the unreal (R §§31, 66, 67). The bearing on GA 3 lies in the criterion. Whatever is represented, as the correlate of an act, is unreal; at R §31 Henry will apply this to the Kantbuch's horizon, which is the represented par excellence. GA 3 also denies the horizon the status of a being: the X is "not a being, but nonetheless 'something'", "a nothing" if object means a being thematically grasped (§25, 122–123). Heidegger's "nothing", though, marks the horizon's ontological rank, not its derivation from an act. Henry's criterion will read the one as the other. The decision about the Kantbuch is taken here, on Hegelian ground.

**¶3.** The unhappy consciousness does not represent the unity of essence and existence; that unity is "for us", and at the end of its experiences it will be "for itself". Every unity that is for consciousness, for us or for itself is equally unreal, and to the unreal separation stands opposed not an unreal unity but the real unity of existence and essence: existence is the essence whether or not it understands itself so, "the original being-for-itself of being itself". GA 3 is not cited, but the argument reaches it. The Kantbuch's Fourth Section is a retrieval that brings Dasein's understanding of being out of forgottenness into explicit grasp (§42, 233); in Henry's terms its result is a "for us" and as unreal as what it overcomes. Henry's critique of Hegel thus disqualifies in advance the ontological significance of the Kantbuch's own procedure. And the closing phrase makes being the subject of its own for-itself, as at R §18 ¶7. GA 3 knows no being-for-itself of being, only a finite self constituted by time.

**¶4.** Hegel's distinction between what is for consciousness and what is for us does not touch the ontological structure of being-for-itself. The *Phenomenology of Spirit*, describing existence as consciousness represents it, never deals with reality; the becoming-for-itself of consciousness's in-itself has only existential meaning; the phenomenology of spirit, the manifestation of manifestation, lies in the in-itself of consciousness and is an ontological structure. The reasoning applies to the Kantbuch's account of its own act, and there it meets resistance. Heidegger holds that metaphysics "is not something merely 'created' by human beings in systems and doctrines": the understanding of being, "its projection and its rejection, happens in Dasein as such", and metaphysics is "the basic happening" of the irruption into beings (§44, 242). Fundamental ontology "makes itself true" by bringing Dasein to its manifestness and letting "its inner metaphysics be there" (§42, 233). For Heidegger philosophizing is itself a happening of the understanding of being, not a representation of it. Henry's separation of ontological from existential denies exactly this: the manifestation of spirit in true knowledge is "a manifestation among others" that presupposes the essence. The two agree that the ground precedes philosophy and disagree on whether philosophy belongs to the ground's own happening. Henry's answer forecloses the Fourth Section's claim to retrieve anything real.

**¶5.** Consciousness is in itself the truth. When it understands itself in its truth it sees that what it took for beings is its own knowledge of beings; but truth so understood is a signification, the correlate of an act, transcendent to philosophical consciousness as what it surpasses itself toward, and to natural consciousness as what it fails to reach. That is existential truth. Ontological truth is what consciousness is in itself, whatever it represents as truth. GA 3 also has two truths: "manifestness of beings (ontic truth) turns on the unveiledness of the constitution of being of beings (ontological truth)" (§2, 13), and ontological knowledge is "the original truth" (§25, 123). The pairs do not match. Heidegger's division runs between beings and being. Henry's runs between what consciousness represents, being included, and what consciousness is. Henry's "ontological truth" is therefore not Heidegger's: Heidegger's is the unveiledness of being, held open in transcendence; Henry's is consciousness being the truth in itself. The shared adjective covers two different divisions.

**¶6.** Ontological truth is the truth "that consciousness is in itself", since its being-in-itself is being-for-itself: truth is "truth for itself", and the being-for-itself of truth is consciousness. Consciousness is never separated from truth, which "is not transcendent to consciousness", never exterior, never "unknown"; only an existential truth can be absent from it. Both key words point at the Kantbuch. "Unknown" anticipates R §25, where Henry quotes GA 3's conclusion that the laying of the ground "points consciously into the unknown" (§6, 37) as proof that the philosophy of transcendence rests truth on an older untruth. "Transcendent" sets the main line of Section II. GA 3 puts original truth in transcendence in so many words: "if truth means unconcealment of ..., then transcendence is the original truth" (§25, 123). Henry's claim that original truth is not transcendent to consciousness is the first statement of the thesis he will turn on the Kantbuch, that the original conditions of truth do not belong to the milieu of exteriority (R §26). The opposition is exact, and Henry has not yet argued for his side.

### § 20. Critique of the Hegelian concept of experience

**¶1.** For Hegel, experience is consciousness's experience of truth: first it measures its knowledge against beings, so that the measure lies outside it; then it learns that beings are only beings for it, so that the measure lies within. Henry finds two senses in which consciousness is the truth of beings. It is that truth ontologically, as the original manifestation of being, and this holds for natural consciousness too. Or it understands itself as that truth, as having the measure in itself, which is a determinate self-representation. The paragraph is about Hegel, but the turn from the measure outside to the measure within is Kant's Copernican turn, which GA 3 reads ontologically: ontic knowledge can never conform to objects on its own, "because without ontological knowledge it would not even have a possible to-what" (§2, 13). And in GA 3 the measure is neither consciousness's being nor its self-image. Truth as "agreement with the object" requires that "something like a with-what of possible agreement" be encountered in advance, "something that regulates as a measure": the horizon of what stands against, which must be open beforehand (§24, 118). Henry's dichotomy, consciousness as the truth or consciousness representing itself as the truth, has no place for this third term, a measure formed in transcendence. His later reading of the Kantbuch will have to assign the horizon to one side or the other, and he will assign it to representation.

**¶2.** Consciousness becomes the truth of beings for itself, in representation, only because it is that truth in itself; the self-representation is a result and cannot be a principle. Hegel makes it one: experience compares knowledge and object because both are "for consciousness" (PhE I 74), so that the movement of experience, and with it reality, is explained by the play of consciousness's representations. "Our history is the product of what we think." GA 3 is not engaged, but the Hegelian target stands in for a position the Kantbuch holds in its own terms. In GA 3 understanding is constitutive of existence: the existing being "must always already have projected" what it encounters upon its being (§41, 228), and understanding is "primarily a basic moment of existing" (§42, 233). Projection is not a representation, yet it is not indifferent to what existence is. Henry's alternative, reality or representation of reality, sorts projection with representation without arguing it; here it only aims at Hegel.

**¶3.** Hegel's statement that consciousness is "on one side consciousness of the object, on the other consciousness of itself" is ambiguous. The object is for consciousness because consciousness represents it; consciousness is for itself because it is the truth in itself, the original being-for-itself. Self-consciousness is therefore not comparable to consciousness of the object but constitutes its essence. To build a dialectic on their comparison, Hegel must treat self-consciousness as a self-representation, confusing the ontological sense of being-for-itself with the existential. The thesis that self-consciousness is the essence of object-consciousness rather than a second consciousness beside it is formally Kant's, as GA 3 reads him. There, pure apperception is "the pure letting-stand-over-against of ..., the I itself" (§34, 190), and "time and the 'I think' ... are the same" (191). Henry agrees with the form and will dispute the content. For Heidegger the self-consciousness that grounds object-consciousness is itself the turning-toward that lets objects stand against it. For Henry it will be a self-manifestation that does not turn toward anything (R §§30–31). The ground on which he refuses Hegel's comparison is the ground on which he will refuse Heidegger's identification.

**¶4.** By confusing self-representation with the original being-for-itself, Hegel places consciousness's representation of its knowledge at the start of experience and makes the comparison consciousness's own act, so that "we" have only to look on (PhE I 74). The principle of experience is indeed immanent to consciousness, but Hegel interprets that immanence as presentation in representation, and being-for-itself falls to the rank of an object. "In such a fall lies the passage from reality to the representation of reality, from reality to unreality." Henry draws the paradox of all intellectualism: having said that everything is for consciousness, Hegel must say that something is not for it. The paradox has a GA 3 instance. The Kantbuch's ground is "unknown": Kant's "root unknown to us", into which, Heidegger says, Kant must have looked, where "the unknown is not that of which we know nothing at all, but what presses toward us within the known as the disquieting" (§31, 160). In Henry's terms, a philosophy that places manifestation in representing must find its ground outside what is represented, and call it unknown. He states the general form here and applies it at R §25.

**¶5.** In experience, consciousness learns that its first object, the in-itself, was only an in-itself for it; the second object is the truth of the first, but "this consideration of the matter is our doing" (PhE I 77), and the genesis of the new object happens "behind its back". What is for us, Henry replies, is as unreal as what is for consciousness. Real experience happens neither before consciousness nor behind it, "in the original ontological dimension of being-for-itself". The confusion of reality with representation forces the intellectualist to place whatever is not displayed before consciousness elsewhere, as another display: intellectualism, "Hegel's or Freud's, is a doctrine of the unconscious". The diagnosis applies to the Kantbuch with uncomfortable precision. GA 3's ground is the transcendental imagination, which Kant called "a blind though indispensable function of the soul ... of which we are seldom even conscious", a sentence Heidegger quotes (§14, 63) and then makes a clue: "is it an accident" that Kant says this of the faculty that will prove to be the "unknown common root" (§27, 137)? Henry's thesis predicts this result. A philosophy that defines manifestation as representing must locate its own ground in what is not represented, a function at work behind consciousness's back. Heidegger presents the unconsciousness of imagination as a sign of its originality; Henry's diagnosis makes it a symptom of the starting point.

**¶6.** Hegel finds the origin of experience in the inequality between consciousness of the object and of itself; Heidegger, glossing him, calls it the difference between natural knowledge and real knowledge. Henry identifies real knowledge with the original manifestation of being that makes the manifestation of beings possible, and concludes that this difference cannot be abolished: it persists in true knowledge, since the existential truth of philosophy is not the ontological truth of real knowledge. Hegel made the difference abolishable by turning real knowledge into a representation, comparable with natural knowledge, and so replaced truth by its representation from the start. The thesis that the difference cannot be abolished is Heidegger's own in the Kantbuch, applied against Hegel. In GA 3 ontological knowledge never absorbs ontic knowledge: it "has only 'empirical use'", serving the experience of beings that show themselves (§25, 124), and the difference between unveiledness of being and manifestness of beings is fixed (§2, 13). Henry reconstructs the irreducibility of the ontological difference faithfully. Where he departs is in what fills the place of real knowledge. For Heidegger it is ontological knowledge, which forms transcendence; for Henry it is the self-manifestation of being. The charge he lays on Hegel here, that real knowledge was made a representation, is the charge he will lay on the Kantbuch, where ontological knowledge is pure representing.

### § 21. The reaffirmation of the originary character of the manifestation of being in the bringing-to-light of its non-historical character

**¶1.** Confusing true with real knowledge leads Hegel to place real knowledge at the end: "the absolute is essentially result" (PhE I 19). What results, Henry answers, is only the absolute's representation; the question how consciousness reaches absolute knowledge must be settled at the beginning, since consciousness neither reaches nor leaves it. "The problem of absolute knowledge is the problem of the internal structure of consciousness in general." The sentence sets the task Henry will pursue through the Kantbuch, and its key phrase transposes GA 3's. Heidegger defines the laying of the ground as "the unveiling of the inner possibility of ontology" (§2, 12), and each of its stages asks after the inner possibility of transcendence. Henry's "internal structure" asks something else: not what makes manifestation possible, but how manifestation is effectively accomplished. Where Heidegger seeks a condition, Henry seeks an act. The shift shows at R §23, where Henry glosses the inner possibility of transcendence as "the possibility of its effective accomplishment".

**¶2.** Absolute knowledge is not natural knowledge but its essence. In what stands at the beginning Henry separates the mode in which consciousness begins its history from what makes that beginning possible, the beginning itself, "the upsurge of the effective dimension of phenomenality" in which anything can be manifest. Absolute knowledge is equally the essence of true knowledge, which represents its own origin but presupposes it, and it is foreign to the history that leads from one to the other. Henry's "dimension of phenomenality" has a precise GA 3 counterpart. In its original turning-toward, finite reason "holds before itself a leeway within which something can 'correspond' to it", and "to hold oneself in advance in such a leeway, to form it originally, is nothing other than transcendence" (§16, 71). In GA 3 the upsurge of the dimension is transcendence. Henry will argue that the dimension transcendence forms is not the origin but what the origin makes effective. The word "effective" carries the argument: Heidegger's leeway is formed and held open, while Henry's dimension must also manifest itself. Here the two descriptions can still be read as one. Section II will split them.

**¶3.** The distinction between ontological and existential truth fixes what is historical. Only the truth existence represents about itself has a history and becomes "equal" to its object at the end; truth, the concept, is there before. Hegel's statement that spirit exists as knowing what it is only after the work by which it creates for its consciousness "the figure of its essence" (PhE II 303–304) holds only of the figure. GA 3 binds the ground to history the other way round. Its Fourth Section defines retrieval as "the disclosure of a basic problem's original, hitherto concealed possibilities" (GA 3, 204), and the transcendental interpretation of historicity on the basis of temporality is to give "a preliminary concept of the kind of being of that happening which occurs in the retrieval of the question of being" (§44, 242). For Heidegger the ground is temporality, and philosophy's retrieval is a happening of it. For Henry the essence is non-historical and the work of philosophy merely historical. The non-historical thesis is the first form of the atemporal thesis of R §24.

**¶4.** The "equality" of consciousness with its essence is improper, since reality and its representation never coincide, and a self-consciousness that arises only after a preliminary process has no ontological meaning. Even the "enthusiasm that begins straight away with absolute knowledge, like a pistol shot" (PhE I 25) is made possible by absolute knowledge as the first act of appearing. History is not "the movement by which appearing comes about" (*Holzwege* 140). "What is at the beginning is not the modesty of an obscure *Grund*; it is the Word." The obscure ground is first Schelling's, which Hegel opposes, but *Grund* also names the Kantbuch's whole project, and the Kantbuch ends its regress before an abyss: "does this laying of the ground not lead before an abyss? ... He saw the unknown. He had to shrink back" (§31, 168). Against both Henry sets the Word, the self-revealing logos he will develop with Eckhart in Section III. The contrast is between a ground that withdraws and a beginning that utters itself, and Henry's thesis that the absolute is "presupposed as the condition of any history" leaves no place at the origin for GA 3's abyss. "Modesty" is polemic rather than argument: it reads Heidegger's admission of the unknown as an evasion, a reading R §25 makes explicit.

**¶5.** Because the absolute has its reality in the origin, the historical representation of truth is no condition of it. To Hyppolite's question how a timeless absolute knowledge can have temporal conditions in the existence of humanity, Henry answers that it has none. It is therefore wrong to say, "as Heidegger does following Hegel", that "representation belongs to the essence of experience" (*Holzwege* 176, 175). The sentences are Heidegger's exegesis of Hegel, but Henry is right to take them as Heidegger's own view as well; the Kantbuch says of finite knowledge, in Kant's terms, that knowledge as "objective perception" "is a representing" (§4, 23). Hyppolite's question receives its most radical answer in GA 3, not in Henry. For Heidegger there is no timeless knowledge of being at all, because the immediate understanding of being moves "through and through in an original, but also self-evident, projection of being upon time" (§44, 240). Henry's denial of temporal conditions is aimed here at history. When he extends it to time at R §24, it will meet the Kantbuch's central thesis head-on.

**¶6.** Representation would belong to the essence only if reversal and history were needed for manifestation to become effective, yet effective manifestation is their condition. That this is forgotten shows that "the original character of revelation" is unclear to the thought that forgets it: representation misrepresents the essence and inserts it into representation's own history. Ontology must respect the origin. "What spirit is, it always was in itself" (Hegel). The essence is not unfinished while representation fails to grasp it, and Henry rejects the statement that consciousness "is something it is not yet" (*Holzwege* 167): what consciousness is not yet is not consciousness and does not belong to reality. The target is Hegel through Heidegger, but the widest target is the futurity GA 3 builds into the self. In the Kantbuch the pure synthesis of recognition is "the original forming of ... the future", and Heidegger asks whether time does not "temporalize itself primarily out of the future" (§33, 186–187); the self is temporal from the ground up (§34). Henry's sentence excludes from reality what Heidegger makes the leading dimension of the self's constitution. The exclusion stays implicit here and becomes explicit when Henry judges the Kantbuch's self-affection.

**¶7.** Philosophical knowledge misses its aim when it questions itself, unless the question becomes one about the essence of all knowing. "Philosophy always comes too late, because what it says was at the beginning." Henry turns Hegel's owl of Minerva against Hegel. Against GA 3 the sentence denies philosophy the power Heidegger claims for retrieval, to disclose possibilities "hitherto concealed" (GA 3, 204) and so transform the problem it retrieves. For Heidegger philosophy can come early, opening what was not yet open; for Henry nothing essential is ever not yet open. The closing sentence of the Hegelian part of Section II states the thesis Henry now brings to the Kantbuch: no forming, retrieving or representing adds anything to the manifestation already there.

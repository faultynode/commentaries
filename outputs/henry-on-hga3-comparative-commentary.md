# Michel Henry, *L'essence de la manifestation*, Section II, read against Heidegger's *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* (GA 3)

## Section II. Repetition of the elucidation of the concept of phenomenon: transcendence and immanence

Henry is cited by section and paragraph (R §23 ¶4), counting the typographical paragraphs of each section, since the edition used carries no page numbers. Heidegger's Kantbuch is cited by GA 3 section and page, read from the running heads of the German text. Henry quotes the book from the 1953 French translation by Alphonse de Waelhens and Walter Biemel ("K" and a page). This commentary reads that translation in a re-OCR of the Gallimard edition whose pagination is Henry's, and the translators' introduction in an earlier conversion. Each of Henry's references is located in the French and in the German and given once as "K n / GA 3 §m, p". Where Henry's French departs from the German, the commentary says whether the departure is the translators' or his own: a translators' rendering counts against his reading only where his argument rests on it, and his own cuts count in full. All English translations are working renderings. Each unit opens by restating Henry's move in his own terms, without a repeated "Henry argues"; everything after that is assigned explicitly to Henry, to Heidegger, to Kant or to this commentary.

The pair has its own traps. GA 3 speaks in three voices: Kant quoted, Kant paraphrased under Heidegger's interpretation, and Heidegger's own fundamental ontology. Henry cites all three as "Heidegger says", so each quotation has to be assigned before his use of it can be judged. Henry's categories (ontological monism, the essence that objectivates itself, the milieu of exteriority, receptivity as the essence's reception of itself) are his, never GA 3's. The 1953 translation supplies much of Henry's vocabulary. It renders letting-stand-over-against (*Gegenstehenlassen*) as "ob-jectivation", hyphenated in most places to keep the "against" visible; Henry's own prose drops the hyphen, and the word then reads as a making-into-an-object. It renders taking-in (*Hinnahme*) as "réceptivité", selfhood (*Selbstheit*) as "ipséité", and pure self-affection (*reine Selbstaffektion*) as "affection pure de soi" or, once, "auto-affection pure", the name the translators' introduction gives the doctrine. Wherever Henry leans on these words, the rendering counts as part of his reading. The introduction is itself an intermediary: it glosses Heidegger's time as what "forms itself by going out of itself to direct itself toward itself", "the becoming of the self in exteriorization" (K 40–41), formulas that anticipate Henry's reading of transcendence as a going out of oneself into exteriority. Heidegger's pure self-affection (§34) forms the movement from itself toward and back to itself that first makes a self. It is not the affecting of a self already there, nor what Henry calls auto-affection, and Henry's verdict on it has to be tested against §34 rather than against his restatement. Taking-in (*Hinnahme*), which defines finitude in GA 3, is not empirical reception: Heidegger makes pure intuition a taking-in that gives itself what it takes, and warns against the narrow concept (§32, 173–174). Finally, chronology. Henry read the second edition in translation and could not know the marginalia printed in GA 3, the 1973 preface, the Davos disputation (first printed in 1973), or the Kant lectures of 1927–28 (GA 25). And his §§17–21 read the essay on Hegel's concept of experience (*Holzwege*, written 1942–43), a later Heidegger than the Kantbuch's, whom Henry treats as one "philosophy of being".

### § 17. The originary character of the manifestation of being and the problem of natural consciousness

**¶1.** Henry splits two senses of "being must be able to show itself": a possibility realized only when phenomenology completes its work of elucidation, or a manifestation already effective as the condition of that work and of every relation to beings. He takes the second and calls it the absolute. No GA 3 passage is cited; the first sense echoes the definition of phenomenology in *Sein und Zeit* §7, where what phenomenology must bring to view is what initially does not show itself. Against the Kantbuch the split is less new than it looks. GA 3 is built on the same "already": knowledge of beings is possible only on the ground of a prior knowledge of their constitution of being (§7, 38), and transcendental inquiry thematizes an understanding of being already at work (§3, 16). It also has the "at last": the laying of the ground unveils its origin by a regress (§§6–8). Henry's departure lies in the word "absolute". In GA 3 the prior understanding of being is "the innermost ground" of human finitude (§41, 228); for Henry the prior manifestation is absolute and complete. He leaves Heidegger's "already" standing and changes its rank.

**¶2.** Henry turns on a sentence he quoted at §16 from the Kantbuch (K 279 / GA 3 §40, 223): to understand the determination of beings by being, "the determining element itself must show itself with a certain clarity". He reads it as deferring being's manifestation to a future elucidation, and answers that the determination of beings by being, hence the manifestation of being, precedes every philosophical understanding of it. Henry quotes de Waelhens and Biemel exactly, and the German says something else. Heidegger writes that the determining must be "sufficiently graspable" and that being "must first be grasped" (*begriffen*): the sentence concerns the order of concept-formation within the question of being, not the moment at which being shows itself. The translators render "graspable" as "se montre" and "grasped" as "comprendre": "il est donc nécessaire de comprendre d'abord l'être" (it is therefore necessary first to understand being). Henry's objection rests on both renderings. At §16 he enlisted the sentence for his own requirement that the essence show itself; here he corrects it; both times he reads a claim about the concept as a claim about manifestation. Three pages on, Heidegger states Henry's thesis himself: "we understand being and yet lack the concept", and we understand it "at all times and in the whole field of the manifestness of beings" (§41, 226–227), so that the question of being "springs from the pre-conceptual understanding of being" (§40, 226). Heidegger's "already" and "at last" are understanding and concept, kept apart exactly as Henry wants them kept apart. In the French they are not kept apart, because "comprendre" serves for both: "nous comprenons donc l'être quoique son concept nous manque" (we therefore understand being though its concept escapes us, K 283) stands beside "il est nécessaire de comprendre d'abord l'être" (K 279). The ambiguity Henry denounces in the sentence is the translation's. The disagreement with Heidegger lies elsewhere, in the status of the pre-conceptual. Heidegger calls the being so understood indeterminate, familiar yet unknown, and questionless (227); Henry calls its manifestation originary and effective. The misreading still isolates the question Section II presses: whether the "already" is an understanding at all.

**¶3.** Henry restates the determination of beings by being as the dependence of what appears on appearing, and insists that the presence of being in which natural consciousness lives is not a presupposition reached by reflection but present in itself: "the reality of the relation is its manifestation". From Heidegger's Hegel essay (*Holzwege* 126) he takes the term Parousia, the presence of the absolute that science reaches "at its first step". Two departures from GA 3 open here. The first is of method. GA 3 reaches the understanding of being as the ground of the possibility of relating to beings, by a regress to conditions (§§3, 6–8). Henry demands that the condition itself be manifest, which no condition of possibility is merely by being one; the demand anticipates his charge that the philosophy of transcendence lacks a phenomenology of the ground (R §§28–29). Heidegger has a partial answer, since in GA 3 the understanding of being is not inferred but lived, in every statement and every mood (§41, 227). What he does not ask is how the understanding of being is manifest to the one who understands. "The reality of the relation is its manifestation" will become that question, addressed to transcendence. The second departure is of rank. By passing to "the absolute" through the Hegel essay, Henry gives being's manifestation a title GA 3 refuses: there, "something like being is given only where finitude has come to exist" (§41, 228).

**¶4.** Henry draws the consequence that consciousness is itself the manifestation of being, so that there is no introduction to phenomenology and the phenomenology of spirit is consciousness itself. Both quotations (*Holzwege* 190, 189) are Heidegger's exegesis of Hegel: natural consciousness cannot be led to where it already is, and it stands in the Parousia "according to the habit of natural consciousness". Henry turns exegesis into thesis, and adopts a word GA 3 avoids. The Kantbuch locates the understanding of being not in consciousness but in "the Dasein in the human being" (§41, 229), and at Davos in 1929 Heidegger said outright that to call Dasein consciousness "is precisely what I rejected" (GA 3, 289; retrospective). Henry does not ignore the refusal so much as dispute its ground. Section I argued that the philosophy of being and the philosophy of consciousness share one concept of manifestation, so the name of its bearer decides nothing. What GA 3 calls the understanding of being in Dasein, Henry calls consciousness, because for him both are defined by their manifestation.

**¶5.** Henry glosses the habit of natural consciousness as its immediate condition: the essence of consciousness is the Parousia, "presence in its presence", being-present as such being present. On that ground he contests Hegel's claim that the immediate manifestation of truth abstracts from its being-present (PhE I 198). Being-present, the absolute concept, is not abstracted from the manifestation of beings; it constitutes it. Nothing from GA 3 is cited. What the unit takes from Heidegger without question is the equation of being with presence, which GA 3 treats as a problem. Heidegger's §44 argues that the ancient understanding of being as *ousia* means "constancy in presence" and asks whether it does not betray a projection of being upon time (§44, 240). Henry's "presence of presence" keeps the presence and drops the question of its time-character. The omission is a commitment: at R §24 Henry will argue that what makes time the ground of manifestation in GA 3 is its intuitive, not its temporal, character.

**¶6.** Henry concludes that being shows itself "originarily": what is first present is not a being but being-present itself, and the origin is at once ontological (it is being) and phenomenological (it shows itself). Hegel's remark that the early religious community confused the origin with the immediate existence of the first manifestation (PhE II 271–272) lets Henry place the concept in the origin as "the first act of showing". GA 3 is the silent foil. Its procedure is a regress to the origin, the "source-ground" of ontological knowledge (§§6, 8), and Heidegger concludes that the laying of the ground does not lead to "the sun-clear absolute evidence of a first proposition and principle" but "goes and points consciously into the unknown" (§6, 37). Henry will quote that sentence at R §25 as proof that the philosophy of transcendence rests truth on an older untruth. Here he sets the counter-thesis in advance: the origin is what shows itself first, and the unknown cannot be original. The two concepts of origin do different work. Heidegger's origin is measured by what it makes possible and is reached by unveiling; Henry's is measured by its own appearing and needs no unveiling.

**¶7.** Consciousness cannot forget the being that is its essence. To say it lives in forgetfulness of being means only that it holds to beings and counts being, the appearing of what appears, as nothing; being never stops showing itself while it is "forgotten". The citations (*Holzwege* 163, 144) aim at the Hegel essay and, through it, at the later doctrine of the forgetting of being. Against the Kantbuch the paragraph lands differently. GA 3 already has forgetting: "the finitude of Dasein, the understanding of being, lies in forgottenness", which "forms itself necessarily and constantly" (§42, 233). What is forgotten there is not being's disclosure. The understanding of being pervades human existence "unknown in its breadth, constancy, indeterminacy and questionlessness" (§41, 228), and being is understood so self-evidently "that it looks as though there were no such thing" (227). Henry's gloss on forgetting restates that description. Against GA 3, then, his correction corrects nothing, since Heidegger's forgetting already presupposes that being never stops being understood. The disagreement concerns the remedy. For Heidegger the forgotten must be wrested out by a re-membering that lets it "come toward us again in its innermost possibility" (§42, 233–234). Henry's next paragraphs deny that any such turn adds to manifestation.

**¶8.** The manifestation of being requires no radical change in the life of consciousness. Such a change occurs only when consciousness turns from beings to the appearing by which they appear: the reversal (*Umkehrung*), in which consciousness represents "the appearing as appearing" (*Holzwege* 178, 175) and appearing reaches representation. Henry is reporting Heidegger's Hegel, whose vocabulary is representation. GA 3 has no reversal but has its function: the explicit grasp of the understanding of being in fundamental-ontological "construction" (§42, 232–233). Henry gives the turn the status of representation, manifestation become an object for consciousness. That decision prepares his reading of the Kantbuch, where representing is everywhere and where he will find transcendence itself to be representation (R §§18, 23). It leaves out what GA 3 says of the turn. Construction there is a projecting, because understanding is "not only a kind of knowing but primarily a basic moment of existing as such" (§42, 233); it does not set the understanding of being before consciousness as an object.

**¶9.** In representing manifestation, the reversal accomplishes the appearing of appearing: consciousness represents itself in its appearing (*Holzwege* 181), the absolute is present to itself, and "the manifestation of the absolute to itself in the Parousia demands the reversal". The last sentence states the position Henry is about to reject and should not be read as his. Its GA 3 counterpart is not a reversal but self-affection. In §34 time as pure self-affection forms "the essence of anything like going-at-oneself", so that the finite self "can be something like self-consciousness" (189–190). There the self's relation to itself precedes and enables any representation of itself. Henry here fixes the alternative he will hold Heidegger to: either self-manifestation is produced by a turn that represents, or it belongs to the essence. GA 3's self-affection fits neither side cleanly, which is why Henry spends two sections on it (R §§24, 31).

**¶10.** Representing appearing rather than what appears makes philosophical knowledge true knowledge, knowledge of truth and not of beings, and the Parousia is the deed of true knowledge. Henry still reports Hegel through Heidegger. GA 3 draws the same distinction without the reversal. Ontological knowledge "is no knowledge, if knowledge means grasping beings", yet it is rightly called knowledge because it is "the original truth" Kant calls transcendental truth (§25, 123); Henry will quote the passage at R §23. For Heidegger this truth is not reached by a philosopher's turn. Ontological knowledge "forms" transcendence by keeping open the horizon in which the being of beings is seen in advance (§25, 123), and natural experience already lives in it. Henry is one step from saying so himself.

**¶11.** In representing the appearing of what appears, true knowledge represents the knowledge of beings itself; it is the self-knowledge of that knowledge, which Fichte and Schelling called transcendental knowledge. Henry takes "transcendental" in the post-Kantian sense of knowledge of knowledge, a sense GA 3 sets aside on purpose. Heidegger glosses Kant's definition thus: transcendental knowledge "investigates not beings themselves but the possibility of the prior understanding of being, that is, at the same time, the constitution of being of beings", and concerns reason's passing over (transcendence) to beings (§3, 16). For the Kantbuch the transcendental is ontological, not reflexive, and the *Critique* is no theory of knowledge. Henry's Fichtean gloss is not a misreading of GA 3, which is not yet his target, but it installs the reading of the transcendental he will bring to the Kantbuch: transcendence as a representation the essence gives itself. The shared word hides a difference of function.

**¶12.** Henry reverses the Hegelian order. Transcendental knowledge is not true knowledge in the sense of the reversal, because it is the knowledge natural consciousness already has; since the manifestation of beings is as such the manifestation of being, "natural consciousness is transcendental consciousness". Here Henry and the Kantbuch converge. In GA 3 transcendence is the constitution of finite knowing as such (§3, 16), and being is understood "in the whole field of the manifestness of beings" (§41, 226–227); natural consciousness, in Heidegger's terms, is transcending Dasein. Because the contest will be fought on this common ground, two differences should be fixed now. Henry identifies the two manifestations: the manifestation of a being "is, as such, the manifestation of being". GA 3 keeps them apart: "manifestness of beings (ontic truth) turns on the unveiledness of the constitution of being of beings (ontological truth)" (§2, 13), and its account of truth depends on the difference. And what Henry calls transcendental is the manifestation of being, whereas what GA 3 calls transcendental is transcendence, the passing-over to beings. Section II will argue that these are not the same.

**¶13.** Being is pure manifestation, and its manifestation is the self-manifestation of pure manifestation; this belongs to the essence and is not the doing of the reversal or of philosophical knowledge, which presuppose it. "Self-manifestation is the essence of manifestation." The thesis is Henry's own, the formal demand for the autonomy of the essence set at the close of Section I (R §16), and nothing in GA 3 corresponds to it. In the Kantbuch being is unveiled in a forming: ontological knowledge "forms" transcendence (§25, 123), and imagination forms the look of the horizon (§19, 90). Unveiling is something finite Dasein accomplishes in projecting, and what is unveiled is held open, not self-manifest. Henry's thesis is the exact counter-position. GA 3 has one place, though, where a self-relation is put at the ground: time as pure self-affection (§34). Henry's reading of the Kantbuch will turn on whether that self-affection is the self-manifestation he here requires or a counterfeit of it.

### § 18. The concept of representation: ontological structure and existential understanding

**¶1.** Henry denounces the ambiguity of "representation". In its first sense representation is a determinate mode of conscious life, an act aimed at a thematic object with its unreal noematic correlate. Natural consciousness represents beings in this sense, philosophical knowledge represents being in this sense, and the self-manifestation of being presupposes both and "does not occur in representation". True knowledge is therefore a particular mode of life and has nothing to do with absolute knowledge. GA 3 is not cited, but the word is the Kantbuch's: representing (*Vorstellen*) runs through it, from knowledge in general (§4) to the pure representing at the core of transcendence (§16). Henry's first concept is Husserl's act, not Kant's genus. Heidegger starts from the genus, taking Kant's "representation in general" in "the wide formal sense in which something indicates, announces, presents another" (§4, 22), and then shows that the pure representing at the root of knowledge is unthematic. Henry cuts the thematic act loose from the essence before he admits the genus at all; GA 3 never separates them in that order, because for Heidegger thematic and unthematic representing are both ways of letting something stand over against a finite knower.

**¶2.** In a second sense, "in conformity with the fundamental presuppositions of ontological monism", representation names the essence of manifestation, being's self-manifestation, and its object is being itself. Natural consciousness represents being in this sense without making it a theme; philosophical consciousness represents being thematically, as an object in the same sense as natural consciousness's beings. Henry's second sense reconstructs GA 3's pure representing, which Heidegger calls letting-stand-over-against (*Gegenstehenlassen*) and makes "the original concept" and original act of the understanding (§16, 74), and whose look stands "unobjectively and moreover unthematically in a fore-view" (§9, 47). The departure is in the subject of the verb. In GA 3 finite reason represents: pure representing is a turning-toward by which a finite being lets objectivity stand against it. In Henry's sentence being represents itself. Calling the identification of the two "monist" already contains the charge he will bring against the Kantbuch: the representing GA 3 describes is the only form of self-manifestation monism knows. On philosophy's objectifying of being, Heidegger's 1927 lectures speak in the same terms, calling ontology an "objectification of being" (GA 24 §22b; retrospective).

**¶3.** To the two representations correspond two objects: the determinate object given to an act, and the object of universal consciousness, which is objectivity, "being itself in the form of a horizon", the condition of every object; "it belongs to the essence of the horizon not to let itself be thematized". The paragraph rests, unacknowledged, on GA 3 §25: "the horizon must be unthematic, but nonetheless in view. Only so can it push what is encountered in it into the theme", and the X, the "object in general", means not a being but "the horizon of a standing-against" (123). Henry will quote that sentence at R §23 (K 180). His reconstruction is faithful; the departure is that he calls this horizon being itself. In GA 3 objectivity is the being of beings as appearances for a finite knower, Kant's horizon, since "only for finite knowledge is there anything like an object" (§5, 31). Henry lifts Heidegger's Kant into the definition of being for the whole philosophy of being. Retrospectively, Heidegger's own note on the Kantbuch from the 1930s, printed in the 1973 preface, pairs "being: beingness, objectness, and 'time'" as the book's frame and says its own path was thereby "blocked and made open to misinterpretation" (GA 3, XIII). Henry's equation names a limit Heidegger later conceded in the book, though not one he conceded in being. Henry's note 13 adds a seed: philosophy may represent only a "representation" of being, and the essence may look obscure only to thematic representation. That is the later charge, announced in advance, that the Kantbuch's "unknown" root (§6, 37) is an artefact of its method.

**¶4.** Heidegger says natural knowledge contains a contradiction: "in its representing of beings it does not heed being and yet must heed it" (*Holzwege* 136). Henry splits the two heedings. One is the representation that is the essence of consciousness, where being is the transcendental horizon; the other is an act that grasps being. Natural knowledge is therefore not merely "the concept of knowledge" but knowledge in an absolute sense, and the opposition between natural and true knowledge is inessential. Henry's target is the Hegel essay, but his remedy is the Kantbuch's own distinction between understanding and concept (§41, 226–227), together with its programme for the analysis of everydayness, which is to show "that and how all dealings with beings, for which it looks as though there were only beings, are already grounded in the transcendence of Dasein" (§43, 235). Against the 1943 formulation Henry restores the 1929 position. He goes beyond both only in the predicate "absolute", where GA 3 says the same understanding is "the most finite in the finite" (§41, 229).

**¶5.** Henry fixes a distinction he will use throughout. Heeding and not heeding being are existential determinations, modes of conscious life; the heeding inherent in the essence is an ontological structure, at once indifferent and immanent to every determination. Natural knowledge therefore *is* ontological, and Heidegger's "natural consciousness is and is not ... ontological" (*Holzwege* 163) is improper. The vocabulary is Heidegger's from *Sein und Zeit* §4, where existentiell understanding is Dasein's self-understanding in its factical possibilities and the existential concerns the structures of existence, and where Dasein is "ontologically" distinguished in being pre-ontological (SZ 12). Henry's correction of the Hegel essay thus agrees with the earlier Heidegger and with GA 3 §41. The departure lies in the relation he sets between structure and determination. "Indifferent and immanent" means that the essence prescribes none of its modes and can be read off none of them. For Heidegger the structures of existence are reached only through its possibilities: "the roots of the existential analytic are ultimately existentiell, that is, ontic" (SZ 13), and GA 3 begins the analytic from everydayness (§43, 234). Henry cuts that root. The cut is the condition of his method, which must describe the essence without depending on any mode of existence, and it is the first point at which he and the Kantbuch part on how the ground is reached.

**¶6.** One says improperly that natural consciousness is not ontological because one qualifies a consciousness by the sense of its object, as one calls a consciousness mathematical; noesis and noema form one structure, so "an existential determination is a determination of the life of consciousness and, together with it, of its transcendent signification". Note 16 separates typical essences, such as perception, from the essence in the absolute sense. GA 3 is not engaged, but the definition reshapes a Heideggerian term. Henry now defines "existential" by Husserl's intentional correlation: an existential determination is an act together with its sense. In GA 3 existence means "dependence on beings as such in being delivered over to the being so dependent" (§41, 228), and the analytic of Dasein is kept from "an anthropological-psychological description of its 'experiences' and 'faculties'" (§43, 234). What Henry calls existential is close to what Heidegger excludes. The point bears on what follows: when Henry later measures the Kantbuch's transcendence as "representation", he measures it with the act-concept defined here.

**¶7.** To live in the presence of a signification is to understand, so every existential determination is an existential understanding, and an implicit self-understanding, regulated by the explicit understanding of the object, always accompanies it. Both presuppose existence itself and the transcendental horizon of being, and these are one: being's self-manifestation. "Existential understanding finds its ground in the ontological understanding of being", which is "the original manifestation of being to itself in consciousness" and precedes every act. Two Heideggerian doctrines are taken up. That existence implicitly understands itself from its objects restates Heidegger's thesis that Dasein tends to understand its own being from the beings it deals with, from the "world" (SZ §5, 15). The "ontological understanding of being" is the Kantbuch's understanding of being (*Seinsverständnis*), and here the reinscription is decisive. In GA 3 that understanding is a projecting: "in order to let beings be what and how they are, the existing being must always already have projected what it encounters upon its being a being" (§41, 228), and existence "is in itself finitude". Henry turns the projecting understanding into the manifestation of being to itself. The subject passes from Dasein to being and the projection drops out. Nothing in GA 3 supports the change, and Henry offers it as a definition rather than a reading. It is the first place where a central concept of the Kantbuch is taken over in Henry's sense, and every later charge against the philosophy of transcendence depends on keeping both senses in view.

**¶8.** Absolute knowledge resides in the ontological understanding of being. Philosophical knowledge is an existentiel or, better, existential (*existentiale*) understanding of that ontological understanding, and the implicit self-understanding of existence is always, implicitly, such an understanding. Philosophy merely thematizes it with the conceptual material required. Henry uses the pair as *Sein und Zeit* defines it; the companion commentary on *L'essence de la manifestation* in this collection inverts the two terms when glossing this paragraph. The thesis has its GA 3 counterpart: the question of being "springs from the pre-conceptual understanding of being" (§40, 226), and fundamental-ontological construction brings out what Dasein already understands (§42). Henry reconstructs it closely, with one omission. For Heidegger thematization is not neutral elaboration: what it takes up must be "wrested from forgottenness" against Dasein's own tendency (§42, 233), and a marginal note calls the philosophical question a "conditioned necessity" (GA 3, 225; retrospective). In Henry thematization is conceptual work on an implicit understanding, and nothing in existence resists it.

**¶9.** The ontological understanding of being is radically independent of every existential understanding: whether existence understands itself truly or falsely, "authentically" or "inauthentically", changes nothing in its original nature. Henry draws from Marx the absolute opposition between what existence is in itself and how it represents itself, reads Marx's "consciousness" as that self-representation, and declares that his inquiry concerns only the pure ontological element prior to any understanding of it. The barb is aimed at Heidegger's authenticity. In *Sein und Zeit*, and in GA 3's summary of its course through conscience, guilt and death to temporality (§44, 242), the existential analytic reaches the ground only by way of authentic existence. Henry denies that any existential modification bears on the ontological element. Heidegger is less far away than the barb suggests: he too holds that inauthentic everydayness already rests on transcendence (§43, 235), so that the structure is present in every mode, as Henry says. The difference is one of access. For Heidegger the structures are disclosed through a mode of existence; for Henry existence's understanding of itself has no bearing on what existence is. The appeal to Marx turns that difference into a principle of method.

### § 19. Being-for-itself from the ontological point of view and from the existential point of view: consciousness and truth

**¶1.** The distinction between ontological structure and existential self-understanding removes an equivocation on the for-itself. The term names both the essence of existence, which is self-manifestation, and a particular determination in which existence understands itself, thematically or implicitly, authentically or not. Ontology concerns existence as it is in itself, not as it understands itself; that self-understanding is existence's "experience", valid only "for it", as Hegel's unhappy consciousness of Judaism understands itself as an existence that leaves the essence outside it. The paragraph closes on a question: how can existence that is the for-itself fail to be "for itself"? Henry is reading Hegel, and "experience" comes from Heidegger's Hegel essay. The GA 3 parallel lies ahead, in §34. There Heidegger also separates an ontological self-relation from every act of self-relating. It is not that a mind exists and "among other things also relates something to itself for itself and performs self-positings"; the movement "from itself toward ... and back to itself" first constitutes the mind as a finite self (191), so that the self "can be something like self-consciousness" (190). Henry's ontological for-itself and Heidegger's pure self-affection occupy the same place, a self-relation prior to every act. At R §31 Henry will contest whether Heidegger's candidate is entitled to it. Here he fixes, in Hegelian terms, the place the contest is about.

**¶2.** Existence is the for-itself because its structure is the self-manifestation of the essence; it fails to be "for itself" only in that it does not grasp its essence in an act. The ontological for-itself is radically independent of the existential for-itself. A separation of essence and existence in representation is unreal, not because it is false but because it occurs in representation, as a signification aimed at in a mode of conscious life; and the unity of the two understood in true knowledge is just as unreal. Note 19 points forward to the problem of the real and the unreal (R §§31, 66, 67). The bearing on GA 3 lies in the criterion. Whatever is represented, as the correlate of an act, is unreal; at R §31 Henry will apply this to the Kantbuch's horizon, which is the represented par excellence. GA 3 also denies the horizon the status of a being: the X is "not a being, but nonetheless 'something'", "a nothing" if object means a being thematically grasped (§25, 122–123). Heidegger's "nothing", though, marks the horizon's ontological rank, not its derivation from an act. Henry's criterion will read the one as the other. The decision about the Kantbuch is taken here, on Hegelian ground.

**¶3.** The unhappy consciousness does not represent the unity of essence and existence; that unity is "for us", and at the end of its experiences it will be "for itself". Every unity that is for consciousness, for us or for itself is equally unreal, and to the unreal separation stands opposed not an unreal unity but the real unity of existence and essence: existence is the essence whether or not it understands itself so, "the original being-for-itself of being itself". GA 3 is not cited, but the argument reaches it. The Kantbuch's Fourth Section is a retrieval that brings Dasein's understanding of being out of forgottenness into explicit grasp (§42, 233); in Henry's terms its result is a "for us" and as unreal as what it overcomes. Henry's critique of Hegel thus disqualifies in advance the ontological significance of the Kantbuch's own procedure. And the closing phrase makes being the subject of its own for-itself, as at R §18 ¶7. GA 3 knows no being-for-itself of being, only a finite self constituted by time.

**¶4.** Hegel's distinction between what is for consciousness and what is for us does not touch the ontological structure of being-for-itself. The *Phenomenology of Spirit*, describing existence as consciousness represents it, never deals with reality; the becoming-for-itself of consciousness's in-itself has only existential meaning; the phenomenology of spirit, the manifestation of manifestation, lies in the in-itself of consciousness and is an ontological structure. The reasoning applies to the Kantbuch's account of its own act, and there it meets resistance. Heidegger holds that metaphysics "is not something merely 'created' by human beings in systems and doctrines": the understanding of being, "its projection and its rejection, happens in Dasein as such", and metaphysics is "the basic happening" of the irruption into beings (§44, 242). Fundamental ontology "makes itself true" by bringing Dasein to its manifestness and letting "its inner metaphysics be there" (§42, 233). For Heidegger philosophizing is itself a happening of the understanding of being, not a representation of it. Henry's separation of ontological from existential denies exactly this: the manifestation of spirit in true knowledge is "a manifestation among others" that presupposes the essence. The two agree that the ground precedes philosophy and disagree on whether philosophy belongs to the ground's own happening. Henry's answer forecloses the Fourth Section's claim to retrieve anything real.

**¶5.** Consciousness is in itself the truth. When it understands itself in its truth it sees that what it took for beings is its own knowledge of beings; but truth so understood is a signification, the correlate of an act, transcendent to philosophical consciousness as what it surpasses itself toward, and to natural consciousness as what it fails to reach. That is existential truth. Ontological truth is what consciousness is in itself, whatever it represents as truth. GA 3 also has two truths: "manifestness of beings (ontic truth) turns on the unveiledness of the constitution of being of beings (ontological truth)" (§2, 13), and ontological knowledge is "the original truth" (§25, 123). The pairs do not match. Heidegger's division runs between beings and being. Henry's runs between what consciousness represents, being included, and what consciousness is. Henry's "ontological truth" is therefore not Heidegger's: Heidegger's is the unveiledness of being, held open in transcendence; Henry's is consciousness being the truth in itself. The shared adjective covers two different divisions.

**¶6.** Ontological truth is the truth "that consciousness is in itself", since its being-in-itself is being-for-itself: truth is "truth for itself", and the being-for-itself of truth is consciousness. Consciousness is never separated from truth, which "is not transcendent to consciousness", never exterior, never "unknown"; only an existential truth can be absent from it. Both key words point at the Kantbuch. "Unknown" anticipates R §25, where Henry quotes GA 3's conclusion that the laying of the ground "points consciously into the unknown" (§6, 37) as proof that the philosophy of transcendence rests truth on an older untruth. "Transcendent" sets the main line of Section II. GA 3 puts original truth in transcendence in so many words: "if truth means unconcealment of ..., then transcendence is the original truth" (§25, 123). Henry's claim that original truth is not transcendent to consciousness is the first statement of the thesis he will turn on the Kantbuch, that the original conditions of truth do not belong to the milieu of exteriority (R §26). The opposition is exact, and Henry has not yet argued for his side.

### § 20. Critique of the Hegelian concept of experience

**¶1.** For Hegel, experience is consciousness's experience of truth: first it measures its knowledge against beings, so that the measure lies outside it; then it learns that beings are only beings for it, so that the measure lies within. Henry finds two senses in which consciousness is the truth of beings. It is that truth ontologically, as the original manifestation of being, and this holds for natural consciousness too. Or it understands itself as that truth, as having the measure in itself, which is a determinate self-representation. The paragraph is about Hegel, but the turn from the measure outside to the measure within is Kant's Copernican turn, which GA 3 reads ontologically: ontic knowledge can never conform to objects on its own, "because without ontological knowledge it would not even have a possible to-what" (§2, 13). And in GA 3 the measure is neither consciousness's being nor its self-image. Truth as "agreement with the object" requires that "something like a with-what of possible agreement" be encountered in advance, "something that regulates as a measure": the horizon of what stands against, which must be open beforehand (§24, 118). Henry's dichotomy, consciousness as the truth or consciousness representing itself as the truth, has no place for this third term, a measure formed in transcendence. His later reading of the Kantbuch will have to assign the horizon to one side or the other, and he will assign it to representation.

**¶2.** Consciousness becomes the truth of beings for itself, in representation, only because it is that truth in itself; the self-representation is a result and cannot be a principle. Hegel makes it one: experience compares knowledge and object because both are "for consciousness" (PhE I 74), so that the movement of experience, and with it reality, is explained by the play of consciousness's representations. "Our history is the product of what we think." GA 3 is not engaged, but the Hegelian target stands in for a position the Kantbuch holds in its own terms. In GA 3 understanding is constitutive of existence: the existing being "must always already have projected" what it encounters upon its being (§41, 228), and understanding is "primarily a basic moment of existing" (§42, 233). Projection is not a representation, yet it is not indifferent to what existence is. Henry's alternative, reality or representation of reality, sorts projection with representation without arguing it; here it only aims at Hegel.

**¶3.** Hegel's statement that consciousness is "on one side consciousness of the object, on the other consciousness of itself" is ambiguous. The object is for consciousness because consciousness represents it; consciousness is for itself because it is the truth in itself, the original being-for-itself. Self-consciousness is therefore not comparable to consciousness of the object but constitutes its essence. To build a dialectic on their comparison, Hegel must treat self-consciousness as a self-representation, confusing the ontological sense of being-for-itself with the existential. The thesis that self-consciousness is the essence of object-consciousness rather than a second consciousness beside it is formally Kant's, as GA 3 reads him. There, pure apperception is "the pure letting-stand-over-against of ..., the I itself" (§34, 190), and "time and the 'I think' ... are the same" (191). Henry agrees with the form and will dispute the content. For Heidegger the self-consciousness that grounds object-consciousness is itself the turning-toward that lets objects stand against it. For Henry it will be a self-manifestation that does not turn toward anything (R §§30–31). The ground on which he refuses Hegel's comparison is the ground on which he will refuse Heidegger's identification.

**¶4.** By confusing self-representation with the original being-for-itself, Hegel places consciousness's representation of its knowledge at the start of experience and makes the comparison consciousness's own act, so that "we" have only to look on (PhE I 74). The principle of experience is indeed immanent to consciousness, but Hegel interprets that immanence as presentation in representation, and being-for-itself falls to the rank of an object. "In such a fall lies the passage from reality to the representation of reality, from reality to unreality." Henry draws the paradox of all intellectualism: having said that everything is for consciousness, Hegel must say that something is not for it. The paradox has a GA 3 instance. The Kantbuch's ground is "unknown": Kant's "root unknown to us", into which, Heidegger says, Kant must have looked, where "the unknown is not that of which we know nothing at all, but what presses toward us within the known as the disquieting" (§31, 160). In Henry's terms, a philosophy that places manifestation in representing must find its ground outside what is represented, and call it unknown. He states the general form here and applies it at R §25.

**¶5.** In experience, consciousness learns that its first object, the in-itself, was only an in-itself for it; the second object is the truth of the first, but "this consideration of the matter is our doing" (PhE I 77), and the genesis of the new object happens "behind its back". What is for us, Henry replies, is as unreal as what is for consciousness. Real experience happens neither before consciousness nor behind it, "in the original ontological dimension of being-for-itself". The confusion of reality with representation forces the intellectualist to place whatever is not displayed before consciousness elsewhere, as another display: intellectualism, "Hegel's or Freud's, is a doctrine of the unconscious". The diagnosis applies to the Kantbuch with uncomfortable precision. GA 3's ground is the transcendental imagination, which Kant called "a blind though indispensable function of the soul ... of which we are seldom even conscious", a sentence Heidegger quotes (§14, 63) and then makes a clue: "is it an accident" that Kant says this of the faculty that will prove to be the "unknown common root" (§27, 137)? Henry's thesis predicts this result. A philosophy that defines manifestation as representing must locate its own ground in what is not represented, a function at work behind consciousness's back. Heidegger presents the unconsciousness of imagination as a sign of its originality; Henry's diagnosis makes it a symptom of the starting point.

**¶6.** Hegel finds the origin of experience in the inequality between consciousness of the object and of itself; Heidegger, glossing him, calls it the difference between natural knowledge and real knowledge. Henry identifies real knowledge with the original manifestation of being that makes the manifestation of beings possible, and concludes that this difference cannot be abolished: it persists in true knowledge, since the existential truth of philosophy is not the ontological truth of real knowledge. Hegel made the difference abolishable by turning real knowledge into a representation, comparable with natural knowledge, and so replaced truth by its representation from the start. The thesis that the difference cannot be abolished is Heidegger's own in the Kantbuch, applied against Hegel. In GA 3 ontological knowledge never absorbs ontic knowledge: it "has only 'empirical use'", serving the experience of beings that show themselves (§25, 124), and the difference between unveiledness of being and manifestness of beings is fixed (§2, 13). Henry reconstructs the irreducibility of the ontological difference faithfully. Where he departs is in what fills the place of real knowledge. For Heidegger it is ontological knowledge, which forms transcendence; for Henry it is the self-manifestation of being. The charge he lays on Hegel here, that real knowledge was made a representation, is the charge he will lay on the Kantbuch, where ontological knowledge is pure representing.

### § 21. The reaffirmation of the originary character of the manifestation of being in the bringing-to-light of its non-historical character

**¶1.** Confusing true with real knowledge leads Hegel to place real knowledge at the end: "the absolute is essentially result" (PhE I 19). What results, Henry answers, is only the absolute's representation; the question how consciousness reaches absolute knowledge must be settled at the beginning, since consciousness neither reaches nor leaves it. "The problem of absolute knowledge is the problem of the internal structure of consciousness in general." The sentence sets the task Henry will pursue through the Kantbuch, and its key phrase transposes GA 3's. Heidegger defines the laying of the ground as "the unveiling of the inner possibility of ontology" (§2, 12), and each of its stages asks after the inner possibility of transcendence. Henry's "internal structure" asks something else: not what makes manifestation possible, but how manifestation is effectively accomplished. Where Heidegger seeks a condition, Henry seeks an act. The shift shows at R §23, where Henry glosses the inner possibility of transcendence as "the possibility of its effective accomplishment".

**¶2.** Absolute knowledge is not natural knowledge but its essence. In what stands at the beginning Henry separates the mode in which consciousness begins its history from what makes that beginning possible, the beginning itself, "the upsurge of the effective dimension of phenomenality" in which anything can be manifest. Absolute knowledge is equally the essence of true knowledge, which represents its own origin but presupposes it, and it is foreign to the history that leads from one to the other. Henry's "dimension of phenomenality" has a precise GA 3 counterpart. In its original turning-toward, finite reason "holds before itself a leeway within which something can 'correspond' to it", and "to hold oneself in advance in such a leeway, to form it originally, is nothing other than transcendence" (§16, 71). In GA 3 the upsurge of the dimension is transcendence. Henry will argue that the dimension transcendence forms is not the origin but what the origin makes effective. The word "effective" carries the argument: Heidegger's leeway is formed and held open, while Henry's dimension must also manifest itself. Here the two descriptions can still be read as one. Section II will split them.

**¶3.** The distinction between ontological and existential truth fixes what is historical. Only the truth existence represents about itself has a history and becomes "equal" to its object at the end; truth, the concept, is there before. Hegel's statement that spirit exists as knowing what it is only after the work by which it creates for its consciousness "the figure of its essence" (PhE II 303–304) holds only of the figure. GA 3 binds the ground to history the other way round. Its Fourth Section defines retrieval as "the disclosure of a basic problem's original, hitherto concealed possibilities" (GA 3, 204), and the transcendental interpretation of historicity on the basis of temporality is to give "a preliminary concept of the kind of being of that happening which occurs in the retrieval of the question of being" (§44, 242). For Heidegger the ground is temporality, and philosophy's retrieval is a happening of it. For Henry the essence is non-historical and the work of philosophy merely historical. The non-historical thesis is the first form of the atemporal thesis of R §24.

**¶4.** The "equality" of consciousness with its essence is improper, since reality and its representation never coincide, and a self-consciousness that arises only after a preliminary process has no ontological meaning. Even the "enthusiasm that begins straight away with absolute knowledge, like a pistol shot" (PhE I 25) is made possible by absolute knowledge as the first act of appearing. History is not "the movement by which appearing comes about" (*Holzwege* 140). "What is at the beginning is not the modesty of an obscure ground; it is the Word." The obscure ground (*Grund*) is first Schelling's, which Hegel opposes, but *Grund* also names the Kantbuch's whole project, and the Kantbuch ends its regress before an abyss: "does this laying of the ground not lead before an abyss? ... He saw the unknown. He had to shrink back" (§31, 168). Against both Henry sets the Word, the self-revealing logos he will develop with Eckhart in Section III. The contrast is between a ground that withdraws and a beginning that utters itself, and Henry's thesis that the absolute is "presupposed as the condition of any history" leaves no place at the origin for GA 3's abyss. "Modesty" is polemic rather than argument: it reads Heidegger's admission of the unknown as an evasion, a reading R §25 makes explicit.

**¶5.** Because the absolute has its reality in the origin, the historical representation of truth is no condition of it. To Hyppolite's question how a timeless absolute knowledge can have temporal conditions in the existence of humanity, Henry answers that it has none. It is therefore wrong to say, "as Heidegger does following Hegel", that "representation belongs to the essence of experience" (*Holzwege* 176, 175). The sentences are Heidegger's exegesis of Hegel, but Henry is right to take them as Heidegger's own view as well; the Kantbuch says of finite knowledge, in Kant's terms, that knowledge as "objective perception" "is a representing" (§4, 23). Hyppolite's question receives its most radical answer in GA 3, not in Henry. For Heidegger there is no timeless knowledge of being at all, because the immediate understanding of being moves "through and through in an original, but also self-evident, projection of being upon time" (§44, 240). Henry's denial of temporal conditions is aimed here at history. When he extends it to time at R §24, it will meet the Kantbuch's central thesis head-on.

**¶6.** Representation would belong to the essence only if reversal and history were needed for manifestation to become effective, yet effective manifestation is their condition. That this is forgotten shows that "the original character of revelation" is unclear to the thought that forgets it: representation misrepresents the essence and inserts it into representation's own history. Ontology must respect the origin. "What spirit is, it always was in itself" (Hegel). The essence is not unfinished while representation fails to grasp it, and Henry rejects the statement that consciousness "is something it is not yet" (*Holzwege* 167): what consciousness is not yet is not consciousness and does not belong to reality. The target is Hegel through Heidegger, but the widest target is the futurity GA 3 builds into the self. In the Kantbuch the pure synthesis of recognition is "the original forming of ... the future", and Heidegger asks whether time does not "temporalize itself primarily out of the future" (§33, 186–187); the self is temporal from the ground up (§34). Henry's sentence excludes from reality what Heidegger makes the leading dimension of the self's constitution. The exclusion stays implicit here and becomes explicit when Henry judges the Kantbuch's self-affection.

**¶7.** Philosophical knowledge misses its aim when it questions itself, unless the question becomes one about the essence of all knowing. "Philosophy always comes too late, because what it says was at the beginning." Henry turns Hegel's owl of Minerva against Hegel. Against GA 3 the sentence denies philosophy the power Heidegger claims for retrieval, to disclose possibilities "hitherto concealed" (GA 3, 204) and so transform the problem it retrieves. For Heidegger philosophy can come early, opening what was not yet open; for Henry nothing essential is ever not yet open. The closing sentence of the Hegelian part of Section II states the thesis Henry now brings to the Kantbuch: no forming, retrieving or representing adds anything to the manifestation already there.

### § 22. The interpretation of the essence of phenomenality within the fundamental presuppositions of monism and the problem of receptivity. Ontological signification of this problem

**¶1.** Henry recapitulates. Sections 17 to 21 showed that manifestation is originary, but within monism that means the manifestation in and for itself of the pure horizon in which the essence objectivates itself. The essence does its work by itself, and in this lies its autonomy (*Selbständigkeit*); the problem is how that autonomy is possible, "how the manifestation of the pure horizon of being is possible". With this sentence Henry turns to the Kantbuch and adopts its vocabulary of horizon and objectivation, with two shifts that should be kept in view. "Objectivation" comes from the 1953 translation, which writes "ob-jectivation" for letting-stand-over-against and keeps the hyphen in most of its uses (K 244, 248). Henry's prose drops the hyphen, and with it the "against" the translators kept visible, so that "the essence objectivates itself" makes the horizon the essence's projection of itself, where in GA 3 it is finite reason that "holds before itself a leeway" (§16, 71). And *Selbständigkeit* is not a GA 3 term for ontological knowledge; the word occurs there only of the supposed independence of logic (§29, 148). Henry's question, though, is one the Kantbuch asks of itself. Having shown that transcendence lets objectivity stand against it, Heidegger asks what belongs to "making the horizon perceptible" (§19, 90). Henry starts where Heidegger turns to schematism.

**¶2.** The horizon is what the essence, as transcendence, opposes to itself. Opposing is a real and lasting opposition only if what is opposed is held near the essence, and it can be held near only by being received. Opposition and reception together make objectivation possible; "the receptivity of the horizon is identically its manifestation"; and the question of what gives the essence its reality concentrates in the problem of receptivity. Henry reconstructs GA 3 §19 almost step by step. There the turning-toward must be "a pre-forming holding-before-itself of what has the character of an offer"; the horizon needs perceptibility, and "perceptible means: immediately takable-in in intuition"; so the horizon must "offer itself in advance and constantly as a pure look" (§19, 90). Henry's "held near" renders Heidegger's holding-before (*Sich-vorhalten*), and at §34 time's holding the look "toward itself as the forming taking-in" (189). His name for the problem comes from his edition. The translators render Heidegger's taking-in (*Hinnahme*) as "réceptivité", so that the finite being "dependent on taking-in" becomes "un être soumis à la réceptivité", a being subject to receptivity (K 244 / GA 3 §34, 189). In Henry's French, Kant's receptivity and Heidegger's taking-in are one word. The departure is Henry's identification: to be received "means to give oneself, to appear, to manifest oneself". Heidegger grounds the horizon's perceptibility in a forming, since the horizon is made perceptible by being formed as a look in imagination (§19, 90–91). Henry makes reception the essence of manifestation and so moves the weight from the forming to the taking-in. His whole reading of the Kantbuch's §§19–35 turns on that shift.

**¶3.** Receptivity is ontological twice over. What it receives is not a being but the pure phenomenological milieu in which beings can show themselves; and receptivity, as the act that holds near the essence what the essence posits before itself, is "the most essential in the essence", the ground of its internal coherence. The first point is the Kantbuch's own. Heidegger makes pure intuition a taking-in whose content "is no being ... but nonetheless not simply nothing", so that "pure taking-in representing must give itself something representable" and pure intuition "must in a certain way be 'creative'" (§9, 44). Later, "taking in purely means: being affected free of experience, that is, affecting oneself" (§34, 190). That receptivity has an ontological sense is not a discovery against GA 3 but one of its theses. The second point is Henry's. In GA 3 pure taking-in never comes before spontaneity: imagination is "the original and not first compounded unity of receptivity and spontaneity" (§29, 153), and pure intuition is "spontaneous receptivity". Henry ranks receptivity above that unity as the most essential in the essence. The move is a radicalization: it takes one moment of Heidegger's unity and makes it the ground of the unity.

**¶4.** The ontological character of receptivity has been masked because receptivity is first thought from the reception of beings and bound to finitude, so that ontological knowledge, which precedes all reception, is first interpreted as creative. Henry quotes the Kantbuch (K 98 / GA 3 §7, 38) and asks whether ontological knowledge is not receptive with respect to being itself. The quotation follows the 1953 French word for word and is misleading in its cut. The translators kept Heidegger's parenthesis, "un connaître non réceptif (apparemment non fini)" (K 98), and Henry's ellipsis removes it, so the cut is his: finite knowledge requires a non-receptive "(seemingly non-finite)" knowing, "something like a 'creative' intuition". The next sentence turns the appearance into the problem: "how can a finite being, which as such is delivered over to beings and dependent on taking them in, know beings before all taking-in, without being their 'creator'?" (§7, 38). Heidegger's answer, pure intuition as a taking-in that gives itself what it takes (§9, 44), is what Henry offers as his own question. Henry's "first interpreted as creative" describes a step Heidegger takes only in order to dismantle it. (The companion commentary on Henry's book first reported that Heidegger "inherits" the contrast, repeating Henry's framing; it was corrected on 2026-10-05.) Henry's conclusion, that referring ontic to ontological knowledge sharpens the problem of receptivity instead of solving it, is also Heidegger's, since the following stages of the Kantbuch are driven by exactly that problem. His edition even states the resolution before the text begins: the translators' introduction poses the same antinomy, "how can a finite, that is, non-creative knowledge nevertheless precede experience?", and answers that a knowledge "at once finite and pure, receptive and giving" is possible in pure intuition (K 22). Henry stands closer to the Kantbuch here than his ellipsis lets him appear.

**¶5.** Ontological knowledge, which forms the horizon and receives it in one act, is creative and receptive at once. The opposition of creative and receptive knowledge therefore holds only relative to beings, and "the Kantian distinction between *Entstand* and *Gegenstand*", what arises and what stands against, is inessential and obscure. The real problem is how a being, created or not, can manifest itself to knowledge; finitude is an ontological category grounded in the finite horizon; intuition is finite in itself. The voices need sorting first. The distinction of appearance and thing in itself as two relations to one and the same being is Kant's (*Opus postumum*, cited §5, 33). The word *Ent-stand* is Heidegger's: absolute knowing reveals beings "in letting them arise" and has them "as Ent-stand" (§5, 31). The translation renders it "création [*Ent-stand*]", creation (K 92), and the translators' introduction sets *Gegen-stand* against *Ent-stand* in German (K 21); Henry keeps the German pair. Henry's objection is aimed at Heidegger's construal under Kant's name. On the substance, Henry's first claim is Heidegger's: imagination's "forming power is at once a taking-in (receptive) and a creating (spontaneous) 'forming'" (§26, 129). His claim that finitude is ontological also follows the Kantbuch's direction, where ontological knowledge, "only ontologically and never ontically 'creative'", may "sink the finite 'subject' into its proper finitude" (§25, 124), and the understanding of being is "the most finite in the finite" (§41, 229). The departure lies in the ground of finitude. In GA 3 finitude is dependence on taking-in, hence on what one is not; for Henry it is the finitude of the horizon, whatever the source of what appears in it. Henry detaches finitude from dependence and attaches it to the form of manifestation.

**¶6.** A being is finite as object, that is, as intuited, because intuition rests on the essence's opposing a finite horizon to itself. The idea of an infinite original intuition "arises in the problematic without any context": either it is still intuition, and then finite, or it names "another mode of manifestation", and then by what right is it called intuition? "The *Entstand* too is an ob-ject, at least as long as it is intuited." Heidegger, Henry says, confuses an ontic character, a being's existing before a knowing that does not create it, with an ontological one, being an ob-ject, when he writes that only finite knowledge has anything like an object and only it is delivered over to the being already there (K 92 / GA 3 §5, 31). Henry's claim that the Entstand is an object contradicts Heidegger's explicit statement a few lines on, that infinite knowing is not even rightly described by saying it "first produces the 'object'" in intuiting (§5, 31). The translation gives that sentence on the page Henry cites (K 92). Henry can hold the claim only on his own premise, that every intuiting places what it intuits before itself. The charge of confusion also misses what *ausgeliefert* names, which the translators render "doit s'exposer à" (must expose itself to), an obligation where Heidegger names a condition (K 92). Being delivered over is in GA 3 the knower's existence, not a property of beings: "existence means dependence on beings as such in being delivered over to the being so dependent" (§41, 228). Heidegger grounds objecthood not in the ontic priority of beings but in the finitude of the knower, which needs "a turning-toward that lets stand against" (§16, 71). The charge fails, but the question survives: why call the absolute mode of revelation intuition at all? GA 3 itself still calls it "an intuiting". A later marginal note of Heidegger's sharpens the point in Henry's direction: "for God nothing at all that is a being, if 'being' belongs to finitude" (GA 3, 32; retrospective). The question had already been raised in Henry's edition: in a note the translators ask how Kant can stress the finitude of knowledge "without reducing it to a pure negativity" while describing human knowledge "by opposition to the intuitus originarius" (K 19 n. 1). Henry reads Kant's original intuition as the misnamed intimation of a revelation that is not intuition, his own immanence.

**¶7.** The critique of the Entstand and the Gegenstand forbids tying the receptive sense of knowledge to a character of beings. Receptivity concerns beings only in making their reception possible; what is received is not the being but its being; "the being of beings resides in its receptivity", and receptivity "constitutes the very essence of being". The sentence has no GA 3 counterpart. In the Kantbuch the being of beings for finite knowledge is objectivity, the horizon formed in letting-stand-over-against, and a later marginal note reads "objectivity is be-ing in the empirical sense" (GA 3, 31; retrospective). Henry takes that being-as-objectivity and moves its seat from the letting-stand-against to the taking-in of what is let stand. Heidegger's analysis contains both moments, the forming of the horizon and its being taken in. Henry's sentence picks one of them as the essence of being.

**¶8.** If the reception of beings rests on the receptivity of being, the receptivity of being must itself be made intelligible: what makes ontologically possible the manifestation of the pure horizon that the essence objectivates? The question is the Kantbuch's own transition. Having shown that the horizon must be perceptible, Heidegger asks "what belongs to this making-perceptible of the horizon of the prior turning-toward?", and answers that the finite being must make the horizon intuitable to itself by "forming" the look of the offer, which pure imagination does (§19, 90). Henry asks the same question and will reject the answer: at R §23 he argues that forming explains nothing about receiving. The two part not over the question but over whether a forming can answer it.

### § 23. The internal possibility of the receptivity of being and the problem of schematism

**¶1.** What must be received is the pure horizon of being, which, being nothing ontic, is the horizon of nothingness; but this nothingness is not nothing. Only a thought that takes beings as the measure of reality calls the horizon "nothing" and the power behind intuition an empty form, and only a thought that reserves "knowledge" for the grasp of beings calls it unknown. Henry quotes the Kantbuch: the horizon "must be unthematic, but nonetheless in view; only so can it push what is encountered in it into the theme" (K 180 / GA 3 §25, 123). The critique of thematism, he objects, is purely negative; the reality of the horizon is its mode of manifestation, and if the horizon is nothingness the task is "a phenomenology of nothingness". The translators' introduction had already made the link Henry draws here: what the turning-toward lets stand against "is not beings but the nothing", and "the experience of the nothing is the prior and necessary condition of any explicit understanding of being" (K 26), in the language of *Was ist Metaphysik?*. Henry's first claims are Heidegger's. GA 3 says the X is "a nothing" in the precise sense of "not a being, but nonetheless 'something'" (§25, 122), and that ontological knowledge "is no knowledge, if knowledge means grasping beings" (123). The departure is the charge of negativity. GA 3 gives the horizon positive determinations: it is a "pure horizon" that serves "only as correlate" (122), and in schematism the "against" is made "beholdable and thereby takable-in" a priori in the image of time (§22, 105). Henry counts these as negative because none of them says how the horizon shows itself to the one who forms it. That is the precise sense of his "phenomenology of nothingness": not a description of the horizon's content but of its self-showing, which the Kantbuch does not supply in §25 and will try to supply in §34.

**¶2.** The determination of what is received cannot explain receiving, since it presupposes it; the question of the horizon's reality must be put to receptivity. One indication remains: the power that posits a horizon that is not a being but nothingness is the power to posit something other than beings, imagination, and transcendental imagination, since it belongs to the essence of objectivation. It forms the horizon and receives it, and Henry quotes Heidegger's formula that imagination is "the original and not compounded unity of receptivity and spontaneity" (K 210 / GA 3 §29, 153). The question becomes how imagination can be receptive with respect to what it forms. Henry reconstructs GA 3 faithfully: imagination is a faculty of forming that is "not dependent on the presence of the intuitable" (§26, 129), and its transcendental form "forms" the horizon of objectivity (§19, 90). His question is also one GA 3 states, since the unity of receptivity and spontaneity is asserted, not yet explained, at §29. The divergence will come over what counts as an explanation.

**¶3.** Henry dismisses a "purely negative, and hence illusory" solution: that imagination receives the horizon because it forms it. Heidegger seems to offer it when he says of the pure intuitions that they take in the pure look of space and time, but that "this taking-in is in itself a forming act that gives itself what offers itself" (K 199 / GA 3 §28, 141). Forming, Henry replies, has a phenomenological sense only if what is formed manifests itself, that is, is received; "the essence of spontaneity lies in receptivity". Henry's report of the context is exact, and so is his French. His choice of sentence follows his edition: the translators' introduction quotes it as the key to the pure intuitions, whose "receptivity is creative" (K 33). The German is stronger than "seems to offer": "this taking-in is in itself precisely the forming self-giving of what gives itself". Heidegger asserts an identity, one act that is both a taking-in and a self-giving. Henry splits the act into two moments and orders them, with receiving first. The argument for the order is Henry's principle that nothing counts as formed unless it appears; GA 3 gives no ground for ranking either moment over the other. Henry is right that §28 asserts the identity rather than explaining it. He is wrong to call the assertion negative, and the explanation GA 3 offers comes later, when time "holds the look toward itself as the forming taking-in" and so "goes at itself" (§34, 189). Henry's real objection is to that later answer, and he will state it at R §§24 and 31.

**¶4.** The solution to imagination's receptivity, Henry claims, is to say no longer that intuition is imagination but that imagination is intuition: "imagination is also, and even above all, a faculty of intuition" (K 210), and, citing Kant, imagination is "always sensible" (K 204). The reversal happens exactly where the horizon's perceptibility, its receptivity, can no longer be deferred. Henry strings together Heidegger's sentences on the horizon's offer and perceptibility (K 148 / GA 3 §19, 90), on the "necessity of its immediate perceptibility in a pure intuition" (K 178–179 / GA 3 §25, 122), and on imagination as belonging to the faculty of intuition (K 187 / GA 3 §26, 128). The voices are mixed. "Always sensible" is Kant's phrase, which Heidegger raises as the apparent obstacle to deriving reason from imagination and then answers by reading sensibility as finite intuition (§29, 146). "Also and precisely a faculty of intuition, that is, receptivity" belongs to an objection Heidegger states in order to answer it, though he keeps its premise (§29, 153). The French keeps the objection's frame, "il faudrait lui opposer que ...", one would have to object that ... (K 210), and strengthens "and precisely" to "et même surtout", "and even above all"; the assignment of the sentence to Heidegger's own thesis is Henry's. "Belongs to the faculty of intuition" is his inference from Kant's definition in the *Anthropology* (§26, 128). Nor does GA 3 move from one thesis to the other: it says that imagination belongs to the faculty of intuition (§26, 128) before it says that pure intuition is imagination (§28, 141), and repeats the first at §29 (153), because imagination is the root of both stems. On the substance Henry inverts §19. There Heidegger makes the horizon perceptible by a forming: the finite being "must be able to make the horizon intuitable to itself, that is, to 'form' the look of the offer from itself" (90). Henry reads the same sentences as saying that intuition, by receiving, is the essence of the forming power.

**¶5.** What makes intuition itself possible? Its essence lies in objectivation: Henry's critique of intuitionism (Introduction §3) showed that intuition is possible only through the opening of the transcendental field. Yet once the horizon must manifest itself to do its work, intuition is asked to manifest it: "transcendence, in objectivation, must make intuitive the horizon formed by it" (K 149 / GA 3 §19, 91). Intuition is thus grounded in a horizon that becomes real only by being intuited, and the thought that asks intuition to realize its own condition moves in a circle. The sentence is Heidegger's, and the French keeps its conditional form: "si donc la transcendance, dans l'ob-jectivation, doit rendre intuitif l'horizon formé par celle-ci et si, d'autre part, on nomme sensibilité l'intuition finie" (if then transcendence, in ob-jectivation, must make intuitive the horizon formed by it, and if finite intuition is called sensibility, K 149). Henry drops the "if", harmlessly, since Heidegger endorses the premise. What the sentence says is that transcendence makes its own horizon intuitable: the subject of the making is transcendence, not a second faculty. In GA 3's terms the structure is reflexive rather than circular. Henry's circle appears only if the intuition that manifests the horizon is the same power that the horizon grounds.

**¶6.** Henry meets the obvious reply: the intuition grounded in transcendence was intuition of beings, the intuition of the horizon is pure, so pure intuition simply conditions empirical intuition. But pure intuition conditions empirical intuition by being its essence; "empirical intuition is pure insofar as it is intuition", since what makes it intuition is its power to make manifest, and that power, considered in itself, is understood as objectivation. The conditioned thus presents itself as the condition of the condition. Henry's reply turns on a sense of "pure" that is not GA 3's. In the Kantbuch "pure" means free of experience: pure intuition has its own content, the look of space and time, "a unified, though not empty, whole" (§28, 142), and it is pure because that content is given before experience. Henry's "pure" means intuition considered in its essence, whatever it intuits. With that sense in place the circle closes: intuition as such is objectivation, and objectivation needs intuition. In GA 3's sense it does not, because pure intuition is not intuition-as-such but imagination's forming of a determinate look (§28, 141–142). What Henry names a circle is the reflexivity Heidegger will call self-affection: transcendence intuiting the horizon it forms. The dispute is whether that reflexivity explains manifestation or merely names it.

**¶7.** Henry recalls the purpose of Heidegger's problematic, "which counts as a Kantian commentary": to show the essential affinity of thought and intuition and so clarify schematism, by grounding both in imagination. Against the traditional division of spontaneous thought and receptive intuition, each is shown to be spontaneous and receptive at once; since the spontaneity of thought and the receptivity of intuition go without saying, the demonstration stresses the spontaneity of intuition, which forms space and time and so is imagination, and the receptivity of thought, whose rules work only by imposing themselves on the mind that receives them. The report is accurate and its acknowledgement of the Kantian frame is rare in Henry. GA 3 §29 argues exactly this: in the pure turning-toward, "this against and nothing else is received"; "the freely forming projecting of affinity is in itself a representing, receiving submission to it"; rules are represented "as binding in their bindingness", so that pure thinking "is in itself, not afterwards, receiving, that is, pure intuition" (§29, 154). Henry's summary of the receptivity of thought is the one place where he states Heidegger's argument without reshaping it.

**¶8.** Intuition and thought are imagination in their essence because spontaneity and receptivity define imagination, which "is transcendence itself", the essence that forms the horizon and receives it. To work as imagination does, presenting a horizon one forms and receives, is to represent: "representation is the process by which the essence objectivates for itself the pure horizon it receives". Henry quotes Heidegger: thought and intuition, though distinct, are not separated "like two things of absolutely different nature", but belong "as species of representation to the common genus of representation in general" (K 205 / GA 3 §29, 147–148); and, citing Kant, "knowledge as representation is intuition or concept" (K 84 / GA 3 §4, 23). Here the Kantbuch supports Henry more than he needs. Heidegger writes "the same genus of *Vor-stellen* in general", hyphenating the word to bring out the setting-before, and adds that "both are ways of representing ..." (148). Henry's equation of representing with objectivation has a basis in Heidegger's own spelling. Two cuts are worth noting. The Kant sentence Henry adds in his note stops at "each of the two is indeed representation"; Kant continues "but not yet knowledge" (§4, 23), which keeps representation and knowledge apart, though Henry's own "representation makes knowledge possible" respects the difference. And Henry's "imagination is transcendence itself" compresses GA 3's claim that imagination is "the root of transcendence" (§35, 196) into an identity.

**¶9.** Receptivity and spontaneity belong not to intuition and thought in their specificity but to imagination; "far from being able to ground receptivity, intuition is made possible by it". Imagination alone forms and receives the horizon whose upsurge makes intuition and thought possible, and so it grounds their affinity and makes schematism possible. But how does imagination receive the horizon it objectivates? "By intuiting it", one says. "So the intuition grounded in receptivity is charged with making receptivity possible." The first half reconstructs GA 3's thesis that imagination is the root of the two stems (§27, 137; §§28–29). The second half again presses the "how". Henry is right that GA 3, having made imagination the unity of forming and taking-in, says that imagination takes in by forming a look, and that a look is what is intuited. His objection is that this repeats the word it should explain. GA 3's own way out is to deepen imagination into time (§§32–34), not to redefine intuition. Henry follows it there in §24.

**¶10.** The concept of intuition is ambivalent. It is one of two specific cognitive powers, subject like thought to transcendence, which makes it a representation; and it is the very essence of transcendence, securing its coherence by originally receiving what transcendence objectivates. How can intuition as a specific power, which "relates immediately to the object and is singular", ground transcendence, which it presupposes? Appealing to intuition here explains intuition by itself and keeps to the Greek tradition in which knowing is intuiting; the transcendental philosophy of being has merely carried the conditions of intuition to the absolute. The ambivalence Henry names is in the Kantbuch, and he identifies it accurately. Heidegger's §4 says that "for any understanding of the *Critique* one must hammer it in: knowing is primarily intuiting" (§4, 21), and his §§19–25 make pure intuition the medium of transcendence. But the first is Heidegger's thesis about Kant, and Heidegger's own later marginalia on the passage read "primacy of intuition!" and "'properly': what does that mean?" (GA 3, 21; retrospective). The charge that the philosophy of transcendence absolutized intuition holds of Kant as the Kantbuch reads him. Whether it holds of Heidegger's fundamental ontology is a separate question, taken up at ¶14.

**¶11.** "Schematism" covers two problems: the union of thought and intuition, solved by their common essence, and the real problem, the internal possibility of that common essence, transcendence itself. That possibility lies in the original manifestation of the horizon, which alone lets transcendence keep near itself what it has formed; in that keeping lies "the internal possibility of transcendence, the possibility of its effective accomplishment". Henry quotes Heidegger: the happening of transcendence "must at its innermost be a schematism", and Kant "necessarily comes upon a 'transcendental schematism' if he wants to bring to light the ground of the inner possibility of transcendence" (K 159 / GA 3 §21, 101); transcendental truth has its essence "illuminated by the transcendental schematism" (K 180 / GA 3 §25, 123). Schematism manifests the horizon by making it sensible, that is, by relating it to intuition; the schema-image is visible because it "springs ... from a possible presentation" and brings the rule "into the sphere of a possible intuition" (K 157 / GA 3 §21, 99). So intuition is what lets schematism do its work. The quotations are exact, and the translators' "accomplissement" for *Geschehen* (happening) suits Henry's gloss of inner possibility as effective accomplishment. The schema-image sentence is cut where it matters, and the cut is Henry's. Heidegger writes that the schema-image has its look-character "not only and not first from its image-content currently visible, but from the fact that and the way in which it springs out of the possible presentation represented in its regulating, and so holds the rule as it were into the sphere of possible intuitability" (99). The French keeps the rule: the image springs "d'une présentation possible, représentée dans sa fonction de règle, amenant ainsi cette règle dans la sphère d'une intuition possible" (K 157). Henry's ellipsis removes "représentée dans sa fonction de règle", represented in its function as rule. What the translators did change is the noun: they render *Anblickcharakter*, look-character, as "caractère intuitif" (K 157), so that in Henry's French the schema-image owes its "intuitive character" to the presentation. In GA 3 the image is visible because it presents a rule of presentation, and its intuitability follows from that. Henry's conclusion that intuition grounds the image builds on that rendering and on his own cut, and reverses the order the German states.

**¶12.** The two problems merge because intuition is the ground of schematism: not thought but its essence, representation, is shown to be intuition. The concept "is nothing beyond the representation of the regulating unity" (K 156 / GA 3 §21, 98). It manifests itself by becoming an image, by "sensible transposition", that is, by its content being intuited. Henry strings together Heidegger's sentences: "the object meant by the concept becomes accessible only through this intuitive character" (K 160 / GA 3 §22, 102); "all conceptual representing is in essence schematism" (K 159 / GA 3 §22, 101); and the pure concepts "need an essentially pure intuitivity, if what is opposed in pure ob-jectivation is to be perceptible as opposed" (K 160 / GA 3 §22, 102). "It is as representation that thought is intuition." The quotations are exact, and the leveling of Heidegger's words is the translators'. "Intuitivity" renders *Erblickbarkeit*, the capacity to be beheld, which belongs to Heidegger's vocabulary of look and image (*Anblick*, *Bild*) rather than to intuition as a faculty; "intuitive character" renders *Anschaulichkeit*. The translation prints the German in brackets, "intuitivité [*Erblickbarkeit*]" (K 160), so Henry had Heidegger's word before him. Henry's argument leans on the leveling, since it needs intuition to be the last word of schematism. Heidegger's own conclusion is different: "the pure concepts must be grounded in pure schemata that procure an image for them" (102), and the pure image is time (§22, 103). In GA 3 what manifests the concept is the schema, a rule for forming a look in time; Henry reduces the schema to the intuition it makes possible.

**¶13.** The schematism problem comes down to showing that thought and intuition are identical in their essence, "that is, insofar as they manifest themselves": as manifesting they are representation, and representation implies intuition as what makes the represented perceptible. The intuition invoked is not the specific faculty opposed to thought but a general power, undetermined in its ground; yet the manifestation of the horizon was posited as the condition of intuition. To the extent that this intuition is determined at all, it "gives itself as belonging to sensibility", and the perceptibility it grounds is a sensible transposition: one and the same intuition is condition and conditioned. Henry calls the intuition at the base of schematism undetermined. GA 3 determines it: the pure image of all objects of the senses is time (§22, 103), and the schemata are "transcendental determinations of time" (§22, 104). Henry's next section takes up exactly this determination, so the complaint of indeterminacy is provisional. The sensibility he invokes is also Heidegger's redefined sense, sensibility as finite intuition, not bodily affection (§29, 146), which leaves Henry's point intact: the manifestation of the horizon is entrusted to the same finite intuition it is supposed to ground.

**¶14.** The circle must be overcome, but it has a use: it keeps bringing the problematic back to the real problem it both reveals and hides, receptivity, for which the intuition that is schematism's last word is "only a name", or, when more than a name, a mysterious power borrowed from a tradition that needs grounding. In his note Henry cites Heidegger's own remark in *Sein und Zeit* that "the idea of intuition has guided every interpretation of knowledge from the beginning of Greek ontology to today" (SZ 358), and Kant's opening of the Aesthetic, which makes intuition that toward which all thought aims (A19/B33). The note collapses two voices. In *Sein und Zeit* the remark is critical: Heidegger had already argued that "'intuition' and 'thinking' are both remote derivatives of understanding" (SZ §31, 147). The primacy of intuition Henry rightly finds in GA 3 is the primacy Heidegger reads in Kant, and Heidegger's own fundamental ontology subordinates intuition to understanding. Henry's charge falls on the Kantbuch insofar as it adopts Kant's starting point. It does not fall on the projective understanding that GA 3 itself places at the ground (§§41–42), which Henry treated in §18 as a manifestation of being to itself. The two lines of his critique, that the philosophy of transcendence absolutizes intuition and that it reduces manifestation to representation, address different strata of Heidegger's text.

**¶15.** Because it concerns thought and intuition in their essence, schematism belongs to the central problem of the essence of manifestation, and clarifying its internal structure has shown receptivity to be what grounds it in its ultimate possibility. The problem of manifestation now concentrates in the problem of receptivity, whose essence alone could ground the "intuition" by which schematism claims to settle the question. Before clarifying it, Henry will reaffirm its centrality "with respect to time". Henry follows the Kantbuch's own order. After schematism GA 3 turns to imagination and time (§§32–33) and then to time as pure self-affection (§34), where the problem of receptivity receives Heidegger's answer: "pure knowledge ... is grounded in a taking-in intuition. But taking in purely means: being affected free of experience, that is, affecting oneself" (§34, 190). Henry's question, what makes the receptivity of being possible, is the question to which GA 3 answers "self-affection". The next section is where the two meet.

### § 24. The reaffirmation of the central character of the problem of receptivity and the ontological interpretation of time as auto-affection

**¶1.** The problem of receptivity is reaffirmed with respect to time because time, since Kant, is "the universal condition of all appearances", the essence of phenomenality, and because the process in which the essence opposes the horizon to itself is thought as time itself. The ego can be credited with objectivation only as time: "the I, in originally forming time, that is, as time ..., forms the letting-stand-against and its horizon" (K 248 / GA 3 §34, 193). And time is not just another name for that process but grounds it: time "belongs to the inner possibility of this letting-stand-against" (K 244 / GA 3 §34, 190). So the problematic of time should solve the central problem, not repeat it. Henry reads GA 3 at its pivot and reads it correctly: §34 makes time the inner possibility of transcendence. Two details of the quotation matter. The first sentence is Heidegger's interpretation of Kant's "standing and abiding I", in a passage that declares the question of the I's timelessness not even asked (193). And Henry's ellipsis cuts the translators' "originel": the French reads "c'est-à-dire comme temps originel", that is, as original time (K 248). The cut is harmless here, since Henry will draw the distinction between original and pure time himself at ¶9. The paragraph sets the test fairly: if time is what GA 3 says, it should answer the question of receptivity.

**¶2.** Time can be the inner possibility of objectivation only by making the horizon perceptible, and Henry quotes Heidegger that time "gives the horizon of transcendence in advance the character of a perceptible offer" and makes "the 'against' of objectivity perceptible to a finite being" (K 166 / GA 3 §22, 108). But perceptibility rests on the essence's capacity to receive what it has formed, that is, to affect itself. "It is because time is understood by Heidegger, following Kant, as auto-affection" that it is the inner possibility of objectivation. The inference is Heidegger's own, in the same order. GA 3 §34 moves from the finitude of knowing as taking-in to pure knowledge as grounded in a taking-in intuition, and then: "taking in purely means: being affected free of experience, that is, affecting oneself" (190). The phrase "following Kant" is also exact, since Heidegger draws self-affection from the second-edition Aesthetic (B67–68, quoted at 190–191). Henry here reconstructs GA 3's argument without reshaping it.

**¶3.** Auto-affection has a double sense. First it means affection by itself (*affection par soi*): time is affected by nothing other than itself, not by beings, so that it is "affected free of experience" (K 245 / GA 3 §34, 190). What affects it is time itself "in the form of the pure horizon of being", for time is essentially horizon. The now cannot be defined from the being that is there, since that being was awaited and will be past; the present would be nothing if it did not consist in "seeing come and seeing go", in foreseeing and holding in view, that is, in the pure horizon. Henry's distinction has a footing in the French he read. GA 3 uses two formulas: "self-affection" (*Selbstaffektion*), where the self is the source, and "pure affection of itself" (*Affektion ihrer selbst*), where it is what is affected (189). The translators render the first now as "affection pure de soi", now as "auto-affection pure", and the second as "affection de lui-même" (K 244). In "affection pure de soi" the "de soi" can mean "by itself" or "of itself", and Henry's two senses split that French genitive. His analysis of the now reconstructs §32 without saying so, in the translators' words: the taking-in of pure intuition "pré-voit le « tantôt » à venir et re-voit le « tantôt » passé", fore-sees the coming moment and re-sees the moment just past (K 229 / GA 3 §32, 174), since the now has "an essentially unbroken stretch" into both. And "what affects time is time in the form of the horizon" renders Heidegger's sentence that pure intuition "goes at itself with what is intuited as formed in it" (189). The French makes the point more explicit than the German: pure intuition "se sollicite elle-même par [l'objet] qu'elle intuitionne", solicits itself by the [object] it intuits (K 244), with a bracketed "object" Heidegger does not write, since in GA 3 what pure intuition intuits is unobjective. The first sense of auto-affection is faithful to the Kantbuch.

**¶4.** What affects is not the horizon itself, which must first be formed, but the original power that forms it: original time affects itself through pure time, "solicits itself in the form of the horizon", and so "forms the essence of all self-solicitation" (K 244 / GA 3 §34, 189). As the power to posit something other than beings it is imagination: "transcendental imagination is original time" (K 242 / GA 3 §33, 187), an original forming that is "at once seeing, foreseeing and seeing back" (K 230 / GA 3 §32, 174). Time is the essence of inner sense, which "receives nothing from outside" (K 246 / GA 3 §34, 191). How can inner sense, which as time is the milieu of objectivity and "the most radical exteriority", receive nothing from outside? Because it formed what it receives: it "holds everything from itself". The translators' "auto-sollicitation" renders *Sich-selbst-angehen*, going-at-oneself, and their "offre sollicitante" renders the horizon's offer-character (K 148); Henry's "time solicits itself in the form of the horizon" joins words the German keeps apart, though the French "se sollicite elle-même par [l'objet]" (K 244) supports the substance. Two further points need sorting. Time as "the most radical exteriority" is not GA 3's category: the Kantbuch calls transcendence a "standing-out toward" (*Ekstasis*, §24, 119) but never calls time exteriority. It is the category of Henry's edition. The translators' introduction describes time as what "forms itself by going out of itself to direct itself toward itself" and as "the becoming of the self in exteriorization" (K 40–41). Henry takes the translators' gloss for Heidegger's thesis. And his answer to the question about inner sense keeps only half of Heidegger's. GA 3 says the inner affection "must in pure taking-in come from the pure self, that is, form itself in the essence of selfhood as such and thereby first make up this selfhood" (191). The French has the second half too, without "first": the affection "doit naître du soi pur, c'est-à-dire se former dans l'essence de l'ipséité même et donc constituer celle-ci", must arise from the pure self, that is, form itself in the essence of ipseity and so constitute it (K 246). Henry keeps "from the self" and drops "make up selfhood". The dropped half is what ¶5 to ¶10 will contest.

**¶5.** Auto-affection is not only affection by itself; more fundamentally it is affection of itself (*affection de soi*). Time is not only what affects but what is affected, and "affection by itself is grounded in affection of itself". The ranking has no counterpart in GA 3, and it inverts Heidegger's. For Heidegger time as pure self-affection "is not an effecting affection that strikes a self already at hand; as pure, it forms the essence of anything like going-at-oneself", and "insofar as it belongs to the essence of the finite subject to be able to be gone-at as a self, time as pure self-affection forms the essential structure of subjectivity" (189). Heidegger grounds the self's capacity to be affected in time's self-affecting. Henry grounds time's self-affecting in the capacity to be affected. The two poles are the same; the order is reversed. Henry's reversal is the decisive move of his reading of the Kantbuch, and it follows from his principle, stated at R §23 ¶3, that what is formed counts as manifest only if it is received.

**¶6.** Henry raises an alternative: might the power by which time affects also posit what is affected, so that affection of itself results from a self-positing in which the affecting time posits itself as the reality to be affected? The alternative has the strongest textual warrant of any reading in the vicinity, and Henry does not say so. Kant's sentence, which Heidegger quotes, has the mind affected "by its own activity, namely this positing of its representation" (B68, at GA 3 §34, 190–191). Heidegger reads past the positing: "it is not at all the case that a mind exists and, among other things, also relates something to itself for itself and performs self-positings; rather this 'from itself toward ... and back to itself' first constitutes the mind-character of the mind as a finite self" (191). Heidegger thus rejects self-positing as an act of an existing mind, but replaces it with a constituting movement. Henry's alternative, self-positing, is the Fichtean option. Heidegger's actual answer, constitution by a movement out and back, is not on Henry's list.

**¶7.** Such questions stay speculative so long as speculation is asked to answer them. What does the link between affecting and affected time mean "from the phenomenological point of view"? GA 3 does not pose the question in these terms. Heidegger calls the self-affecting of time "almost 'self-evident'" once the perspective of the foregoing stages is secured (§34, 190), and he describes its structure rather than how it appears to the self it forms. Henry's demand is the one he made at R §17 ¶3: the reality of the relation is its manifestation. Addressed to §34, it asks how the self-affecting of time is manifest to the time it affects, a question the Kantbuch does not raise.

**¶8.** The affecting time is the imaginative power that deploys the horizon, the pure succession. Kant's "time by itself cannot be perceived" (Analogies of Experience, B225, B233) can only mean that it cannot be grasped thematically; the horizon must be apprehended in its pure content, and "the task of apprehending the ontological content of pure time is that of time itself. Such is the sense of affection of itself." Henry quotes Heidegger: "to affect purely the letting-stand-against, as pure turning-toward ..., means to bring an against before it" (K 244 / GA 3 §34, 189). Bringing an against, Henry argues, is the doing of transcendence not as forming the horizon but as receiving it; manifestation is grounded in the time that is affected; and he quotes that time "is by nature pure affection of itself" (189), that "pure inner sense is pure self-affection, that is, original time" (K 253 / GA 3 §35, 198), and that pure self-affection "determines the innermost essence of transcendence" (K 245 / GA 3 §34, 190). The quotations are accurate, and the reading of Kant's dictum is the Kantbuch's own, since GA 3 has pure intuition intuit the succession "unobjectively" (§32, 173). One detail of the first quotation supports Henry more than he notices. Heidegger names the affected pole: the against is brought "to it, to the pure letting-stand-against, that is, to pure apperception, to the I itself" (189–190). GA 3 does have an affected self, and it is the I as letting-stand-against. Henry here accepts that self-affection determines the essence of transcendence. At R §31 he will deny it, on the ground that the I so affected is transcendence itself.

**¶9.** Understanding time as affection of itself leads Henry to "institute a radical distinction" between pure time, the horizon of succession formed in transcendence, comparable to space as the content of an intuition, and original time, which forms that horizon by receiving it. "Pure time is not real time": it neither forms nor manifests itself. Original time conditions pure time and all representation, spatial representation included, and Henry quotes Heidegger: time as "this formed product" stands "in a certain sense" level with space, but "not as this formed product is time the original ground of transcendence, rather as pure self-affection; as such it is also the condition of possibility of the representing forming, that is, the making-manifest, of pure space" (K 254 / GA 3 §35, 200). The distinction is not instituted by Henry; it is the Kantbuch's. Heidegger writes that the now-sequence "is by no means time in its originality", that imagination lets it spring forth and "is therefore, as what lets it spring forth, original time" (§32, 175–176), and that what springs from time as self-affection cannot determine "the full essence of time" (§34, 194). Henry reconstructs the distinction faithfully and changes its criterion. For Heidegger original time is original because it lets the succession spring forth, a forming. For Henry it is original because it receives, and pure time lacks reality because it does not manifest itself. Same distinction, relocated ground.

**¶10.** Henry returns to self-positing and finds it abstract. Time posited by the affecting time would be posited outside it, in the milieu of exteriority, and exteriority cannot be affected; it needs to be received in order to appear. "The self-posited time is pure time", so it presupposes the affected time as the condition of its manifestation; hence "the time that is affected is the same as the time that affects", original time as the time of affection, whose affected side is "the most essential in the essence of transcendence". The conclusion restates Heidegger's going-at-oneself, and Henry and Heidegger both reject self-positing. Their reasons differ. Heidegger rejects it because the self is constituted by the movement "from itself toward ... and back to itself" (§34, 191); Henry rejects it because whatever is posited is exterior and cannot be affected. Henry's reason excludes Heidegger's answer as well: a self reached by a return from the horizon would be reached through exteriority. The affected time must be one that never went out. That requirement is the seed of R §31, where Henry separates auto-affection from Heidegger's self-affection.

**¶11.** How does time secure the reception of the horizon? By intuiting it. "It is insofar as time is by nature intuition that it is possible as affection of itself", and "what matters in time ... is not its temporal character but its intuitive character". Time as "the pure one-after-another of the now-sequence" (K 229 / GA 3 §32, 173) is the content of an intuition, like space; intuition is neither time nor space but the power that receives them. This is Henry's sharpest departure from the Kantbuch, and §32 refuses it in so many words: "time as pure intuition means neither only what is intuited in pure intuiting, nor only the intuiting that lacks the 'object'. Time as pure intuition is in one the forming intuiting of what it intuits" (175). Henry separates exactly what this sentence joins, assigning the temporal to the intuited succession and the intuitive to the intuiting. Once the split is made, original time has nothing temporal left in it. For Heidegger original time is temporal as the threefold forming of the look of present, past and future, which "temporalizes itself primarily out of the future" (§33, 186–187). Henry's de-temporalizing of the ground is not a reading of GA 3 but a thesis against it, and it anticipates his own concept of a non-ecstatic immanence.

**¶12.** Schematism shows why time is central: it forms a pure look before all objects, whose possibility lies in the reception of the pure place the image unfolds, and time is the essence of schematism because, as intuition, it secures that reception. When Kant says "the pure image of all objects of the senses in general is time" (K 161 / GA 3 §22, 103), the reason is not that time is "the horizon of all understanding and interpretation of being" (SZ 17) but that it is intuition. Henry quotes that what is formed in schematism "necessarily gives itself intuitively in the pure image of time" (K 166 / GA 3 §22, 108), that in imagination "what is decisive is forming in the sense of procuring an image, by intuiting" (K 188 / GA 3 §26, 129), and that "in transcendental schematism imagination is originally presenting in the pure image of time" (K 190 / GA 3 §26, 132). Henry sets aside *Sein und Zeit*'s thesis on time as the horizon of the understanding of being, and the Kantbuch's §35 sets aside his alternative: the schematism "cannot be grasped in its innermost essence as long as time is understood only as the pure one-after-another of the now-sequence. It must be understood as pure self-affection, for otherwise its function in forming schemata lacks all intelligibility" (200). For Heidegger time grounds schematism as self-affection, not as intuition simply. The K 188 sentence, moreover, reports why Kant counts imagination among the sensible faculties: "here forming in the sense of procuring an image (intuiting) is decisive" for the classification (129). It is Kant's division of faculties as Heidegger reports it, not Heidegger's account of schematism, and the French keeps the inference, "C'est donc que ...", it follows that ... (K 188). At K 190 the translators write "la forme [*Bild*] pure du temps", the pure form of time, replacing "image" with "form" while printing the German; Henry's argument does not depend on the change.

**¶13.** What is intuition? Henry quotes Heidegger on the pure succession: "pure intuition intuits this succession unobjectively", which is only negative, and then the positive determination: "to intuit means: to take in what gives itself" (K 229 / GA 3 §32, 173). "Intuition is only a name for the receptivity that secures the manifestation of the pure horizon of being"; determining time as intuition makes the problem more urgent. The translators render "unobjectively" as "sans l'objectiver", "without objectivating it", so that in Henry's French the pure intuition of time seems exempt from the very objectivation he names transcendence; Henry glosses it correctly as non-thematic. He stops one sentence early. GA 3 continues: "Pure intuition gives itself in taking-in what can be taken in. Taking-in of ... is first understood as receiving something present at hand. But this narrow concept of taking-in, still oriented to empirical intuition, must be kept away from pure intuition and its taking-in character" (173). The French has the same continuation on the same page: "cette notion encore étroite de l'acte réceptif, inspirée de l'intuition empirique, ne peut s'appliquer à l'intuition pure", this still narrow notion of the receptive act, drawn from empirical intuition, cannot apply to pure intuition (K 229). Heidegger's answer to "only a name" is that pure taking-in is a self-giving, and he warns against hearing it as reception of something present. Henry's verdict holds of the narrow concept Heidegger sets aside. It touches Heidegger's own concept only through the argument of R §23 ¶3, that a self-giving forming does not explain how what is formed is received; that argument, not the quotation, carries the weight.

**¶14.** Time's intervention thus repeats the problematic despite its claim to be the inner possibility of objectivation: because time plays its role as intuition, and intuition is made the condition of the transcendence that grounds it, the circle of schematism is made more evident, not removed. Its merit is to confirm the task: to elucidate receptivity. Henry's reading of §§32–35 is most faithful where it reconstructs: self-affection as affection by a horizon time has formed, the identity of affecting and affected time, the split between original time and the now-sequence. It is least faithful where it decides what in time counts, intuition rather than temporality, since that decision rests on separating the intuiting from the intuited, which §32 forbids. The verdict of "repetition" depends on that separation. What survives it is Henry's question at ¶7. GA 3 asserts the unity of forming and taking-in in time and describes it as a movement from itself toward and back to itself, but it does not say how that movement is manifest to the self it constitutes. Henry will argue at R §31 that a self-relation by way of a movement out and back cannot be the self-manifestation the essence requires.

### § 25. The elucidation of the essence of receptivity and the problem of the phenomenological determination of the original reality of transcendence

**¶1.** Within monism the essence manifests itself only by objectivating itself in the horizon, and the horizon does this only by manifesting itself, which is the act of receiving it. "The reality of the act that receives lies in its manifestation"; to determine the essence of receptivity is to determine its phenomenological status. But the receiving act is the essence itself. Is its manifestation, then, simply the process by which the essence objectivates itself in a horizon? Henry now puts to transcendence as a whole the question he put to time at R §24 ¶7: how does the act that receives manifest itself? GA 3 names that act. The "against" is brought "to the pure letting-stand-over-against, that is, to pure apperception, to the I itself" (§34, 189–190), so the receiving act is transcendence as the I. GA 3 does not ask how this act is manifest. Its nearest statement is that time as pure self-affection forms the finite self "so that the self can be something like self-consciousness" (190). Henry's question asks what that "can be" consists in.

**¶2.** If the receiving act manifests itself in objectivation, its reality lies in what it produces. Transcendence springs forward and, going out of itself, creates "the foreground of light" that is the dimension of phenomenality; the milieu of exteriority, its telos, would contain its reality. But to transfer the reality of the act that opens the milieu to the opened milieu is to give the act the status of its own product. The horizon's perceptibility is transcendence's possibility of being affected by it; separated from the power that receives it, the horizon is abstract, as pure time is not real time. And "the radical separation instituted between original time and pure time implies in fact their non-separation", the impossibility of grasping pure time apart from the act by which original time forms it in affecting itself. The view Henry rejects has a footing in GA 3. Transcendence is a "standing-out toward" that "forms and holds before itself a horizon" (§24, 119), and "if ontological knowledge unveils the horizon, its truth lies precisely in letting beings be encountered within the horizon" (§25, 124). But GA 3 does not reduce the act to its product: ontological knowledge "forms" transcendence, "which forming is nothing other than holding open the horizon" (123). The truth of the act lies in what it lets be encountered; its being lies in the holding-open. Henry's diagnosis is half right. The closing sentences also concede a point to the Kantbuch. Henry's "non-separation" of original and pure time is Heidegger's "in one" (§32, 175), which R §24 ¶11 seemed to break. Henry restores it in one direction only: pure time depends on original time, not original time on pure time.

**¶3.** Having refused to locate the reality of transcendence in the horizon, thought turns to the act itself, whose essence was understood as receptivity, the power to hold near itself the horizon the essence has formed. But is holding-near not identical with opposing the horizon and maintaining it before oneself, the original act by which transcendence deploys the horizon? GA 3's vocabulary answers yes. The turning-toward is "a pre-forming holding-before-itself" (§19, 90); finite reason "holds before itself a leeway" (§16, 71); the I "holds before itself" constancy and persistence (§34, 193). In GA 3 holding-near and setting-before are one act, a holding-before-oneself (*Sich-vorhalten*). For the length of a question Henry takes the Kantbuch's side.

**¶4.** The thesis that receiving lies in forming was criticized at R §23, but it differs from the view that puts the act's reality in the horizon. It thinks the act explicitly as the opening of the horizon, transcendence itself, whose forming and receiving were shown to be one: the affecting time is "the same" as the affected time. To grasp that unity is to grasp that "the horizon becomes perceptible in the very act by which the essence posits it before itself". To form the horizon is to posit it before, "so that what is posited before by this act is also, for that reason, seen by it. Being seen, however, means being received." "Because the act of positing-before is identically the act of seeing", reception is included in forming. Must the thesis that forming lies in receptivity be reversed, and was "the essence of spontaneity lies in receptivity" hasty? Henry gives the Kantbuch's position its strongest form, more explicitly than GA 3 does. The equation of setting-before with seeing has its basis in Heidegger's spelling *Vor-stellen* (§29, 148), in the I that "as 'I think', that is, 'I represent', holds before itself" standing and persistence (§34, 193), and in the look (*Anblick*) that imagination forms (§26, 129). Henry's self-objection restores the identity of forming and taking-in that §28 asserted, "this taking-in is in itself precisely the forming self-giving of what gives itself" (141), and that he rejected at R §23 ¶3. It is the most generous reconstruction of the Kantbuch in Section II, and the closing question is a real one. Henry's answer to it decides his reading.

**¶5.** Reception lies in the act that forms the horizon by setting it before itself. But "the act of positing-before lacks, to be real, self-manifestation". What manifests itself is not the act but "the original distance" it shapes into a horizon; phenomenality shines "over there", in the free space transcendence opened. The reality of transcendence is presupposed by the horizon's phenomenality, and the presupposition stays undetermined until the act manifests itself; the forming thesis "borrows all its force" from the thesis that puts reality in the horizon. Henry's answer is that a seeing which sees what it sets before itself does not thereby see itself. Against GA 3 the point lands. Heidegger's account of the self's relation to itself runs through the formed look: pure intuition "goes at itself with what is intuited as formed in it" (§34, 189), and the I forms the horizon of sameness by holding constancy before itself (193). The self GA 3 constitutes is reached by way of what it holds before itself, which is what Henry calls borrowing. GA 3 could reply that the movement "back to itself" (191) is not borrowed from the horizon but belongs to time's own forming. Henry's question is how that "back" is manifest if not as a looking-back from the horizon, and §34 offers no other description of it. The paragraph states Henry's first precise thesis against the Kantbuch's self-affection.

**¶6.** If the horizon's phenomenality does not contain the reality of the act it presupposes, must that reality be sought "outside the ontological milieu of truth"? Can the philosophy of transcendence claim its inability to grasp the act as fidelity to the real, if truth has its condition in "an untruth older than it"? Henry quotes the Kantbuch: the Kantian laying of the ground "does not lead to the sun-clear absolute evidence of a first proposition and principle, but ... goes and points consciously into the unknown" (K 97–98 / GA 3 §6, 37); and with transcendental imagination thought stands before "the abyss of metaphysics" (K 272 / GA 3 §38, 215; cf. §31, 168). The unknown, Henry concludes, is what conceals itself essentially in thought's movement toward it, as the essence of that movement. Henry reads the Kantbuch's "unknown" through a later text. An untruth "older" than truth is the thesis of *Vom Wesen der Wahrheit* (lecture 1930, printed 1943), which Henry knew in de Waelhens and Biemel's translation of 1948 (n. 105). In GA 3 the unknown is first Kant's "root unknown to us" (A15/B29), which the laying of the ground goes on to name, and Heidegger defines it: "the unknown is not that of which we know nothing at all, but what presses toward us within the known as the disquieting" (§31, 160). That unknown is not the principled non-manifestation Henry describes; it is the disquieting within the manifest. Henry's explanation, that the forming act is in principle incapable of being exhibited because it is the movement toward, is his own thesis from ¶5, not GA 3's account of why its ground is unknown.

**¶7.** Section I denounced the paradox of a philosophy of consciousness that seeks the principle of consciousness in the non-conscious, and the philosophy of being shared it while it sought in beings a condition of manifestation. What both aimed at was the object, and so the power that makes objects possible, objectivation; the real problem is the relation between effective manifestation and the power that grounds it. That relation becomes unintelligible when production stays in shadow, the horizon in the light and the act of transcendence in the night: "ontological truth and the untruth that grounds it are two juxtaposed essences". Does the "non-essence of untruth" give a positive ontological character to what is only the absence of any phenomenological character? The vocabulary is that of *Vom Wesen der Wahrheit*, but the question is the Kantbuch's last. GA 3 ends by asking how the transcendental essence of truth "and the non-essence of untruth are at once originally one in the ground of the finitude of Dasein" (§45, 246), and earlier calls "the idea of transcendental untruth" one of the most central problems of finitude, "not only unsolved but not even posed" (§27, 138). In GA 3 untruth is first Kant's "transcendental illusion" (*Schein*), which Kant calls necessary and Heidegger asks to be "positively grounded" in the finitude of Dasein (§45, 245). Illusion is a mode of appearing, not an absence of phenomenality. Against the Kantbuch's version Henry's charge misses. Against the later version, where untruth is a concealment older than unconcealment, it applies: concealment is defined by not showing itself, and Henry rightly asks what positive phenomenological content it has.

**¶8.** The indeterminacy of the non-essence also infects the essence of truth it grounds. If the conditions of truth belong to the indeterminate non-essence, the open milieu of exteriority cannot be the essence of truth, and the two essences stand isolated, unable together to make up the reality of manifestation. "The division in reality between what shows itself and what simply does not show itself only seemingly satisfies the demands of phenomenology." Henry demands that the ground be given phenomenally as producing what it grounds. GA 3 claims as much for its own account of transcendence: the supreme principle of synthetic judgments "is no principle gained by inference back", but "the expression of the most original phenomenological knowledge of the innermost unified structure of transcendence" (§24, 119). Heidegger asserts phenomenological access to what Henry says remains in the night. The two claims concern different things. Heidegger claims phenomenological knowledge of the structure of transcendence, as the stages of the laying of the ground exhibit it. Henry asks for the self-manifestation of the act that has that structure. The Kantbuch answers the first and does not raise the second, and Henry's charge that reality has been dismembered into lifeless essences is aimed at that silence.

### § 26. The intervention of man in the problematic of receptivity and the non-belonging of the original conditions of truth to the absolute milieu of exteriority

**¶1.** Realizing the essence of truth in the separated being of the horizon goes with a dialectical reversal: by placing the principle of intelligibility in the absolute milieu of exteriority, the philosophy of being forbids itself to seek it in a human subjectivity, and it reads the exteriority of the ontological milieu as an exteriority of truth, of being itself, with respect to man. If phenomenality lies in the space opened by the horizon, which "is not in the subject" (SZ 111) but constitutes its being, then man no longer possesses the light; he is what it illuminates, and the philosophy of being can count itself a rejection of idealism. Henry's target is the later Heidegger, and the Kantbuch fits it only in part. GA 3 does refuse to make the understanding of being a human property: it is not "the harmless generality of a frequently occurring property of man", and "more original than man is the finitude of Dasein in him" (§41, 229). But GA 3 says "in him", not outside him: "on the ground of the understanding of being man is the There, with whose being the opening irruption into beings happens" (229). The Kantbuch displaces man in favour of something more original within him; it does not set truth over against him. The exteriority of truth to man belongs to the postwar texts Henry is about to quote.

**¶2.** Whether phenomenality has its principle in man's essence cannot be settled before we know what man is, but one question arises at once: if truth is realized outside man, how does man receive it? Separated from the ontological milieu, man is something ontic. Being illuminates man not as it illuminates a tree but so that he receives its light and becomes it, able to illuminate other beings and himself; Henry quotes a postwar text, in Joseph Rovan's translation: "it belongs to the truth of being to relate to itself, *in a privileged way*, the essence of man". The source is a late text, and the Kantbuch would not pose Henry's question in his form. In GA 3 man's relation to beings rests on his understanding of being: "man could not be the thrown being as a self if he could not let beings as such be" (§41, 228). Reception of truth is not a problem added to a man already separated from it, because the understanding of being is what man's existence is. Henry's question bites on the late Heidegger, where truth "relates" man to itself from outside; against GA 3 it becomes the question he has been asking since R §22, how the understanding of being receives itself.

**¶3.** Man's essence, as related to truth in a privileged way, is the capacity to receive its light and become it, which a being as such cannot do. How does man receive truth, if not on the ground of the original essence of manifestation in him? "More essential than the question how man receives the truth of the essence is the question how this essence receives itself", since man receives truth only through the action in him of the essence that can receive itself. Henry makes the move GA 3 makes, with a different term. The Kantbuch also turns from man to something more original in him, "the finitude of Dasein" (§41, 229), and describes man's relation to being as a need: in transcendence Dasein "shows itself as needing the understanding of being" (§43, 236), and man is "a being thrown into beings that has to understand something like being" (§45, 246). Heidegger's displacement ends in a need, a mark of finitude. Henry's ends in a self-reception, a power of the essence. The parallel of structure makes the difference of content sharp.

**¶4.** The intervention of man diverts the problematic from its real problem, which lies wholly in the essence. That truth does not reside in man does not mean that it coincides with the milieu of exteriority, or that this milieu contains the condition of its own phenomenality. Yet this is the philosophy of being's implicit presupposition when, "opposing truth to man, it realizes truth outside him in exteriority as such". The abstraction shows when man must be added to the truth so realized, and to man is then "surreptitiously entrusted the essential power of the essence, that of receiving itself". Henry's diagnosis assumes the late scheme of truth outside man. Against GA 3 it needs adjusting: there is no truth realized outside man to which man must be added, because the original truth is transcendence (§25, 123), and transcendence is Dasein's. What Henry would say of GA 3 is different and he says it elsewhere: that the Kantbuch entrusts self-reception not to man but to transcendence, as time's self-affection (§34, 190), and that transcendence cannot carry it. That charge, not the one in this paragraph, is the one the Kantbuch has to answer.

**¶5.** The intervention of man has a double meaning. It masks the essence's ultimate possibility by placing the conditions of truth in exteriority and setting before it a man stripped of the ontological power. And what must then be given back to man is not the milieu of exteriority but the possibility of opening onto it, receptivity as such: "receptivity is the ultimate possibility of truth". Henry's conclusion converges with GA 3 at its root. The Kantbuch's finite being is defined by dependence on taking-in, and §34 grounds that dependence: "only on the ground of this selfhood can the finite being be what it must be: dependent on taking-in" (189). Receptivity is the ultimate mark of finitude in GA 3 and the ultimate possibility of truth in Henry. The difference is in the word "possibility". For Heidegger dependence on taking-in is grounded in a selfhood formed by time; for Henry receptivity is itself the ground. R §24 already showed where the order of grounding is reversed.

**¶6.** This second meaning is masked by the themes that accompany the realization of truth in exteriority, above all the finitude of man. To be finite, for a man who no longer carries the principle of phenomenality, is to be separated from truth; "the intrinsic obscurity of human nature is the meaning of its finitude". Malebranche, who places the conditions of truth in intelligible extension, consigns the soul to the obscure sphere of feeling and the theme of the nothingness of finite and sinful man. The concept of finitude Henry describes is the opposite of GA 3's. In the Kantbuch finitude is not separation from truth but its ground: transcendence is "the original truth" (§25, 123), and the understanding of being is "the most finite in the finite", which is why it can make possible man's so-called "creative" capacities (§41, 229). The companion commentary on GA 3 warns that finitude in the Kantbuch must not be heard as a lament, and the warning holds against Henry's description. One strand does tie it to obscurity: the understanding of being has breadth and constancy "but also concealment" (229) and "lies in forgottenness" (§42, 233). Henry's "obscurity" fits that strand, but in GA 3 the concealment belongs to the understanding of being itself, not to a man cut off from it.

**¶7.** That the human understanding has no ideas in it, intelligibility being outside it, does not solve the problem of its inner possibility, the possibility of receiving ideas and being illuminated by them; that possibility becomes unintelligible when man is defined only by his heterogeneity to intelligibility. Henry quotes Martial Gueroult's question whether Malebranche's understanding, reduced to a passive and affective faculty, can still unite with truth. The passive character of the understanding, Henry answers, must not be denied but grounded, and the conditions that ground it, receptivity itself, cannot lie in a humanity defined as what is not truth. GA 3 is not engaged, but the demand Henry makes of Malebranche is one the Kantbuch meets in form. Heidegger grounds the finite being's dependence on taking-in in a selfhood that time as pure self-affection forms (§34, 189). He does not deny passivity or leave it ungrounded; he gives it a positive ground in the structure of the self. Henry's objection to GA 3 can therefore not be the one he makes to Malebranche. It has to be the one he has been building since R §23: that the ground GA 3 offers, a self-affection that runs through the formed horizon, is not a self-reception.

**¶8.** It is not man who is obscure. "The obscurity attributed to man by the existential and religious theme of finitude is in fact that of the power which secures in him the reception of truth", an obscurity that signifies only the absence of any elucidation of the essence's most ultimate possibility, or, when it claims a positive sense, the mere negation of the phenomenality of exteriority. "The obscurity of the human soul hides the ontological essence of untruth": man's inability to receive truth covers untruth's inability to unite with truth and ground it. Henry joins this section to R §25 ¶¶7–8. The Kantbuch's last question is exactly the one Henry says cannot be answered on its premises: how the essence of truth "and the non-essence of untruth are at once originally one in the ground of the finitude of Dasein, with the basic neediness of man ... to have to understand something like being" (§45, 246). Heidegger poses the unity of truth and untruth in finitude as a task. Henry's verdict is that, as long as untruth is defined as what does not show itself, the unity cannot be achieved and the ground stays dark. His verdict is aimed at answers given after 1929; it agrees with GA 3 that the question was still open there.

### § 27. The comprehension of the central character of the problematic of receptivity and the putting in question of the ultimate ontological presuppositions of monism

**¶1.** The problem of receptivity becomes central when it is no longer read only in relation to the horizon to be received. Receptivity of the horizon proved to be the original act by which transcendence forms the look of the horizon by setting it before itself; since the horizon's perceptibility, being itself as the horizon's manifestation, is grounded in that act, the act is the ground, the essence of manifestation and of being. Taken radically, receptivity concerns not the reception of the horizon but "the possibility of the reception of transcendence itself": "what must be received is not originally what is formed in the imaginative act of the essence, but what makes this formation possible, the imaginative power itself, identical at bottom with the essence". The step from the received horizon to the receiving power has a precise counterpart in GA 3. In §34 what is affected is not the formed look but the forming self: the "against" is brought "to the pure letting-stand-over-against, that is, to pure apperception, to the I itself" (189–190), and time, which forms, is what "goes at itself" (189). Heidegger also makes the forming power the ground of being's manifestation, since "transcendence is the original truth" (§25, 123). Henry's radicalized question is GA 3's own question at §34. What differs is the answer he is preparing.

**¶2.** "To be received means to manifest oneself." The receptivity of the original act of transcendence is the possibility of its manifestation, so the radical problem of receptivity puts in question the phenomenological status of that act, the reality of what in the essence is its ultimate possibility. The paragraph restates R §25 ¶1 as the central problem. Its equation of being received with manifesting, made at R §22 ¶2, now does its full work: once receiving means manifesting, the question how transcendence is received is the question how it appears. GA 3 does not make the equation. In §34 being affected, "being gone-at as a self", is what self-affection makes possible for the finite subject (189), and Heidegger does not say that it consists in the self appearing to itself. Henry's equation turns Heidegger's structural claim into a phenomenological demand.

**¶3.** Taken radically, receptivity is still only the title of a problem; in general it designates manifestation, as the receptivity of the horizon is its perceptibility. How does the horizon become perceptible? Through transcendence, which is "the how" of that perceptibility; transcendence is an essence precisely in being this how, the ontological power that makes phenomenality arise, by setting the horizon before itself. Henry's definition of essence as the "how" of manifestation, rather than its "what", recalls the definition of phenomenology in *Sein und Zeit* §7 as concerned with the how, not the what, of its objects. Against GA 3 the reconstruction is exact: transcendence "forms" the horizon, and this forming is "the holding open of the horizon in which the being of beings becomes visible in advance" (§25, 123). Transcendence is the how of the horizon's appearing in both texts.

**¶4.** Since the receptivity of the horizon refers to its how, transcendence, the receptivity of transcendence refers to a how of its own. Is it the same how? That is: does transcendence secure the possibility of its own receptivity; "is transcendence the essence"? GA 3 answers the question, and answers yes. The idea of pure self-affection "determines the innermost essence of transcendence" (§34, 190), and time as pure self-affection is precisely what "forms anything like the 'from itself toward ...', in such a way that the toward-which thus formed looks back and into the aforesaid toward" (189). For Heidegger the how of transcendence's self-relation is transcendence's own temporal structure: the horizon it forms looks back into the turning-toward that forms it. Henry formulates the question as if it were open. In the Kantbuch it is closed, by the one sentence of §34 that Henry never quotes in Section II. In his French the sentence reads differently: time "forme [la visée] qui, partant de soi, se dirige vers ..., de telle manière que le but ainsi constitué jaillit et reflue sur cette visée", forms [the aim] which, starting from itself, is directed toward ..., so that the goal thus constituted springs up and flows back onto this aim (K 244). The translators turn the looking back into a flowing back, *reflue*, an image of return without a look. Henry passes over that version too.

**¶5.** Transcendence is the how of the horizon's receptivity because it forms the look of the horizon by setting it before itself, so that exteriority as such has the sense of manifestation and "defines a dimension of phenomenality". Is what appears originally in that dimension transcendence itself? R §25 showed that the reality of transcendence does not lie in the open milieu of exteriority. Hence what appears there is not transcendence, the original manifestation of transcendence is not that of the horizon, and the how of the one is not the how of the other: "transcendence is not what secures the possibility of its own receptivity; the original manifestation of transcendence is not the work of transcendence itself". Henry's inference rests on a premise drawn from monism and turned against it: transcendence can produce only the kind of manifestation exteriority has. On that premise, a self-manifestation of transcendence would have to occur in the horizon, and R §25 has shown it does not. The unquoted sentence of §34 denies the premise. There the horizon formed by time "looks back and into" the turning-toward (189): the self-relation of transcendence is neither the horizon's appearing nor something outside it, but a return through the horizon into the act. Henry's conclusion holds only if that return is itself a manifestation of the exterior kind, a looking-back that sets the act before itself as the horizon is set before it. That is the claim he must make good, and R §31 is where he tries.

**¶6.** The incapacity of transcendence to secure its own manifestation is "identically the putting in question of the fundamental ontological presuppositions of monism": transcendence cannot ground itself or constitute the essence of a ground, and this is why the essence of manifestation remains abstract within monism and why the problematic fails to determine the being of its ground. On one point Henry and the Kantbuch agree. GA 3 does not make transcendence a self-grounding ground. Its ground is finite, needy (§43, 236), and the laying of the ground ends before an "abyss" (§31, 168; §38, 215). Both deny that transcendence grounds itself absolutely. They draw opposite conclusions. For Heidegger the absence of self-grounding is finitude, and finitude is the ground. For Henry it is the abstraction of a ground that does not manifest itself, and the ground must be sought in another mode of manifestation. The disagreement is over whether a finite, non-self-grounding transcendence can be the essence of manifestation at all.

### § 28. The abstract character of the essence of manifestation within the ontological presuppositions of monism and the problem of building a phenomenology of the ground

**¶1.** The autonomy of the essence was first thought as the non-belonging of beings to its internal structure. If the for-itself designates the essence of manifestation, it is absurd to call it non-autonomous, "in no way an autonomous substance" (Sartre, EN 679, 712), and more absurd to read its ontological insufficiency as a lack of the in-itself, something by principle non-ontological: "consciousness is an abstraction, since it conceals in itself an ontological origin toward the in-itself" (EN 37). Sartre's themes of man as lack, need and desire, of a project to become something ontic and of its failures rest on confused concepts. The target is Sartre, but the vocabulary of need reaches back to the Kantbuch, which speaks of man's "basic neediness" to understand being (§45, 246) and of "the distress of needing the understanding of being" (§41, 228). Henry's objection does not transfer. In GA 3 what Dasein needs is the understanding of being, an ontological structure, not the in-itself. Sartre's lack is a lack of being in the sense of beings; Heidegger's need is the finitude of the understanding of being. Henry's critique of the ontological element said to lack something ontic hits *L'Être et le Néant*, not the Kantbuch's finitude.

**¶2.** "If the cogito necessarily leads outside itself, ... it is because by itself it has no sufficiency of being as absolute subjectivity; it refers first to the thing" (EN 712). Henry reads the presupposition: the movement outward of the cogito is not originally a movement toward beings but toward the outside as such, pure exteriority, and phenomenality is implicitly identified with exteriority. Henry corrects Sartre with the Kantbuch. GA 3 makes transcendence a passing-over that concerns "not beings themselves but the possibility of the prior understanding of being" (§3, 16), and it asks of ontological knowledge: it "relates not at all thematically and directly to beings. To what, then? ... A nothing" (§25, 120–121). The outward movement Henry describes, toward exteriority and not toward things, is Heidegger's ontological transcendence, and Henry's diagnosis that phenomenality is here identified with exteriority is the reading of GA 3 he has been building. The Sartrean confusion is that the in-itself, a being, takes the place the horizon holds in Heidegger.

**¶3.** Sartre defines the concrete as "the synthetic totality of which consciousness and the phenomenon are only articulations" (EN 219). Behind "phenomenon", Henry reads not the in-itself but what makes it a phenomenon, objectivity, exteriority; so consciousness is abstract insofar as it is separated from phenomenality, that is, insofar as it has not yet realized itself in exteriority. "Consciousness is abstract 'in itself', real outside itself"; going outward, it goes toward its own reality. GA 3 is not engaged. The equation Henry draws out of Sartre, that the reality of consciousness lies outside it, is the position he attributed to the philosophy of transcendence at R §25 ¶2, and found half supported by GA 3, where the truth of ontological knowledge lies in letting beings be encountered within the horizon (§25, 124). Sartre states outright what the Kantbuch only implies.

**¶4.** What is consciousness "in itself", abstracted from phenomenality? Not an empty interiority opposed to the milieu of exteriority but the power that generates that milieu: "consciousness in itself is transcendence", and "the abstraction of consciousness considered in itself is the abstraction of transcendence". Henry reaches, through Sartre, the identification that structures his reading of the Kantbuch: consciousness taken in itself is the act of transcendence. GA 3 would accept the content and refuse the word, since it locates that act in Dasein and not in consciousness (GA 3, 289; retrospective). The identification lets Henry treat Sartre's "abstract consciousness" and the Kantbuch's unmanifest transcendence as one problem.

**¶5.** How can transcendence be abstract, when it is the power that grounds the horizon's phenomenality? Taken in its pure act, it is the concrete term, and the horizon's manifestation is, in Hegel's words, "the abstraction of its being-present". It is called non-autonomous only because "in itself it does not manifest itself": "the abstraction of transcendence means its non-phenomenality". Hence the paradox that withdraws reality from the ground at the moment its reality is to be determined: "being is withdrawn from the ground at the very moment the problem of the being of the ground is posed". "Reality moves as manifestation moves." The paradox has a GA 3 instance, though not on Sartre's terms. When Kant comes upon transcendental imagination as the ground, he recoils: imagination is "the disquieting unknown" that motivates the rewriting of the deduction in the second edition (§31, 162), and in the second edition it is pushed back into a function of the understanding. In Heidegger's account the ground withdraws because Kant, holding to reason, shrinks from it, not because it fails to appear. Henry's paradox explains the same withdrawal differently: a ground that is defined by what it makes manifest has no manifestation of its own to hold on to. Heidegger's retrieval claims to hold the ground fast where Kant let go. Henry's paradox predicts that it will hold it only as what it makes possible.

**¶6.** Monism defines the being of the ground as "being outside its being". What remains wholly undetermined is the ground as ground, since its being-outside-itself is possible only on the ground of the ground founding its own being-outside-itself. The "abstract" consciousness that one criticizes and cannot do without reappears whenever the being of the ground is in question. Henry's formula aims at Sartre, but the "outside-itself" is Heidegger's. *Sein und Zeit* calls temporality "the original 'outside-itself' in and for itself" (SZ 329), and GA 3 calls transcendence "a standing-out toward ... (Ekstasis)" that is "in itself ecstatic-horizonal" (§24, 119) and constitutes the self by its movement "from itself toward ... and back to itself" (§34, 191). Heidegger claims for the ecstatic exactly what Henry denies it: to be in and for itself as the outside-itself, with no ground behind it. Henry's argument that an outside-itself presupposes a ground founding it begs this question unless the founding can be shown to differ from the ecstatic movement itself. That is the burden of R §§30–31.

**¶7.** Monism is thrown back and forth between the essence and its being-outside-itself, and resolves the oscillation dialectically by declaring the synthetic totality alone concrete, "as if autonomy could result from the union of two non-autonomous elements". The passage from one term to the other is not a real bond but "the panic of a thought" that declares its own inability to grasp reality to be reality itself. Henry's objection to a synthesis of two abstract terms is one the Kantbuch shares. Heidegger insists that transcendental imagination "is not merely an external band that ties two ends together", but "originally unifying" (§27, 137), "the original and not first compounded unity of receptivity and spontaneity" (§29, 153). GA 3's ground is a root, not a synthesis. Henry's critique of dialectical totality applies to Sartre and Hegel and leaves the Kantbuch's original unity untouched; against GA 3 the question remains whether that unity manifests itself, not whether it is composed.

**¶8.** The oscillation is resolved, not removed, when essence and exteriority are identified outright, as when Heidegger writes, glossing Hegel, that "the field in which *Erscheinen* (appearing) comes to the intuition of itself is made of *Erscheinen* itself and by it" (*Holzwege* 134). Taken generally, the sentence is true: it states the essence's decisive character, to be the ground of its own manifestation. But "made by it" reveals an ambiguity. What creates the field is not the field but transcendence, so "appearing" names both the phenomenality of the horizon and transcendence itself, and transcendence can be identified with the horizon only because its own mode of revelation remains unexplained, so that the horizon's mode is substituted for it. The Hegel sentence has a structural twin in the Kantbuch, the sentence of §34 that Henry does not quote: time forms the "from itself toward ..." "in such a way that the toward-which thus formed looks back and into the aforesaid toward" (189). There too transcendence comes to itself through the field it forms. Henry's diagnosis here, the substitution of the horizon's mode of manifestation for transcendence's own, is his answer to that sentence, though addressed to the Hegel essay. It says that the "looking back" of the horizon into the act is the horizon's way of appearing lent to the act. Whether a looking-back must be a mode of the horizon's appearing is exactly what GA 3 and Henry dispute.

**¶9.** That the being of the ground escapes the problematic in the horizon shows also in this: transcendence is rather what does not show itself in the truth of that field, the ontological essence of untruth, "older than it". Withdrawn from the light of the horizon, transcendence is simply placed in the night of untruth, which attests the problematic's failure in both directions. Henry joins his two lines. His own thesis, that transcendence does not manifest itself in the horizon, is read as what the later Heidegger affirms when he places a concealment older than unconcealment at the ground of truth. The Kantbuch had posed the unity of truth and untruth in the finitude of Dasein as a question (§45, 246). Henry reads the later answer as an admission of his thesis: the philosophy of transcendence, having failed to give its ground a positive phenomenological status, declares the failure a feature of the ground.

### § 29. Exhibiting the ontological motive of the problematic's inability to build a phenomenology of the ground and to give content to the idea of the formal structure of autonomy

**¶1.** The failure to determine the reality of the ground leads the problematic to reflect on its reasons. With beings excluded from the internal structure of the essence, it is the essence that manifests itself, and "the essence is active if it shows itself"; as the essence of manifestation, it also manifests itself through itself: "the essence is the ground of its manifestation", and in this it is autonomous. Both conditions are satisfied by the Hegel essay's sentence that "the field where *Erscheinen* comes to the intuition of itself is made of *Erscheinen* itself and by it" (*Holzwege* 134). Henry restates the two conditions of autonomy fixed at R §16 and measures them against Heidegger's gloss of Hegel. The Kantbuch's counterpart of the second condition is self-affection: time as pure self-affection "forms the essence of anything like going-at-oneself" (§34, 189). In GA 3 the ground affects itself; whether it thereby manifests itself is the question Henry has pressed since R §24 ¶7.

**¶2.** One more presupposition belongs to the nature of *Erscheinen*: if the appearing that comes to self-intuition is the appearing that creates the field, then the act that grounds its own manifestation appears *as* that ground. Here the positive sense of autonomy, the manifestation of the essence, coincides with its possibility, which ceases to be "a condition = x" if it shows itself in the field it grounds as what grounds it. Determining that reality belongs to "the phenomenology of the ground". The phrase "a condition = x" borrows Kant's transcendental object, which GA 3 interprets as the horizon, "a nothing" that is "not a being, but nonetheless 'something'" (§25, 122). Henry turns Kant's term against the Kantbuch's ground. A ground that is only what makes the horizon possible would be such an x, and the phenomenology of the ground is Henry's name for the demand that it show itself as ground. GA 3 has no phenomenology of the ground in this sense. Its stages exhibit transcendence as what makes ontological knowledge possible, and §24 calls the supreme principle "phenomenological knowledge of the innermost unified structure of transcendence" (119). It exhibits the ground as a structure, not as appearing to itself.

**¶3.** The formal structure stays formal, its conditions empty, until one answers: what is appearing? If the same undetermined "appearing" stands under both terms, the act that appears and the field in which it appears, the distinction between them is merely logical, their unity the empty identity of tautology, "the pure and simple act of appearing ... named twice". Is the idea of a formal structure of autonomy then empty? Henry turns against himself the charge of tautology that would follow from Heidegger's sentence on *Erscheinen*. Against GA 3 the charge would be partly misplaced: the Kantbuch does differentiate its terms, manifestness of beings from unveiledness of being (§2, 13), and the formed look from the forming that gives it (§28, 141). What it does not differentiate is the manifestation of transcendence from the unveiling of being, since for Heidegger transcendence simply is "the original truth" (§25, 123). Henry's question "what is appearing?" asks for exactly the distinction GA 3 does not draw.

**¶4.** The formal structure is not in itself formal. It emerged as an elucidation, not as a presupposition; it is "a question that is a result", posed when the analysis reached what makes the essence an essence, and only now does it become a guiding idea. Henry's account of his method resembles the Kantbuch's account of its own. Heidegger says the way of unveiling the origin is at first undetermined, and that its "certainty and determinacy grow, as it were, only in the advance into the hitherto concealed field and in the confrontation with what shows itself there" (§8, 40). Both authors treat the guiding idea as earned in the course of the work rather than laid down before it. The parallel shows that Henry's disagreement with the Kantbuch is not over method, which he shares, but over what the method finds at the ground.

**¶5.** That autonomy is still only an idea "for us" shows that the means to reach the goal are lacking. The idea is the index of concrete ontological work, which becomes aware of its own insufficiency when it cannot give the idea effective content, that is, when it cannot determine the being of the ground. "The idea of autonomy is only the idea of this necessary determination." The paragraph has no GA 3 locus. It applies to Henry's own inquiry the distinction he drew at R §18 between ontological structure and existential understanding: the idea of autonomy belongs to philosophical understanding, and its emptiness reflects the problematic's state, not the essence's. That is why he can call the Kantbuch's ground undetermined without calling it unreal.

**¶6.** The idea is "formal" and "empty" because it gives appearance as the ground of appearance. In context the terms are not empty. Appearance is the visibility of the horizon; the ground is transcendence; "to determine the proper being of the ground is to bring to light the original mode of revelation of transcendence itself". Lacking the idea of such a mode, monism can determine the being of transcendence only by giving it the phenomenological status of the horizon, and then ground and appearance coincide and their identity is tautological: "the appearing that appears, that grounds its appearing and appears as such". The analysis repeats R §28 ¶8 in Henry's own terms. Against GA 3 its value is a precise formulation of what the Kantbuch would have to supply: not the structure of transcendence, which it supplies, but transcendence's own mode of revelation, distinct from the horizon's visibility. GA 3's self-affection is offered as that mode. Henry has argued that, as described in §34, it borrows the horizon's mode, because the formed look "looks back" into the act (189).

**¶7.** If appearing has a sense, it is the manifestation of being as horizon, possible only in and through transcendence; the problematic faces the task of determining the being of transcendence, which the idea of autonomy formulated as a project. That the idea loses its sense when the problematic cannot carry out the task brings the question why the determination always fails. Henry restates his result as a question. GA 3 frames its own culminating task in parallel terms: the laying of the ground is transformed into "the illumination of the inner possibility of the understanding of being" (§40, 226). Heidegger's task asks what makes the understanding of being possible. Henry's asks how the understanding of being, as transcendence, is itself manifest. GA 3 answers the first with temporality (§44, 242). The second is not on its list.

**¶8.** Henry asks why transcendence eludes the thought that wants to grasp it. To elude means not to show itself, and it does not show itself to the thought that conceives manifestation as manifestation of the horizon: transcendence does not manifest itself in the form of the horizon. Understanding this impotence "repeats ... the problematic that aims at the essence of receptivity when that problematic understands itself in its central role", and brings to light "the radical insufficiency" of monism's presuppositions. The paragraph restates R §27 as a result about thought. Its claim about GA 3 is that the Kantbuch's thought is one that "conceives manifestation as manifestation of the horizon". That holds for GA 3's analysis of transcendence, where every manifestation is a letting-stand-against. It is less clear for the Kantbuch as a whole, as ¶9 shows.

**¶9.** The insufficiency leads to a paradox. How can a problematic miss the ground when the idea of the ground presents itself to it as the determinate idea of transcendence? Is it so destitute, when it possesses the power that secures the manifestation of being? But philosophy often gives itself what it does not have: is it in possession of the ground until it has clarified "the origin of the idea of transcendence itself"? Something is given to it "that does not belong to the presuppositions it recognizes as its own", if transcendence's manifestation is not transcendence's own work. The ground's inability to produce itself in phenomenality is transcendence's inability to secure its own manifestation. Henry's question, where the philosophy of transcendence gets its knowledge of transcendence, has an answer in GA 3, though not in §34. In §41 Heidegger writes that we understand being in every statement and in silent comportment, and: "in every mood, in which 'one is so or so', our Da-sein becomes manifest to us" (227). In Henry's French the line reads "notre propre existence [Da-sein] nous devient manifeste en chacune de nos dispositions affectives" (K 283): manifest "in each of our affective dispositions". The Kantbuch's one statement of how Dasein is manifest to itself names mood, not transcendence's self-affecting. Henry does not engage this line in Section II. He takes up Heidegger's account of mood in Section IV (R §65), as a theory of affectivity. The answer GA 3 gives to Henry's question lies in a register Henry reserves for his own positive doctrine.

**¶10.** Does the horizon opened by transcendence not provide a real dimension of phenomenality? Because transcendence opens it, it is nothing so long as the being of transcendence is not given. The philosophy of transcendence, lacking the conditions of the horizon's effective manifestation, cannot ground transcendence's manifestation either, and "the totality of the conditions of any manifestation whatever escapes it". Manifestation as monism understands it is not only one-sided but abstract: "on the ground of these presuppositions alone, it does not occur". The conclusion is the strongest Henry draws in Section II, and it runs against the Kantbuch's central claim, that transcendence is "the original truth" (§25, 123). For Heidegger the horizon is effectively manifest because transcendence holds it open, and nothing more is needed. For Henry holding open is not yet manifesting, since the holding itself must appear. The two positions do not meet on the text. They divide over whether manifestation requires a self-manifestation of what manifests.

**¶11.** If the horizon does appear, transcendence is something rather than nothing. Its reality is constantly presupposed by monism without belonging to the presuppositions by which monism defines itself. "How can transcendence be a presupposition foreign to the philosophy of transcendence?" Only if transcendence presupposes something other than itself, if its reality is not constituted by itself. To be real is to be given, received; since transcendence's manifestation is not its own work, its reality lies in the power that secures its manifestation, "the essence that receives it", "the original essence of revelation". Henry's argument reverses the order of GA 3's §34. Heidegger grounds the self that can be affected in time's self-affecting (189). Henry grounds transcendence in a receiving essence prior to it. The reversal first announced at R §24 ¶5 here takes its final form: the receiving essence is not a moment of transcendence but its condition.

**¶12.** An ontological determination of the original essence of revelation is needed, and it shows that essence to be immanence. With this sentence Henry leaves the Kantbuch's terms. The main text of GA 3 has no concept of immanence, and its nearest counterparts, the self constituted by time and the self manifest in mood, are both forms of transcendence for Heidegger. R §30 will define immanence by its opposition to the horizon. The question for the remaining sections is whether the Kantbuch's self-affection can be reread as immanence or must be opposed to it.

### § 30. Ontological determination of the original essence of revelation as immanence. Immanent content and transcendent content

**¶1.** Understanding its failure to give content to the idea of autonomy brings the problematic before the poverty of its means and the indeterminacy of its ultimate horizons. But an understood failure is not merely negative: the reason why the being of the ground escapes is now available, and "this reason is contained in the ground, it is the ground itself". The paragraph turns the "indeterminate and unknown" into evidence. In GA 3 the unknown also carries information: it is "what presses toward us within the known as the disquieting" (§31, 160), and Kant's recoil before it is read by Heidegger as a sign of what the ground is. Both authors make the ground's withdrawal a clue to its nature. Heidegger reads it as the abyss of finitude; Henry is about to read it as the trace of a revelation that is not transcendence.

**¶2.** The ground escapes because transcendence's manifestation is not transcendence's work. For transcendence, to work is to open a horizon; a manifestation that is not its work arises "independently of the movement by which the essence springs forward and projects itself in the form of a horizon", "in the absence of all transcendence". Since that manifestation is the manifestation of transcendence itself, the act of transcendence reveals itself independently of its projection, "without surpassing itself, without going out of itself". "What does not surpass itself ... but remains in itself without leaving itself is, in its essence, immanence", "the original essence of revelation". Henry defines immanence by negating the Kantbuch's own definitions of transcendence. GA 3 calls transcendence a passing-over (*Überschreiten*, §3, 16) and "a constant standing-out toward ... (Ekstasis)" (§24, 119). "Without surpassing itself" negates the first, "without going out of itself" the second. The definition is exact as an antithesis. Its claim, that the act of transcendence reveals itself in a way that is not transcendence, is the contradictory of GA 3 §34, where the self of transcendence is constituted precisely by the movement "from itself toward ... and back to itself" (191).

**¶3.** The positive result of understanding the failure is the doubling of the concept of receptivity: one sense refers to the horizon, the other to the power that unfolds it, and they differ not only in what is received but in the mode of reception. For ontological realities the reality is not separable from its mode of reception: "it is precisely because the mode in which their reception is originally accomplished is not the same that transcendence and horizon are different ontological realities". The horizon is received by transcendence, and so is a horizon. The Kantbuch also distinguishes transcendence from its horizon, as letting-stand-against from what stands against, but holds them together in one structure: "transcendence is in itself ecstatic-horizonal" (§24, 119). Henry's principle, that a reality is defined by the mode in which it is received, turns the two moments of Heidegger's one structure into two realities. The principle comes from his equation of reception with manifestation (R §22 ¶2), not from GA 3.

**¶4.** The mode that grounds the receptivity of transcendence is not the power that forms the horizon by going out of itself, and its positive characterization follows: "in the internal structure of this original mode of receptivity transcendence is not comprised". An essence without transcendence does not divide or separate from itself; "the essence of the original receptivity that secures the reception of transcendence itself is immanence". It reaches its content "without moving or surpassing itself toward it", so that the content is in no way transcendent to it and is not set before it as a horizon: an "immanent content". The closest GA 3 concept is the content of pure intuition, which "stands unobjectively and moreover unthematically in a fore-view" (§9, 47). Heidegger's pure content is non-objective and non-thematic, as Henry's is, but it stands "in a fore-view", held before. Henry's immanent content lacks exactly that "fore-". The difference between the two authors at this point is one prefix.

**¶5.** Henry gives the "immanent" character the strictest ontological sense. In the loose sense, "immanent to consciousness" means only that a content is in fact given, received by consciousness; what this leaves undetermined is "the very mode in which the reception takes place, that is, 'consciousness' itself", and the indeterminacy of the mode is that of the content. The paragraph has no GA 3 locus and targets the common phenomenological usage. It applies to "immanence" the principle of ¶3: a content is determined by the mode of its reception. The Kantbuch would agree that "consciousness" names an undetermined mode, which is why it avoids the word (GA 3, 289; retrospective).

**¶6.** When the mode of reception is determined, it is determined in the light of a philosophy of transcendence: the content is "really given" insofar as consciousness reaches it in the act by which it transcends toward it. "Immanence paradoxically receives the sense of objectivity." The Kantbuch provides an instance. Kant's inner sense, the traditional seat of immanent contents, "the intuiting of ourselves and our inner state" (§10, 48), is reinterpreted in GA 3 as time, and time as pure self-affection that forms the horizon of objectivity: "pure inner sense is pure self-affection, that is, original time", and what it forms is the play-space in which objects are encountered (§35, 198). GA 3 takes the classical sphere of immanence and finds transcendence at its root. Henry's paragraph describes this move as a general law of the philosophy of transcendence, and he is right that GA 3 makes it.

**¶7.** Does calling a content immanent not oppose it to transcendent content? If the distinction has a sense, the mode of reception must differ in each case. The Husserlian distinction is inessential because the essence of both contents is the same: "the ontological power that makes them ultimately possible ... is in every case transcendence"; the supposedly immanent content becomes transcendent once one asks what makes it given. Henry here agrees with Heidegger against Husserl, though not by citing him. Heidegger's lectures of 1925 had already argued that Husserl's region of immanent consciousness is never interrogated as to its being (GA 20 §§10–13; retrospective, published 1979). Henry's reason differs. Heidegger asks for the being of consciousness and finds intentionality grounded in Dasein's transcendence; Henry finds that Husserl's immanent contents are reached by transcendence and so are not immanent at all.

**¶8.** Contents are called immanent or transcendent according as they are given in person or merely aimed at, like the unseen faces of a cube; both depend on the act of "orientation toward", transcendence, and so belong to one milieu, the horizon. The distinction is internal to that milieu, and contingent, since contents pass from one status to the other; the contingency is grounded in the finitude of the horizon, in which contents can appear only successively. Henry's ground for the contingency is the Kantbuch's account of finite intuition. GA 3 says that finite intuition remains bound "to the particular intuited individual" (§4, 27), and quotes Kant that the finite being meets what it encounters "scattered and singly" (§17, 82). Henry will quote that phrase at R §35 (K 141). The finitude that makes the immanent/transcendent distinction contingent is the finitude GA 3 places at the root of knowledge.

**¶9.** These contents are ontic. For a pure ontological content the mode of givenness is not contingent but strictly determined by the essence: the pure content of transcendence, the horizon, is transcendent in essence, and "to be immanent, likewise, for a pure ontological content, is to be radically determined in its being by a specific mode of appearing and revelation, by the original essence of revelation as residing in immanence". The first half restates GA 3: the horizon is the pure content that transcendence forms, "the horizon of a standing-against" (§25, 123). The second half posits a pure ontological content that GA 3 lacks, a content determined by a revelation that is not transcendence. Henry builds his concept of immanent content by strict analogy with the Kantbuch's horizon, as its counterpart.

**¶10.** An immanent content offers itself "without pro-posing itself in the manner of a horizon", reveals itself "otherwise than in the form of an outside". Any content given through a horizon is thereby excluded from immanence, and immanence must be understood as defined by the exclusion of all transcendence from its internal structure. The hyphen is the translators'. GA 3 writes *Vor-stellen* to bring out the setting-before in representing (§29, 148), and the 1953 French renders such setting-before with a hyphenated "pro-poser": time "se pro-pose" the look of succession, the I "se pro-pose" permanence (K 244, 248). Henry takes the translators' word, *pro-poser*, to pro-pose or set before, and denies it of immanence. The exclusion of transcendence from the internal structure of the essence is the exact negative of GA 3's thesis that self-affection "determines the innermost essence of transcendence" (§34, 190).

**¶11.** Defining immanence by the exclusion of transcendence also determines immanent content positively. Since transcendence is excluded from the essence of immanence, its content is not exterior to it: "the essence of immanence is not separated from its content", does not hold it before itself or receive it as something other. Its content "is constituted by it"; the reality of an immanent content "is identically the ontological reality of the essence itself". GA 3 has a formal parallel and a material difference. Heidegger also refuses to separate time's intuiting from its intuited: "time as pure intuition is in one the forming intuiting of what it intuits" (§32, 175). But Heidegger's unity is a forming, in which the intuited is a look held before the intuiting; Henry's is an identity without forming and without distance. Both make the receiving power one with its pure content. Heidegger's unity includes a setting-before; Henry's excludes it.

**¶12.** The concept of immanence designates the mode of reception of a content, the internal structure of any original reception. Since original receptivity is defined by immanence, it constitutes the pure content it receives: "what the original essence of receptivity receives is itself". The conclusion is the formula Henry has pursued since R §22. GA 3 says something formally similar of pure intuition, which "gives itself in taking-in what can be taken in" (§32, 173). The two formulas differ in their object. Heidegger's pure intuition gives itself the look of succession, a content distinct from the giving. Henry's receptivity receives itself.

**¶13.** To receive a content is to be affected by it. Since original receptivity receives a content constituted by itself, it is affected by itself: "being affected by oneself, affecting oneself, is to constitute oneself as auto-affection". "Auto-affection is the constitutive structure of the original essence of receptivity." Henry reaches by his own road the equation the Kantbuch states at its pivot: "taking in purely means: being affected free of experience, that is, affecting oneself" (§34, 190). The inference is the same, from pure receptivity to self-affection. The premises differ. Heidegger's pure taking-in is time's forming of a look it holds toward itself; Henry's is an immanence that holds nothing before itself. The same word now names two structures, and Henry's next section is devoted to keeping them apart.

**¶14.** Henry raises the objection himself. Does auto-affection not designate the way the essence affects itself within monism, by setting itself before itself as a horizon? Does the self-affection of time in schematism not show this as the ultimate structure of any self-affection: original time affects itself through the horizon of pure time, the threefold unity of succession, "because this affection through a horizon is the only way for the essence to affect itself, that is, to manifest itself"? The objection states the Kantbuch's position accurately. In GA 3 time affects itself by forming "the look of the one-after-another" and holding it "toward itself as the forming taking-in" (§34, 189); there is no other self-affection in the book. Henry poses as an objection what GA 3 offers as its thesis, and his answer will have to show that this is not the only form self-affection can take.

**¶15.** The task is now clear: to bring to light and dissipate "the fundamental ambiguity of the concept of auto-affection", if the original essence just disclosed is to be preserved in its purity. Henry's next section takes as its object the term the Kantbuch made central. The ambiguity he will dissipate is one between his concept and Heidegger's, and between the two phrasings of Heidegger's own text, self-affection and affection of itself (§34, 189), which he separated at R §24 ¶3.

### § 31. The fundamental ambiguity of the concept of auto-affection. Auto-affection and affection by itself

**¶1.** From the moment the manifestation of the essence was posed as the first condition of its activity, it was understood, spontaneously, as manifestation to itself: the appearing of appearing as a self-appearing. "The ontological possibility of the manifestation of the essence lies in the retro-reference of the essence to itself", which is not an added property but what makes appearing what it is. Henry's "retro-reference" (*rétro-référence*) names the structure GA 3 describes as the movement "back to itself" (*Zurück-auf-sich*, §34, 191) and as the "correlation of the back-to-into" that forms the against (192). The prefix echoes Henry's French, which renders the movement out and back as "cette « visée de soi vers ... » et cette « rétrospection »", this "aim from oneself toward ..." and this "retrospection" (K 246). Both authors put a reference of the self back to itself at the ground. They differ, as the rest of the section shows, over whether that reference passes through anything.

**¶2.** Auto-affection designates this retro-reference: the essence affects itself because it is active only if it manifests itself, and it manifests itself only if its manifestation is not lost to it but gathered and held in it; "or rather, the essence manifests itself to itself only because it is capable of affecting itself", of taking itself back and holding itself near itself, of being "for itself". "Auto-affection determines the essence of manifestation as what makes it possible." The formula reproduces the Kantbuch's: "the idea of pure self-affection ... determines the innermost essence of transcendence" (§34, 190). Henry writes "manifestation" where Heidegger wrote "transcendence". The substitution is the whole dispute in miniature: Henry agrees that self-affection is the ground and denies that what it grounds is transcendence.

**¶3.** The more evident the work of auto-affection, the more urgent it becomes to clarify its internal structure. If that structure is the essence's relation to itself, nothing is said until the relation is grasped in its inner possibility; the nature of the power that lets the essence enter into relation with itself, the internal structure of auto-affection, is now in question. GA 3 identifies the power: it is time, which forms the self by going at itself (§34, 189). Henry's question is whether the power GA 3 names can be the one his formula requires.

**¶4.** The problematic already has the elements to define that structure: "the possibility for the essence to affect itself is identically that of receiving itself". Insofar as receptivity is the essence's doing, the essence is affected; insofar as it concerns the essence, which is the content received, the essence is affected by itself. Auto-affection is the internal structure of an essence that receives itself, and its understanding depends on how the essence receives a content constituted by itself. Henry here runs GA 3's inference in reverse. The Kantbuch passes from pure taking-in to self-affection: "taking in purely means ... affecting oneself" (§34, 190). Henry passes from self-affection to self-reception. The equivalence is common to both, and Henry has made it explicit since R §30 ¶13.

**¶5.** What grounds the essence's possibility of receiving itself was first understood as objectivation, the essence setting itself before itself as a horizon, which is then the pure content it receives. But the problematic of receptivity showed that the power receiving the horizon is itself a content: "transcendence is received". Reception was always read as reception of an external content because the essence of manifestation was understood as representation, which projects before itself what it represents; this made inaccessible "the very idea of a reception that would not by essence be the reception of an external content". The Kantbuch does not fit this description well. Heidegger explicitly frees pure taking-in from the reception of something external: the narrow concept of taking-in "as receiving something present at hand" must be "kept away from pure intuition" (§32, 173), and pure intuition "gives itself in taking-in what can be taken in" (173). GA 3 has the idea of a reception whose content is not foreign to the receiver. Henry acknowledges this at once.

**¶6.** Can the incapacity really be imputed to monism, if the pure content represented by the essence is constituted by the essence itself? The reproach holds only against a thought that understands receptivity as reception of an ontic content; once the content is the pure ontological element, "it is manifestly the essence itself that constitutes the content it receives". The problematic is then in possession of "the idea of a receptivity whose content is not foreign to it". Henry grants GA 3 its best case. The Kantbuch's pure intuition gives itself what it takes in (§32, 173); its taking-in is "the forming self-giving of what gives itself" (§28, 141). Monism, in its Heideggerian form, does reach a reception of a self-constituted content. The question becomes how that content is received.

**¶7.** Receiving such a content means representing it, and representation sets its content before itself; so the essence's reception of itself in representation is still reception of an external content, "a reception that takes place in and through exteriority". The content is not external in the sense of different from the essence; but exteriority names the mode in which it belongs to the essence, and in that mode the content is external "in a radical sense". The essence manifests itself to itself in the form of a horizon, so that the identity of essence and content is "an identity in difference", and the essence manifests itself to itself "as other than itself". Henry's description fits GA 3 §34 closely, and GA 3 supplies the word for otherness. Time holds the look it forms "toward itself" (*zu-hält*, 189), and to affect the letting-stand-against purely "means to bring before it anything like an 'against-it', the against" (*Gegen-es*, *Dawider*), "to it", the I itself (189–190). In GA 3 the self is affected by being brought an "against-it". Henry's "manifests itself to itself as other than itself" restates the Kantbuch, and Heidegger would not dispute it as a description. He would dispute only that it disqualifies the structure as self-affection.

**¶8.** Here the foundations of phenomenological ontology seem threatened, since that ontology rests on "the identity of ontological reality and appearance as such". If the manifestation of a radically other content is read as the manifestation of the essence itself, by what right does the problematic pass from the appearance of the horizon to the claim that this is the essence's appearance? Or does it simply identify the essence with that appearance, and then how account for the otherness in it? GA 3 does not make the identification Henry questions. It does not say the essence appears as the horizon; it says time as self-affection forms the self (§34, 190–191). Henry's dilemma, a metaphysical claim beyond appearance or an identification with it, presupposes that self-affection must be read as a manifestation of the self. That presupposition is Henry's equation of reception with manifestation (R §22 ¶2). Within it the dilemma is sharp; GA 3 declines its premise.

**¶9.** The problematic must keep to its results: "the transcendental horizon of being is not the essence, but the product of its imagination". As creative intuition the essence produces only nothing, nothing ontic, and the created does not coincide with its creator. What manifests itself in the horizon is the horizon's own form, not the essence; or "manifest" takes the sense of an appearance that hides what it indicates, and then the horizon is "mere appearance" under which the essence hides, and Fichte's "always the form veils the essence" applies. Henry's first claim is GA 3's own: time "as this formed product" (*Gebilde*) "is not the original ground of transcendence; rather as pure self-affection" (§35, 200). Heidegger distinguishes the formed from the forming as Henry does. What GA 3 never says is that the essence manifests itself in the horizon; Henry's target here is his own formula for monism, not a sentence of the Kantbuch.

**¶10.** Even this is incorrect: to say the horizon veils the essence presupposes that the essence appears in it under an inadequate form. Strictly, "what appears 'in the form of the horizon' is the phenomenological content of that horizon and nothing more", and "ontological reality is not dissociable from the form in which it shows itself, being itself this form as such". The reality of the essence is "the pure phenomenological content in which it shows itself as such". Henry's principle is Heidegger's own phenomenological maxim: behind the phenomena of phenomenology "there is essentially nothing else" (SZ §7, 35–36), and GA 3 insists that appearances "are not mere semblance but beings themselves" (§5, 32). Henry turns the maxim against the Kantbuch's ground. If the essence shows itself only in its own phenomenological content, it does not show itself in the horizon, and if it shows itself nowhere else, the Kantbuch's ground does not show itself at all.

**¶11.** "In receiving the pure ontological content it represents, the essence does not receive itself." So "the inner ontological possibility of the essence of manifestation does not reside in representation". There are "two receptivities": one that receives the horizon, in representation, and one that receives itself, in immanence. "To receive in the first sense means to create the content one receives, in such a way that the creation of this content is identically its reception." "To receive in the second sense means ... to be this content", and to receive it passively, as something that does not depend on oneself, "as this uncreated something that one is oneself". The first definition is the Kantbuch's, now accepted. At R §23 ¶3 Henry rejected the identity of forming and receiving that §28 asserts ("this taking-in is in itself precisely the forming self-giving of what gives itself", 141). Here he grants it, as an exact description of the first receptivity. His final position incorporates GA 3's analysis and confines it. The second receptivity, passive and "uncreated", has no GA 3 counterpart: Heidegger's pure taking-in is a "spontaneous receptivity" (§29, 153), never a pure passivity.

**¶12.** In the receptivity of representation form and content differ: the form is the essence in its imaginative act, the content a purely imaginary product. "The transcendental horizon of being defines the ontological milieu of unreality", and form and content differ "as reality and unreality". In original receptivity form and content are identical. "There are two ways for the essence to receive, there is only one to receive itself." GA 3 rejects in advance a reading of the horizon as imaginary. What transcendental imagination forms "can essentially never be 'mere imagining'"; rather the horizon it forms, the understanding of being, "first makes possible anything like a distinction between ontic truth and ontic illusion ('mere imagining')" (§27, 138). Henry's "unreality" is not the ontic illusion Heidegger excludes. It is the ontological status of what does not reveal itself, as he defined it at R §19 ¶2. But his phrase "purely imaginary product" plays on imagination in exactly the way §27 warns against, and the play carries no argument the definition does not already carry.

**¶13.** The possibility of receiving itself is auto-affection, which is the essence's possibility of manifesting itself; this ultimate possibility, a mere presupposition so long as its own possibility was not grasped, is now determined: "the possibility of auto-affection lies in the original essence of receptivity, that is, in immanence". With this sentence Henry gives his answer to the question the Kantbuch answers with time. In GA 3 what makes self-affection possible is time's forming of the look it holds toward itself (§34, 189); in Henry it is immanence, which forms nothing.

**¶14.** To the doubling of receptivity corresponds a doubling of affection. Inasmuch as receptivity is objectivation, the essence is affected first by the horizon: "to affect purely the act of objectivation, as pure orientation toward ..., means to arouse an opposition to it", as Heidegger said (K 244 / GA 3 §34, 189). But before receiving the content that affects it, the act of objectivation receives itself: "more original than the affection of transcendence by the horizon is the affection of transcendence by itself". In the act by which it projects the horizon, "transcendence is already affected", independently of that act, and "the original essence of affection resides in immanence". The translation had lent Henry a pre-given subject at one point. Heidegger writes that "taking in purely means: being affected free of experience, that is, affecting oneself" (190); the French inserts a bearer, "une réceptivité pure [se trouve chez un sujet] affecté en dehors de l'expérience, c'est-à-dire s'affectant lui-même", a pure receptivity [is found in a subject] affected outside experience, that is, affecting itself (K 245), and Henry quotes "affected outside experience" from that sentence (R §24 ¶3). The thesis that transcendence is "already affected" before it forms its horizon contradicts §34 at its most explicit. Heidegger denies that self-affection is "an effecting affection that strikes a self already at hand" and says it forms the capacity "to be gone-at as a self" (189); the self is first constituted by the movement "from itself toward ... and back to itself" (191). Henry's "already affected" is not Heidegger's rejected pre-existing self struck from outside, since it is a self-affection, not an affection by something else. Both reject a ready-made self with an affection added to it. They place the self's constitution in opposite places: Heidegger in the ecstatic forming, Henry in an affection that precedes all forming.

**¶15.** Does the doubling introduce an ambiguity as a positive result, or does it expose a negative one, the constant confusion of affection with auto-affection? Since in receiving the represented content the essence does not receive itself, the affection of transcendence by the horizon is not an affection of transcendence by itself and cannot be called auto-affection. "Auto-affection resides neither in transcendence nor in representation." Henry's verdict follows: it is false to say that "pure affection of itself ... determines ... the innermost essence of transcendence" (K 245 / GA 3 §34, 190), if by pure affection of itself one means, "as Heidegger does", auto-affection. Nor can auto-affection be interpreted, "as Heidegger still wants", as the essence of time insofar as time belongs to the inner possibility of objectivation (n. 103: K 244 / GA 3 §34, 189, "time as pure self-affection forms the essential structure of subjectivity"). The quotations are exact, and they show where Henry's vocabulary comes from. Both French phrases in his note render one German term, *reine Selbstaffektion*: the translators write "affection pure de soi" some ten times and "auto-affection pure" once, in the sentence Henry quotes (K 244), while their introduction makes "auto-affection pure" the name of the doctrine (K 40). Henry's central term reaches him from that sentence and that introduction. His verdict therefore takes Heidegger's word and denies it Heidegger's referent. As a refutation it depends on two premises. The first, that affection by the horizon is not self-affection, Henry has argued at ¶¶7–11. The second, that Heidegger's self-affection is affection by the horizon, holds for one layer of §34: time "goes at itself with what is intuited as formed in it" (189). It does not obviously hold for the layer Heidegger marks with "yes, even more": time forms the very structure "from itself toward ...", in which the toward-which "looks back and into" the toward (189). Henry's ¶18 addresses that second layer, by a different argument.

**¶16.** The interpretation of auto-affection as transcendence is not accidental. Once auto-affection is the ultimate possibility of manifestation, its structure must be determined, and within monism transcendence is the only essence available; since transcendence creates an affection, it is taken to give content to the concept of auto-affection. Hence the confusion between affection in general and auto-affection, between the affection of objectivation by its content and the original affection of that act by itself. The ambiguity "does not mean that there are two ways for the essence to affect itself"; it is the work of a problematic lacking the means to do otherwise. Henry's genealogy fits the Kantbuch exactly. GA 3 introduces self-affection as what "belongs to the inner possibility of this letting-stand-against" (§34, 190), that is, as the ground of transcendence, and it has no other candidate. Henry's claim that self-affection is not two things but one, wrongly assigned, is the formal thesis of his reading of the Kantbuch.

**¶17.** Does auto-affection not have a second meaning, the affection of objectivation by the pure content it represents? That content is produced by objectivation itself: transcendence creates the horizon that affects it, hence its own affection, and so affects itself through the horizon. Is this affection by itself not an auto-affection? The objection is the first layer of §34 in Henry's words: time forms the look of succession and "goes at itself" with it (189). Henry states it fairly.

**¶18.** "Affected by itself" has two senses. In the first, transcendence is affected by a content that originates in it: the affection by itself has "a metaphysical signification concerning the ultimate origin of this affection". But the phenomenological content of the affection by the horizon is only the horizon's appearance; the power that arouses it is not included in it. That this appearance is transcendence's doing can be affirmed only if the problematic has "an affection whose effective phenomenological content is constituted by the essence of transcendence itself", which is auto-affection. "Far from coinciding with the affection by itself of transcendence in the horizon, the concept of auto-affection is its simple presupposition." Here Henry meets the second layer of §34. GA 3 holds that forming and formed are "in one" (§32, 175) and that the self is first constituted in the movement out and back (§34, 191); it needs no prior self to own the horizon. Henry's argument is transcendental in form: for the horizon to be given as the self's own, the self must already be revealed to itself; a self constituted only by the movement would not know the horizon as its own. The argument does not refute GA 3 on its terms, since GA 3 never claims that the self knows the horizon as its own. It shows what the Kantbuch would have to add if self-affection is to mean self-manifestation, which is the sense Henry's "auto-affection" requires and the 1953 French lets him read into "Selbstaffektion".

**¶19.** In Henry's second sense, transcendence affects itself in that it is itself the pure content that affects it: to be affected by itself means not to create the affecting content but "to be this content". Only in this sense can the affection by itself of transcendence be defined rigorously as an auto-affection. The definition repeats ¶11's second receptivity, now applied to transcendence: transcendence is self-affected only by being revealed to itself in immanence. GA 3 has no place for transcendence being its own content in this sense, since its content is always the look it forms.

**¶20.** Calling the original auto-affection of transcendence an "affection by itself" risks renewing the ambiguity. Applied to transcendence, "affection by itself" must mean an affection whose mode of accomplishment is transcendence, that is, objectivation; affecting in this way is raising a horizon. "When the affection of transcendence is the work of transcendence itself, the content of this affection is no longer constituted by transcendence." Auto-affection and affection by itself then exclude each other. Henry's exclusion is the strict form of his thesis: what transcendence does, it does by forming a horizon, so any affection it produces has the horizon as content. GA 3's "yes, even more" layer is exactly a claim that transcendence produces, by its forming, something other than a horizon-content: the looking-back into the turning-toward (§34, 189). Henry's exclusion denies that this looking-back is anything but the horizon looking. The denial is consistent with his reading of ¶18, and it is not argued from the text of §34. His own French offered a different image: the formed goal "reflue sur cette visée", flows back onto the aim (K 244), a return with no look in it.

**¶21.** The paradox follows: it is precisely not "through itself", by its own process, that transcendence can be affected "by itself", by a content constituted by its own essence. Understanding the relation between the two modes of affection, affection by itself and auto-affection, is understanding the relation between transcendence and immanence. Henry ends where the Kantbuch could not follow. In GA 3 transcendence is self-affecting through itself; that is the meaning of time as the "innermost essence of transcendence" (§34, 190). Henry has made "through itself" and "by itself" incompatible for transcendence. Whether his reading of the Kantbuch holds depends on that incompatibility, which rests on his equation of being affected with being manifest and on his denial that the looking-back of §34 is more than the horizon's appearing.

### § 32. Immanence and transcendence

**¶1.** That the auto-affection of transcendence excludes the affection accomplished by transcendence is not a paradox but the problematic's own affirmation: reality is subordinated to the inner structure of the mode in which it is received. An affection whose mode is transcendence has a horizon for its content, and the reality of transcendence is not a horizon; so for transcendence to be the content of an affection, the mode of receptivity must not be transcendence but one that reaches its content "without surpassing itself toward it". Henry's principle, that what is received is determined by the mode of reception, generalizes the Kantbuch's own order of dependence. In GA 3 the unveiledness of being depends on transcendence, which "is the original truth" (§25, 123), and the horizon is what it is because transcendence forms it. Heidegger subordinates the being of the horizon to its mode of disclosure; Henry extends the rule to transcendence itself and concludes that transcendence must be disclosed by a mode that is not transcendence. The rule is Heidegger's; its application to transcendence is Henry's.

**¶2.** In a mode of receptivity without transcendence, the received reality does not take the form of a horizon, is nothing that stands before, and "in this absence of all distance identifies itself with the essence". Such a mode exists, immanence, and it means the possibility of a reality not defined by exteriority, "the original ontological possibility of transcendence itself". What makes a thing possible is properly its essence: "immanence is the essence of transcendence". The definition of essence as what makes possible is the Kantbuch's: GA 3 recalls that *essentia* "makes a being possible in what it is", hence the name *possibilitas*, "inner possibility" (§40, 223), and its whole inquiry seeks "the inner possibility" of transcendence. Henry's thesis has the exact form of GA 3's: "pure self-affection ... determines the innermost essence of transcendence" (§34, 190). Both name the essence of transcendence. Heidegger names time's self-affecting; Henry names immanence. The sentence is the positive counterpart of the verdict at R §31 ¶15.

**¶3.** Is this a positive determination of transcendence's reality, or a negative one? That the content of an essence without transcendence is nothing exterior leaves room for a pure content essentially different from any horizon, and transcendence differs from the horizon, since "the pure imaginative product of the act of imagination does not coincide with the ontological reality of that act". What follows so far is only a similarity of formal structure; that transcendence is in fact the content of original receptivity "must be shown". Henry's scruple concerns his own argument, and GA 3 offers no locus. It does supply the distinction he relies on, between imagination's product and the act: time "as this formed product" is not the ground of transcendence, but time "as pure self-affection" is (§35, 200).

**¶4.** Receiving means making manifest. "The concept of receptivity belongs to phenomenology": the modes of receptivity are modes of revelation, and the differentiation of reality they produce "is a real differentiation only by reason of its phenomenological character". The content of original receptivity is therefore "not simply something = x", of which we would know only that it is not the horizon; it is "the phenomenological reality of transcendence itself", and immanence is the essence of transcendence "because it reveals it and so makes it possible in its very being". "Something = x" is Kant's transcendental object, and the Kantbuch makes that X the pure content of ontological knowledge: "a nothing", "not a being, but nonetheless 'something'", the horizon of a standing-against (§25, 121–123). Henry sets his immanent content against GA 3's X on the point where GA 3 is most explicit: the X is "absolutely no possible object of a knowing" (122), whereas the content of immanence is revealed as what it is. The contrast is exact. In GA 3 the pure ontological content is a nothing held open; in Henry it is the self-revealed reality of the act that holds it open.

**¶5.** It is not enough to state what original receptivity reveals; *how* it reveals must be understood, and "the revelation of the content of this original receptivity is not dissociable from this 'how', being itself this how as such". Transcendence shows itself within a specific mode of receptivity, so that the reality revealed, the act forming the horizon, "is in itself nothing transcendent". "Immanence is the essence of transcendence because it reveals it, but more precisely ... because it reveals it in this determinate way that makes it possible in its essence." The emphasis on the "how" recalls R §27 ¶3, where transcendence was defined as the how of the horizon's appearing. Henry now assigns transcendence a how of its own. In GA 3 the how of transcendence's self-relation is its temporal structure, the movement from itself toward and back (§34, 189–191); in Henry it is a revelation in which nothing goes out and nothing returns.

**¶6.** The identity of transcendence with the immanent content of the essence is seen and also understood, but this understanding "is not our doing": it belongs to the immediate revelation of transcendence. "The immanence of transcendence is its revelation." The word "understanding" (*compréhension*) is inadequate, since the grasp "does not take place in the mode of an understanding, that is, on the ground of transcendence in it". "The structure of reason is the structure of phenomenality", and that structure is revealed in "our vision itself as it is", because "we live this vision as it is". The immanent character of transcendence is not posited a priori by analysis; it arises with transcendence's reality and is "a phenomenological character in a radical sense". Henry's refusal of the word "understanding" is aimed at the Kantbuch's ground. "Compréhension de l'être" is the French for *Seinsverständnis*, and GA 3 makes understanding projective: "understanding ... is not only a kind of knowing but primarily a basic moment of existing" and has "the character of projection" (§42, 233). Henry places the revelation of transcendence below any understanding so defined: it is not projected, not grasped "on the ground of transcendence", but lived. The verb "live", used since R §17 for the life of consciousness, here names the revelation of transcendence itself. It anticipates the doctrine of life of Henry's later work and has no counterpart in GA 3, where existing and understanding are one.

### § 33. The ontological interpretation of the essence of transcendence as immanence and the internal possibility of surpassing

**¶1.** The interpretation of transcendence as immanence clarifies the inner possibility of the transcendental relation of being-in-the-world, which is not a mere relation but a "relating oneself to" (*se rapporter à*), in which the relation itself relates to what it relates to. The sides of a figure stand in relations but do not relate themselves to one another; the table, as Heidegger says, cannot "touch the wall" (SZ 55), because neither can "surpass itself toward" the other. "The 'relating oneself to' of the relation does not find its condition in transcendence; it is on the contrary what makes it possible." Henry's argument turns on the reflexive pronoun, and the Kantbuch's vocabulary of transcendence is full of it: turning-oneself-toward (*Sich-zuwenden-zu*, §16, 71), holding-before-oneself (*Sich-vorhalten*, §19, 90), going-at-oneself (*Sich-selbst-angehen*, §34, 189). For Heidegger the "self" of these reflexives is formed: time as self-affection forms "anything like the 'from itself toward ...'" (§34, 189). For Henry the "self" of relating-oneself is what makes the relating possible. Both place the reflexive at the ground. They disagree whether it is formed by the movement or presupposed by it.

**¶2.** "Relating oneself to" means "bringing oneself near" (*s'apporter soi-même auprès de*): the movement toward does not separate from itself but stays near itself, so that the self kept in the movement is carried with it to what it goes toward, and "this keeping-near-itself of the movement in its very accomplishment is identically its original revelation to itself". "Where there is a relation, it exists for itself." The original self-revelation that makes possible the movement of bringing oneself near "is immanence". The contrast with GA 3 is precise. In §34 the self of transcendence is constituted by a movement out and back: "this 'from itself toward ... and back to itself' first constitutes the mind as a finite self" (191). In Henry the self is kept in the movement and never needs to come back, because it never left. Heidegger's self is a return; Henry's is a persistence.

**¶3.** Grave confusions arise when transcendence is grasped in its "essence" as "surpassing toward". The surpassing is usually understood negatively, from beings: the table cannot touch the wall because it cannot surpass itself toward it; it stays "in itself", shut in. To surpass toward the wall would be for the table to go out of itself, to be outside itself, and to create a "neighbourhood" where it would find all things and itself in the light. "Being outside oneself near" is the possibility the table lacks. The example comes from *Sein und Zeit* (55), and the definition of transcendence as surpassing (*Überstieg*) from *Vom Wesen des Grundes*, which Henry knew in Henry Corbin's translation (WG). The Kantbuch refers to that essay for its account of transcendence (GA 3, 119 n. 172), but its own definition is different: transcendence is the passing-over of pure reason "to beings" (§3, 16) and the prior turning-toward that lets them stand against (§16, 71). GA 3 does not describe transcendence as a being going out of itself. Henry's account of the negative derivation fits the 1929 essay better than the 1929 book.

**¶4.** This possibility, which the table lacks, is taken as the essence of relation, of transcendence. The understanding stays obscure and passes over the essence of transcendence. The equivocation concerns what is surpassed: for the table, the table itself; and "the surpassing of beings is the world", so phenomenality appears enclosed in a surpassing in which the reality that surpasses is also surpassed. Is man not the being able to surpass himself and find himself outside, "a man among men, a thing among things"? The target is again *Vom Wesen des Grundes*, where what is surpassed in transcendence includes the being that Dasein itself is, and the toward-which of the surpassing is called world. GA 3 states the link to world only in passing: the analysis of everydayness is to show that all dealings with beings are grounded in "the transcendence of Dasein, being-in-the-world" (§43, 235). The Kantbuch's own analysis of transcendence concerns the turning-toward and its horizon, not a self-surpassing of a being. Henry's equivocation is real in the essay; in the Kantbuch it is at most implied by the equation of transcendence with being-in-the-world.

**¶5.** Man as a being cannot surpass anything either: his hand, as an ontic reality, no more touches the wall than the table does. Is the world, which unfolds beyond all beings, not the surpassing itself, exteriority, "what is more objective than any object", the light? But "in objectivity there is no relation"; "it is not the particular nature of the hand but its objective nature that prevents it from relating itself to the wall". A manifestation, even effective, does not ground a relation; the phenomenological reality of the transcendental relation "is not the phenomenality of the world". Henry draws from Heidegger's own example the opposite moral. In *Sein und Zeit* the table cannot touch the wall because it is merely present-at-hand and lacks being-in-the-world (SZ 55); the remedy is Dasein's transcendence. For Henry the hand cannot touch because it is an object, and the world as objectivity contains no relation at all. Against GA 3 the point is that objectivity, the horizon formed in letting-stand-against (§25, 123), is not itself a relating. GA 3 would agree; it locates the relating in the letting, not in the horizon. Henry's next step denies that the letting is the relating's ground.

**¶6.** The formal identity between the surpassing of beings in the world and the essence of transcendence is illusory. For the human being to surpass toward the world is to be the reality surpassed, to manifest itself as a being, and in that manifestation there is no relation. "To surpass toward, in the sense of transcendence, to relate oneself to, is to be oneself the surpassing, a surpassing that does not surpass itself and that is possible as such ... as immanence." Transcendence never goes beyond itself, which means it does not manifest itself in a world, and never stays short of its surpassing, which means it is never a being. Henry's formula, "a surpassing that does not surpass itself", has a curious near-echo in GA 3, which he does not cite: transcendence is "a constant standing-out toward ... (Ekstasis). But this essential standing-out toward ... forms precisely in the standing, and holds before itself, a horizon" (§24, 119). Heidegger's ecstasis includes a "standing" in which the forming takes place. Henry's surpassing that "remains in itself" names a persistence of the same kind and denies that it is ecstatic. The difference is whether the standing in the standing-out is itself a standing-out.

**¶7.** Understanding transcendence from the surpassing of beings dismembers it between beings and world; their synthetic union would not contain it either. "Transcendence is neither beings nor the world." The confusion shows when transcendence is spelled out as "transcendence of the world": the surpassing of beings in the horizon is the only phenomenological reference of the concept, so the inner possibility of surpassing is sought in the world, and "the living bond of a relating oneself to" is lost "in the relation of indifference between light and things". The phrase "transcendence of the world" again points to *Vom Wesen des Grundes* and *Sein und Zeit*. GA 3's §43 makes the same identification (235). Henry's verdict, that the phenomenological reference of "transcendence" is in fact the world, restates his reading of the Kantbuch since R §25: what appears in transcendence is its horizon, never its act.

**¶8.** If transcendence is the being-outside-itself of beings, immanence becomes the being-inside-itself of beings, "an ontic category", and the mere privation of going out. A commentator writes that "a non-free being would be a being so absolutely folded upon itself that it would be constitutionally incapable of being near the other" (Alphonse de Waelhens, n. 105). On the ruin of these concepts a "so-called philosophy of existence" builds on the empty opposition of "existence" and "thing". "In the 'privilege' that man has to 'exist' the absurdity of the philosophy of existence is concentrated." The quoted words are the translators': "ce privilège, en un mot, d'exister", this privilege, in a word, of existing (K 284), for GA 3's "this privilege, to exist" (*dieser Vorzug, zu existieren*): "not merely to be present at hand among other beings ... but, in the midst of beings, to be delivered over to them as such and handed over to oneself as a being" (§41, 228). Heidegger's privilege is not an interiority opposed to things; it is the need of the understanding of being that it "harbours" (228). Henry's barb hits the existentialist reading of the privilege, which makes existence a going-out opposed to the thing's staying-in. It does not hit the Kantbuch's version, where existing is defined by dependence on beings and by the understanding of being, not by an outside opposed to an inside.

**¶9.** "The table is no more 'in itself' than it is 'outside itself'." The thought that gives beings an interiority shares the illusion of the thought that makes them the origin of a relation. It is not because the table stays in itself that it cannot touch the wall, but "because it does not stay in itself". Immanence is not a category of beings and neither is transcendence; immanence is "the fundamental ontological category that makes surpassing itself possible". Henry's claim that beings have no "in itself" in his sense agrees with Heidegger's treatment of the present-at-hand, which has neither an inside nor a world (SZ 55). The disagreement is over what grounds surpassing. GA 3 grounds it in finitude: "a finite being needs this basic faculty of a turning-toward that lets stand against" (§16, 71). Henry grounds it in a non-ecstatic self-relation that only a being which is not a thing has.

**¶10.** Freed of their ontic misreadings, transcendence and immanence stand not in opposition but in a relation of foundation. Merleau-Ponty's extension of transcendence to the whole phenomenological field (PhP 192–193; n. 106), which leads him to reject immanence, shows instead the universality of immanence, since the two concepts have the same extension. "Where there is a relation, it exists first in itself." If man surpasses toward the world, then it is false to say, with the Letter on Humanism, that "man is never first on this side of the world" (n. 107): "on this side of the world remains and persists, if not man himself, at least the reality that accomplishes the surpassing". The thesis contradicts GA 3 as well as the 1946 letter. The Kantbuch says there is no self prior to the movement that constitutes it: the from-itself-toward-and-back-to-itself "first constitutes" the mind as a self (§34, 191), and "more original than man is the finitude of Dasein in him" (§41, 229). Henry's "on this side of the world" names a reality that accomplishes the surpassing and remains behind it. GA 3 has no room for such a reality; for Heidegger nothing remains on this side of transcendence except what transcendence makes possible.

**¶11.** The abstract denial of immanence goes with an abstract affirmation of the relation of transcendence, posed without any account of its inner possibility, and its groundlessness is read as the mark of the ground, the absolute: the relation flashes like lightning, "der reine Bezug" (the pure relation), "a ground without ground", "the contingent nature of the absolute". A commentator calls existence "a kind of absolute without origin or destiny, *Relation itself*" (G. Varet, n. 108). Realism is the realization of what presupposes reality, and the philosophy of being, omitting the essence of transcendence, stays at the level of the philosophy of consciousness that declares consciousness "only relation" (n. 109). "Der reine Bezug" is Rilke's phrase as Heidegger reads it in *Holzwege*. "A ground without ground" names the abyss (*Ab-grund*) of *Vom Wesen des Grundes*, and the Kantbuch's laying of the ground ends before the same abyss (§31, 168; §38, 215). Henry reads the abyss as an absence of ground passed off as a feature of the ground, as at R §25 ¶6. GA 3 does not present the abyss as the absolute; it presents it as what Kant shrank from and what finitude opens. Henry's charge of "pathos" (here and at R §26) addresses the existentialist reception more than the Kantbuch, whose abyss is a result of its analysis of finitude, stated without the vocabulary of the absolute.

### § 34. Consciousness of the world and consciousness without world

**¶1.** With the essence of transcendence determined as immanence, the transcendental relation of being-in-the-world is grasped in its inner possibility, which lies in the manifestation of the relation itself. Understood so, the relation stands in radical opposition to the world and its phenomenality: "transcendence is not in the world", neither as a being within it nor "as what the world is". "The not-being-in-the-world of being-in-the-world" is its ownmost possibility, which lies in "the original immanent revelation of the movement itself". "The movement is essentially possible as non-objective", always "on this side of the world, outside the field of the horizon". The first half of the paragraph agrees with the Kantbuch. GA 3's transcendence is not a being and not an object: ontological knowledge "relates not at all thematically and directly to beings" (§25, 120), and pure intuition intuits its content "unobjectively" (§32, 173). Heidegger would accept that transcendence is not in the world as beings are. The second half departs. For GA 3 transcendence simply is being-in-the-world (§43, 235); there is no "on this side" of it. Henry's phrase "not-being-in-the-world of being-in-the-world" opens inside Heidegger's term the place his immanence will occupy.

**¶2.** "The reality of the movement is the essence of imagination", and that essence is its manifestation, its "consciousness". To Husserl's question of a consciousness without world (*Ideen I* §49, n. 110), the answer is given with the consciousness of imagination: "the consciousness of imagination is a consciousness without world". Imagination is the power that raises a world and so is a consciousness of the world, but that consciousness must not be confused with the consciousness of imagination: the world belongs to imagination as what it imagines, the manifestation of its nothingness, but it does not belong to the consciousness of imagination, from which it is excluded. The world becomes phenomenal "in the original revelation immanent to this act and constitutive of its reality". Henry sets his thesis against a sentence of Kant that the Kantbuch quotes twice: imagination is "a blind though indispensable function of the soul ... of which we are, however, seldom even conscious" (§14, 63), and Heidegger asks whether it is "an accident" that Kant says this of the faculty that proves to be the root (§27, 137). For Kant and for GA 3 the forming power is the one we are least conscious of. For Henry it is the one whose consciousness grounds every other. The two readings of the same faculty are exact opposites, and Henry's account of intellectualism as a doctrine of the unconscious (R §20 ¶5) is his explanation of why the Kantbuch finds its ground unconscious. Husserl serves here as an intermediary: Henry uses the thought-experiment of the annihilated world to describe the self-revelation of the Kantbuch's transcendental imagination. His "manifestation of the nothingness of the world" matches GA 3's horizon, "a nothing" (§25, 122–123).

**¶3.** "Consciousness of the world" implies a division of phenomenality, not a single concept. One cannot say that consciousness of the world is always also consciousness of itself, as if one concept covered both, since what varies is the very structure of phenomenality; hence Henry's reserve about the word "consciousness", which otherwise means only manifestation, understood within monism as consciousness of the world. Self-consciousness is then interpreted from the structure of world-consciousness, as in Hegel's "negation of the negation" (n. 111). "If the world becomes phenomenal in the original immanent revelation of the act of imagination, consciousness of the world is effective only on the ground of a consciousness to which the world does not belong." The criticism of Hegel fits the Kantbuch too. In GA 3 self-consciousness is made possible by time's self-affection, which forms "the from itself toward" and the "against" (§34, 189–190): time as self-affection forms the finite self "so that the self can be something like self-consciousness" (190). Heidegger derives self-consciousness from the same structure that forms the horizon. That is what Henry calls interpreting self-consciousness from world-consciousness, and his reserve about the word "consciousness" echoes Heidegger's own refusal of it (GA 3, 289; retrospective) for the opposite reason.

**¶4.** "With" or "without world" are structural determinations of the phenomenality of consciousness. Consciousness is nonetheless one: consciousness of the world is also, "in the unity of one and the same phenomenological event", consciousness of itself, because consciousness of the world is not self-sufficient but is effective only as the world-less consciousness of imagination. "The possibility of a consciousness without world is the original ontological possibility of consciousness itself and, as such, its essence." Henry's unity of world-consciousness and self-consciousness has a GA 3 counterpart stated as identity. Heidegger concludes that time, the pure form of intuition, and the "I think" "no longer stand opposed as incompatible and heterogeneous; they are the same" (§34, 191). For Heidegger the unity is sameness: the self is the time that forms the horizon. For Henry it is foundation: the self-consciousness without world grounds the consciousness of the world. Henry's "one event" with two irreducible structures is his answer to Heidegger's identification of the I with time.

**¶5.** The essence of consciousness as consciousness without world means the reality of a revelation that does not have the form of the world, a dimension of phenomenality not phenomenalized in exteriority, in which the phenomenality of exteriority itself is made possible. That possibility lies in the essence where transcendence does not act: consciousness is possible without world insofar as it does not transcend itself toward the world. "The phenomenological reality of the original dimension of revelation in which there is no world, because transcendence does not act in it, is the phenomenological reality of transcendence itself." The paragraph restates R §32's thesis in the vocabulary of world. GA 3 offers no counterpart. Its nearest is the claim that time as self-affection forms "the essential structure of subjectivity" (§34, 189), but that structure is the forming of a horizon. Henry's dimension "in which there is no world" is defined by the absence of what GA 3 makes essential.

**¶6.** A double evidence results: phenomenality phenomenalizes itself originally in a sphere without transcendence, and transcendence itself becomes real in that sphere. "Not every moment of the essence has the character of transcendence"; the structure of the whole ontological process of transcendence includes, as its ultimate possibility, a moment that does not transcend itself, "a sphere of existence without transcendence as the sphere of existence of transcendence itself". The sentence denies two theses of GA 3 at once. The Kantbuch holds that "transcendence is in itself ecstatic-horizontal" (§24, 119), so that every moment of it stands out toward a horizon, and that pure self-affection, the ground, "determines the innermost essence of transcendence" (§34, 190), so that the ground is transcendence's own. Henry's "moment of the essence that does not transcend itself" is the innermost moment GA 3 calls self-affection, now declared non-ecstatic. The disagreement is no longer over interpretation but over whether a non-ecstatic moment exists at all.

**¶7.** With the grasp of the element in being-in-the-world that does not relate to the world and does not transcend itself, the movement of transcendence is grounded in its phenomenological possibility; and the determination of the essence of transcendence as immanence is "the bringing to light of what makes possible the coherence of the internal structure of the essence of manifestation". The closing phrase returns to R §22 ¶3, where receptivity was called "what ensures the possibility of the internal coherence" of the essence. GA 3 places the unity of its ground in the "original and not first compounded unity" of imagination (§29, 153) and finally in time (§35, 196). Henry announces that the coherence lies in immanence. The next section tests that claim against GA 3's own account of the unity of transcendence.

### § 35. The coherence of the internal structure of the essence

**¶1.** What secures the coherence of the essence's internal structure was understood as what is most essential in it, but within monism: since the essence was an act of going out of itself, its coherence appeared as the holding and receiving of the horizon it raised, and since that reception was identical with the opposing itself, transcendence, "intuitive in its nature", seemed to secure the coherence of the structure it unfolds. Henry summarizes R §§22–24 as the Kantbuch's position, and accurately: GA 3 places the unity of its ground in imagination as "the original and not first compounded unity of receptivity and spontaneity" (§29, 153), and makes transcendence intuitive at its core through time (§§32–34).

**¶2.** With the coherence of "the unified structure of transcendence" placed in transcendence, the problematic is referred to the ground as a problem: the coherence of the structure transcendence unfolds "depends on the coherence of the internal structure of the act of objectivation considered in itself". Transcendence can hold the horizon it creates only if it can first hold itself in the originally coherent unity of its own structure. That unity lies in the movement's keeping-near-itself in its accomplishment, which is its original self-revelation: "the originally coherent unity of the movement of transcendence ... lies in immanence". GA 3 has a precise account of the act's unity with itself, and it is the account Henry rejects. The "standing and abiding" I is so called because "only insofar as the I as such holds before itself in advance anything like constancy and abiding does it form the horizon of sameness within which what is objective can be experienced as the same in change" (§34, 193). In the Kantbuch the I's sameness with itself is a horizon of sameness it holds before itself. Henry's argument, that the act can hold the horizon only if it first holds itself, denies that a held-before sameness can be the act's own unity.

**¶3.** The unity of the act of objectivation also makes possible the unity of the whole structure it engenders, the coherent unity of transcendence and horizon. Henry again rejects the result of the schematism: "with the unity of spontaneity and receptivity of transcendence with respect to the horizon, the unity that grounds the internal coherence of the act of transcendence considered in itself has nothing to do". That unity designates only that the act forming the horizon is the act receiving it, one act; it does not designate "the inner possibility of this act remaining one in itself in its accomplishment". It concerns the act in its relation to the horizon and so is "the unity of the essence and of the pure milieu it imagines, not the inner unity of the essence itself". Henry now grants the identity of forming and receiving that §28 and §29 assert (as at R §31 ¶11) and denies it the rank of an inner unity. The distinction he draws, between an act being one act and an act remaining one with itself, is not drawn in GA 3, where the "original and not compounded unity" of imagination (§29, 153) is offered as the innermost unity there is.

**¶4.** The phrase "coherence of the internal structure of the essence" is equivocal. The internal structure contains only the essence's reality; the synthetic totality of the whole structure contains also the imaginary product. "The coherence of this whole structure is not that of the essence in its internal structure; it presupposes it." "Schematism inverts the terms", giving the unity of the whole structure for the unity of transcendence, and this whole-structure coherence is "improperly designated as 'unified structure ... of transcendence'" (K 176 / GA 3 §24, 119), so that immanence, the essence of transcendence, sinks into oblivion. The cut in the quotation is significant. Heidegger writes that the supreme principle expresses "the most original phenomenological knowledge of the innermost unified structure of transcendence" (119). The French keeps the word: "la structure intime et unifiée de la transcendance", the intimate and unified structure of transcendence (K 176). Henry's ellipsis removes "intime et", the translators' "innermost", the word that states the claim he calls improper. Heidegger does locate that innermost unity where Henry says: the "essential unity of the full structure of transcendence" lies in the turning-toward letting-stand-against "as such forming the horizon of objectivity in general" (119), so that transcendence is "in itself ecstatic-horizontal". Henry's description of what Heidegger means is exact; what he omits is that Heidegger calls it innermost on purpose. The disagreement is not a confusion on Heidegger's part between two unities. Heidegger holds that there is no unity of the act more inward than its ecstatic-horizonal unity.

**¶5.** That the unity of spontaneity and receptivity, of thought and intuition, cannot be "the inner possibility of the essential unity of the pure veritative synthesis" (K 99 / GA 3 §7, 39–40) shows in how schematism achieves it: by determining both as "representations", so that the inner unity of each is "each time the unity of transcendence and horizon". The unity of thought becomes the unity of thought with what it represents. Intuition, though, claims to secure the unity of representation by receiving the horizon, so "the unity of intuition no longer rests on the unity of representation: it grounds it"; and intuition, that is transcendence, can unify representation only if it is itself one. Henry's reconstruction is faithful: GA 3 unites thought and intuition in "the same genus of *Vor-stellen* in general" (§29, 148), and finds their essential unity in the pure synthesis of imagination (§§13–14). His conclusion repeats the reversal of R §24 ¶5 at the level of unity. GA 3 grounds the unity of the I in synthesis: Kant "does not hesitate to say that transcendental apperception 'presupposes' the synthesis", and "all synthesis is effected by imagination" (§17, 80). Henry grounds synthesis in a unity of the act that precedes it.

**¶6.** The unity of intuition refers to reality and so names the unity of thought, imagination and transcendence alike: the inner, immanent unity, "the unity of reality itself". Thought and intuition are one on the ground of this unity, not through the unity of representation, which presupposes it. The inner unity of thought, of intuition and of the act of transcendence "are in truth only one and the same unity, the unity of reality", and it cannot be found in the structure of representation as its unity, since it makes representation possible. GA 3's counterpart to this one unity is its root. Heidegger names imagination the "unknown common root" of the two stems (§27, 137) and then original time (§35, 196). Henry's unity of reality occupies the place of the root, with the same function: what intuition and thought have in common and what makes them possible. The difference is that GA 3's root is a forming and Henry's is a self-revelation.

**¶7.** Representation opens the horizon and unifies it originally, and three kinds of unity must be distinguished. First, the unity of the horizon: beings are reached not "in dispersion and isolation" (K 141 / GA 3 §17, 82) but in an already synthesized whole, since "unity comes to beings only insofar as they manifest themselves"; "all unity is in principle phenomenological" (n. 115, citing Husserl on time as the unifying field). Manifestation can unify only if it is itself one, which refers to a synthetic power that constantly unifies the horizon: "all synthesis is produced by imagination" (K 138 / GA 3 §17, 80). Hence, second, the unity of representation as unity of the essence with what it represents, transcendence and horizon; and, third, "the internal unity of the essence itself". GA 3 recognizes the first two exactly as Henry describes them. The finite being meets beings "scattered and singly", and so "something like 'connection' must be understood in advance" (§17, 82), and that connection is the pure synthesis of imagination (§17, 80). For the third, GA 3 has the unity of transcendental apperception, and it places that unity after synthesis, not before: apperception "presupposes" synthesis and is "essentially related to pure imagination" (80). Henry's third unity reverses the order of §17. His note 117 extends the diagnosis to Hegel, whose unity "identical with difference" would be the same substitution of the whole structure's unity for the essence's. The schematism of GA 3 is, for Henry, the Kantian form of that Hegelian error.

### § 36. The essential ontological signification of the concept of immanence: the immediate

**¶1.** In its inner, radically immanent unity lies the essence's fundamental possibility, to be itself. The essence is action, the becoming of exteriority, the power that produces it; what lets it unite with its action, take possession of its own power, is "its original action", "its fundamental power": "the ontological structure in which the essence's reunion with itself is accomplished is that of the original receptivity by which the essence receives itself in immanence". The Kantbuch also names an original act. Letting-stand-over-against is "the original concept and ... the original act (*Urhandlung*) of the understanding" (§16, 74), and the I as original time "forms the letting-stand-against and its horizon" (§34, 193). For Heidegger the original act is the forming of the horizon. For Henry it is the act by which the forming act comes into possession of itself. Henry places an act beneath the Kantbuch's original act.

**¶2.** The concept of immanence refers to the inner ontological structure of the essence as what makes it possible, "the essence of the essence". That sense is lost when immanence is taken to mean the essence's belonging to its determinations, its presence in its modes, a usage that "covers the impotence of thought" to grasp the inner structure of the essence. Henry's "essence of the essence" echoes a phrase of the Kantbuch, which says that without working out the question of being every horizon is lacking for the attempt "to 'define' the essentiality of essence" (*die Wesenheit des Wesens*, §40, 224). Heidegger's answer to that question, in the same section, is the understanding of being and its inner possibility; Henry's answer is immanence. Both turn from the essence present in its instances to the essence's own possibility.

**¶3.** What can the classical interpretation of the essence's relation to its modes be worth without that determination? The essence can make its modes possible only if it is itself possible; classical philosophy is content with the immanence of the essence in natural consciousness, or of "Spirit" or "Reason" in individuals, and so merely names the possibility of knowledge in a tautology. GA 3 is not engaged. The charge of tautology repeats R §29 ¶3, there against the Hegel essay's "appearing that appears". The Kantbuch's own procedure is not tautological in this sense, since it seeks the inner possibility of transcendence in stages and names it (time); Henry's dispute with it concerns whether what it names is the right ground, not whether it names one.

**¶4.** The relation of immanence of the essence to its modes nonetheless points, confusedly, to the idea that drives ontology, the inner possibility of the essence, the essence's union with itself. That is why it persists even in philosophies that deny immanence any ontological sense, for instance as the immanence of real knowledge in natural knowledge. "The obscurity, in Hegel as in Heidegger, of the status of natural knowledge and of the immanence in it of real knowledge" belongs to the unworked concept of that immanence. The essence can act only if it is there, and that possibility "belongs to it as its own" and must be grasped in its structure: "the immanent essence is the essence of immanence". The Kantbuch has the relation Henry describes and also the demand he makes. The understanding of being "pervades" human existence (§41, 228) and underlies all dealings with beings (§43, 235); and the task of the laying of the ground is "the illumination of the inner possibility of the understanding of being" (§40, 226). GA 3 answers the demand with temporality as "transcendental primal structure" (§44, 242). Henry's charge of obscurity is therefore aimed at the answer, not at a failure to ask.

**¶5.** Does saying that the essence comes into itself mean only that it exists, is present and acts, a tautology that names the essence and says it "is"? "But what does being mean?" The determination of the essence's fundamental possibility as its coming into itself describes "the original structure of being itself": only on the ground of this structure, where the essence receives itself in immanence, can being unite with itself and "be". "Being is not something dead or ready-made", and unity is "a work and an accomplishment", "the inner work of being". Henry poses the Kantbuch's question in its words: "how is the question, what does being mean? to find its answer ...?" (§40, 224). The answers diverge. GA 3 finds being projected upon time: the ancient understanding of being as "constancy in presence" betrays "an original, but also self-evident, projection of being upon time" (§44, 240). Henry finds being as self-reception. Both reject being as something simply given. Heidegger traces the ready-made to a temporal projection; Henry traces being's reality to a work that no projection accomplishes.

**¶6.** Coming originally into itself determines being as "the immediate": not the naive immediacy of the given, nor the thought that takes the ontological condition as simply given and self-sufficient, but what makes the condition self-sufficient. "The immediate is being itself as originally given to itself in immanence", not a name for a tautology but the inner possibility of being, an essence. The Kantbuch records the vertigo Henry's "immediate" answers. Before the question of being as such, "does dizziness not seize us? ... Is being not something like nothing? In fact no less a thinker than Hegel has said: 'Pure being and pure nothing are therefore the same'" (§41, 226). Hegel's pure being is the indeterminate immediate, and GA 3 takes his equation as a sign of the darkness over being. Henry's immediate is the opposite of that indeterminacy: being given to itself, determinate in its mode of givenness. Heidegger leaves the immediate to Hegel and seeks being in the understanding of it. Henry restores the immediate as the ground of any understanding.

**¶7.** The determination of the immediate rejects the claim to find the condition of all presence in objectivation as "the universal mediation". Everything that is finds its being through the mediation of the essence, but traditional ontology confuses the reference of beings to the pure ontological element with the inner development by which that element reaches its own phenomenal condition, and calls both "mediation". Schelling makes the self-positing of the I a positing before itself, identical with the act by which every objective opposite arrives in it (IT 54). In a note (n. 119) Henry finds the same in Heidegger, "following Kant": ontological truth is the possibility of ontic knowledge, "if ontological knowledge unveils the horizon, its truth consists in letting beings be encountered within that horizon", and "truth itself must be understood at once as unveiling of being and as the manifest character of beings" (K 180 / GA 3 §25, 123–124). So objectivation is the law of beings and of being, "the universal mediation". The first quotation is exact and supports Henry: GA 3 does place the truth of ontological knowledge "precisely in letting beings be encountered within the horizon", and Heidegger attributes the point to Kant's "empirical use" of ontological knowledge (124). The second quotation is where the translation carries the argument. Heidegger writes that truth itself "must fork into the unveiledness of being and the manifestness of beings" (*muß sich gabeln*). The German divides truth into two; the translators' "doit être entendue à la fois comme", must be understood at once as (K 180), joins them, and Henry's thesis that the two manifestations have the same structure leans on the joining. The divergence is the translators', but Henry's argument depends on it, so it counts against his reading of this passage. What remains is the first quotation: GA 3 does measure ontological truth by what it lets be encountered, and that is enough for Henry's milder claim, that the Kantbuch thinks the essence's truth from the side of beings' manifestation.

**¶8.** What universal mediation lacks is its own possibility: "objectivation is possible only if the essence that objectivates itself comes originally into itself as into this objectivation itself"; and this original coming-into-itself is not constituted by objectivation and cannot be, "as Hegel and Heidegger want, its consequence", because it is its condition. The charge fits Hegel better than the Kantbuch. GA 3 does not make selfhood a consequence of objectivation. Time as pure self-affection "belongs to the inner possibility of this letting-stand-against" and "forms originally the finite selfhood" (§34, 190), and time and the "I think" "are the same" (191). Self and objectivation are co-original in time, neither the consequence of the other. What Henry can claim is that GA 3 constitutes the self by the same movement that forms the horizon, the movement "from itself toward ... and back to itself" (191). Henry reads constitution by that movement as derivation from it. The difference matters: Heidegger's self is not after objectivation, but it is not before it either, and "before" is what Henry requires.

**¶9.** The essence's original coming into itself cannot be understood as a logical requirement, that what mediates everything cannot itself be mediated and so is the immediate. Such a determination follows from a unilateral decision of thought which, seeing the need for the essence to come into itself, declares that it does: obeying the axiom "one must stop somewhere", thought "takes its wishes for reality". The immediate must be exhibited in reality as its fundamental structure. Henry and the Kantbuch agree in refusing a logical first. Heidegger says Kant's laying of the ground "does not lead to the sun-clear absolute evidence of a first proposition and principle" (§6, 37). They part over what replaces it. For Heidegger the regress "points consciously into the unknown" (37); for Henry the first must be exhibited, as immediate, in the reality it grounds. Henry's refusal of the "stop" is directed against Hegel's and Schelling's absolute, but it applies equally to the Kantbuch's acceptance of an unknown ground, which Henry read at R §25 ¶6 as the shadow of an unexhibited first.

**¶10.** "The bringing to light of the immediate as constituting not a mere logical requirement but the very structure of reality and its essence is identically that of the internal structure of immanence." Section II ends with the program of Section III, which bears the title "the internal structure of immanence and the problem of its phenomenological determination: the invisible". The Kantbuch has nothing to say about that structure, since it has no concept of immanence; the remaining engagement with GA 3 in Henry's book returns in Section IV, where the Kantian problem of respect (R §58) and Heidegger's account of mood (R §65) are read against affectivity.

Across Section II Henry makes three arguments of the Kantbuch load-bearing for his own project. The first is the regress from ontic to ontological knowledge, which gives him the "already" of §17 and the problem of receptivity of §22; here his reading holds, and often restates GA 3 against later Heidegger. The second is the identity of forming and taking-in in imagination and time (§§28–29, 32–34 of GA 3), which he first rejects (R §23 ¶3), then restates more generously than GA 3 does (R §25 ¶4), and finally accepts as the exact description of one kind of receptivity, representation (R §31 ¶11). Here his reading is faithful in its reconstructions and selective in its quotations: at four points (GA 3 §7, 38; §21, 99; §24, 119; §32, 173) the words Henry omits, all present in his French, are those in which Heidegger qualifies or answers what Henry objects. The third is time as pure self-affection (§34), whose wording gives Henry his central term, through one sentence of the 1953 translation and its introduction, and whose thesis he denies. Henry's verdict that it is "false" to say self-affection determines the innermost essence of transcendence (R §31 ¶15) is decisive against one layer of §34, time's being affected by the look it forms, and is a counter-thesis, not a refutation, against the other, the forming of the movement from itself toward and back to itself, whose key sentence Henry never quotes. His reading does not hold where it splits what GA 3 holds together, time as intuiting and as intuited (§32, 175), or joins what GA 3 keeps apart, the two truths into which truth "forks" (§25, 123–124), or sets aside the "innermost" unity Heidegger claims for transcendence (§24, 119). The edition Henry read did part of this work. Its translators fused taking-in with receptivity, turned the look-vocabulary of schematism into intuition, made truth "at once" unveiling and manifestness, and glossed time as the self's becoming in exteriorization; on each of these points Henry follows his French more closely than Heidegger's German. Where it holds most firmly is in the question it presses and the Kantbuch never raises, how the self that time constitutes is manifest to itself. GA 3's only answer in Henry's sense is a line on mood (§41, 227), which his French renders as manifestation "in each of our affective dispositions" (K 283), and Henry's own answer, an immanence that forms nothing and holds nothing before itself, is the one point at which Section II leaves the Kantbuch's terms behind.

# Husserl, *Vorlesungen über Ethik und Wertlehre 1908–1914* (Hua XXVIII), Part A: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the block of the [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md) that it extends. They cover Part A of that commentary only: the 1914 course *Grundfragen zur Ethik und Wertlehre*, §§1–21, with Beilagen I and II (Hua XXVIII 3–162). The German is quoted from the Husserliana edition (Hua XXVIII, ed. Ullrich Melle) in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-1914-lectures.md`). The commentary cites § numbers only. Headings here add the Hua XXVIII page, taken from the running-head comments in the file. Many even pages carry a running head without a number, so their numbers are counted from the neighbours, and a page can be off by one.

All English renderings of Husserl are working renderings. They stay close to the German so that its structure shows, and they are not taken from a published translation. Where the file gives the editor's angle brackets as LaTeX markup, the quotations print them as ⟨…⟩.

The file needs four cautions that affect more than one reading. First, the conversion often reads *Vermeinen* (meaning, intending: Husserl's word for the position-taking of an act) as *Verneinen* (negating). Genuine *Verneinung* (negation) also occurs, above all in §17(d), so each case is decided from the sense, and the reading says which it takes. Second, there is scattered OCR damage elsewhere: *Gefahren* (dangers) for *Gefallen* (liking), *Mittelwohnung* for *Mittelwollung* (willing of the means), "kategorialen Imperativ" (categorial imperative) for *kategorischen* (categorical), and margin line numbers spliced into the text. A misspelt word is corrected silently only where nothing turns on it; otherwise the quotation gives what the file has and a note says so. Third, two page markers are wrong: the one that reads "p. 154" stands where p. 102 falls, and the one that reads "p. 111" where p. 126 falls. The pages given here are the corrected ones. Fourth, italics survive, but sparsely, and spaced type does not survive at all, so no reading argues from the absence of emphasis. The file contains no editorial introduction, so nothing here relies on what the editor says about the 1914 redaction.

## §1, Hua XXVIII 4: the "must" of the parallel

Reads [§1, paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#logic-as-art-of-method-and-pure-logic).

The course's program is a claim of correspondence: to formal logic there *must* answer a formal *Praktik* (theory of practice) and a formal axiology (theory of values), and to each of these a phenomenology of the acts it governs. The "must" is not the conclusion of an argument. It is the form in which an analogy presents itself to someone who follows the parallel of the disciplines back to the parallel of the kinds of reason. The program thus rests on a demanded symmetry, and the course's real work, from §5 on, is to find out how much of the demand the will and the emotions can honour.

> If one now follows the parallels of logic and ethics, or rather the parallel of the kinds of act and reason to which these disciplines are essentially referred, judging reason on the one side and practical reason on the other, the thought presses itself upon us that to logic too, in the determinate and narrowly bounded sense of a formal logic, there must correspond as its parallel a *Praktik* that is formal in an analogous sense and likewise apriori. Something similar holds for the parallel with valuing reason, and indeed valuing in the widest sense and not merely aesthetic valuing. This leads to the idea of a formal axiology, intimately interwoven with formal *Praktik* for essential reasons, as an apriori formal discipline of values, or of value-contents and value-meanings. To delimit these ideas of new formal disciplines, which in the philosophical tradition never came to conception, and to realize them in pieces, that is the main theme of these lectures. So far as time remains, we shall then go into the great problem-groups of the phenomenology and critique of reason that orient themselves by these radical formal disciplines. Just as to formal logic there corresponds a system of fundamental structures of belief-consciousness (of doxic consciousness, as I am accustomed to say), and thus a phenomenology and theory of formal knowledge, so it is in a similar way with formal axiology and *Praktik* in respect of the discipline of phenomenology that belongs to them in principle, or of the theory of valuing and of willing (where these words are used in a sense analogous to the word "theory of knowledge"). Let us now go to the matters themselves!

German:

> Geht man nun den Parallelen von Logik und Ethik nach bzw. der Parallele der Akt- und Vernunftarten, auf welche diese Disziplinen wesentlich zurückbezogen sind, der urteilenden Vernunft auf der einen Seite, der praktischen Vernunft auf der anderen, so drängt sich der Gedanke auf, daß nun auch der Logik in dem bestimmt und eng begrenzten Sinn einer formalen Logik als Parallele entsprechen muß eine in analogem Sinn formale und ebenfalls apriorische Praktik. Ähnliches gilt für die Parallele mit der wertenden Vernunft, und zwar der wertenden im weitesten Sinn und nicht etwa der bloß ästhetisch wertenden. Das führt auf die Idee einer aus Wesensgründen mit der formalen Praktik innig verflochtenen formalen Axiologie als apriorischer formaler Disziplin von Werten bzw. Wertinhalten und Wertbedeutungen. Diese Ideen von neuen formalen Disziplinen, in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen, zu begrenzen und in Stücken wirklich zu realisieren, das ist das Hauptthema dieser Vorlesungen. Soweit Zeit übrig bleibt, soll dann auf die großen Problemgruppen der Phänomenologie und Kritik der Vernunft eingegangen werden, die sich nach diesen radikalen formalen Disziplinen orientieren. Wie der formalen Logik ein System fundamentaler Strukturen des Glaubensbewußtseins (des doxischen, wie ich zu sagen pflege) entspricht und somit eine Phänomenologie und Theorie der formalen Erkenntnis, so ähnlich verhält es sich mit der formalen Axiologie und Praktik hinsichtlich der ihnen prinzipiell zugehörigen Disziplin der Phänomenologie bzw. der Wertungs- und Willenstheorie (wobei diese Worte in analogem Sinn gebraucht sind wie das Wort „Erkenntnistheorie“). Gehen wir nun an die Sachen!

*Note on the text.* The relative clause "die … in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen" lacks its relative pronoun in the file ("Diese Ideen von neuen formalen Disziplinen, in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen"). The sense is not in doubt, and the rendering supplies "which".

The first sentence moves twice before it reaches its "must". It begins with the disciplines, logic and ethics, and then corrects itself with "or rather" (*bzw.*) to the kinds of act and reason to which the disciplines are "essentially referred back" (*wesentlich zurückbezogen*). The correction carries the program. A parallel of disciplines would be a fact of the curriculum; a parallel of kinds of reason is a claim about consciousness, and only that claim could ground a "must". Yet the sentence does not assert the second parallel either. It says that, for one who follows it, "the thought presses itself upon us" (*drängt sich der Gedanke auf*). The necessity sits inside a thought that imposes itself, not in a proof. And the word order delays the subject: "there must correspond as its parallel" comes before the thing that must correspond, "a *Praktik* formal in an analogous sense". The analogy is in place before its second term has been found.

Two qualifiers fix what the analogy is to deliver. The logic in question is formal logic "in the determinate and narrowly bounded sense", and the *Praktik* must be formal "in an analogous sense" and "likewise apriori". The course will test exactly these two words. If practical form turns out to be formal only in a looser sense than logical form, or apriori without being analytic, the parallel holds at a lower level than the "must" announces. The third sentence adds axiology with a looser joint ("something similar holds", *Ähnliches gilt*) and widens valuing beyond the aesthetic, so that the old triad of truth, goodness and beauty, with which §1 opens, gives way to a pair of reasons, theoretical and emotive, the second split into valuing and willing. The axiology is "intimately interwoven" with the *Praktik* "for essential reasons": the course will found willing on valuing (§§9–10), and the interweaving is announced before it is shown.

The claim of novelty is strong: these disciplines "never came to conception" in the tradition. It is aimed at the obvious counterexamples. At §5(a) (Hua XXVIII 37) Aristotle is "the father of logic" because he created the analytic, but the *Nicomachean Ethics*, "however much beauty it may offer", did not make him "in the same sense the father of ethics". At §5(b) Kant's formal principle is set aside as a principle of positive decision rather than a formal law (row 3). The novelty claim therefore has a precise content. Nobody before has abstracted from the "matter" of practice in the way the analytic abstracts from the matter of knowledge.

The sentence that closes the paragraph pitches the analogy at its third level. Formal logic corresponds to "a system of fundamental structures of belief-consciousness", and so to a phenomenology of knowledge. Axiology and *Praktik* correspond in "a similar way" to a phenomenology of valuing and willing. Husserl guards the new words with a parenthesis: "theory of valuing" (*Wertungstheorie*) and "theory of willing" (*Willenstheorie*) are used "in a sense analogous to the word 'theory of knowledge'". The quotation marks around *Erkenntnistheorie* concede that the new terms borrow their sense from the old one. The program then ends with the slogan "Let us now go to the matters themselves!" (*Gehen wir nun an die Sachen!*). The matters are what will confirm or limit the "must".

The course does not keep the modality it began with. When §5(a) returns to the program, the "must" has become a conjecture: "One would like to conjecture that theoretical reason *must* have its analogue in a practical reason" (*Man möchte vermuten, daß theoretische Vernunft ihr Analogon haben müsse*, Hua XXVIII 37–38), and a formal *Praktik* "would have to" exist "if the conjecture guiding us in our analogizing considerations is correct" (*Ist die uns … leitende Vermutung richtig, so müßte …*, Hua XXVIII 37). At §5(c) the demand is a string of questions: "Must not the distinction between logic and material science … have its strict analogue in the practical sphere too?" (Hua XXVIII 45). §8 looks back on all this as the use of formal logic as a "parallelistic clue" (*parallelistischen Leitfaden*) for "the parallel structures we conjectured" (Hua XXVIII 66). The course thus states the necessity of §1 as a conjecture, a question and a clue. The necessity is that of a heuristic. What turns it into results is description: the means–end law of §6, which is not analytic (row 5), and the will-modalities of §17. The heuristic is cut back where description finds no parallel. §12(a) turns to formal laws "that have no analogue in the narrower apophantic-logical sphere" (Hua XXVIII 90). §18(a) closes its iteration laws with "propositions that have no proper parallel in the doxic domain" (*keine rechte Parallele im doxischen Gebiet*, Hua XXVIII 128). §21 finds that subjectivity plays a role in practical possibility "that has no analogy in the judgment- or …" domain (Hua XXVIII 149), where the file breaks off after "bzw." and the next paragraph begins. The "must" of §1 is the course's working hypothesis, and its last sections record where the hypothesis fails.

The earlier course of 1908/09 already states the analogy as a guide rather than a theorem. Its part on theoretical and axiological reason says that "in the formulation and solution of the questions to be posed for a truly scientific construction of ethics … the analogy with logic must lead us; it proves itself step by step to be infinitely fruitful", and adds that "first there is need of a truly systematic logic" (Hua XXVIII 244). There the "must" governs method, and it is justified by its fruitfulness. The 1914 sentence moves the "must" from the method to the matter, so that a formal *Praktik* must exist. That is the stronger claim, and the rest of the course qualifies it again. A Beilage to the same earlier course also complicates "never came to conception": Brentano, Husserl writes there, "first recognized that a parallelism holds here between objectivating and non-objectivating acts", and urged the need to treat the valuing sphere "parallel with the logical sphere" (Hua XXVIII 345). In 1914 what is new is not the parallel but the formal discipline. Brentano had the analogue of evidence, and nobody had the analogue of the analytic.

## §4(b), Hua XXVIII 29: practical countersense

Reads [§4(b), paragraph 9](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-analogue-of-the-skeptical-countersense-in-the-practical-sphere-the-practical-countersense).

A practical countersense (*praktischer Widersinn*) is the self-cancellation of a demand whose content declares irrational what its sense as a demand presupposes as rational. The skeptic's imperative "Recognize no practical rule!" does not contradict itself in what it says. It cancels itself in what it is: a rule, and so something offered to be recognized as rational and followed. The argument therefore lives in the grammatical mood. The skeptical demand fails because it is an imperative, and the failure is practical, not logical.

> If I now say "Recognize no rule as valid for your action!", how does it stand then? If I say "Do not recognize a certain rule, this one or that!", there lies in it: Recognize that it is not rationally binding, do not let yourself be positively determined by it, do not take it as a rule! But there also lies in it a rule, namely, on the one hand, to see that to act as that rule demands is not rational action, and on the other hand to see that the contradictorily opposed will, not to bind oneself to that rule in the groups of action in question, is a rational one, and that accordingly the negative will-rule, not to proceed by the original rule, is also to be recognized in accordance with reason. If it now says "Recognize no practical rule!", there lies in it the rule, which is to be recognized as rational, that no practical rule at all is rational, that every will bound by any rules whatever is irrational, or that the will to follow no rule is rational. On the other hand, if I say "Recognize no practical rule!", I utter a practical rule, and indeed a rule which it would be rational to recognize and right to follow practically. That lies in the sense of every rule of this form, of every rule that is more than merely suggestive. On the other hand, in the content of this rule that is set up as practically irrational which the rule, by its sense, presupposes as rational. So there we have the exact analogue of the skeptical countersense.

German:

> Sage ich nun „Erkenne keine Regel als gültig für dein Handeln ⟨an⟩!“, wie steht es da? Sage ich „Erkenne eine gewisse, die und die Regel nicht an!“, so liegt darin: Erkenne, daß sie vernünftig nicht bindend ist, lasse dich von ihr positiv nicht bestimmen, nimm sie nicht als Regel! Darin liegt aber auch eine Regel, nämlich einerseits einzusehen, daß so ⟨zu⟩ handeln, wie jene Regel fordert, nicht vernünftiges Handeln sei, und andererseits einzusehen, daß der kontradiktorisch entgegengesetzte Wille, in den fraglichen Handlungsgruppen sich nicht an jene Regel zu binden, ein vernünftiger sei und daß demgemäß auch vernunftgemäß die negative Willensregel anzuerkennen sei, nach der ursprünglichen Regel nicht zu verfahren. Heißt es nun „Erkenne keine praktische Regel an!“, so liegt darin die Regel, die als vernünftig anerkannt werden soll, daß keine praktische Regel überhaupt vernünftig sei, daß jeder durch irgendwelche Regeln gebundene Wille unvernünftig sei bzw. daß der Wille, keiner Regel zu folgen, vernünftig sei. Andererseits, sage ich „Erkenne keine praktische Regel an!“, so spreche ich eine praktische Regel aus, und zwar eine Regel, die anzuerkennen vernünftig und der praktischen zu folgen richtig wäre. Das liegt im Sinn jeder Regel dieser Form, jeder mehr als bloß suggestiven Regel. Andererseits wird im Inhalt dieser Regel das als praktisch unvernünftig hingestellt, was die Regel ihrem Sinn nach als vernünftig voraussetzt. Also da haben wir das genaue Analogon des skeptischen Widersinns.

*Note on the text.* The file splices the printed edition's margin line numbers into this paragraph ("20", "25", "30", "35") and keeps two line-break hyphens with a number between them ("einer- 25 seits", "anzu- 30 erkennen"). The quotation leaves the numbers out and closes the hyphens. Both "Andererseits" are "Ackererseits" in the file, an obvious OCR slip. The phrase "der praktischen zu folgen" is as the file has it; a word may be missing ("ihr praktisch zu folgen" would be the expected form), but the sense, "right to follow in practice", is not in doubt.

The paragraph climbs in three steps, each marked by "there lies in it" (*darin liegt*, *so liegt darin*). The phrase says what a sense contains, and here it is applied to imperatives. First, a single negative rule ("Do not recognize this rule!") contains a positive one: to see that the rejected rule is not rational, and that the opposed will is. Negating a rule is itself legislating. Second, the universal negative ("Recognize no practical rule!") contains the universal positive that "no practical rule at all is rational". So far the skeptic has only said something extreme. The third step needs no further content. The skeptic "utters a practical rule" (*spreche … eine praktische Regel aus*), and that act makes the claim to rational recognition, because "that lies in the sense of every rule of this form".

Two words carry the third step: sense (*Sinn*) and content (*Inhalt*). "In the content of this rule" the rational is declared irrational; "by its sense" the rule presupposes it as rational. The pair repeats the definition of skeptical countersense given at §4(a) for the theoretical case, where a theory cancels itself if its validity "according to its essential sense" presupposes what the thesis denies and "what this theory according to its content" claims to ground (*ihrem wesentlichen Sinn gemäß … ihrem Inhalt nach*, Hua XXVIII 21). "The exact analogue" (*das genaue Analogon*) is therefore earned by the wording. The sense/content structure transfers unchanged, and only the predicate changes from true to rational-to-follow.

The phrase "more than merely suggestive" (*mehr als bloß suggestiv*) limits the argument and keeps it from being trivial. The preceding paragraph (Hua XXVIII 28) separates a demand that works suggestively, like the hypnotist's command to his medium, the master's to a slave raised in blind obedience, or a demand made of a child not yet rational, from the demand "Recognize this rule!", which asks for insight into its rightness and expects to motivate "through the addressee's having understood it". Only a demand of the second kind can cancel itself. The skeptic who merely wanted to condition behaviour would escape the argument, but at the price of no longer arguing. The argument does not show that every practical utterance presupposes practical reason. It shows that every utterance which asks to be recognized does.

The next two paragraphs make the result precise, and the precision matters more than the analogy. The logical counterpart, "Recognize no logical rule as binding for your judging!", cannot be refuted by saying that every rule asserts an ought and every assertion presupposes the law of contradiction. "The rule denies no logical rule as a logical truth." It says only that no logical truth should determine the striving for truth. Husserl concludes: "Purely logically no practical rule can cancel itself; such a rule cancels itself through practical countersense, which can indeed be connected with logical countersense, but no more than that" (*Rein logisch kann sich keine praktische Regel aufheben, eine solche hebt sich auf durch praktischen Widersinn, der allerdings mit logischem zusammenhängen kann; aber nicht mehr*, Hua XXVIII 29). The parallel thus yields a second, distinct kind of self-cancellation, not a reduction of the practical to the logical. That is the first concrete result of the course's "must" (row 1), and it already shows the parallel's shape: an analogy of structure between two irreducible kinds of reason.

The Prolegomena had defined skeptical theories as those whose theses "either expressly state or analytically include" that the logical or noetic conditions of the possibility of a theory are false (*entweder ausdrücklich besagen oder analytisch in sich schließen*, Hua XVIII 120). There the self-cancellation is analytic. The 1914 course keeps the form of the definition and gives up the word "analytic": the practical countersense is a matter of what "lies in the sense" of a rule as a rule, and Husserl says it is not logical. The same restraint returns when §6 denies that the means–end law is analytic (row 5).

The result is also narrower than it first looks, and the course says so. The recapitulation at §4(c) states that there are extreme skeptical practical demands that cancel themselves "as 'formally countersensical'" (*als „formal widersinnig“*), in a way "strictly analogous" to extreme theoretical skepticism (Hua XXVIII 35). The quotation marks around "formally countersensical" hold the word "formal" at a distance, since the form at issue is the form of a demand and not a logical form. And §5(a) admits at once that the parallel refutation reaches only the open ethical skeptic. The usual ethical empiricism, "which does not regard itself as a skepticism at all", is not touched, because ethics lacks what logic had: a formal discipline whose principles could be shown to be presupposed (Hua XXVIII 36). The practical countersense refutes the skeptic who speaks in imperatives. To refute the empiricist the course needs a formal *Praktik*, and §5 sets out to find one.

## §5(b), Hua XXVIII 43: "however 'formal'": formal as non-prejudging

Reads [§5(b), paragraph 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-traditional-sense-of-the-ethical-principles-formal-correctness-according-to-kants-categorical-imperative-versus-logical-formal-correctness).

For Husserl a principle is formal in the logician's sense when its correctness does not prejudge material correctness. Formal logic states conditions that every truth must meet and that no falsehood can meet merely by its form. It never says, positively, what is true. Kant's categorical imperative is called "formal" because it excludes material ends, but it is meant as a sufficient criterion of the morally good in each case. A principle that decides positively is not formal in Husserl's sense, however little matter it contains. The passage separates two senses of "formal" that the history of ethics had run together, and the course's own formal *Praktik* is defined by the first.

> Note that it makes no difference in this respect whether, in the historical tradition, ethical principles are sought and held possible as so-called material or as so-called formal ones. Whether in the ethical principles pleasure, general welfare, perfection and the like are set up as the highest practical goals, or whether one, with Kant, rejects all such principles as "material" and declares a categorical imperative, as a "purely formal principle", to be alone admissible: it is all one. How far such an opposition of "material" and "formal" has analogy with the like-named logical opposition is the question, and needs closer investigation. But from the outset it is clear to the connoisseur of Kantian ethics and of the disputes attached to it that, given the way the opposition is introduced and the way the formal principle is used in Kantian ethics, the analogy fails at the essential point that interests us. For Kant wanted to give, with the categorical imperative, the one single criterion of morality, and one not merely necessary but sufficient. He himself instructs us how we are to proceed with the categorical imperative in the given case in order to determine what is morally good in it, what is truly dutiful, or to judge ethically a willing and acting already carried out. Purely and exclusively the general valuation from which the demand of the categorical imperative wells up determines the value of the individual case. However "formal" the principle is, however much it may exclude what is material in the Kantian sense, it is a principle of positive decision, completely sufficient for the given case taken in its full concretion and individuality. This formal conformity-to-correctness according to the categorical imperative therefore leaves no room for a material incorrectness. Quite the contrary, we heard: in the sphere of cognition, logical-formal correctness in no way prejudges material correctness.

German:

> Beachten Sie, daß es in dieser Hinsicht keinen Unterschied ausmacht, ob in der historischen Tradition ethische Prinzipien als sogenannte materiale oder als sogenannte formale gesucht und für möglich erachtet werden. Ob in den ethischen Prinzipien Lust, allgemeine Wohlfahrt, Vollkommenheit und dergl. als die obersten praktischen Ziele hingestellt werden oder ob man mit Kant alle solchen Prinzipien als „materiale“ ablehnt und einen kategorischen Imperativ als ein „rein formales Prinzip“ für allein zulässig erklärt: es ist einerlei. Inwieweit ein solcher Gegensatz von „material“ und „formal“ mit dem gleich benannten logischen Gegensatz Analogie hat, ist die Frage und bedarf näherer Untersuchung. Aber von vornherein ist es für den Kenner der Kantischen Ethik und der an sie geknüpften Streitigkeiten klar, daß bei der Art, wie der Gegensatz eingeführt und wie vom formalen Prinzip in der Kantischen Ethik Gebrauch gemacht wird, die Analogie in dem wesentlichen, uns interessierenden Punkt versagt. Denn Kant wollte mit dem kategorischen Imperativ das eine einzige und nicht bloß notwendige, sondern zureichende Kriterium der Sittlichkeit geben. Er leitet uns selbst an, wie wir mit dem kategorischen Imperativ im gegebenen Fall zu verfahren haben, um das in ihm sittlich Gute, das wahrhaft Pflichtmäßige zu bestimmen bzw. das schon vollzogene Wollen und Handeln ethisch zu beurteilen. Rein und ausschließlich die allgemeine Wertung, aus der die Forderung des kategorischen Imperativs entquillt, bestimmt den Wert des Einzelfalls. Wie „formal“ das Prinzip auch ist, wie sehr es das im Kantischen Sinn Materiale ausschließen mag, es ist ein Prinzip positiver Entscheidung, für den gegebenen und in seiner vollen Konkretion und Individualität genommenen Fall vollkommen zureichend. Diese formale Richtigkeitsgemäßheit dem kategorischen Imperativ läßt also keinen Raum für eine materiale Unrichtigkeit. Ganz im Gegenteil dazu hörten wir: Es präjudiziert in der Erkenntnissphäre die logisch formale Richtigkeit ganz und gar nicht die materiale Richtigkeit.

*Note on the text.* "Diese formale Richtigkeitsgemäßheit dem kategorischen Imperativ" is as the file has it. The dative needs a preposition or an adjective ("Richtigkeit gemäß dem kategorischen Imperativ", formal correctness according to the categorical imperative), and a word may have been run together or lost. The rendering follows the evident sense, and nothing below turns on the compound.

The paragraph's first move is to make the received division of ethics irrelevant. Material and formal ethics, the hedonist's pleasure and Kant's imperative, are all "so-called" (*sogenannte*), and the difference between them is "all one" (*einerlei*) in the respect that matters. Husserl's quotation marks then do the work of a thesis. "Material" and "formal" are Kant's words, put in Kant's mouth ("rejects … as 'material'", "a 'purely formal principle'"). The marks keep the words from being taken in the sense the course gives them. The sentence after them states the open question in so many words: how far the ethical opposition "has analogy with the like-named logical opposition" (*mit dem gleich benannten logischen Gegensatz*). The phrase "like-named" concedes a shared name and nothing more.

The criterion of the formal follows in a pair borrowed from logic: "not merely necessary but sufficient" (*nicht bloß notwendige, sondern zureichende*). Kant is not faulted for having too little content in his principle. He is faulted for asking it to do too much. A sufficient criterion of morality is, by the preceding paragraph's account of ethical "principles", one under which the right act in any case could be found "by mere subsumption" (*durch bloße Subsumption*, Hua XXVIII 42). Logic's principles cannot work that way. They "will not and cannot decide positively what is true in every possible sphere of knowledge"; they can prejudge "only analytic, not synthetic truth" (Hua XXVIII 42). The verb Husserl uses for this, "prejudge" (*präjudizieren*), returns in the last sentence of the passage. It names the relation that formal correctness must lack. A formal principle that prejudges material correctness has ceased to be formal.

"However 'formal'" (*Wie „formal“*) is the turn of the paragraph. The concession grants Kant his exclusion of the material "in the Kantian sense" and denies that the exclusion makes the principle formal in the sense at issue. Husserl's name for what the imperative is, "a principle of positive decision" (*ein Prinzip positiver Entscheidung*), puts the weight on "positive". The consequence is drawn with "therefore" (*also*): the imperative "leaves no room for a material incorrectness". In logic there is such room. A syllogism can be formally valid and materially false, and that gap is what makes form and matter two dimensions of correctness and gives "double truths", formal and material. A principle that closes the gap has only one dimension, whatever it is called.

The inference is quicker than the wording suggests. Husserl takes Kant at his word that the imperative is sufficient, and "he himself instructs us" (*Er leitet uns selbst an*) appeals to Kant's procedure, not to a reading of it. Whether the universalization test really decides the concrete case without any prior valuation is exactly what "the disputes attached to it", which Husserl's sentence mentions, had been about. Husserl does not need to settle this. His claim is conditional on Kant's intention: if the imperative does what Kant meant it to do, it is not formal in the logician's sense.

§8 restates the charge as a dilemma. Kant's "formalistic ethics" could not count as the analogue of formal logic, and "if Kant was right", the analogy between knowing and practical consciousness "went to pieces" (*ging … in die Brüche*, Hua XXVIII 65). The next paragraph removes the dilemma by history. Kant's formal ethics "was not designed at all as an analogue of formal, analytic logic", since Kant did not let himself be led "in a radical way" by the thought of a thoroughgoing parallelism (Hua XXVIII 66). The quarrel is therefore about a word. Kant's "formal" means free of matter, and Husserl's means non-prejudging.

At §19 the charge returns reversed. There a "formal rule-giving of valuing and willing" which supposes that the matter of valuing and willing "could remain out of account" is "a countersense" (*ein Widersinn*): without a material apriori, without kinds of objects that carry value-predicates by their essence, "the concept of objective value would have no hold". "In this respect we must therefore sharply separate our ways from the Kantian doctrine" (*müssen wir also unsere Wege von der Kantischen Lehre scharf sondern*, Hua XXVIII 139; row 21). The two charges fit together. At §5 Kant's principle decides too much to be formal; at §19 a principle that tried to be formal by excluding matter would ground nothing. Husserl's formal *Praktik* avoids both. It decides nothing positively, as §5 requires, and it presupposes in formal generality that material value-laws exist, as §19 requires. The sense of "formal" fixed at §5 thus also fixes the limit §19 sets on formal ethics as a whole: it is a necessary condition of rightness, never a sufficient criterion.

## §5(c), Hua XXVIII 47–48: the good as the existential value

Reads [§5(c), paragraph 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#c-on-the-possibility-of-formal-practical-and-formal-axiological-principles).

In this passage the good (*das Gute*) is the value of something *as existing*. The beautiful is valued without regard to whether its object exists. The good is the beautiful once existence is taken into the valuing, and Husserl defines it through an act: goods (*Güter*) are the correlates of rational valuings "of the genus joy" (*der Gattung Freude*). From that definition follows an apriori chain in which one value passes into the next as the doxic standing of its object changes: beautiful, good if it exists, desire-worthy if it does not, a will-value if it is realizable. The chain is the first sample of a formal axiology. It is also the doctrine that Beilage I calls an "obliquity" (*Schiefheit*), because it identifies valuing something as good with rejoicing in it.

> If we separate off, excluding for the moment the domain of desiring and willing, a domain of non-existential values as a domain of beauty-values in the widest sense; if we thus comprise under it the values for which the existence or non-existence of the value-giving object is irrelevant to the sense and validity of the value-predication; if we set over against it the sphere of existential valuings, in which the reverse holds, and if we call the values that form the correlate of rational existential valuings of the genus joy *goods*, and the corresponding negative values *evils*: this presupposed, it is clear that every beautiful object is at the same time a good one; that is, what proves itself beautiful in rational aesthetic valuing must necessarily prove itself good in rational existential valuing; that is, if it exists, or stands there in conviction as existing, it is necessarily, that is, generally in accordance with reason, the object of a justified joy; likewise in the reverse case of non-existence ⟨it is⟩ the object of justified grief. Again: let us suppose that the character of existence of the object is lacking for consciousness, but that the assumption of existence grounds, in accordance with reason, an assumptive liking. We think ourselves into the existence, then, and in this thought of "supposing it were" there is grounded a quasi-joy that can be rationally justified, namely of the kind: that would be a real joy! Then it is clear that the same object or the same state of affairs would be the justified bearer of a rational wish, and, if it stands there as realizable, of a justified will. If we call the value-correlates of the rational wish desire-values, and those of the rational will will-values or practical values, then it is clear that all these values cohere apriori. *Apriori* it holds: the beautiful is at the same time a good; every good is, if it does not exist, a desire-value; every desire-value is, if it is realizable, a will-value, so that the concept of the good is as a rule extended. What can rightly be desired is also called good, and the practical value is practically good.

The first sentence is a chain of four hortatory subjunctives ("if we separate off … if we comprise … if we set over against … if we call"). The whole chain is a stipulation, and its last clause is a definition by naming: "let us call" (*nennen wir*) the correlates of rational joy "goods". Everything after "this presupposed" (*Dies vorausgesetzt*) depends on that act of naming. The good is introduced as a value-correlate of an emotion of a determinate kind, and the "it is clear" (*ist es klar*) that follows is clear only under the stipulation. When Beilage I asks whether "that A is, is good" is "equivalent" to "that A is, is gladdening" (*erfreulich*), it questions exactly the clause that the presupposition hides.

Two things vary through the chain, and the wording keeps them apart. The first is the doxic standing of the object: "exists, or stands there in conviction as existing" (*existiert es bzw. steht es als existierend in der Überzeugung da*), non-existence, "the assumption of existence" (*die Assumption der Existenz*), "stands there as realizable" (*als realisierbar dasteht*). The second is the act and its correlate: joy or grief, an "assumptive liking" or "quasi-joy", a wish, a will. Each emotive act is matched to one doxic standing. This is the course's model of founding, already at work. The emotive act is "motivated", as §9 puts it, by a value-presupposition and by "the conviction, the judgment, that the beautiful is" (Hua XXVIII 73). The verbs in the chain show the founding: what is beautiful "proves itself" (*sich … ausweist*) good in existential valuing. It does not become good by being judged to exist. It shows itself good to a valuing that takes existence in.

The step to the wish is the most carefully worded. Where existence is lacking, the assumption of existence grounds an "assumptive" liking, and the dash introduces the experience that carries it: "we think ourselves into the existence", and in the thought "supposing it were" there arises a "quasi-joy" of the form "that would be a real joy!" (*Das wäre eine rechte Freude!*). The subjunctive of the exclamation is the wish's own grammar. A wish is a joy held in the mood of supposition. That makes the desire-value a modification of the good rather than a new genus of value, and it is why Husserl can write that "the concept of the good is as a rule extended" (*sich … in der Regel erweitert*). "As a rule" here means "in the usual way of speaking": ordinary language calls the rightly desirable "good", and the practically valuable "practically good". The chain thus explains a usage. One word covers four values because the four are one value under four doxic standings.

The extension of the word hides a gap that the course later finds in the thing. The last link, "every desire-value is, if it is realizable, a will-value", makes the will a further modification of the same joy, a quasi-joy in the realizable. Section III takes this back. At §14 the will is not a wish with a realizability-clause attached: the wish means an "it may be", the will an "it shall be" (row 14). At §15 the will is creative, and its "Let it be!" is not founded on an expectation of what will be (row 15). The chain of §5(c) treats the will as the last of the valuings. The phenomenology of the will treats it as a position of its own kind.

The identification of the good with the joy-value is stated again in the formal axiology itself. In §10 the objective law runs: "If A is a beautiful thing and is in reality, then it is a good one", and "'good' is here understood as the axiological correlate of rational joy, thus the joy-value" (*„Gut“ ist hier verstanden als das axiologische Korrelat der vernünftigen Freude, also das der Freude Werte*, Hua XXVIII 74–75). That is the page to which, together with Hua XXVIII 47 f., Beilage I is keyed. Its correction is explicit: "In the lectures I identified the two. But one must consider carefully whether rightly. I inclined repeatedly to the view (and so again now!) that a distinction is to be made here" (*In den Vorlesungen habe ich beides identifiziert … Ich neigte wiederholt zu der Ansicht (und so auch jetzt wieder!), daß hier zu scheiden sei*, Hua XXVIII 155; row 24). What the Beilage separates is the valuing of the being of A from the joy at it. "I value, taking existence into account, the being: that A is, that is valuable." A valuing that takes existence in need not be a rejoicing. The definition by naming in §5(c) had made it one. The Beilage does not touch the chain's form: the dependence of values on the doxic standing of their objects survives the correction, and so does the formal character Husserl claims for it in the next paragraph, where the connections "seem already to be of a formal nature", since "it does not depend on the 'matter' of beauty and goodness" (Hua XXVIII 48). What the Beilage removes is the act in which §5(c) had grounded the good.

## §6, Hua XXVIII 55: formal but not analytic

Reads [§6, paragraph 7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-formal-relation-between-means-and-end-is-no-logical-relation-the-omni-dominion-of-logical-reason).

The means–end law, "whoever wills the end must rationally will the necessary means", is formal without being analytic. It is formal because it holds whatever the end and the means are. It is not analytic because its terms, will and cause, are not logical concepts: replace them by indeterminate ones and the inference fails. The passage takes Kant's word "analytic" away from this law and so separates two properties that in logic coincide. In the theoretical sphere the formal and the analytic are one. In the practical sphere a law can be independent of the matter of the will and still not follow from the form of judgment. That gap is where the course places its formal *Praktik*.

> In this it is of special importance to bring oneself to the insight that the will-situation is not, say, a mere special case of the intellectual, the logical state of affairs, and so that will-reason is not a special case of logical reason in inferring. Above all it is evident that the rational enclosure of the means in the end, or of the willing of the means in the willing of the end, is not an "analytic" relation, not something like a logical relation of inference. This comes out when we bring the will-situation to predicative expression. From the proposition "I will the end *Z*" and from the proposition "*M* is a necessary means to *Z*" no one can infer by formal-logical principles "I must will *M*, that is, I ought rationally to will *M*", unless he inserts the general proposition: whoever wills an end at all "must" will the necessary means to it. But just this proposition states the principle of the mode of inference generally, and is therefore in question. It is evidently no logical-analytic proposition, none that is evident from the essence of judgment in general, none that would be valid from the form of judgment in general regardless of its particular judgment-matter. If we analyse what this proposition says, and so first what "means and end" says, we are led to a causal relation: the becoming-real of the fact *M* draws, or would draw, after it the becoming-real of the fact *Z*. To this is added (for the mode of inference in question) that the agent cannot realize *Z* immediately, but can immediately realize a causal way to *Z*, and that on the other hand he can realize no other sufficient cause for *Z* than one that implies *M* as a necessary member. Where all this obtains, there we can indeed infer: in such cases we must will *M*. We "must": that says here no psychological necessity, but a necessity of reason, a rational ought. But after this analysis we see all the more, and quite clearly, that this mode of inference from the willing of *Z* to the rational ought-to-will of *M* is no analytic one, none grounded in the mere form of judgment. For then it would have to remain valid if we replaced all the concepts occurring here, cause, effect, will, in short all the not purely logical concepts, by indeterminate ones. Of that there is of course no question. It is no logical contradiction at all, for all the unreason, to will the end and not to will the means.

The paragraph's first claim is negative and is aimed at a reduction: the will-situation is not "a mere special case" (*ein bloßer Sonderfall*) of the logical one. That is the thesis of the section's title ("no logical relation"), and the passage proves it from Kant's own example. The phrase "rational enclosure" (*vernunftgemäßes Eingeschlossensein*) concedes the image on which Kant's "analytic" rested. Something is enclosed in the willing of the end, and Husserl does not deny it. He denies that the enclosure is the containment of a concept in a concept. The quotation marks around "analytic" (*„analytisches“*) cite Kant's word in order to refuse it. Two pages earlier Husserl had quoted the *Groundwork*: the proposition "whoever wills the end wills also the means indispensably necessary to it" is, "as regards the willing, analytic", because "in the willing of an object as my effect my causality as acting cause … is already thought" (Hua XXVIII 51, citing Kant, Ak. IV 417). His objection there is phenomenological: the willing of the end is not "a complex that really implies the willing of the only necessary means", and one can will an end "without being in the least clear about the means" (Hua XXVIII 52). Kant's "already thought" (*schon … gedacht*) is false of the act. The present paragraph adds that it is false of the proposition too.

The proof works through the quotation marks around "must". In the inserted premise, whoever wills an end "must" (*„muß“*) will the means. The marks flag a modal word that cannot be read off the form of judgment, and Husserl's gloss later in the paragraph fixes its sense: "We 'must': that says here no psychological necessity, but a necessity of reason, a rational ought". The syllogism is valid only with the premise, and the premise "states the principle of the mode of inference" itself. A principle cannot be derived by the inference it governs, so the will-inference has a principle of its own.

The decisive sentence is a test of formality borrowed from logic: an analytic inference "would have to remain valid if we replaced all the concepts occurring here, cause, effect, will, in short all the not purely logical concepts, by indeterminate ones". It is the criterion of the Third Logical Investigation: "in an analytic proposition it must be possible to replace every material content, with full preservation of the logical form of the proposition, by the empty form something" (*jede sachhaltige Materie, bei voller Erhaltung der logischen Form des Satzes, durch die leere Form etwas zu ersetzen*, LU III §12, Hua XIX/1 259). Applied to the will-inference, it shows exactly what the inference lacks. Its validity depends on "will" and "cause", so it is not analytic. But the inference is not material either, since nothing in it depends on what *Z* and *M* are. The variables stay variables. The law is formal in respect of the will's matter and contentful in respect of the form "will". The final sentence states the result in the register of the contradiction it denies: willing the end and not the means is "no logical contradiction at all, for all the unreason" (*gar kein logischer Widerspruch, trotz aller Unvernunft*). "Unreason" names a failure that is real and not logical. The concessive phrase holds both claims in one breath.

The next paragraph asks whether the law then belongs to empirical reason, the reason of causal knowledge, since it rests on a causal relation, and answers that it does not: causal facts contain "nothing of means and end and of reason", and the rational ought "is no fact of experience". The law is apriori, "evidently valid in unconditioned generality and necessity" (Hua XXVIII 56–57). Neither analytic logic nor experience accounts for it, and that leaves room for a third reason.

§8 draws the positive consequence and gives Kant's word back in a restricted sense. The task is "not to miss the legitimate sense of this talk of 'analytic'": this "analytic" does not mean, "as Kant meant it", the same as the "analytic" of formal logic, but "a mere analogue of the logical-analytic". "And there now was a point at which we entered the genuine and sought-for parallel sphere to the formal-logical." The means–end laws are formal, "in so far as they include not the least of the matter of what is willed. Only the form, and indeed as will-form, comes into question" (*Nur die Form, und zwar als Willensform, kommt in Frage*, Hua XXVIII 68). The phrase "as will-form" answers the substitution test of §6. In logic the form is the form of judgment, and "will" is matter. In the formal *Praktik* "will" belongs to the form. Every formal practical law will therefore fail the logician's test of analyticity, and the §6 result defines the discipline, it is not a defect in it. The "must" of §1 is fulfilled here for the first time (row 1), and fulfilled as an analogue: the practical sphere has its own formal laws, which are not logical laws.

## §7, Hua XXVIII 64: the emotions mean

Reads [§7, paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-analogies-for-the-judging-position-taking-of-belief-in-the-classes-of-emotive-and-will-acts-as-the-ground-for-the-parallelism-of-the-kinds-of-reason).

The parallelism of the kinds of reason rests on one verb. A liking or a wishing "means" (*vermeint*): it takes a position on its object, holds it for good or bad, and so can be right or wrong, as a judgment can. A mere sensation means nothing. *Vermeinen* here is not the meaning of a word (*bedeuten*) but meaning-*that*: intending, holding-for, the position-taking of an act. Husserl's claim is that the emotions have their own kind of it, an "analogue of judging" that is not a judging. Logical reason can bring values to light only because the emotions have already "decided" before cognition arrives. The passage answers Rickert and Windelband, who read the position-taking of judgment as a feeling, by reversing the direction of their analogy: feeling is not the core of judgment, but feeling has the structure of position-taking that judgment has.

> Of course this critique is directed against the theories of Rickert and Windelband, so popular in our time. The axiological reason disregarded by the tradition breaks through in these theories in a certain way. Its very peculiarity is felt so strongly by these researchers, who lean on Fichte, that they fall into the exaggeration of reinterpreting logical reason itself axiologically. On the other hand, and to return to our starting point, the omni-dominion of logical reason need not make us go astray about the parallelism of the kinds of reason. In it there is expressed a certain essential interweaving of doxic consciousness with emotive consciousness, and so with every consciousness at all, according to which every position-taking, every valuing-as-beautiful or valuing-as-good, can *apriori* be converted into a judging position-taking. […] Bringing out, establishing, determining, objectivating in the specific sense, is the affair of logical reason. Axiological reason with its holdings is, so to speak, hidden from itself. It first becomes manifest through the cognition that is carried out on the ground of the emotive acts. But cognition does not invent; it only draws out what is in a certain way already there. Were the emotions not a domain of meanings, did they not already in themselves, but precisely in the manner of the emotions, make decisions, cast their vote, then cognition would find nothing of values and value-contents before it. It would then find only blind experiences, such as, say, experiences of sensing red and blue. A mere sensing, a mere living-through of a sensuous datum, means nothing; but a liking means, a wishing means, and so on. Meaning is not always judging (in the ordinary sense), a manner of believingly holding-for-something, but it is an analogue of judging.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "eine Domäne von Verneinungen" (a domain of negations) and "Verneinen ist nicht immer Urteilen" (negating is not always judging). Both are taken here as *Vermeinungen* and *Vermeinen* (meanings, meaning), and rendered so. The sense requires it. What the emotions are said to be is the condition of their containing values, and they are opposed to "blind experiences" that "mean nothing"; the sentence between gives "vermeint" three times, correctly spelt; and a negating would be a judging in the ordinary sense, which is what the last sentence denies of the word it glosses. The same confusion runs through §7. In the paragraph before this one the file has "alle Vermengungen, alle intentionalen Stellungnahmen" (all mixtures, all intentional position-takings) and "sofern sie eben verneinen" (in so far as they negate), where the sense needs *Vermeinungen* and *vermeinen*. At Hua XXVIII 59–60 "Erkennen ist ein Vermeinen, verneinen, es sei etwas" and "als verneinendes Verhalten das Analogon zum urteilenden Vermeinen, allgemeiner, zum Seinsverneinen" are probably of the same kind. Genuine *Verneinung* (negation) is treated in §17(d), and none of these places is about negation. The omitted sentence (marked […]) says that the emotive acts, their right and wrong, and the values correlative to them become fields of determinations carried out in logical acts.

The passage moves from a polemic to a thesis, and the hinge is "on the other hand" (*Andererseits*). The polemic grants Rickert and Windelband a discovery and charges them with an overreach. Axiological reason "breaks through" (*bricht … durch*) in their theories, but "these researchers, who lean on Fichte" (*an Fichte sich anlehnenden*) feel its peculiarity so strongly that they "reinterpret" (*umdeuten*) logical reason as valuing. The preceding paragraph names the doctrine: what brings the value-difference of truth and falsity into judgment is supposed to be the addition of "feelings of a transcendental ought, … feelings of the necessity of thought" (Hua XXVIII 63). The naming of Fichte locates the error: the primacy of the practical, carried into the theory of judgment. Husserl's own term for the result is "emotionalism", the mirror image of the intellectualism that dissolves all reason into logical reason (Hua XXVIII 63).

The thesis begins by conceding what the polemic might seem to deny, "the omni-dominion of logical reason" (*Allherrschaft der logischen Vernunft*). Every valuing can be "converted" (*umgewandelt*) into a judgment, and everything that is established about values is established by logical acts. The concession is phrased so that it cannot become a reduction. Logical reason does the "bringing out, establishing, determining", and the series of verbs gives it the work of exhibiting. "Cognition does not invent, it only draws out what is in a certain way already there." What is "already there" is axiological reason, "hidden from itself" (*sich selbst verborgen*). The image divides the labour cleanly. Valuing reason has holdings it cannot see; logical reason sees but owns nothing.

The counterfactual that follows gives the ground of the division, and its verbs are judicial. The emotions "make decisions" (*Entscheidungen treffen*) and "cast their vote" (*ihr Votum abgeben*). Both words are taken from judgment, and both are qualified at once by "but precisely in the manner of the emotions". The next paragraph makes the borrowing explicit: the will does not judge, "and yet in a certain sense and speaking in a likeness it does judge", for "*it itself speaks*, it *casts as it were a vote*" (*er selbst spricht, er gibt gleichsam ein Votum*); "we also call judging a deciding-oneself" (Hua XXVIII 64). The italics there survive in the file. A vote is a position taken by someone who is entitled to take it, and it can be right or wrong. That is all the parallelism needs.

The contrast with sensation fixes what *Vermeinen* adds. Sensing red and blue is a "blind experience", a "mere living-through" (*bloßes Erleben*) that "means nothing". A liking "means". The verb has no object in the sentence, and that is the point: it names the act's own claim, not a content. The final sentence then sets the word against its doxic model. Meaning "is not always judging (in the ordinary sense), a manner of believingly holding-for-something" (*eine Weise des glaubend Für-etwas-Haltens*), "but an analogue of judging". "In the ordinary sense" leaves room for a wider sense of judging in which the emotions do judge. The earlier pages of §7 had prepared that sense: "new basic kinds of position-taking appear here, new kinds of meaning, of holding-for" (*neue Arten des Vermeinens, des Dafürhaltens*), and in every wishing "there lies a holding-for-good" (*ein Für-gut-Halten*, Hua XXVIII 60). Holding-for-good is the emotive analogue of holding-for-true. The hyphenated compounds make the structure visible: *Für-wahr-*, *Für-seiend-*, *Für-gut-Halten*, one form with different predicates.

The English "means" loses a distinction that the argument needs. *Vermeinen* names the act's claim, not a signification (*Bedeutung*): a liking does not signify anything, and Husserl does not say it does. The German verb also keeps the edge of *vermeintlich* (supposed). What the emotion claims may be wrong, which is why its claims fall under a reason of their own.

The word returns at the points where the course decides what the emotions are. §8 defines doxic consciousness as "a meaning that something is" (*ein Vermeinen, es sei etwas*), and splits it into the act, "the judging meaning", the proposition and the state of affairs (Hua XXVIII 66). The emotive analogues of that triad are what Section II studies. §8 also explains why valuing reason is "mute and in a certain way blind" (row 7), and the blindness there is not the blindness of sensation: a meaning act can be right without seeing that it is. At §14 belief is "the meaning of being", and the emotive acts are modalizations of being "of a new dimension" (Hua XXVIII 105; row 14). Beilage I puts the word in quotation marks and asks of the non-rational emotions: "Are the blind acts not 'meanings'?" (*Sind die blinden Akte nicht „Verneinungen“?*, again for *Vermeinungen*, Hua XXVIII 156). "Blind" there means without insight, not without intention. The question shows that the clean division of §7, the meaning emotions against blind sensations, left unsettled whether every emotion, rational or not, contains a valuing. That is the question the Beilage turns into a research programme (row 25).

# Husserl, *Vorlesungen über Ethik und Wertlehre 1908–1914* (Hua XXVIII), Part A: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the block of the [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md) that it extends. They cover Part A of that commentary only: the 1914 course *Grundfragen zur Ethik und Wertlehre*, §§1–21, with Beilagen I and II (Hua XXVIII 3–162). The German is quoted from the Husserliana edition (Hua XXVIII, ed. Ullrich Melle) in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-1914-lectures.md`). The commentary cites § numbers only. Headings here add the Hua XXVIII page, taken from the running-head comments in the file. Many even pages carry a running head without a number, so their numbers are counted from the neighbours, and a page can be off by one.

All English renderings of Husserl are working renderings. They stay close to the German so that its structure shows, and they are not taken from a published translation. Where the file gives the editor's angle brackets as LaTeX markup, the quotations print them as ⟨…⟩.

The file needs four cautions that affect more than one reading. First, the conversion often reads *Vermeinen* (meaning, intending: Husserl's word for the position-taking of an act) as *Verneinen* (negating). Genuine *Verneinung* (negation) also occurs, above all in §17(d), so each case is decided from the sense, and the reading says which it takes. Second, there is scattered OCR damage elsewhere: *Gefahren* (dangers) for *Gefallen* (liking), *Mittelwohnung* for *Mittelwollung* (willing of the means), "kategorialen Imperativ" (categorial imperative) for *kategorischen* (categorical), and margin line numbers spliced into the text. A misspelt word is corrected silently only where nothing turns on it; otherwise the quotation gives what the file has and a note says so. Third, two page markers are wrong: the one that reads "p. 154" stands where p. 102 falls, and the one that reads "p. 111" where p. 126 falls. The pages given here are the corrected ones. Fourth, italics survive, but sparsely, and spaced type does not survive at all, so no reading argues from the absence of emphasis. The file contains no editorial introduction, so nothing here relies on what the editor says about the 1914 redaction.

## §1, Hua XXVIII 4: the "must" of the parallel

Reads [§1, paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#logic-as-art-of-method-and-pure-logic).

The course's program is a claim of correspondence: to formal logic there *must* answer a formal *Praktik* (theory of practice) and a formal axiology (theory of values), and to each of these a phenomenology of the acts it governs. The "must" is not the conclusion of an argument. It is the form in which an analogy presents itself to someone who follows the parallel of the disciplines back to the parallel of the kinds of reason. The program thus rests on a demanded symmetry, and the course's real work, from §5 on, is to find out how much of the demand the will and the emotions can honour.

> If one now follows the parallels of logic and ethics, or rather the parallel of the kinds of act and reason to which these disciplines are essentially referred, judging reason on the one side and practical reason on the other, the thought presses itself upon us that to logic too, in the determinate and narrowly bounded sense of a formal logic, there must correspond as its parallel a *Praktik* that is formal in an analogous sense and likewise apriori. Something similar holds for the parallel with valuing reason, and indeed valuing in the widest sense and not merely aesthetic valuing. This leads to the idea of a formal axiology, intimately interwoven with formal *Praktik* for essential reasons, as an apriori formal discipline of values, or of value-contents and value-meanings. To delimit these ideas of new formal disciplines, which in the philosophical tradition never came to conception, and to realize them in pieces, that is the main theme of these lectures. So far as time remains, we shall then go into the great problem-groups of the phenomenology and critique of reason that orient themselves by these radical formal disciplines. Just as to formal logic there corresponds a system of fundamental structures of belief-consciousness (of doxic consciousness, as I am accustomed to say), and thus a phenomenology and theory of formal knowledge, so it is in a similar way with formal axiology and *Praktik* in respect of the discipline of phenomenology that belongs to them in principle, or of the theory of valuing and of willing (where these words are used in a sense analogous to the word "theory of knowledge"). Let us now go to the matters themselves!

German:

> Geht man nun den Parallelen von Logik und Ethik nach bzw. der Parallele der Akt- und Vernunftarten, auf welche diese Disziplinen wesentlich zurückbezogen sind, der urteilenden Vernunft auf der einen Seite, der praktischen Vernunft auf der anderen, so drängt sich der Gedanke auf, daß nun auch der Logik in dem bestimmt und eng begrenzten Sinn einer formalen Logik als Parallele entsprechen muß eine in analogem Sinn formale und ebenfalls apriorische Praktik. Ähnliches gilt für die Parallele mit der wertenden Vernunft, und zwar der wertenden im weitesten Sinn und nicht etwa der bloß ästhetisch wertenden. Das führt auf die Idee einer aus Wesensgründen mit der formalen Praktik innig verflochtenen formalen Axiologie als apriorischer formaler Disziplin von Werten bzw. Wertinhalten und Wertbedeutungen. Diese Ideen von neuen formalen Disziplinen, in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen, zu begrenzen und in Stücken wirklich zu realisieren, das ist das Hauptthema dieser Vorlesungen. Soweit Zeit übrig bleibt, soll dann auf die großen Problemgruppen der Phänomenologie und Kritik der Vernunft eingegangen werden, die sich nach diesen radikalen formalen Disziplinen orientieren. Wie der formalen Logik ein System fundamentaler Strukturen des Glaubensbewußtseins (des doxischen, wie ich zu sagen pflege) entspricht und somit eine Phänomenologie und Theorie der formalen Erkenntnis, so ähnlich verhält es sich mit der formalen Axiologie und Praktik hinsichtlich der ihnen prinzipiell zugehörigen Disziplin der Phänomenologie bzw. der Wertungs- und Willenstheorie (wobei diese Worte in analogem Sinn gebraucht sind wie das Wort „Erkenntnistheorie“). Gehen wir nun an die Sachen!

*Note on the text.* The relative clause "die … in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen" lacks its relative pronoun in the file ("Diese Ideen von neuen formalen Disziplinen, in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen"). The sense is not in doubt, and the rendering supplies "which".

The first sentence moves twice before it reaches its "must". It begins with the disciplines, logic and ethics, and then corrects itself with "or rather" (*bzw.*) to the kinds of act and reason to which the disciplines are "essentially referred back" (*wesentlich zurückbezogen*). The correction carries the program. A parallel of disciplines would be a fact of the curriculum; a parallel of kinds of reason is a claim about consciousness, and only that claim could ground a "must". Yet the sentence does not assert the second parallel either. It says that, for one who follows it, "the thought presses itself upon us" (*drängt sich der Gedanke auf*). The necessity sits inside a thought that imposes itself, not in a proof. And the word order delays the subject: "there must correspond as its parallel" comes before the thing that must correspond, "a *Praktik* formal in an analogous sense". The analogy is in place before its second term has been found.

Two qualifiers fix what the analogy is to deliver. The logic in question is formal logic "in the determinate and narrowly bounded sense", and the *Praktik* must be formal "in an analogous sense" and "likewise apriori". The course will test exactly these two words. If practical form turns out to be formal only in a looser sense than logical form, or apriori without being analytic, the parallel holds at a lower level than the "must" announces. The third sentence adds axiology with a looser joint ("something similar holds", *Ähnliches gilt*) and widens valuing beyond the aesthetic, so that the old triad of truth, goodness and beauty, with which §1 opens, gives way to a pair of reasons, theoretical and emotive, the second split into valuing and willing. The axiology is "intimately interwoven" with the *Praktik* "for essential reasons": the course will found willing on valuing (§§9–10), and the interweaving is announced before it is shown.

The claim of novelty is strong: these disciplines "never came to conception" in the tradition. It is aimed at the obvious counterexamples. At §5(a) (Hua XXVIII 37) Aristotle is "the father of logic" because he created the analytic, but the *Nicomachean Ethics*, "however much beauty it may offer", did not make him "in the same sense the father of ethics". At §5(b) Kant's formal principle is set aside as a principle of positive decision rather than a formal law (row 3). The novelty claim therefore has a precise content. Nobody before has abstracted from the "matter" of practice in the way the analytic abstracts from the matter of knowledge.

The sentence that closes the paragraph pitches the analogy at its third level. Formal logic corresponds to "a system of fundamental structures of belief-consciousness", and so to a phenomenology of knowledge. Axiology and *Praktik* correspond in "a similar way" to a phenomenology of valuing and willing. Husserl guards the new words with a parenthesis: "theory of valuing" (*Wertungstheorie*) and "theory of willing" (*Willenstheorie*) are used "in a sense analogous to the word 'theory of knowledge'". The quotation marks around *Erkenntnistheorie* concede that the new terms borrow their sense from the old one. The program then ends with the slogan "Let us now go to the matters themselves!" (*Gehen wir nun an die Sachen!*). The matters are what will confirm or limit the "must".

The course does not keep the modality it began with. When §5(a) returns to the program, the "must" has become a conjecture: "One would like to conjecture that theoretical reason *must* have its analogue in a practical reason" (*Man möchte vermuten, daß theoretische Vernunft ihr Analogon haben müsse*, Hua XXVIII 37–38), and a formal *Praktik* "would have to" exist "if the conjecture guiding us in our analogizing considerations is correct" (*Ist die uns … leitende Vermutung richtig, so müßte …*, Hua XXVIII 37). At §5(c) the demand is a string of questions: "Must not the distinction between logic and material science … have its strict analogue in the practical sphere too?" (Hua XXVIII 45). §8 looks back on all this as the use of formal logic as a "parallelistic clue" (*parallelistischen Leitfaden*) for "the parallel structures we conjectured" (Hua XXVIII 66). The course thus states the necessity of §1 as a conjecture, a question and a clue. The necessity is that of a heuristic. What turns it into results is description: the means–end law of §6, which is not analytic (row 5), and the will-modalities of §17. The heuristic is cut back where description finds no parallel. §12(a) turns to formal laws "that have no analogue in the narrower apophantic-logical sphere" (Hua XXVIII 90). §18(a) closes its iteration laws with "propositions that have no proper parallel in the doxic domain" (*keine rechte Parallele im doxischen Gebiet*, Hua XXVIII 128). §21 finds that subjectivity plays a role in practical possibility "that has no analogy in the judgment- or …" domain (Hua XXVIII 149), where the file breaks off after "bzw." and the next paragraph begins. The "must" of §1 is the course's working hypothesis, and its last sections record where the hypothesis fails.

The earlier course of 1908/09 already states the analogy as a guide rather than a theorem. Its part on theoretical and axiological reason says that "in the formulation and solution of the questions to be posed for a truly scientific construction of ethics … the analogy with logic must lead us; it proves itself step by step to be infinitely fruitful", and adds that "first there is need of a truly systematic logic" (Hua XXVIII 244). There the "must" governs method, and it is justified by its fruitfulness. The 1914 sentence moves the "must" from the method to the matter, so that a formal *Praktik* must exist. That is the stronger claim, and the rest of the course qualifies it again. A Beilage to the same earlier course also complicates "never came to conception": Brentano, Husserl writes there, "first recognized that a parallelism holds here between objectivating and non-objectivating acts", and urged the need to treat the valuing sphere "parallel with the logical sphere" (Hua XXVIII 345). In 1914 what is new is not the parallel but the formal discipline. Brentano had the analogue of evidence, and nobody had the analogue of the analytic.

## §4(b), Hua XXVIII 29: practical countersense

Reads [§4(b), paragraph 9](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-analogue-of-the-skeptical-countersense-in-the-practical-sphere-the-practical-countersense).

A practical countersense (*praktischer Widersinn*) is the self-cancellation of a demand whose content declares irrational what its sense as a demand presupposes as rational. The skeptic's imperative "Recognize no practical rule!" does not contradict itself in what it says. It cancels itself in what it is: a rule, and so something offered to be recognized as rational and followed. The argument therefore lives in the grammatical mood. The skeptical demand fails because it is an imperative, and the failure is practical, not logical.

> If I now say "Recognize no rule as valid for your action!", how does it stand then? If I say "Do not recognize a certain rule, this one or that!", there lies in it: Recognize that it is not rationally binding, do not let yourself be positively determined by it, do not take it as a rule! But there also lies in it a rule, namely, on the one hand, to see that to act as that rule demands is not rational action, and on the other hand to see that the contradictorily opposed will, not to bind oneself to that rule in the groups of action in question, is a rational one, and that accordingly the negative will-rule, not to proceed by the original rule, is also to be recognized in accordance with reason. If it now says "Recognize no practical rule!", there lies in it the rule, which is to be recognized as rational, that no practical rule at all is rational, that every will bound by any rules whatever is irrational, or that the will to follow no rule is rational. On the other hand, if I say "Recognize no practical rule!", I utter a practical rule, and indeed a rule which it would be rational to recognize and right to follow practically. That lies in the sense of every rule of this form, of every rule that is more than merely suggestive. On the other hand, in the content of this rule that is set up as practically irrational which the rule, by its sense, presupposes as rational. So there we have the exact analogue of the skeptical countersense.

German:

> Sage ich nun „Erkenne keine Regel als gültig für dein Handeln ⟨an⟩!“, wie steht es da? Sage ich „Erkenne eine gewisse, die und die Regel nicht an!“, so liegt darin: Erkenne, daß sie vernünftig nicht bindend ist, lasse dich von ihr positiv nicht bestimmen, nimm sie nicht als Regel! Darin liegt aber auch eine Regel, nämlich einerseits einzusehen, daß so ⟨zu⟩ handeln, wie jene Regel fordert, nicht vernünftiges Handeln sei, und andererseits einzusehen, daß der kontradiktorisch entgegengesetzte Wille, in den fraglichen Handlungsgruppen sich nicht an jene Regel zu binden, ein vernünftiger sei und daß demgemäß auch vernunftgemäß die negative Willensregel anzuerkennen sei, nach der ursprünglichen Regel nicht zu verfahren. Heißt es nun „Erkenne keine praktische Regel an!“, so liegt darin die Regel, die als vernünftig anerkannt werden soll, daß keine praktische Regel überhaupt vernünftig sei, daß jeder durch irgendwelche Regeln gebundene Wille unvernünftig sei bzw. daß der Wille, keiner Regel zu folgen, vernünftig sei. Andererseits, sage ich „Erkenne keine praktische Regel an!“, so spreche ich eine praktische Regel aus, und zwar eine Regel, die anzuerkennen vernünftig und der praktischen zu folgen richtig wäre. Das liegt im Sinn jeder Regel dieser Form, jeder mehr als bloß suggestiven Regel. Andererseits wird im Inhalt dieser Regel das als praktisch unvernünftig hingestellt, was die Regel ihrem Sinn nach als vernünftig voraussetzt. Also da haben wir das genaue Analogon des skeptischen Widersinns.

*Note on the text.* The file splices the printed edition's margin line numbers into this paragraph ("20", "25", "30", "35") and keeps two line-break hyphens with a number between them ("einer- 25 seits", "anzu- 30 erkennen"). The quotation leaves the numbers out and closes the hyphens. Both "Andererseits" are "Ackererseits" in the file, an obvious OCR slip. The phrase "der praktischen zu folgen" is as the file has it; a word may be missing ("ihr praktisch zu folgen" would be the expected form), but the sense, "right to follow in practice", is not in doubt.

The paragraph climbs in three steps, each marked by "there lies in it" (*darin liegt*, *so liegt darin*). The phrase says what a sense contains, and here it is applied to imperatives. First, a single negative rule ("Do not recognize this rule!") contains a positive one: to see that the rejected rule is not rational, and that the opposed will is. Negating a rule is itself legislating. Second, the universal negative ("Recognize no practical rule!") contains the universal positive that "no practical rule at all is rational". So far the skeptic has only said something extreme. The third step needs no further content. The skeptic "utters a practical rule" (*spreche … eine praktische Regel aus*), and that act makes the claim to rational recognition, because "that lies in the sense of every rule of this form".

Two words carry the third step: sense (*Sinn*) and content (*Inhalt*). "In the content of this rule" the rational is declared irrational; "by its sense" the rule presupposes it as rational. The pair repeats the definition of skeptical countersense given at §4(a) for the theoretical case, where a theory cancels itself if its validity "according to its essential sense" presupposes what the thesis denies and "what this theory according to its content" claims to ground (*ihrem wesentlichen Sinn gemäß … ihrem Inhalt nach*, Hua XXVIII 21). "The exact analogue" (*das genaue Analogon*) is therefore earned by the wording. The sense/content structure transfers unchanged, and only the predicate changes from true to rational-to-follow.

The phrase "more than merely suggestive" (*mehr als bloß suggestiv*) limits the argument and keeps it from being trivial. The preceding paragraph (Hua XXVIII 28) separates a demand that works suggestively, like the hypnotist's command to his medium, the master's to a slave raised in blind obedience, or a demand made of a child not yet rational, from the demand "Recognize this rule!", which asks for insight into its rightness and expects to motivate "through the addressee's having understood it". Only a demand of the second kind can cancel itself. The skeptic who merely wanted to condition behaviour would escape the argument, but at the price of no longer arguing. The argument does not show that every practical utterance presupposes practical reason. It shows that every utterance which asks to be recognized does.

The next two paragraphs make the result precise, and the precision matters more than the analogy. The logical counterpart, "Recognize no logical rule as binding for your judging!", cannot be refuted by saying that every rule asserts an ought and every assertion presupposes the law of contradiction. "The rule denies no logical rule as a logical truth." It says only that no logical truth should determine the striving for truth. Husserl concludes: "Purely logically no practical rule can cancel itself; such a rule cancels itself through practical countersense, which can indeed be connected with logical countersense, but no more than that" (*Rein logisch kann sich keine praktische Regel aufheben, eine solche hebt sich auf durch praktischen Widersinn, der allerdings mit logischem zusammenhängen kann; aber nicht mehr*, Hua XXVIII 29). The parallel thus yields a second, distinct kind of self-cancellation, not a reduction of the practical to the logical. That is the first concrete result of the course's "must" (row 1), and it already shows the parallel's shape: an analogy of structure between two irreducible kinds of reason.

The Prolegomena had defined skeptical theories as those whose theses "either expressly state or analytically include" that the logical or noetic conditions of the possibility of a theory are false (*entweder ausdrücklich besagen oder analytisch in sich schließen*, Hua XVIII 120). There the self-cancellation is analytic. The 1914 course keeps the form of the definition and gives up the word "analytic": the practical countersense is a matter of what "lies in the sense" of a rule as a rule, and Husserl says it is not logical. The same restraint returns when §6 denies that the means–end law is analytic (row 5).

The result is also narrower than it first looks, and the course says so. The recapitulation at §4(c) states that there are extreme skeptical practical demands that cancel themselves "as 'formally countersensical'" (*als „formal widersinnig“*), in a way "strictly analogous" to extreme theoretical skepticism (Hua XXVIII 35). The quotation marks around "formally countersensical" hold the word "formal" at a distance, since the form at issue is the form of a demand and not a logical form. And §5(a) admits at once that the parallel refutation reaches only the open ethical skeptic. The usual ethical empiricism, "which does not regard itself as a skepticism at all", is not touched, because ethics lacks what logic had: a formal discipline whose principles could be shown to be presupposed (Hua XXVIII 36). The practical countersense refutes the skeptic who speaks in imperatives. To refute the empiricist the course needs a formal *Praktik*, and §5 sets out to find one.

## §5(b), Hua XXVIII 43: "however 'formal'": formal as non-prejudging

Reads [§5(b), paragraph 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-traditional-sense-of-the-ethical-principles-formal-correctness-according-to-kants-categorical-imperative-versus-logical-formal-correctness).

For Husserl a principle is formal in the logician's sense when its correctness does not prejudge material correctness. Formal logic states conditions that every truth must meet and that no falsehood can meet merely by its form. It never says, positively, what is true. Kant's categorical imperative is called "formal" because it excludes material ends, but it is meant as a sufficient criterion of the morally good in each case. A principle that decides positively is not formal in Husserl's sense, however little matter it contains. The passage separates two senses of "formal" that the history of ethics had run together, and the course's own formal *Praktik* is defined by the first.

> Note that it makes no difference in this respect whether, in the historical tradition, ethical principles are sought and held possible as so-called material or as so-called formal ones. Whether in the ethical principles pleasure, general welfare, perfection and the like are set up as the highest practical goals, or whether one, with Kant, rejects all such principles as "material" and declares a categorical imperative, as a "purely formal principle", to be alone admissible: it is all one. How far such an opposition of "material" and "formal" has analogy with the like-named logical opposition is the question, and needs closer investigation. But from the outset it is clear to the connoisseur of Kantian ethics and of the disputes attached to it that, given the way the opposition is introduced and the way the formal principle is used in Kantian ethics, the analogy fails at the essential point that interests us. For Kant wanted to give, with the categorical imperative, the one single criterion of morality, and one not merely necessary but sufficient. He himself instructs us how we are to proceed with the categorical imperative in the given case in order to determine what is morally good in it, what is truly dutiful, or to judge ethically a willing and acting already carried out. Purely and exclusively the general valuation from which the demand of the categorical imperative wells up determines the value of the individual case. However "formal" the principle is, however much it may exclude what is material in the Kantian sense, it is a principle of positive decision, completely sufficient for the given case taken in its full concretion and individuality. This formal conformity-to-correctness according to the categorical imperative therefore leaves no room for a material incorrectness. Quite the contrary, we heard: in the sphere of cognition, logical-formal correctness in no way prejudges material correctness.

German:

> Beachten Sie, daß es in dieser Hinsicht keinen Unterschied ausmacht, ob in der historischen Tradition ethische Prinzipien als sogenannte materiale oder als sogenannte formale gesucht und für möglich erachtet werden. Ob in den ethischen Prinzipien Lust, allgemeine Wohlfahrt, Vollkommenheit und dergl. als die obersten praktischen Ziele hingestellt werden oder ob man mit Kant alle solchen Prinzipien als „materiale“ ablehnt und einen kategorischen Imperativ als ein „rein formales Prinzip“ für allein zulässig erklärt: es ist einerlei. Inwieweit ein solcher Gegensatz von „material“ und „formal“ mit dem gleich benannten logischen Gegensatz Analogie hat, ist die Frage und bedarf näherer Untersuchung. Aber von vornherein ist es für den Kenner der Kantischen Ethik und der an sie geknüpften Streitigkeiten klar, daß bei der Art, wie der Gegensatz eingeführt und wie vom formalen Prinzip in der Kantischen Ethik Gebrauch gemacht wird, die Analogie in dem wesentlichen, uns interessierenden Punkt versagt. Denn Kant wollte mit dem kategorischen Imperativ das eine einzige und nicht bloß notwendige, sondern zureichende Kriterium der Sittlichkeit geben. Er leitet uns selbst an, wie wir mit dem kategorischen Imperativ im gegebenen Fall zu verfahren haben, um das in ihm sittlich Gute, das wahrhaft Pflichtmäßige zu bestimmen bzw. das schon vollzogene Wollen und Handeln ethisch zu beurteilen. Rein und ausschließlich die allgemeine Wertung, aus der die Forderung des kategorischen Imperativs entquillt, bestimmt den Wert des Einzelfalls. Wie „formal“ das Prinzip auch ist, wie sehr es das im Kantischen Sinn Materiale ausschließen mag, es ist ein Prinzip positiver Entscheidung, für den gegebenen und in seiner vollen Konkretion und Individualität genommenen Fall vollkommen zureichend. Diese formale Richtigkeitsgemäßheit dem kategorischen Imperativ läßt also keinen Raum für eine materiale Unrichtigkeit. Ganz im Gegenteil dazu hörten wir: Es präjudiziert in der Erkenntnissphäre die logisch formale Richtigkeit ganz und gar nicht die materiale Richtigkeit.

*Note on the text.* "Diese formale Richtigkeitsgemäßheit dem kategorischen Imperativ" is as the file has it. The dative needs a preposition or an adjective ("Richtigkeit gemäß dem kategorischen Imperativ", formal correctness according to the categorical imperative), and a word may have been run together or lost. The rendering follows the evident sense, and nothing below turns on the compound.

The paragraph's first move is to make the received division of ethics irrelevant. Material and formal ethics, the hedonist's pleasure and Kant's imperative, are all "so-called" (*sogenannte*), and the difference between them is "all one" (*einerlei*) in the respect that matters. Husserl's quotation marks then do the work of a thesis. "Material" and "formal" are Kant's words, put in Kant's mouth ("rejects … as 'material'", "a 'purely formal principle'"). The marks keep the words from being taken in the sense the course gives them. The sentence after them states the open question in so many words: how far the ethical opposition "has analogy with the like-named logical opposition" (*mit dem gleich benannten logischen Gegensatz*). The phrase "like-named" concedes a shared name and nothing more.

The criterion of the formal follows in a pair borrowed from logic: "not merely necessary but sufficient" (*nicht bloß notwendige, sondern zureichende*). Kant is not faulted for having too little content in his principle. He is faulted for asking it to do too much. A sufficient criterion of morality is, by the preceding paragraph's account of ethical "principles", one under which the right act in any case could be found "by mere subsumption" (*durch bloße Subsumption*, Hua XXVIII 42). Logic's principles cannot work that way. They "will not and cannot decide positively what is true in every possible sphere of knowledge"; they can prejudge "only analytic, not synthetic truth" (Hua XXVIII 42). The verb Husserl uses for this, "prejudge" (*präjudizieren*), returns in the last sentence of the passage. It names the relation that formal correctness must lack. A formal principle that prejudges material correctness has ceased to be formal.

"However 'formal'" (*Wie „formal“*) is the turn of the paragraph. The concession grants Kant his exclusion of the material "in the Kantian sense" and denies that the exclusion makes the principle formal in the sense at issue. Husserl's name for what the imperative is, "a principle of positive decision" (*ein Prinzip positiver Entscheidung*), puts the weight on "positive". The consequence is drawn with "therefore" (*also*): the imperative "leaves no room for a material incorrectness". In logic there is such room. A syllogism can be formally valid and materially false, and that gap is what makes form and matter two dimensions of correctness and gives "double truths", formal and material. A principle that closes the gap has only one dimension, whatever it is called.

The inference is quicker than the wording suggests. Husserl takes Kant at his word that the imperative is sufficient, and "he himself instructs us" (*Er leitet uns selbst an*) appeals to Kant's procedure, not to a reading of it. Whether the universalization test really decides the concrete case without any prior valuation is exactly what "the disputes attached to it", which Husserl's sentence mentions, had been about. Husserl does not need to settle this. His claim is conditional on Kant's intention: if the imperative does what Kant meant it to do, it is not formal in the logician's sense.

§8 restates the charge as a dilemma. Kant's "formalistic ethics" could not count as the analogue of formal logic, and "if Kant was right", the analogy between knowing and practical consciousness "went to pieces" (*ging … in die Brüche*, Hua XXVIII 65). The next paragraph removes the dilemma by history. Kant's formal ethics "was not designed at all as an analogue of formal, analytic logic", since Kant did not let himself be led "in a radical way" by the thought of a thoroughgoing parallelism (Hua XXVIII 66). The quarrel is therefore about a word. Kant's "formal" means free of matter, and Husserl's means non-prejudging.

At §19 the charge returns reversed. There a "formal rule-giving of valuing and willing" which supposes that the matter of valuing and willing "could remain out of account" is "a countersense" (*ein Widersinn*): without a material apriori, without kinds of objects that carry value-predicates by their essence, "the concept of objective value would have no hold". "In this respect we must therefore sharply separate our ways from the Kantian doctrine" (*müssen wir also unsere Wege von der Kantischen Lehre scharf sondern*, Hua XXVIII 139; row 21). The two charges fit together. At §5 Kant's principle decides too much to be formal; at §19 a principle that tried to be formal by excluding matter would ground nothing. Husserl's formal *Praktik* avoids both. It decides nothing positively, as §5 requires, and it presupposes in formal generality that material value-laws exist, as §19 requires. The sense of "formal" fixed at §5 thus also fixes the limit §19 sets on formal ethics as a whole: it is a necessary condition of rightness, never a sufficient criterion.

## §5(c), Hua XXVIII 47–48: the good as the existential value

Reads [§5(c), paragraph 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#c-on-the-possibility-of-formal-practical-and-formal-axiological-principles).

In this passage the good (*das Gute*) is the value of something *as existing*. The beautiful is valued without regard to whether its object exists. The good is the beautiful once existence is taken into the valuing, and Husserl defines it through an act: goods (*Güter*) are the correlates of rational valuings "of the genus joy" (*der Gattung Freude*). From that definition follows an apriori chain in which one value passes into the next as the doxic standing of its object changes: beautiful, good if it exists, desire-worthy if it does not, a will-value if it is realizable. The chain is the first sample of a formal axiology. It is also the doctrine that Beilage I calls an "obliquity" (*Schiefheit*), because it identifies valuing something as good with rejoicing in it.

> If we separate off, excluding for the moment the domain of desiring and willing, a domain of non-existential values as a domain of beauty-values in the widest sense; if we thus comprise under it the values for which the existence or non-existence of the value-giving object is irrelevant to the sense and validity of the value-predication; if we set over against it the sphere of existential valuings, in which the reverse holds, and if we call the values that form the correlate of rational existential valuings of the genus joy *goods*, and the corresponding negative values *evils*: this presupposed, it is clear that every beautiful object is at the same time a good one; that is, what proves itself beautiful in rational aesthetic valuing must necessarily prove itself good in rational existential valuing; that is, if it exists, or stands there in conviction as existing, it is necessarily, that is, generally in accordance with reason, the object of a justified joy; likewise in the reverse case of non-existence ⟨it is⟩ the object of justified grief. Again: let us suppose that the character of existence of the object is lacking for consciousness, but that the assumption of existence grounds, in accordance with reason, an assumptive liking. We think ourselves into the existence, then, and in this thought of "supposing it were" there is grounded a quasi-joy that can be rationally justified, namely of the kind: that would be a real joy! Then it is clear that the same object or the same state of affairs would be the justified bearer of a rational wish, and, if it stands there as realizable, of a justified will. If we call the value-correlates of the rational wish desire-values, and those of the rational will will-values or practical values, then it is clear that all these values cohere apriori. *Apriori* it holds: the beautiful is at the same time a good; every good is, if it does not exist, a desire-value; every desire-value is, if it is realizable, a will-value, so that the concept of the good is as a rule extended. What can rightly be desired is also called good, and the practical value is practically good.

The first sentence is a chain of four hortatory subjunctives ("if we separate off … if we comprise … if we set over against … if we call"). The whole chain is a stipulation, and its last clause is a definition by naming: "let us call" (*nennen wir*) the correlates of rational joy "goods". Everything after "this presupposed" (*Dies vorausgesetzt*) depends on that act of naming. The good is introduced as a value-correlate of an emotion of a determinate kind, and the "it is clear" (*ist es klar*) that follows is clear only under the stipulation. When Beilage I asks whether "that A is, is good" is "equivalent" to "that A is, is gladdening" (*erfreulich*), it questions exactly the clause that the presupposition hides.

Two things vary through the chain, and the wording keeps them apart. The first is the doxic standing of the object: "exists, or stands there in conviction as existing" (*existiert es bzw. steht es als existierend in der Überzeugung da*), non-existence, "the assumption of existence" (*die Assumption der Existenz*), "stands there as realizable" (*als realisierbar dasteht*). The second is the act and its correlate: joy or grief, an "assumptive liking" or "quasi-joy", a wish, a will. Each emotive act is matched to one doxic standing. This is the course's model of founding, already at work. The emotive act is "motivated", as §9 puts it, by a value-presupposition and by "the conviction, the judgment, that the beautiful is" (Hua XXVIII 73). The verbs in the chain show the founding: what is beautiful "proves itself" (*sich … ausweist*) good in existential valuing. It does not become good by being judged to exist. It shows itself good to a valuing that takes existence in.

The step to the wish is the most carefully worded. Where existence is lacking, the assumption of existence grounds an "assumptive" liking, and the dash introduces the experience that carries it: "we think ourselves into the existence", and in the thought "supposing it were" there arises a "quasi-joy" of the form "that would be a real joy!" (*Das wäre eine rechte Freude!*). The subjunctive of the exclamation is the wish's own grammar. A wish is a joy held in the mood of supposition. That makes the desire-value a modification of the good rather than a new genus of value, and it is why Husserl can write that "the concept of the good is as a rule extended" (*sich … in der Regel erweitert*). "As a rule" here means "in the usual way of speaking": ordinary language calls the rightly desirable "good", and the practically valuable "practically good". The chain thus explains a usage. One word covers four values because the four are one value under four doxic standings.

The extension of the word hides a gap that the course later finds in the thing. The last link, "every desire-value is, if it is realizable, a will-value", makes the will a further modification of the same joy, a quasi-joy in the realizable. Section III takes this back. At §14 the will is not a wish with a realizability-clause attached: the wish means an "it may be", the will an "it shall be" (row 14). At §15 the will is creative, and its "Let it be!" is not founded on an expectation of what will be (row 15). The chain of §5(c) treats the will as the last of the valuings. The phenomenology of the will treats it as a position of its own kind.

The identification of the good with the joy-value is stated again in the formal axiology itself. In §10 the objective law runs: "If A is a beautiful thing and is in reality, then it is a good one", and "'good' is here understood as the axiological correlate of rational joy, thus the joy-value" (*„Gut“ ist hier verstanden als das axiologische Korrelat der vernünftigen Freude, also das der Freude Werte*, Hua XXVIII 74–75). That is the page to which, together with Hua XXVIII 47 f., Beilage I is keyed. Its correction is explicit: "In the lectures I identified the two. But one must consider carefully whether rightly. I inclined repeatedly to the view (and so again now!) that a distinction is to be made here" (*In den Vorlesungen habe ich beides identifiziert … Ich neigte wiederholt zu der Ansicht (und so auch jetzt wieder!), daß hier zu scheiden sei*, Hua XXVIII 155; row 24). What the Beilage separates is the valuing of the being of A from the joy at it. "I value, taking existence into account, the being: that A is, that is valuable." A valuing that takes existence in need not be a rejoicing. The definition by naming in §5(c) had made it one. The Beilage does not touch the chain's form: the dependence of values on the doxic standing of their objects survives the correction, and so does the formal character Husserl claims for it in the next paragraph, where the connections "seem already to be of a formal nature", since "it does not depend on the 'matter' of beauty and goodness" (Hua XXVIII 48). What the Beilage removes is the act in which §5(c) had grounded the good.

## §6, Hua XXVIII 55: formal but not analytic

Reads [§6, paragraph 7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-formal-relation-between-means-and-end-is-no-logical-relation-the-omni-dominion-of-logical-reason).

The means–end law, "whoever wills the end must rationally will the necessary means", is formal without being analytic. It is formal because it holds whatever the end and the means are. It is not analytic because its terms, will and cause, are not logical concepts: replace them by indeterminate ones and the inference fails. The passage takes Kant's word "analytic" away from this law and so separates two properties that in logic coincide. In the theoretical sphere the formal and the analytic are one. In the practical sphere a law can be independent of the matter of the will and still not follow from the form of judgment. That gap is where the course places its formal *Praktik*.

> In this it is of special importance to bring oneself to the insight that the will-situation is not, say, a mere special case of the intellectual, the logical state of affairs, and so that will-reason is not a special case of logical reason in inferring. Above all it is evident that the rational enclosure of the means in the end, or of the willing of the means in the willing of the end, is not an "analytic" relation, not something like a logical relation of inference. This comes out when we bring the will-situation to predicative expression. From the proposition "I will the end *Z*" and from the proposition "*M* is a necessary means to *Z*" no one can infer by formal-logical principles "I must will *M*, that is, I ought rationally to will *M*", unless he inserts the general proposition: whoever wills an end at all "must" will the necessary means to it. But just this proposition states the principle of the mode of inference generally, and is therefore in question. It is evidently no logical-analytic proposition, none that is evident from the essence of judgment in general, none that would be valid from the form of judgment in general regardless of its particular judgment-matter. If we analyse what this proposition says, and so first what "means and end" says, we are led to a causal relation: the becoming-real of the fact *M* draws, or would draw, after it the becoming-real of the fact *Z*. To this is added (for the mode of inference in question) that the agent cannot realize *Z* immediately, but can immediately realize a causal way to *Z*, and that on the other hand he can realize no other sufficient cause for *Z* than one that implies *M* as a necessary member. Where all this obtains, there we can indeed infer: in such cases we must will *M*. We "must": that says here no psychological necessity, but a necessity of reason, a rational ought. But after this analysis we see all the more, and quite clearly, that this mode of inference from the willing of *Z* to the rational ought-to-will of *M* is no analytic one, none grounded in the mere form of judgment. For then it would have to remain valid if we replaced all the concepts occurring here, cause, effect, will, in short all the not purely logical concepts, by indeterminate ones. Of that there is of course no question. It is no logical contradiction at all, for all the unreason, to will the end and not to will the means.

The paragraph's first claim is negative and is aimed at a reduction: the will-situation is not "a mere special case" (*ein bloßer Sonderfall*) of the logical one. That is the thesis of the section's title ("no logical relation"), and the passage proves it from Kant's own example. The phrase "rational enclosure" (*vernunftgemäßes Eingeschlossensein*) concedes the image on which Kant's "analytic" rested. Something is enclosed in the willing of the end, and Husserl does not deny it. He denies that the enclosure is the containment of a concept in a concept. The quotation marks around "analytic" (*„analytisches“*) cite Kant's word in order to refuse it. Two pages earlier Husserl had quoted the *Groundwork*: the proposition "whoever wills the end wills also the means indispensably necessary to it" is, "as regards the willing, analytic", because "in the willing of an object as my effect my causality as acting cause … is already thought" (Hua XXVIII 51, citing Kant, Ak. IV 417). His objection there is phenomenological: the willing of the end is not "a complex that really implies the willing of the only necessary means", and one can will an end "without being in the least clear about the means" (Hua XXVIII 52). Kant's "already thought" (*schon … gedacht*) is false of the act. The present paragraph adds that it is false of the proposition too.

The proof works through the quotation marks around "must". In the inserted premise, whoever wills an end "must" (*„muß“*) will the means. The marks flag a modal word that cannot be read off the form of judgment, and Husserl's gloss later in the paragraph fixes its sense: "We 'must': that says here no psychological necessity, but a necessity of reason, a rational ought". The syllogism is valid only with the premise, and the premise "states the principle of the mode of inference" itself. A principle cannot be derived by the inference it governs, so the will-inference has a principle of its own.

The decisive sentence is a test of formality borrowed from logic: an analytic inference "would have to remain valid if we replaced all the concepts occurring here, cause, effect, will, in short all the not purely logical concepts, by indeterminate ones". It is the criterion of the Third Logical Investigation: "in an analytic proposition it must be possible to replace every material content, with full preservation of the logical form of the proposition, by the empty form something" (*jede sachhaltige Materie, bei voller Erhaltung der logischen Form des Satzes, durch die leere Form etwas zu ersetzen*, LU III §12, Hua XIX/1 259). Applied to the will-inference, it shows exactly what the inference lacks. Its validity depends on "will" and "cause", so it is not analytic. But the inference is not material either, since nothing in it depends on what *Z* and *M* are. The variables stay variables. The law is formal in respect of the will's matter and contentful in respect of the form "will". The final sentence states the result in the register of the contradiction it denies: willing the end and not the means is "no logical contradiction at all, for all the unreason" (*gar kein logischer Widerspruch, trotz aller Unvernunft*). "Unreason" names a failure that is real and not logical. The concessive phrase holds both claims in one breath.

The next paragraph asks whether the law then belongs to empirical reason, the reason of causal knowledge, since it rests on a causal relation, and answers that it does not: causal facts contain "nothing of means and end and of reason", and the rational ought "is no fact of experience". The law is apriori, "evidently valid in unconditioned generality and necessity" (Hua XXVIII 56–57). Neither analytic logic nor experience accounts for it, and that leaves room for a third reason.

§8 draws the positive consequence and gives Kant's word back in a restricted sense. The task is "not to miss the legitimate sense of this talk of 'analytic'": this "analytic" does not mean, "as Kant meant it", the same as the "analytic" of formal logic, but "a mere analogue of the logical-analytic". "And there now was a point at which we entered the genuine and sought-for parallel sphere to the formal-logical." The means–end laws are formal, "in so far as they include not the least of the matter of what is willed. Only the form, and indeed as will-form, comes into question" (*Nur die Form, und zwar als Willensform, kommt in Frage*, Hua XXVIII 68). The phrase "as will-form" answers the substitution test of §6. In logic the form is the form of judgment, and "will" is matter. In the formal *Praktik* "will" belongs to the form. Every formal practical law will therefore fail the logician's test of analyticity, and the §6 result defines the discipline, it is not a defect in it. The "must" of §1 is fulfilled here for the first time (row 1), and fulfilled as an analogue: the practical sphere has its own formal laws, which are not logical laws.

## §7, Hua XXVIII 64: the emotions mean

Reads [§7, paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-analogies-for-the-judging-position-taking-of-belief-in-the-classes-of-emotive-and-will-acts-as-the-ground-for-the-parallelism-of-the-kinds-of-reason).

The parallelism of the kinds of reason rests on one verb. A liking or a wishing "means" (*vermeint*): it takes a position on its object, holds it for good or bad, and so can be right or wrong, as a judgment can. A mere sensation means nothing. *Vermeinen* here is not the meaning of a word (*bedeuten*) but meaning-*that*: intending, holding-for, the position-taking of an act. Husserl's claim is that the emotions have their own kind of it, an "analogue of judging" that is not a judging. Logical reason can bring values to light only because the emotions have already "decided" before cognition arrives. The passage answers Rickert and Windelband, who read the position-taking of judgment as a feeling, by reversing the direction of their analogy: feeling is not the core of judgment, but feeling has the structure of position-taking that judgment has.

> Of course this critique is directed against the theories of Rickert and Windelband, so popular in our time. The axiological reason disregarded by the tradition breaks through in these theories in a certain way. Its very peculiarity is felt so strongly by these researchers, who lean on Fichte, that they fall into the exaggeration of reinterpreting logical reason itself axiologically. On the other hand, and to return to our starting point, the omni-dominion of logical reason need not make us go astray about the parallelism of the kinds of reason. In it there is expressed a certain essential interweaving of doxic consciousness with emotive consciousness, and so with every consciousness at all, according to which every position-taking, every valuing-as-beautiful or valuing-as-good, can *apriori* be converted into a judging position-taking. […] Bringing out, establishing, determining, objectivating in the specific sense, is the affair of logical reason. Axiological reason with its holdings is, so to speak, hidden from itself. It first becomes manifest through the cognition that is carried out on the ground of the emotive acts. But cognition does not invent; it only draws out what is in a certain way already there. Were the emotions not a domain of meanings, did they not already in themselves, but precisely in the manner of the emotions, make decisions, cast their vote, then cognition would find nothing of values and value-contents before it. It would then find only blind experiences, such as, say, experiences of sensing red and blue. A mere sensing, a mere living-through of a sensuous datum, means nothing; but a liking means, a wishing means, and so on. Meaning is not always judging (in the ordinary sense), a manner of believingly holding-for-something, but it is an analogue of judging.

Verneinen ist nicht immer Urteilen (im gewöhnlichen Sinn), eine Weise des glaubend Für-etwas-Haltens, aber ein Analogon des Urteilens ist es.

Both are taken here as *Vermeinungen* and *Vermeinen* (meanings, meaning), and rendered so. The sense requires it. What the emotions are said to be is the condition of their containing values, and they are opposed to "blind experiences" that "mean nothing"; the sentence between gives "vermeint" three times, correctly spelt; and a negating would be a judging in the ordinary sense, which is what the last sentence denies of the word it glosses. The same confusion runs through §7. In the paragraph before this one the file has "alle Vermengungen, alle intentionalen Stellungnahmen" (all mixtures, all intentional position-takings) and "sofern sie eben verneinen" (in so far as they negate), where the sense needs *Vermeinungen* and *vermeinen*. At Hua XXVIII 59–60 "Erkennen ist ein Vermeinen, verneinen, es sei etwas" and "als verneinendes Verhalten das Analogon zum urteilenden Vermeinen, allgemeiner, zum Seinsverneinen" are probably of the same kind. Genuine *Verneinung* (negation) is treated in §17(d), and none of these places is about negation. The omitted sentence (marked […]) says that the emotive acts, their right and wrong, and the values correlative to them become fields of determinations carried out in logical acts.

The passage moves from a polemic to a thesis, and the hinge is "on the other hand" (*Andererseits*). The polemic grants Rickert and Windelband a discovery and charges them with an overreach. Axiological reason "breaks through" (*bricht … durch*) in their theories, but "these researchers, who lean on Fichte" (*an Fichte sich anlehnenden*) feel its peculiarity so strongly that they "reinterpret" (*umdeuten*) logical reason as valuing. The preceding paragraph names the doctrine: what brings the value-difference of truth and falsity into judgment is supposed to be the addition of "feelings of a transcendental ought, … feelings of the necessity of thought" (Hua XXVIII 63). The naming of Fichte locates the error: the primacy of the practical, carried into the theory of judgment. Husserl's own term for the result is "emotionalism", the mirror image of the intellectualism that dissolves all reason into logical reason (Hua XXVIII 63).

The thesis begins by conceding what the polemic might seem to deny, "the omni-dominion of logical reason" (*Allherrschaft der logischen Vernunft*). Every valuing can be "converted" (*umgewandelt*) into a judgment, and everything that is established about values is established by logical acts. The concession is phrased so that it cannot become a reduction. Logical reason does the "bringing out, establishing, determining", and the series of verbs gives it the work of exhibiting. "Cognition does not invent, it only draws out what is in a certain way already there." What is "already there" is axiological reason, "hidden from itself" (*sich selbst verborgen*). The image divides the labour cleanly. Valuing reason has holdings it cannot see; logical reason sees but owns nothing.

The counterfactual that follows gives the ground of the division, and its verbs are judicial. The emotions "make decisions" (*Entscheidungen treffen*) and "cast their vote" (*ihr Votum abgeben*). Both words are taken from judgment, and both are qualified at once by "but precisely in the manner of the emotions". The next paragraph makes the borrowing explicit: the will does not judge, "and yet in a certain sense and speaking in a likeness it does judge", for "*it itself speaks*, it *casts as it were a vote*" (*er selbst spricht, er gibt gleichsam ein Votum*); "we also call judging a deciding-oneself" (Hua XXVIII 64). The italics there survive in the file. A vote is a position taken by someone who is entitled to take it, and it can be right or wrong. That is all the parallelism needs.

The contrast with sensation fixes what *Vermeinen* adds. Sensing red and blue is a "blind experience", a "mere living-through" (*bloßes Erleben*) that "means nothing". A liking "means". The verb has no object in the sentence, and that is the point: it names the act's own claim, not a content. The final sentence then sets the word against its doxic model. Meaning "is not always judging (in the ordinary sense), a manner of believingly holding-for-something" (*eine Weise des glaubend Für-etwas-Haltens*), "but an analogue of judging". "In the ordinary sense" leaves room for a wider sense of judging in which the emotions do judge. The earlier pages of §7 had prepared that sense: "new basic kinds of position-taking appear here, new kinds of meaning, of holding-for" (*neue Arten des Vermeinens, des Dafürhaltens*), and in every wishing "there lies a holding-for-good" (*ein Für-gut-Halten*, Hua XXVIII 60). Holding-for-good is the emotive analogue of holding-for-true. The hyphenated compounds make the structure visible: *Für-wahr-*, *Für-seiend-*, *Für-gut-Halten*, one form with different predicates.

The English "means" loses a distinction that the argument needs. *Vermeinen* names the act's claim, not a signification (*Bedeutung*): a liking does not signify anything, and Husserl does not say it does. The German verb also keeps the edge of *vermeintlich* (supposed). What the emotion claims may be wrong, which is why its claims fall under a reason of their own.

The word returns at the points where the course decides what the emotions are. §8 defines doxic consciousness as "a meaning that something is" (*ein Vermeinen, es sei etwas*), and splits it into the act, "the judging meaning", the proposition and the state of affairs (Hua XXVIII 66). The emotive analogues of that triad are what Section II studies. §8 also explains why valuing reason is "mute and in a certain way blind" (row 7), and the blindness there is not the blindness of sensation: a meaning act can be right without seeing that it is. At §14 belief is "the meaning of being", and the emotive acts are modalizations of being "of a new dimension" (Hua XXVIII 105; row 14). Beilage I puts the word in quotation marks and asks of the non-rational emotions: "Are the blind acts not 'meanings'?" (*Sind die blinden Akte nicht „Verneinungen“?*, again for *Vermeinungen*, Hua XXVIII 156). "Blind" there means without insight, not without intention. The question shows that the clean division of §7, the meaning emotions against blind sensations, left unsettled whether every emotion, rational or not, contains a valuing. That is the question the Beilage turns into a research programme (row 25).

## §8, Hua XXVIII 68–69: mute and blind reason

Reads [§8, paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#recapitulation-formal-logic-as-the-clue-to-the-discovery-of-the-parallel-structures-in-the-emotive-sphere-the-privilege-of-logical-predicative-reason).

Valuing and practical reason are "mute and in a certain way blind": they can be right without seeing that they are right and without being able to say so. Seeing, including the "seeing" of insight, is a doxic act. So the rightness of a valuing is there before any act has grasped it, and only logical reason can bring it to givenness, form it into concepts and state it as a law. The division of labour is exact. Logical reason contributes the forms in which a right is stated; the valuing acts contribute the rightness stated. This settles the problem of the omni-dominion of logical reason that §§6–7 had raised (row 6). Logic rules everywhere as the reason that formulates, and no other reason is reduced to it.

> Logical reason, however, has the unique privilege that not only in its own field, but in the field of every other genus of meaning, and so in every other sphere of reason, it formulates right, determines rightfulness, predicates and utters laws of right as laws. Valuing and practical reason are, so to speak, mute and in a certain way blind. Even seeing in the narrower and wider sense, and so also in the sense of "insight", is a doxic act. A right can be there without being seen; it can be there without being brought under concepts of right, explicated and thought in the form of judgment, uttered as a law in the form of an assertion. It is there as a right of valuing reason when valuing acts of a certain essential kind and formation are carried out or can be carried out. But merely valuing reason does not see, does not grasp, does not explicate, does not predicate. With it, therefore, acts of the logical sphere in the widest sense of the word, the doxic sphere, must interweave. Only in the carrying out of such acts can acts at all, and ⟨that⟩ which they mean, come to objective givenness for us, and can ⟨we⟩ then see that valuing acts are "meaning" acts, acts that hold for beautiful, for good, and further that they stand under ideal predicates of rightness and wrongness, and so on. So the torch of logical reason must be lit, so that what is hidden of forms and norms in the sphere of the emotions and the will can come into the bright light. But the logical acts only shine in and only make visible what is there. They constitute only the logical forms, not the peculiar contents of reason of the parallel spheres of reason that are grasped in the forms.

German:

> Die logische Vernunft hat nun aber den einzigartigen Vorzug, daß sie nicht nur in ihrem eigenen Feld, sondern im Feld jeder anderen Gattung des Vermeinens, also in jeder anderen Vernunftsphäre Recht formuliert, Rechtmäßigkeit bestimmt, Rechtsgesetze als Gesetze prädiziert und ausspricht. Wertende und praktische Vernunft sind sozusagen stumm und in gewisser Weise blind. Schon das Sehen im engeren und weiteren Sinn, also auch im Sinne des „Einsehens“ ist ein doxischer Akt. Ein Recht kann da sein, ohne gesehen zu sein, es kann da sein, ohne unter Rechtsbegriffe gebracht, in Urteilsform expliziert und gedacht, in Aussageform als Gesetz ausgesprochen zu sein. Es ist da als Recht wertender Vernunft, wenn wertende Akte gewisser Wesensartung und -formung vollzogen bzw. vollziehbar sind. Aber bloß wertende Vernunft sieht nicht, begreift nicht, expliziert nicht, prädiziert nicht. Mit ihr müssen sich also Akte der im weitesten Wortsinn logischen, der doxischen Sphäre verflechten. Nur im Vollzug solcher Akte können uns Akte überhaupt und ⟨das⟩, was sie vermeinen, zur gegenständlichen Gegebenheit kommen und können ⟨wir⟩ dann einsehen, daß wertende Akte „vermeinende“, für schön, für gut haltende sind, dann weiter, daß sie unter idealen Prädikaten der Rechtheit und Unrechtheit stehen usw. Also die Fackel der logischen Vernunft muß aufgesteckt werden, damit, was an Formen und Normen in der Gemüts- und Willenssphäre verborgen ist, an das helle Licht treten kann. Die logischen Akte leuchten aber nur hinein und machen nur sichtbar, was da ist. Sie konstituieren nur die logischen Formen, nicht aber die in den Formen gefaßten eigentümlichen Vernunftgehalte der parallelen Vernunftsphären.

*Note on the text.* This stretch of the file is hard-wrapped with line-break hyphens ("Vermei-nens", "Recht-mäßigkeit", "wei-teren", "prädi-ziert", "hal-tende", "logi-schen" and others); the quotation closes them. The page marker for Hua XXVIII 69 falls between "ein doxischer" and "Akt". Here "Vermeinens" and "vermeinende" are spelt correctly, and they confirm the reading of the damaged forms in §7.

The privilege is described in verbs, and all of them are verbs of saying. Logical reason "formulates right, determines rightfulness, predicates and utters laws of right as laws" (*Recht formuliert, Rechtmäßigkeit bestimmt, Rechtsgesetze als Gesetze prädiziert und ausspricht*). None of them makes anything right. The privilege extends "into the field of every other genus of meaning" (*jeder anderen Gattung des Vermeinens*): the genitive repeats §7's result that the emotions are a genus of meaning, so logic's privilege presupposes the plurality it might seem to threaten. The next sentence supplies the deficit that makes the privilege necessary, in two metaphors of unequal force. Valuing reason is "mute" without qualification, since it cannot assert (§7 had called this the will's "non-self-sufficiency", Hua XXVIII 64). It is blind only "in a certain way" (*in gewisser Weise*).

The reason for the blindness is given in one sentence that the argument depends on: "Even seeing in the narrower and wider sense, and so also in the sense of 'insight', is a doxic act." The quotation marks around *Einsehens* (insight) mark the word as the evidence-term of the theory of knowledge, and the sentence assigns it to belief. If insight is doxic, a valuing cannot be insightful in its own right. Its rightness must then be the kind of thing that "can be there without being seen". The phrase "is there" (*ist da*) carries the ontology of the passage. A right "is there" whenever "valuing acts of a certain essential kind and formation are carried out or can be carried out". The "or can be" (*bzw. vollziehbar*) makes the right independent even of its actual exercise. It belongs to the essence of the act, as truth belongs to the proposition whether or not anyone judges it.

The four negations ("does not see, does not grasp, does not explicate, does not predicate") are answered by an act that the passage then performs on itself. Only in doxic acts can we "see that valuing acts are 'meaning' acts" (*„vermeinende“*). The quotation marks around "meaning" here are a mark of the step from one reason to the other: the predicate is the logician's term for what the valuing does without naming it. The image of the torch closes the step, and it is weighed carefully. The torch must be "lit" (*aufgesteckt*) for what is "hidden" (*verborgen*, the same word as §7's "hidden from itself") to come to light. But the logical acts "only shine in and only make visible what is there" (*leuchten aber nur hinein und machen nur sichtbar*). The double "only" limits what the omni-dominion can claim. Logical acts "constitute only the logical forms, not … the contents of reason". *Konstituieren* is the strongest verb in Husserl's vocabulary, and he allows it to logic only for form.

The inference is weaker than the image. That a right can be there unseen does not by itself show that the valuing act is its source rather than the judgment that first states it. Husserl concedes the gap in the next sentence: to understand "from the deepest grounds" how emotive acts can be "originary sources" of their own values "requires very difficult demonstrations" (Hua XXVIII 69). §8 therefore closes Section I with a division that it has stated but not yet proved.

The division governs the rest of the course. Section II's formal axiology is logic's statement of rights it does not constitute. Its laws are "the objective expression" of motivation-laws among the emotive acts, as §10's title puts it. But the course does not keep the claim that only doxic acts see. At §20 it asks whether a will can be right and "wholly blind": "without any insight, following a blind drive", someone may hit on the best, as a judge "in full insight" can recognize (Hua XXVIII 143). The goal it then sets is "insight into the rightness and therefore not blind but insightful willing" (*nicht blindes, sondern einsichtiges Wollen*, Hua XXVIII 143). The closing paragraph of the course makes the will's own reckoning the condition: only if the one willing "does not cast his will-vote blindly", so that "the rightness of the goal constitutes itself originarily in his willing", is he "in himself consciousness of an ought" (Hua XXVIII 153; row 23). "Blind" there is no longer the blindness of all valuing reason. It is the blindness of a will that lacks insight, and its opposite is a will in which rightness "constitutes itself originarily". By the end, then, the will itself does what §8 allowed only to doxic acts. Whether the insightful will sees by means of an interwoven doxic act, as §8 requires, or in its own way, the course does not say. §8's "in a certain way blind" keeps the question open.

## §9, Hua XXVIII 72: directed upon values

Reads [§9, paragraph 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#apriori-motivation-laws-as-laws-of-rational-consequence-motivational-connections-between-the-logical-and-the-axiological-sphere).

A valuing act is founded on an objectivating act, but it is not directed upon what that act presents. It is directed upon a value. Founding settles what the value is the value *of*: the presented thing, which is or is not. Directedness settles what the valuing takes up: the thing's being good or bad. The two relations run on different axes, and the course's formal axiology needs both. Without founding, valuing would float free of the world; without its own direction, valuing would have no correlate of its own for laws of value to be about, and Section II would be a chapter of the logic of belief. §9 states the distinction in one paragraph, and it states it as a promise.

> This interweaving easily leads astray, since it suggests saying that the valuing acts are intentional only in the sense that they are directed upon the presented objectivities. But we shall still have to carry out that in the proper sense only objectivating acts are directed upon the objective, upon what is or is not, but valuing acts upon values, and more closely, upon positive and negative values. To be sure, the one and the other are essentially fused in the matter, or correspondingly in the acts: values have their object-side and at the same time their specific value-side, the former founding the latter, and when values themselves become objects of judging cognition, the value-side itself is objectivated. But however that may be: with these somewhat difficult relations there hangs together a reason-motivation that connects the intellective and the axiological. For example, whoever is surely convinced that A is cannot will A; whoever is convinced that A is not cannot will that not A is; likewise if instead of "whoever is convinced" we say "whoever surmises with convincing probability". The talk here is of course not of a psychological can or cannot, but of an apriori of reason.

German:

> Diese Verflechtung führt leicht in die Irre, da sie nahelegt zu sagen, daß die wertenden Akte nur in dem Sinn intentionale sind, daß sie auf die vorgestellten Gegenständlichkeiten gerichtet sind. Aber wir werden noch ausführen müssen, daß im eigentlichen Sinn nur objektivierende Akte auf Gegenständliches, auf Seiendes oder Nichtseiendes gerichtet sind, wertende Akte aber auf Werte, und näher, auf positive und negative Werte. Freilich, eins und das andere ist sachlich bzw. in den Akten wesentlich verschmolzen: Werte haben ihre Gegenstandsseite und zugleich ihre spezifische Wertseite, die erstere für die letztere fundierend, und wenn Werte selbst zu Gegenständen der urteilenden Erkenntnis werden, so wird die Wertseite selbst objektiviert. Doch wie immer: Mit diesen etwas schwierigen Verhältnissen hängt eine Vernunftmotivation zusammen, die Intellektives und Axiologisches verknüpft. Zum Beispiel, wer sicher überzeugt ist, daß A ist, kann nicht A wollen; wer überzeugt ist, daß A nicht ist, kann nicht, daß nicht A ist, wollen; ebenso wenn wir, statt „wer überzeugt ist“ sagen „wer mit überzeugender Wahrscheinlichkeit vermutet“. Die Rede ist hier natürlich nicht von einem psychologischen Können oder Nichtkönnen, sondern von einem Apriori der Vernunft.

The misreading and its correction turn on where the word "only" (*nur*) stands. The misreading puts it on the valuing acts: they are intentional "only in the sense" that they are directed upon the presented objectivities, so that their intentionality is borrowed from the presentation beneath them. Husserl's answer moves the "only" to the other side: "in the proper sense only objectivating acts" are directed upon the objective. The restriction is not withdrawn but redistributed. What had limited valuing now limits objectivation, and the limit is drawn by an apposition that defines the objective: "upon the objective, upon what is or is not" (*auf Gegenständliches, auf Seiendes oder Nichtseiendes*). To be an object, in the proper sense, is to be the correlate of a positing of being or non-being. Values fall outside that definition, and so outside objectivity in the proper sense, without falling outside intentionality.

The second clause is elliptical, and the ellipsis is the claim. "But valuing acts upon values" (*wertende Akte aber auf Werte*) has no verb of its own; it borrows "are directed" (*gerichtet sind*) from the clause before. Direction is one relation with two kinds of correlate, not a primary relation of the doxic acts and a derivative one of the emotions. The correlates are then made exactly parallel. Objectivating acts are directed upon "what is or is not"; valuing acts, "more closely" (*näher*), upon "positive and negative values". The polarity of being and non-being has its counterpart in the polarity of plus and minus, and this pairing is what §10 and §11 will formalize, down to the law of excluded fourth (row 12), which adds value-freedom as a third case that being and non-being lack. The hedge "in the proper sense" (*im eigentlichen Sinn*) also concedes something. In an improper sense valuing acts are directed upon the presented object after all, through the presentation that founds them, and the misreading is not false but partial.

"To be sure" (*Freilich*) then gives back what the correction seemed to take. The two directions are "essentially fused", and the paragraph carries the founding relation over from the acts to the correlate. The paragraph before had said that the valuing act is founded in the intellective act "precisely insofar as it constitutes the value-appearance" (*Werterscheinung*, Hua XXVIII 72). Now the value itself has two sides, an "object-side" (*Gegenstandsseite*) and a "specific value-side" (*spezifische Wertseite*), "the former founding the latter". The coinages divide one correlate, not two objects. The last clause adds a third relation: when values become objects of judging cognition, "the value-side itself is objectivated" (*objektiviert*). That is §8's torch of logical reason (row 7), now stated of the correlate. The value-side is first given in valuing and only afterwards made an object.

Here the inference is thinner than the wording. If the value-side can be objectivated, the question is what it was before: a correlate that is not yet an object, given in an act that does not posit being. The paragraph names this a matter of "somewhat difficult relations" and breaks off with "however that may be" (*Doch wie immer*). The distinction rests on a future tense: "we shall still have to carry out" (*wir werden noch ausführen müssen*). It is asserted, not yet shown.

What the paragraph does show is why the axiology needs the distinction now. The sentence after the break draws a sphere-connecting law from the "difficult relations": whoever is surely convinced that A is cannot will A. The law presupposes that belief and will have different correlates. If the will were directed only upon the presented A, the conviction that A is would be no obstacle; it is an obstacle because the will's correlate is something to be made to be, and that conflicts with what is already posited as being. The preceding paragraph had listed sphere-connecting motivations within the intellective domain (certainty excludes doubt, surmise and question); this one carries them across into willing, and the next (Hua XXVIII 73) into joy, grief, hope and fear under a presupposed valuing. Each of these laws joins two acts that keep their own direction. The closing sentence fixes the status of the "cannot": not a psychological inability but "an apriori of reason", as §4 had insisted against psychologism.

The promise is not carried out in a passage of its own. Section III turns to the will instead, and the distinction returns there in the will's terms. In §15 the will's thesis implies belief in future being and yet "is not directed upon it, but precisely upon the willed process" (*Die Willensthesis ist nicht darauf gerichtet, sondern eben auf den gewollten Vorgang*, Hua XXVIII 109): the same refusal to let a founding belief absorb the founded act's direction. In §11(a) the general valuing consciousness, "taken intellectually, i.e. objectivatingly" (*intellektuell d. i. objektivierend gefaßt*), has the givenness of a general value, and Husserl calls this "something wholly new" (Hua XXVIII 86). That is the objectivation of the value-side, which the universal value-laws presuppose. Beilage I goes furthest: it sorts the theses into three classes with three correlates, "the existent, the good (value), the ought-to-be (the practically good)" (*Seiendes, Gutes (Wert), Seinsollendes (praktisch Gutes)*, Hua XXVIII 157; row 25). Value there is the correlate of a thesis of its own kind, as this paragraph required.

The promise was older than 1914. The 1908/09 course had asked whether emotive acts contain "meaning and possibly appearance … not merely through the founding acts but as emotive acts", and answered with a "must": "Somehow the question must admit of an affirmative answer." In valuing "value-objects appear, and indeed not merely the objects that have value, but the values as such" (*nicht bloß die Objekte, die Wert haben, sondern die Werte als solche*, Hua XXVIII 323). The 1914 "value-appearance" takes up this word. Between the two courses, *Ideen I* found terms for the correlate: "The value-objectivity implies its thing; it brings in, as a new objective stratum, valueness" (*Die Wertobjektität impliziert ihre Sache, sie bringt als neue objektive Schicht herein die Wertheit*, §95, Hua III/1 221). There "objective" is extended to the value-stratum, and no objectivating act is needed to make it so. The 1914 paragraph keeps the narrower word: "objective" belongs to what is or is not, and the value-side is objectivated only when judgment takes it up. The course therefore holds the value-correlate in a place its own vocabulary has no name for, and §9 marks that place with a future tense.

## §10, Hua XXVIII 76: the axiological "and"

Reads [§10, paragraph 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#value-laws-as-the-objective-expression-of-the-motivation-laws-the-formal-axiological-laws-of-consequence).

A value-inference has two premisses of different kinds: a valuing (W is a value) and a judgment (if A, then W). What joins them is not the "and" of logic. A logical inference joins two judgments into one theoretical content; here a valuing and a judging, directed upon different correlates (row 8), would give "no unity" if simply set side by side. Husserl's name for what unites them is "bringing into axiological consideration" (*in axiologische Erwägung ziehen*). The judgment stays a judgment, but it takes on a new function: it motivates feeling instead of further belief. The autonomy of the value-inference rests on this change of function, and so does §10's claim that value-laws are laws of their own and not applications of logic to a subject matter.

> One must well distinguish this axiological "and" from the merely logical one that unites the premisses of an intellectual inference. If we go back to the consciousness on either side in inferring, in carrying out consequence, then in the theoretical consciousness of inference there stands before our eyes, in the together of the premisses, a unified theoretical whole. In the case of valuing, however, there is present on the one hand a valuing of W and on the other the judging: if A holds, W holds. That by itself gives as yet no unity. An intellectual situation of unity would be present if we united intellectually the judgment "W is a value" and the judgment "If A holds, W holds"; but we make no intellectual inference at all. The unity between the act of valuing and the judging comes about through what we express as "bringing into axiological consideration". We value W, and we bring into consideration that W would be if A were. This "consideration" is no theoretical act. We bring into axiological consideration, and that is precisely this: the theoretical conviction gains an emotive function and becomes emotion-motivating, not theoretically motivating. The inference therefore runs: if B is a value, then in consideration of the situation "if A were, B would be", A too is a value. Instead of "in consideration" we also say "with regard to this", or simply: if B is a value "and" if there is added that if A were, B would be. It is therefore by no means a simple "and", and no logical "with regard to", "in consideration that". This is to be taken into account in the same way in all similar cases; so with disjunctives.

German:

> Man muß dieses axiologische „und“ wohl unterscheiden von dem bloß logischen, das die Prämissen eines intellektuellen Schlusses vereint. Gehen wir auf das beiderseitige Bewußtsein im Schließen, im Vollzug der Konsequenz zurück, so steht uns beim theoretischen Schlußbewußtsein im Zusammen der Prämisse ein vereinigter theoretischer Bestand vor Augen. Im Fall des Wertens aber liegt vor einmal ein Werten des W und andererseits das Urteilen: Wenn A gilt, gilt W. Das gibt in sich noch keine Einheit. Es liegt eine intellektuelle Einheitslage vor, wenn wir das Urteil „W ist ein Wert“ und wenn wir das Urteil „Wenn A gilt, gilt W“ intellektuell einigten; aber wir machen ja gar keinen intellektuellen Schluß. Die Einheit zwischen dem Akt des Wertens und dem Urteilen kommt zustande durch das, was wir ausdrücken als „in axiologische Erwägung ziehen“. Wir werten W und wir ziehen in Erwägung, daß W wäre, wenn A wäre. Diese „Erwägung“ ist kein theoretischer Akt. Wir ziehen in axiologische Erwägung, und das ist eben: Die theoretische Überzeugung gewinnt eine Gemütsfunktion und wird gemütsmotivierend, nicht theoretisch motivierend. Der Schluß also lautet: Ist B ein Wert, so ist in Erwägung der Sachlage, wenn A wäre, so wäre B, auch A ein Wert. Statt „in Erwägung“ sagen wir auch „in bezug darauf“ oder einfach: Wenn B ein Wert „und“ wenn dazukommt, daß wenn A wäre, B wäre. Es ist also keineswegs ein einfaches „und“ und kein logisches „in bezug auf“, „in Erwägung, daß“. Dies ist bei allen ähnlichen Fällen in gleicher Weise zu berücksichtigen; so bei Disjunktivis.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "im Zusammen der Prämisse" (in the together of the premiss), singular, where the sense requires the plural *Prämissen*; the rendering gives the plural. In the formula the variable changes from W to B ("Ist B ein Wert …"), though the law stated in the paragraph before, and the first half of this one, use W. Whether the change is Husserl's, the editor's or the conversion's cannot be settled from the file; the reading treats B and W as the same term.

The paragraph corrects its own predecessor. The objective law had just been stated with the premisses "taken together … through the form of the 'together', the 'and'" (*durch die Form des „Zusammen“ eben, des „und“*, Hua XXVIII 76). The quotation marks there already held the words at a distance, and this paragraph says why. The "and" written in the formula is a logical form; what it expresses is not a logical uniting. That is §8's division of labour applied to a single word (row 7). Logical reason formulates the value-inference, and its connective appears in the formulation, but the unity it states was made elsewhere.

Husserl's argument proceeds by subtraction. On the theoretical side, "in the together of the premisses" there stands "a unified theoretical whole" (*ein vereinigter theoretischer Bestand*). On the valuing side there is "on the one hand" (*einmal*) a valuing and "on the other" (*andererseits*) a judging, and the paired adverbs keep them apart in the syntax as in the acts. "That by itself gives as yet no unity" (*Das gibt in sich noch keine Einheit*): the "as yet" (*noch*) announces that unity will come, but from somewhere other than juxtaposition. The next sentence names the alternative that would make the inference logical, and puts it in the subjunctive: an intellectual unity "would be present if we united intellectually" the judgment "W is a value" and the hypothetical judgment. Here "W is a value" is the objectivated value-side of row 8, a judgment about value and no longer a valuing. That inference is possible, and it would be a piece of logic applied to value-predicates. Husserl rules it out as a description, not by argument: "but we make no intellectual inference at all" (*aber wir machen ja gar keinen intellektuellen Schluß*). The particle *ja* appeals to what anyone finds who carries out the valuing inference. That is the paragraph's weakest point. The claim is phenomenological, and someone who reports that he does infer intellectually from "W is a value" is not refuted by it, only told that he has done something else.

The positive account is a phrase in quotation marks, "bringing into axiological consideration", and the paragraph glosses it twice. Negatively: "This 'consideration' is no theoretical act." Positively: "the theoretical conviction gains an emotive function" (*gewinnt eine Gemütsfunktion*). The verb is the centre of the paragraph. The conviction is not replaced by a feeling, nor joined to one by a third act; it "gains" a function while remaining a conviction. The second gloss spells the function out as a direction of motivation: the conviction becomes "emotion-motivating, not theoretically motivating" (*gemütsmotivierend, nicht theoretisch motivierend*). This is the language of §9, where every law of consequence was a law of motivation. The axiological unity is a unity of motivation, in which a judgment counts as a premiss because it moves a valuing.

The last sentences return to wording and refuse every candidate. "In consideration" can be replaced by "with regard to this" (*in bezug darauf*), or "simply" by an "and" in quotation marks, followed by "if there is added" (*wenn dazukommt*). Then: "It is therefore by no means a simple 'and', and no logical 'with regard to', 'in consideration that'." The phrases language offers are all logical in form, and none of them is logical in function. The objective law in the paragraph before had already used one of them: "then with regard to this A too is a value" (*so ist mit Rücksicht darauf auch A ein Wertes*). So the "objective expression" that §10's title promises is not free of the act. It keeps a connective whose sense lies in what a valuing subject does with a conviction. Husserl does not hide this; he states that it holds "in all similar cases", disjunctives included, and so for the whole system of value-inferences.

The phrase that names the unity becomes one of the course's working words, and its sense widens. In the next pages it enters the norms for valuing inference: "If one has already held a W to be a value and brings such and such existential connections into consideration (*in Erwägung*), then one must consistently continue to value thus and so" (Hua XXVIII 79). In §17(c) "consideration" becomes the weighing of grounds: when the motivations are laid out, "the deliberation takes the form of a 'consideration'" (*die Überlegung nimmt die Form einer „Erwägung“ an*), and the weights (*Gewichte*) of the possibilities show (Hua XXVIII 121). The quotation marks recur, and the etymology of *Erwägung* in *wägen*, to weigh, now does work that §10 did not ask of it. On the will's side there is "value-consideration" (*Werterwägung*, Hua XXVIII 122), and §18(b) defines its terms over what is considered: a "considered action" (*erwogene Handlung*), or a state of affairs "standing under consideration" (*in Erwägung stehend*), is "demanded" when the will directed upon it would be convenient (Hua XXVIII 135; row 19). Beilage II's practical realm is bounded by the same word: "for my practical consideration only those goods and evils come into question for whose attainability something speaks" (Hua XXVIII 159; row 26). What began as the device that joins one judgment to one valuing becomes the field within which the will chooses.

The connective has a history in the *Logical Investigations*. There Husserl had defended the logical "and" against the "essential error" of logicians who supposed that conjunction rests on "a mere being-together-in-consciousness of the nominal and propositional acts" (*ein bloßes Zusammenbewußtsein der nominalen und propositionalen Akte*). The "and" is a categorial form, given only in "the new act of conjoining (colligating)" (*den neuen Akt des Konjungierens (Kolligierens)*), founded on the acts it unites (LU VI §51, Hua XIX/2 689). The 1914 paragraph has the same two rivals in view: mere juxtaposition ("no unity") and a founded act of synthesis (the intellectual uniting). It takes neither. The axiological "and" is not a new act that constitutes a new object, as colligation constitutes the collective; it is a change of function in an act already there. That is how the parallelism of the course can hold without logical form constituting the value-inference. The price is that the unity of the value-inference has no correlate of its own beside the values it relates. It exists in the motivation, and the formula can only quote it.

## §10, Hua XXVIII 79–80: consequence without objectivity

Reads [§10, paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#value-laws-as-the-objective-expression-of-the-motivation-laws-the-formal-axiological-laws-of-consequence).

The laws of consequence govern how values follow from values. They do not decide whether the first values are objective. A value can be "first" in the order of grounding, underived and valued for its own sake, and still be valued today and its opposite tomorrow, by one person and its opposite by another. Husserl lets a skeptic say this, and the skeptic is a new figure: he accepts the whole consequence-axiology and is refuted by none of it. Being first and being objective, both of which German can say with "in itself" (*in sich*), come apart here, and §11 is written to bring them back together.

> But it must at once be added that one can doubt whether with the necessity of assuming non-derived values the question of the unconditioned objectivity of such values is already decided. Whoever has let himself be convinced by us that consequence in valuing is not merely an empirical peculiarity of the valuing-experiences, but that it stands, just like consequence in judging, under apriori norms to which there correspond apriori laws for the ideal states of value, whoever, I say, has let himself be convinced of this can perhaps still try to defend the following standpoint. If I value A, he may say, then under the given motivation-situations I have to value B, C … in descending direction; reason in consequence demands this, the formal laws of consequence demand it; and certainly I must in doing so, if A is not already valuable in itself but only derivatively, come in the end to a first A that is valuable in itself. But that does not say that A is valuable in itself in the sense of genuine objectivity, of independence from the contingently valuing subject and its contingent mood. There are, precisely, in each case unmotivated valuings, and there must be such; but one time I value A unmotivated and the other time non-A; and just so the one values A and the other non-A. Someone could reject skepticism in the theoretical respect and yet be a skeptic in the axiological ⟨respect⟩, and that although he may acknowledge the consequence-axiology. And that I should have to behave consistently also in the sense that I would have to value, and ought to value, the same A again and again for all eternity, that, he would say, no reason prescribes. What matters here is that the axioms so far do not exclude axiological skepticism. With the clarification of axiological reason in the form of axiological consequence we are not, however, at an end. At any rate this is a first and important field. We shall see how we get further.

German:

> Es muß aber gleich hinzugefügt, daß man zweifeln kann, ob mit der Notwendigkeit der Annahme nicht-derivierter Werte auch schon die Frage der unbedingten Objektivität solcher Werte entschieden ist. Wer sich durch uns hat überzeugen lassen, daß Konsequenz im Werten nicht nur eine empirische Eigentümlichkeit der Wertungserlebnisse ist, sondern daß sie genau so wie die Konsequenz im Urteilen unter apriorischen Normen steht, denen apriorische Gesetze für die idealen Wertverhalte entsprechen, wer, sage ich, davon sich hat überzeugen lassen, kann vielleicht noch folgenden Standpunkt zu vertreten versuchen: Wenn ich, mag er sagen, A werte, dann habe ich unter den gegebenen Motivationslagen in absteigender Richtung B C ... zu werten, das fordert die Vernunft in der Konsequenz, das fordern die formalen Gesetze der Konsequenz; und gewiß muß ich dabei, wenn A nicht schon in sich selbst wert ist, sondern nur abgeleitet, schließlich auf ein erstes, in sich wertes A kommen. Aber damit ist nicht gesagt, daß A in sich wert sei im Sinne der echten Objektivität, der Unabhängigkeit vom zufällig wertenden Subjekt und seiner zufälligen Stimmung. Es gibt eben jeweils unmotivierte Wertungen und es muß solche geben; aber einmal werte ich unmotiviert A und das andere Mal non-A; und ebenso wertet der eine A und der andere non-A. Es könnte einer in theoretischer Beziehung den Skeptizismus ablehnen und doch in der axiologischen ⟨Beziehung⟩ Skeptiker sein, und das, trotzdem er die Konsequenzaxiologie anerkennen mag. Und daß ich mich auch in dem Sinn konsequent verhalten müßte, daß ich immer wieder dasselbe A in alle Ewigkeit werten müßte und werten sollte, das, würde er sagen, schreibe keine Vernunft vor. Worauf es hier ankommt, ist, daß die bisherigen Axiome den axiologischen Skeptizismus nicht ausschließen. Mit der Klarstellung der axiologischen Vernunft in Form der axiologischen Konsequenz sind wir aber auch nicht zu Ende. Jedenfalls ist das ein erstes und wichtiges Gebiet. Wir werden schon sehen, wie wir weiterkommen.

*Note on the text.* The first sentence lacks its auxiliary in the file ("Es muß aber gleich hinzugefügt", for *hinzugefügt werden*); the rendering supplies it. The word *Konsequenz* is split across the page break to Hua XXVIII 80 ("Konse- … quenz"), with a comment of margin line numbers between the halves; the quotation closes the word. The editor's angle brackets around *Beziehung* are given as ⟨…⟩.

The skeptic's foothold is supplied by the law stated just before. Every motivation-connection is finite, so value-grounding must come to "first value-grounds that are no longer, so to speak, 'inferred' values" (*nicht mehr sozusagen „erschlossene“ Werte*, Hua XXVIII 79). The law was meant to secure the system of derivations against regress. The skeptic draws a different consequence from it: "There are, precisely, in each case unmotivated valuings, and there must be such" (*und es muß solche geben*). The "must" is Husserl's own, borrowed from the finitude law. What stops the regress is a valuing that nothing motivates, and of such a valuing the laws of motivation can say nothing. Their whole jurisdiction is the step from a valuing already made to the next one.

The argument is carried by an equivocation in "in itself", and the skeptic names it. The first occurrence is the finitude law's: "a first A that is valuable in itself" (*ein erstes, in sich wertes A*) is simply an A that is not "only derivatively" valuable. The adjectives "first" and "in itself" say the same thing, a position in the order of grounding. The second occurrence adds a qualification that the first lacked: "valuable in itself in the sense of genuine objectivity" (*im Sinne der echten Objektivität*), glossed as "independence from the contingently valuing subject and its contingent mood" (*seiner zufälligen Stimmung*). "Mood" is the skeptic's word for what the finitude law leaves at the root. The word "genuine" (*echten*) concedes that the first sense of "in itself" was a sense of objectivity too, but not the one that matters.

A second word divides in the same way. The skeptic accepts that "reason in consequence demands" (*das fordert die Vernunft in der Konsequenz*) the descending series B, C … once A is valued. He denies that he must behave "consistently" (*konsequent*) "also in the sense" that he would have to value "the same A again and again for all eternity" (*immer wieder dasselbe A in alle Ewigkeit*). *Konsequenz* means inferential coherence, the following of consequent from ground; the skeptic refuses to let it mean constancy over time. The repeated "and the other time non-A", "the one … and the other non-A", extends the refusal from one person's moments to several persons. The theoretical parallel shows what is missing. No one thinks that consequence in judging guarantees the truth of the premisses; the premisses' truth is a matter of truth, not of consequence. Axiology has so far only consequence. It has not yet stated its analogue of truth.

The skeptic is given his position in reported speech, and the mood of the verbs keeps Husserl at a distance from it: "he may say" (*mag er sagen*), "that, he would say, no reason prescribes" (*schreibe keine Vernunft vor*, subjunctive). But Husserl does not answer him. He concedes the point in his own voice: "the axioms so far do not exclude axiological skepticism." This is a different skeptic from the one §4 refuted. §4's skeptic, who denied practical rules, contradicted himself in the form of his own demand (row 2). This skeptic accepts every form; "he may acknowledge the consequence-axiology". The countersense argument cannot reach him, because he has made no formal claim for it to cancel. He also marks where the parallel between the reasons is weakest: "someone could reject skepticism in the theoretical respect and yet be a skeptic in the axiological ⟨respect⟩". The parallelism of §1 claimed that the two reasons stand or fall together, and this sentence allows that they can come apart in a single mind.

The answer comes in §11, and its wording shows how directly it addresses this passage. §11(b) defines "being-valuable-in-itself" (*In-sich-wert-Sein*) as before: value-being "not derived from another value-being", so that no values are presupposed (Hua XXVIII 87). That is the skeptic's first sense, the order of grounding. The step to objectivity is taken by an axiom: for every matter, "one of the three cases is true", positive value in itself, negative value in itself, or value-freedom, the law of excluded fourth, which then "transfers itself to the derived values, which thereby become objective values" (Hua XXVIII 88; row 12). The axiom closes the gap that the skeptic's "one time A, the other time non-A" exploited, because it lets only one of the cases be true of a given matter. But it closes it by stipulation. What follows is stated under a condition: "what stands firm, if the axioms are correct" (*wenn die Axiome richtig sind*) is that value does not dissolve into the subjectivity of valuing, and that "as little as there is a true and false for someone", so little is there a good and bad for someone (Hua XXVIII 89). The defence of the axioms is then declined in so many words. Objectivism "must be fought through" against relativist, psychologist, anthropologist and biologist skepticism, but "that is a chapter of its own. We will not go into it and will treat the fight as one that is decided" (*den Kampf wie einen entschieden behandeln*, Hua XXVIII 89).

So the skeptic of Hua XXVIII 80 is never refuted in the course; he is excluded by an axiom whose ground is left for another occasion. The promise in the passage's last sentence, "we shall see how we get further", is kept in the sense that the course goes further, not in the sense that it answers him. His point also returns in another form at the end of the course. §21 asks how far practical possibilities and the "convenience" of a will are relative to the subject, and finds there a role for subjectivity that has "no analogy" in the sphere of judgment (Hua XXVIII 149, a sentence that breaks off in the file; row 22). The skeptic's "contingent mood" is not readmitted there, but the clean separation of "for someone" from "in itself" that §11 claimed for values does not hold without remainder for the will.

## §11(a), Hua XXVIII 86: bad is not not-good

Reads [§11(a), paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#a-the-reference-to-the-motivation-situation-in-the-analogue-of-the-law-of-contradiction-in-the-emotive-sphere-value-freedom-and-the-appertaining-axioms).

"Good" and "bad" are contraries, not contradictories. Logic excludes "good" and "not-good" of the same thing, as it excludes any predicate and its negation; but "not-good" covers the bad and also the value-free, so logic says nothing about whether good and bad exclude each other. That they do, under the same presuppositions, is an axiological law. The axiological law of contradiction therefore cannot be derived from the logical one, and the formal axiology is a discipline of its own and not a chapter of logic applied to value-words. The argument turns on the difference between two German forms, *schlecht* or *übel* (bad, evil) and *nicht-gut* (not-good), and on a third, *ungut* (un-good), which ordinary speech uses for the first while its form suggests the second.

> With regard to our laws one must beware of taking them for analytic-logical self-evidences. For example, one could think that an A's being good under certain presuppositions and its being not good under the same presuppositions is analytically-logically self-evidently ⟨impossible⟩. To this, however, two things are to be replied. First: when in our axioms there is talk of positive and negative value, of good and bad, this is by no means the same as if the talk were of good and not-good. In ordinary speech, to be sure, "un-good" and "not-good" are also used for "evil", for "bad". But that is an equivocal distinction. Analytically-logically, predicates such as "good" and "not-good" exclude each other in the same sense as ⟨a⟩ and non-a in general as arbitrary predicates, that is, as the ascribing of a (of "good") and the denying of "good". But analytically-logically "evil" can as little be slipped under "not-good" as "black" under "not-white" ⟨in place of⟩ that which has some other visual quality.

German:

> In Bezug auf unsere Gesetze muß man sich hüten, sie für analytisch-logische Selbstverständlichkeiten zu halten. Zum Beispiel könnte man meinen, daß ein A unter gewissen Voraussetzungen gut sei und daß es unter denselben Voraussetzungen nicht gut sei, das sei analytisch logisch selbstverständlich ⟨unmöglich⟩. Darauf aber ist zweierlei zu entgegnen. Fürs erste: Wenn in unseren Axiomen von positivem und negativem Wert, von gut und schlecht die Rede ist, so ist das keineswegs dasselbe, als ob die Rede sei von gut und nicht-gut. In gewöhnlicher Rede wird allerdings „ungut“ und „nicht-gut“ auch für „übel“, für „schlecht“ gebraucht. Aber das ist eine äquivoke Unterscheidung. Analytisch-logisch schließen sich Prädikate wie „gut“ und „nicht-gut“ in demselben Sinn aus wie und non-a überhaupt als beliebige Prädikate, das heißt, wie Zusprechen des a (des „gut“) und Absprechen des „gut“. Aber analytisch-logisch kann ebensowenig dem „nicht-gut“ das „übel“ unterschoben werden wie dem „nicht-weiß“ das „schwarz“ ⟨statt⟩ das irgendeine andere visuelle Qualität Habende.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "wie und non-a", with the first term missing; the sense and the next clause ("Zusprechen des a") require "wie a und non-a", and the rendering supplies ⟨a⟩. The two words in angle brackets, ⟨unmöglich⟩ and ⟨statt⟩, are the editor's additions, given as LaTeX in the file. The last clause is compressed even with ⟨statt⟩: "black" cannot be put under "not-white" in place of "whatever has some other visual quality", which is what "not-white" means.

The opponent's sentence is built to look like a contradiction. "An A's being good under certain presuppositions and its being not good under the same presuppositions" pairs "good" (*gut*) with "not good" (*nicht gut*), and the pair is impossible "analytically-logically". Husserl grants this and denies that it is the axiom. The axioms speak "of positive and negative value, of good and bad" (*von gut und schlecht*), and the phrase "by no means the same" (*keineswegs dasselbe*) marks the whole difference between the two disciplines. If the axiom were the exclusion of good and not-good, it would be the law of contradiction with "good" put in for "a", and §11's title, "the law of contradiction in the axiological sphere", would name an application of logic. Because the axiom excludes good and bad, it states a relation between two determinate value-qualities, which logic, abstracting from all matter, cannot see.

The sentence on ordinary speech says where the confusion comes from. "Un-good" (*ungut*) and "not-good" are "also" (*auch*) used for "evil" and "bad". The German prefix *un-* is the hinge. In form it negates, as "not" does; in use it often names the opposite quality, as *unschön* names the ugly and not merely the not-beautiful. A language that says *ungut* for *übel* has folded a contrary into the shape of a contradictory. Husserl calls this "an equivocal distinction" (*eine äquivoke Unterscheidung*), an odd but exact phrase: the distinction between "good" and its opposite is drawn with a word that means two things. English is in the same position. "Not good" in ordinary use often means "bad", so a rendering that says "not good" where Husserl writes *schlecht* would reproduce the equivocation he is exposing. That is why the original stays beside the English here.

The comparison with colour makes the logical point and strains the axiological one. "Not-white" is satisfied by anything that has "some other visual quality", and "black" is one such quality among many; it cannot be "slipped under" (*unterschoben*) "not-white" as if it were what "not-white" means. The verb *unterschieben* has a sense of substitution by sleight, and it charges the logicist reading with a substitution of that kind. The analogy shows that "evil" is narrower than "not-good". But it does not show the structure that the axioms need. Colours form a field with many species, so that black is one of indefinitely many non-whites. The value-field, as the surrounding axioms describe it, has exactly three places: positive, negative and value-free. "Not-good" covers just two of them, the bad and the *Adiaphoron* (the value-indifferent). The colour comparison is evidence that the contrary is not the contradictory, and no more than that. The polarity of value, which makes "bad" the opposite of "good" rather than one of its many others, is the thing the axiology asserts, and the comparison neither shows nor needs it.

The paragraph also answers a looseness in the section's own earlier wording. At its opening §11(a) had stated the correlate of the motivation-law by saying that "'contradictorily' opposed values" exclude each other in the same content, "for example that … it be joyful and be unjoyful" (*daß es erfreulich sei und unerfreulich sei*, Hua XXVIII 81). The quotation marks around *kontradiktorisch* already held the logical term at a distance, and the example used the prefix *un-* in exactly the sense this paragraph calls equivocal: *unerfreulich* there means grief-worthy, not merely not-joyful. Just before the passage, Husserl had said that the axiology needs "its own concept 'value'" (*einen eigenen Begriff „Wert“*), as what is general to positive and negative value, and had stated an axiom in which the contradictory of "value" in general is the value-free: "If A is a value, then it is not not a value, i.e. it is no Adiaphoron" (*so ist es nicht nicht ein Wert gleich so ist es kein Adiaphoron*, Hua XXVIII 85). The logical "not" thus has a place in the axiology, but at the level of value in general, where it separates the valuable from the indifferent. It has no place between good and bad. The passage under reading draws that line in the vocabulary.

Its second point (the same paragraph's "Second", which follows the quotation) says why the line matters for the whole discipline. Logical laws transfer to value-predicates, "and first to the general predicate 'value'", only on the presupposition of axiological laws, those that establish "that value can be treated as a predicate" at all (Hua XXVIII 86). The direction of dependence is the reverse of the logicist's. Logic does not ground the axiology by lending it the law of contradiction; the axiology must first make value an objective predicate before logic has anything to work on. The next subsection supplies the law that does this. Because there are three places, not two, the axiological counterpart of excluded middle becomes "a law of excluded fourth" (Hua XXVIII 88; row 12), and the name records what this paragraph established: the value-field cannot be cut in two by a "not".

The prefix returns on the will's side, with the same warning. In §17(d) Husserl analyses will-negation, the "I will not" that crosses out a soliciting but uncompleted "I will", and observes that "we sometimes designate this negativity with the ⟨expression⟩ 'to be unwilling'" (*„unwillig sein“*), "but the ⟨expression⟩ is ambiguous (*zweideutig*), since *Unwille* and 'being unwilling' very often designate the phenomenon of indignation" (Hua XXVIII 125; row 17). *Unwille* is to the will what *ungut* is to value: a word whose prefix promises a negation and whose use names an opposed quality, here an emotion of rejection. In both places the course separates the "not" of grammar from the opposition in the sphere it is describing, and in both it finds ordinary German blurring the two with the same prefix. The new formal disciplines are built on that separation.

## §11(b), Hua XXVIII 87–88: the law of excluded fourth

Reads [§11(b), paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-law-of-excluded-fourth-for-the-basic-values-the-objectivity-of-values).

For any matter, within a given region of values, exactly one of three cases holds: it is in itself positively valuable, in itself negatively valuable, or in itself value-free. This is the law of excluded fourth (*Gesetz vom ausgeschlossenen Vierten*). It is the axiology's counterpart of the logical law of excluded middle, which German calls the law of excluded *third* (*Satz vom ausgeschlossenen Dritten*), and its name counts the cases: where logic has two and excludes a third, the value-sphere has three and excludes a fourth. The law does more than classify. It makes every valuing answerable to a fact about its matter, so that a valuing is valid or invalid as a judgment is true or false. With it the derived values of §10 cease to be values merely "if one lets A stand" and become objective. The axiom is the course's answer to the skeptic who accepted consequence and denied objectivity (row 10).

> We have already said earlier that all value-derivation leads to last basic-values that are not themselves derived. But now it is not merely the case that, once the valuing of an A is factually carried out, it is rational in consequence of it to value B, and that B is therefore relatively valuable if one presupposes A and lets it stand. Rather it is to be claimed as an evidence that one can express this first objectively thus: "If A is a value, then B is a value"; and further that one can say: "Every value-presupposition is to be decided objectively as to validity and non-validity." To this, however, there belongs first that, if M is the matter of the value-positing A, it can be said *apriori*: if M is the matter of a positive value-positing, then, if this positing is valid, the corresponding negative positing or adiaphoric positing is invalid, and conversely, according to all disjunctive combinations. Precisely this we have stated as a law, and indeed as valid for any matter whatever.
>
> Parallel to this we set up the axiom: if M is any matter whatever, then (and always within any axiological region whatever) one of the three cases is true: either M is the matter of a positive value in itself, or of a negative one, or it is in itself value-free. So for the sphere of in-itself-values and value-freedoms we have here an analogue of the law of contradiction and of excluded third, only that the latter is here a law of excluded fourth, which then, as we shall hear presently, transfers itself to the derived values, which thereby become objective values.

*Note on the text.* A page break (Hua XXVIII 88) falls between the two paragraphs, marked in the file by a bare "88" comment followed by an unnumbered running head. Neither paragraph is damaged.

The first paragraph moves from fact to claim. Its opening sentence restates the finitude law, and its second describes the position the skeptic of §10 was happy to hold: once a valuing of A "is factually carried out" (*nun einmal faktisch vollzogen ist*), valuing B is rational "in consequence", and B is "relatively" valuable "if one presupposes A and lets it stand" (*wenn man das A eben voraussetzt und gelten läßt*). The phrase "lets it stand" names a permission, not a validity. The word that turns the paragraph is "Rather" (*Vielmehr*), and what follows it is not an argument but a claim staked: "it is to be claimed as an evidence" (*als eine Evidenz in Anspruch zu nehmen*). *In Anspruch nehmen* is to lay claim to something as one's due. Husserl does not show that value-presuppositions can be decided; he asserts that their decidability is self-evident. The claim is then given two formulations, both in quotation marks, as sentences one "can say". The first converts the subject's consequence into an objective hypothetical ("If A is a value, then B is a value"). The second is the stronger: "Every value-presupposition is to be decided objectively as to validity and non-validity." The skeptic of §10 allowed the first and denied the second.

The second formulation needs a law about what is decided, and that is where the count of cases enters. If M is the matter of a positive valuing, and that valuing is valid, "the corresponding negative positing or adiaphoric positing is invalid" (*die entsprechende negative Setzung oder adiaphorische Setzung ungültig*). There are three positings, not two, because the *Adiaphoron* (the value-indifferent) is itself a position taken on the matter, not the absence of one. §11(a) had said so: indifference too is "objectively decided by valuing behaviour and value-consideration" (Hua XXVIII 85). The phrase "according to all disjunctive combinations" (*nach allen disjunktiven Kombinationen*) extends the exclusion to every pair of the three. That is the analogue of the law of contradiction, and it is stated as already proved: "Precisely this we have stated as a law" (*Eben das haben wir gesetzmäßig ausgesprochen*).

The axiom is introduced by a different verb. The law of contradiction was "stated"; the excluded fourth is "set up" (*stellen wir das Axiom auf*). It says that "one of the three cases is true" (*einer der drei Fälle wahr*), and this is the step from exclusion to completeness. Exclusion says that no two cases hold together; completeness says that one always holds. The skeptic's "one time A, the other time non-A" (Hua XXVIII 80) is ruled out only by the second, since he could grant that no single valuing is both positive and negative and still deny that the matter decides between them. The word "true" is the logician's, and Husserl uses it here without quotation marks, because the axiom is a judgment about values and its truth is doxic. Only a page before he had written "axiological validity or 'truth'" with the second word in quotation marks (*einer axiologischen Geltung oder „Wahrheit“*, Hua XXVIII 85), and a footnote to this subsection regrets that "we have here unfortunately no words that correspond exactly to the words truth and falsity" (*Wir haben hier leider keine Worte, die genau den Worten Wahrheit und Falschheit entsprechen*). The value-sphere has validity (*Geltung*) and non-validity; truth belongs to what is said about it.

The axiom has two qualifications, and both fix what objectivity can mean here. It holds "always within any axiological region" (*immer innerhalb einer beliebigen axiologischen Region*). §11(a) had illustrated value-freedom one category at a time: a content may or may not "come into question" as a content of wishing, may or may not concern "the idea of beauty" (Hua XXVIII 85). So "value-free" is not absolute. It is the third value of a determinate category, and the axiom is a law for each category, not for value as such. The second qualification is "in itself" (*in sich*). The paragraph before defined it: value-being "not derived from another value-being", hanging, if on presuppositions at all, only on presuppositions that are "not axiological", such as "a being or non-being" (Hua XXVIII 87). That was the first of the two senses of "in itself" that the skeptic separated (row 10): position in the order of grounding. The axiom joins it to the second, independence from the valuer, by fiat. The basic values are the values that are underived, and of these the axiom says that one case is true.

The name of the law records all this. The logical *Satz vom ausgeschlossenen Dritten* gets its name from what it excludes: between "it is so" and "it is not so" there is no third (Hua XXVIII 82 had stated it in just those terms). Husserl keeps the construction and changes the number. The new law does not exclude a middle between two poles. The Adiaphoron is the middle, and it is admitted as a case. What is excluded is a fourth possibility, a matter that is neither valuable, nor disvaluable, nor indifferent in a given respect, a matter about which there would be no fact for valuing to be right about. The count is deliberate. Where there are two cases, Husserl keeps the old name: in the 1911 course the correlates of realizing will and of omission stand under a law "analogous to the law of contradiction and of excluded third of logic" (*analog ist dem Satz vom Widerspruch und vom ausgeschlossenen Dritten der Logik*, Hua XXVIII 215), since one either realizes or omits. The 1914 coinage marks the place where the value-sphere, unlike the will and unlike belief, has a neutral case of its own (row 11).

The paragraph's last clause announces the payoff: the law "transfers itself to the derived values, which thereby become objective values". The next paragraph draws it: every derived value gains "its objectivity and its not merely hypothetical validity", and value-consequences hold "objectively, in itself, independently of subjectivity" (Hua XXVIII 88). Then the condition already noted under row 10 is added: this stands firm "if the axioms are correct" (Hua XXVIII 89), and the fight against relativism is treated "as one that is decided". The excluded fourth is therefore the course's point of maximal commitment. It is what turns consequence into objectivity, and it is set up, as an axiom, and claimed as evident, rather than derived. Everything that follows in the formal axiology and the *Praktik*, down to the "objectively demanded" of §18 (row 19), presupposes that for every matter in every region there is a fact of the matter about its value.

# Husserl, *Vorlesungen über Ethik und Wertlehre 1908–1914* (Hua XXVIII), Part A: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the block of the [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md) that it extends. They cover Part A of that commentary only: the 1914 course *Grundfragen zur Ethik und Wertlehre*, §§1–21, with Beilagen I and II (Hua XXVIII 3–162). The German is quoted from the Husserliana edition (Hua XXVIII, ed. Ullrich Melle) in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-1914-lectures.md`). The commentary cites § numbers only. Headings here add the Hua XXVIII page, taken from the running-head comments in the file. Many even pages carry a running head without a number, so their numbers are counted from the neighbours, and a page can be off by one.

All English renderings of Husserl are working renderings. They stay close to the German so that its structure shows, and they are not taken from a published translation. Where the file gives the editor's angle brackets as LaTeX markup, the quotations print them as ⟨…⟩.

The file needs four cautions that affect more than one reading. First, the conversion often reads *Vermeinen* (meaning, intending: Husserl's word for the position-taking of an act) as *Verneinen* (negating). Genuine *Verneinung* (negation) also occurs, above all in §17(d), so each case is decided from the sense, and the reading says which it takes. Second, there is scattered OCR damage elsewhere: *Gefahren* (dangers) for *Gefallen* (liking), *Mittelwohnung* for *Mittelwollung* (willing of the means), "kategorialen Imperativ" (categorial imperative) for *kategorischen* (categorical), and margin line numbers spliced into the text. A misspelt word is corrected silently only where nothing turns on it; otherwise the quotation gives what the file has and a note says so. Third, two page markers are wrong: the one that reads "p. 154" stands where p. 102 falls, and the one that reads "p. 111" where p. 126 falls. The pages given here are the corrected ones. Fourth, italics survive, but sparsely, and spaced type does not survive at all, so no reading argues from the absence of emphasis. The file contains no editorial introduction, so nothing here relies on what the editor says about the 1914 redaction.

## §1, Hua XXVIII 4: the "must" of the parallel

Reads [§1, paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#logic-as-art-of-method-and-pure-logic).

The course's program is a claim of correspondence: to formal logic there *must* answer a formal *Praktik* (theory of practice) and a formal axiology (theory of values), and to each of these a phenomenology of the acts it governs. The "must" is not the conclusion of an argument. It is the form in which an analogy presents itself to someone who follows the parallel of the disciplines back to the parallel of the kinds of reason. The program thus rests on a demanded symmetry, and the course's real work, from §5 on, is to find out how much of the demand the will and the emotions can honour.

> If one now follows the parallels of logic and ethics, or rather the parallel of the kinds of act and reason to which these disciplines are essentially referred, judging reason on the one side and practical reason on the other, the thought presses itself upon us that to logic too, in the determinate and narrowly bounded sense of a formal logic, there must correspond as its parallel a *Praktik* that is formal in an analogous sense and likewise apriori. Something similar holds for the parallel with valuing reason, and indeed valuing in the widest sense and not merely aesthetic valuing. This leads to the idea of a formal axiology, intimately interwoven with formal *Praktik* for essential reasons, as an apriori formal discipline of values, or of value-contents and value-meanings. To delimit these ideas of new formal disciplines, which in the philosophical tradition never came to conception, and to realize them in pieces, that is the main theme of these lectures. So far as time remains, we shall then go into the great problem-groups of the phenomenology and critique of reason that orient themselves by these radical formal disciplines. Just as to formal logic there corresponds a system of fundamental structures of belief-consciousness (of doxic consciousness, as I am accustomed to say), and thus a phenomenology and theory of formal knowledge, so it is in a similar way with formal axiology and *Praktik* in respect of the discipline of phenomenology that belongs to them in principle, or of the theory of valuing and of willing (where these words are used in a sense analogous to the word "theory of knowledge"). Let us now go to the matters themselves!

German:

> Geht man nun den Parallelen von Logik und Ethik nach bzw. der Parallele der Akt- und Vernunftarten, auf welche diese Disziplinen wesentlich zurückbezogen sind, der urteilenden Vernunft auf der einen Seite, der praktischen Vernunft auf der anderen, so drängt sich der Gedanke auf, daß nun auch der Logik in dem bestimmt und eng begrenzten Sinn einer formalen Logik als Parallele entsprechen muß eine in analogem Sinn formale und ebenfalls apriorische Praktik. Ähnliches gilt für die Parallele mit der wertenden Vernunft, und zwar der wertenden im weitesten Sinn und nicht etwa der bloß ästhetisch wertenden. Das führt auf die Idee einer aus Wesensgründen mit der formalen Praktik innig verflochtenen formalen Axiologie als apriorischer formaler Disziplin von Werten bzw. Wertinhalten und Wertbedeutungen. Diese Ideen von neuen formalen Disziplinen, in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen, zu begrenzen und in Stücken wirklich zu realisieren, das ist das Hauptthema dieser Vorlesungen. Soweit Zeit übrig bleibt, soll dann auf die großen Problemgruppen der Phänomenologie und Kritik der Vernunft eingegangen werden, die sich nach diesen radikalen formalen Disziplinen orientieren. Wie der formalen Logik ein System fundamentaler Strukturen des Glaubensbewußtseins (des doxischen, wie ich zu sagen pflege) entspricht und somit eine Phänomenologie und Theorie der formalen Erkenntnis, so ähnlich verhält es sich mit der formalen Axiologie und Praktik hinsichtlich der ihnen prinzipiell zugehörigen Disziplin der Phänomenologie bzw. der Wertungs- und Willenstheorie (wobei diese Worte in analogem Sinn gebraucht sind wie das Wort „Erkenntnistheorie“). Gehen wir nun an die Sachen!

*Note on the text.* The relative clause "die … in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen" lacks its relative pronoun in the file ("Diese Ideen von neuen formalen Disziplinen, in der philosophischen Tradition nie zur Konzeption kamen"). The sense is not in doubt, and the rendering supplies "which".

The first sentence moves twice before it reaches its "must". It begins with the disciplines, logic and ethics, and then corrects itself with "or rather" (*bzw.*) to the kinds of act and reason to which the disciplines are "essentially referred back" (*wesentlich zurückbezogen*). The correction carries the program. A parallel of disciplines would be a fact of the curriculum; a parallel of kinds of reason is a claim about consciousness, and only that claim could ground a "must". Yet the sentence does not assert the second parallel either. It says that, for one who follows it, "the thought presses itself upon us" (*drängt sich der Gedanke auf*). The necessity sits inside a thought that imposes itself, not in a proof. And the word order delays the subject: "there must correspond as its parallel" comes before the thing that must correspond, "a *Praktik* formal in an analogous sense". The analogy is in place before its second term has been found.

Two qualifiers fix what the analogy is to deliver. The logic in question is formal logic "in the determinate and narrowly bounded sense", and the *Praktik* must be formal "in an analogous sense" and "likewise apriori". The course will test exactly these two words. If practical form turns out to be formal only in a looser sense than logical form, or apriori without being analytic, the parallel holds at a lower level than the "must" announces. The third sentence adds axiology with a looser joint ("something similar holds", *Ähnliches gilt*) and widens valuing beyond the aesthetic, so that the old triad of truth, goodness and beauty, with which §1 opens, gives way to a pair of reasons, theoretical and emotive, the second split into valuing and willing. The axiology is "intimately interwoven" with the *Praktik* "for essential reasons": the course will found willing on valuing (§§9–10), and the interweaving is announced before it is shown.

The claim of novelty is strong: these disciplines "never came to conception" in the tradition. It is aimed at the obvious counterexamples. At §5(a) (Hua XXVIII 37) Aristotle is "the father of logic" because he created the analytic, but the *Nicomachean Ethics*, "however much beauty it may offer", did not make him "in the same sense the father of ethics". At §5(b) Kant's formal principle is set aside as a principle of positive decision rather than a formal law (row 3). The novelty claim therefore has a precise content. Nobody before has abstracted from the "matter" of practice in the way the analytic abstracts from the matter of knowledge.

The sentence that closes the paragraph pitches the analogy at its third level. Formal logic corresponds to "a system of fundamental structures of belief-consciousness", and so to a phenomenology of knowledge. Axiology and *Praktik* correspond in "a similar way" to a phenomenology of valuing and willing. Husserl guards the new words with a parenthesis: "theory of valuing" (*Wertungstheorie*) and "theory of willing" (*Willenstheorie*) are used "in a sense analogous to the word 'theory of knowledge'". The quotation marks around *Erkenntnistheorie* concede that the new terms borrow their sense from the old one. The program then ends with the slogan "Let us now go to the matters themselves!" (*Gehen wir nun an die Sachen!*). The matters are what will confirm or limit the "must".

The course does not keep the modality it began with. When §5(a) returns to the program, the "must" has become a conjecture: "One would like to conjecture that theoretical reason *must* have its analogue in a practical reason" (*Man möchte vermuten, daß theoretische Vernunft ihr Analogon haben müsse*, Hua XXVIII 37–38), and a formal *Praktik* "would have to" exist "if the conjecture guiding us in our analogizing considerations is correct" (*Ist die uns … leitende Vermutung richtig, so müßte …*, Hua XXVIII 37). At §5(c) the demand is a string of questions: "Must not the distinction between logic and material science … have its strict analogue in the practical sphere too?" (Hua XXVIII 45). §8 looks back on all this as the use of formal logic as a "parallelistic clue" (*parallelistischen Leitfaden*) for "the parallel structures we conjectured" (Hua XXVIII 66). The course thus states the necessity of §1 as a conjecture, a question and a clue. The necessity is that of a heuristic. What turns it into results is description: the means–end law of §6, which is not analytic (row 5), and the will-modalities of §17. The heuristic is cut back where description finds no parallel. §12(a) turns to formal laws "that have no analogue in the narrower apophantic-logical sphere" (Hua XXVIII 90). §18(a) closes its iteration laws with "propositions that have no proper parallel in the doxic domain" (*keine rechte Parallele im doxischen Gebiet*, Hua XXVIII 128). §21 finds that subjectivity plays a role in practical possibility "that has no analogy in the judgment- or …" domain (Hua XXVIII 149), where the file breaks off after "bzw." and the next paragraph begins. The "must" of §1 is the course's working hypothesis, and its last sections record where the hypothesis fails.

The earlier course of 1908/09 already states the analogy as a guide rather than a theorem. Its part on theoretical and axiological reason says that "in the formulation and solution of the questions to be posed for a truly scientific construction of ethics … the analogy with logic must lead us; it proves itself step by step to be infinitely fruitful", and adds that "first there is need of a truly systematic logic" (Hua XXVIII 244). There the "must" governs method, and it is justified by its fruitfulness. The 1914 sentence moves the "must" from the method to the matter, so that a formal *Praktik* must exist. That is the stronger claim, and the rest of the course qualifies it again. A Beilage to the same earlier course also complicates "never came to conception": Brentano, Husserl writes there, "first recognized that a parallelism holds here between objectivating and non-objectivating acts", and urged the need to treat the valuing sphere "parallel with the logical sphere" (Hua XXVIII 345). In 1914 what is new is not the parallel but the formal discipline. Brentano had the analogue of evidence, and nobody had the analogue of the analytic.

## §4(b), Hua XXVIII 29: practical countersense

Reads [§4(b), paragraph 9](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-analogue-of-the-skeptical-countersense-in-the-practical-sphere-the-practical-countersense).

A practical countersense (*praktischer Widersinn*) is the self-cancellation of a demand whose content declares irrational what its sense as a demand presupposes as rational. The skeptic's imperative "Recognize no practical rule!" does not contradict itself in what it says. It cancels itself in what it is: a rule, and so something offered to be recognized as rational and followed. The argument therefore lives in the grammatical mood. The skeptical demand fails because it is an imperative, and the failure is practical, not logical.

> If I now say "Recognize no rule as valid for your action!", how does it stand then? If I say "Do not recognize a certain rule, this one or that!", there lies in it: Recognize that it is not rationally binding, do not let yourself be positively determined by it, do not take it as a rule! But there also lies in it a rule, namely, on the one hand, to see that to act as that rule demands is not rational action, and on the other hand to see that the contradictorily opposed will, not to bind oneself to that rule in the groups of action in question, is a rational one, and that accordingly the negative will-rule, not to proceed by the original rule, is also to be recognized in accordance with reason. If it now says "Recognize no practical rule!", there lies in it the rule, which is to be recognized as rational, that no practical rule at all is rational, that every will bound by any rules whatever is irrational, or that the will to follow no rule is rational. On the other hand, if I say "Recognize no practical rule!", I utter a practical rule, and indeed a rule which it would be rational to recognize and right to follow practically. That lies in the sense of every rule of this form, of every rule that is more than merely suggestive. On the other hand, in the content of this rule that is set up as practically irrational which the rule, by its sense, presupposes as rational. So there we have the exact analogue of the skeptical countersense.

German:

> Sage ich nun „Erkenne keine Regel als gültig für dein Handeln ⟨an⟩!“, wie steht es da? Sage ich „Erkenne eine gewisse, die und die Regel nicht an!“, so liegt darin: Erkenne, daß sie vernünftig nicht bindend ist, lasse dich von ihr positiv nicht bestimmen, nimm sie nicht als Regel! Darin liegt aber auch eine Regel, nämlich einerseits einzusehen, daß so ⟨zu⟩ handeln, wie jene Regel fordert, nicht vernünftiges Handeln sei, und andererseits einzusehen, daß der kontradiktorisch entgegengesetzte Wille, in den fraglichen Handlungsgruppen sich nicht an jene Regel zu binden, ein vernünftiger sei und daß demgemäß auch vernunftgemäß die negative Willensregel anzuerkennen sei, nach der ursprünglichen Regel nicht zu verfahren. Heißt es nun „Erkenne keine praktische Regel an!“, so liegt darin die Regel, die als vernünftig anerkannt werden soll, daß keine praktische Regel überhaupt vernünftig sei, daß jeder durch irgendwelche Regeln gebundene Wille unvernünftig sei bzw. daß der Wille, keiner Regel zu folgen, vernünftig sei. Andererseits, sage ich „Erkenne keine praktische Regel an!“, so spreche ich eine praktische Regel aus, und zwar eine Regel, die anzuerkennen vernünftig und der praktischen zu folgen richtig wäre. Das liegt im Sinn jeder Regel dieser Form, jeder mehr als bloß suggestiven Regel. Andererseits wird im Inhalt dieser Regel das als praktisch unvernünftig hingestellt, was die Regel ihrem Sinn nach als vernünftig voraussetzt. Also da haben wir das genaue Analogon des skeptischen Widersinns.

*Note on the text.* The file splices the printed edition's margin line numbers into this paragraph ("20", "25", "30", "35") and keeps two line-break hyphens with a number between them ("einer- 25 seits", "anzu- 30 erkennen"). The quotation leaves the numbers out and closes the hyphens. Both "Andererseits" are "Ackererseits" in the file, an obvious OCR slip. The phrase "der praktischen zu folgen" is as the file has it; a word may be missing ("ihr praktisch zu folgen" would be the expected form), but the sense, "right to follow in practice", is not in doubt.

The paragraph climbs in three steps, each marked by "there lies in it" (*darin liegt*, *so liegt darin*). The phrase says what a sense contains, and here it is applied to imperatives. First, a single negative rule ("Do not recognize this rule!") contains a positive one: to see that the rejected rule is not rational, and that the opposed will is. Negating a rule is itself legislating. Second, the universal negative ("Recognize no practical rule!") contains the universal positive that "no practical rule at all is rational". So far the skeptic has only said something extreme. The third step needs no further content. The skeptic "utters a practical rule" (*spreche … eine praktische Regel aus*), and that act makes the claim to rational recognition, because "that lies in the sense of every rule of this form".

Two words carry the third step: sense (*Sinn*) and content (*Inhalt*). "In the content of this rule" the rational is declared irrational; "by its sense" the rule presupposes it as rational. The pair repeats the definition of skeptical countersense given at §4(a) for the theoretical case, where a theory cancels itself if its validity "according to its essential sense" presupposes what the thesis denies and "what this theory according to its content" claims to ground (*ihrem wesentlichen Sinn gemäß … ihrem Inhalt nach*, Hua XXVIII 21). "The exact analogue" (*das genaue Analogon*) is therefore earned by the wording. The sense/content structure transfers unchanged, and only the predicate changes from true to rational-to-follow.

The phrase "more than merely suggestive" (*mehr als bloß suggestiv*) limits the argument and keeps it from being trivial. The preceding paragraph (Hua XXVIII 28) separates a demand that works suggestively, like the hypnotist's command to his medium, the master's to a slave raised in blind obedience, or a demand made of a child not yet rational, from the demand "Recognize this rule!", which asks for insight into its rightness and expects to motivate "through the addressee's having understood it". Only a demand of the second kind can cancel itself. The skeptic who merely wanted to condition behaviour would escape the argument, but at the price of no longer arguing. The argument does not show that every practical utterance presupposes practical reason. It shows that every utterance which asks to be recognized does.

The next two paragraphs make the result precise, and the precision matters more than the analogy. The logical counterpart, "Recognize no logical rule as binding for your judging!", cannot be refuted by saying that every rule asserts an ought and every assertion presupposes the law of contradiction. "The rule denies no logical rule as a logical truth." It says only that no logical truth should determine the striving for truth. Husserl concludes: "Purely logically no practical rule can cancel itself; such a rule cancels itself through practical countersense, which can indeed be connected with logical countersense, but no more than that" (*Rein logisch kann sich keine praktische Regel aufheben, eine solche hebt sich auf durch praktischen Widersinn, der allerdings mit logischem zusammenhängen kann; aber nicht mehr*, Hua XXVIII 29). The parallel thus yields a second, distinct kind of self-cancellation, not a reduction of the practical to the logical. That is the first concrete result of the course's "must" (row 1), and it already shows the parallel's shape: an analogy of structure between two irreducible kinds of reason.

The Prolegomena had defined skeptical theories as those whose theses "either expressly state or analytically include" that the logical or noetic conditions of the possibility of a theory are false (*entweder ausdrücklich besagen oder analytisch in sich schließen*, Hua XVIII 120). There the self-cancellation is analytic. The 1914 course keeps the form of the definition and gives up the word "analytic": the practical countersense is a matter of what "lies in the sense" of a rule as a rule, and Husserl says it is not logical. The same restraint returns when §6 denies that the means–end law is analytic (row 5).

The result is also narrower than it first looks, and the course says so. The recapitulation at §4(c) states that there are extreme skeptical practical demands that cancel themselves "as 'formally countersensical'" (*als „formal widersinnig“*), in a way "strictly analogous" to extreme theoretical skepticism (Hua XXVIII 35). The quotation marks around "formally countersensical" hold the word "formal" at a distance, since the form at issue is the form of a demand and not a logical form. And §5(a) admits at once that the parallel refutation reaches only the open ethical skeptic. The usual ethical empiricism, "which does not regard itself as a skepticism at all", is not touched, because ethics lacks what logic had: a formal discipline whose principles could be shown to be presupposed (Hua XXVIII 36). The practical countersense refutes the skeptic who speaks in imperatives. To refute the empiricist the course needs a formal *Praktik*, and §5 sets out to find one.

## §5(b), Hua XXVIII 43: "however 'formal'": formal as non-prejudging

Reads [§5(b), paragraph 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-traditional-sense-of-the-ethical-principles-formal-correctness-according-to-kants-categorical-imperative-versus-logical-formal-correctness).

For Husserl a principle is formal in the logician's sense when its correctness does not prejudge material correctness. Formal logic states conditions that every truth must meet and that no falsehood can meet merely by its form. It never says, positively, what is true. Kant's categorical imperative is called "formal" because it excludes material ends, but it is meant as a sufficient criterion of the morally good in each case. A principle that decides positively is not formal in Husserl's sense, however little matter it contains. The passage separates two senses of "formal" that the history of ethics had run together, and the course's own formal *Praktik* is defined by the first.

> Note that it makes no difference in this respect whether, in the historical tradition, ethical principles are sought and held possible as so-called material or as so-called formal ones. Whether in the ethical principles pleasure, general welfare, perfection and the like are set up as the highest practical goals, or whether one, with Kant, rejects all such principles as "material" and declares a categorical imperative, as a "purely formal principle", to be alone admissible: it is all one. How far such an opposition of "material" and "formal" has analogy with the like-named logical opposition is the question, and needs closer investigation. But from the outset it is clear to the connoisseur of Kantian ethics and of the disputes attached to it that, given the way the opposition is introduced and the way the formal principle is used in Kantian ethics, the analogy fails at the essential point that interests us. For Kant wanted to give, with the categorical imperative, the one single criterion of morality, and one not merely necessary but sufficient. He himself instructs us how we are to proceed with the categorical imperative in the given case in order to determine what is morally good in it, what is truly dutiful, or to judge ethically a willing and acting already carried out. Purely and exclusively the general valuation from which the demand of the categorical imperative wells up determines the value of the individual case. However "formal" the principle is, however much it may exclude what is material in the Kantian sense, it is a principle of positive decision, completely sufficient for the given case taken in its full concretion and individuality. This formal conformity-to-correctness according to the categorical imperative therefore leaves no room for a material incorrectness. Quite the contrary, we heard: in the sphere of cognition, logical-formal correctness in no way prejudges material correctness.

German:

> Beachten Sie, daß es in dieser Hinsicht keinen Unterschied ausmacht, ob in der historischen Tradition ethische Prinzipien als sogenannte materiale oder als sogenannte formale gesucht und für möglich erachtet werden. Ob in den ethischen Prinzipien Lust, allgemeine Wohlfahrt, Vollkommenheit und dergl. als die obersten praktischen Ziele hingestellt werden oder ob man mit Kant alle solchen Prinzipien als „materiale“ ablehnt und einen kategorischen Imperativ als ein „rein formales Prinzip“ für allein zulässig erklärt: es ist einerlei. Inwieweit ein solcher Gegensatz von „material“ und „formal“ mit dem gleich benannten logischen Gegensatz Analogie hat, ist die Frage und bedarf näherer Untersuchung. Aber von vornherein ist es für den Kenner der Kantischen Ethik und der an sie geknüpften Streitigkeiten klar, daß bei der Art, wie der Gegensatz eingeführt und wie vom formalen Prinzip in der Kantischen Ethik Gebrauch gemacht wird, die Analogie in dem wesentlichen, uns interessierenden Punkt versagt. Denn Kant wollte mit dem kategorischen Imperativ das eine einzige und nicht bloß notwendige, sondern zureichende Kriterium der Sittlichkeit geben. Er leitet uns selbst an, wie wir mit dem kategorischen Imperativ im gegebenen Fall zu verfahren haben, um das in ihm sittlich Gute, das wahrhaft Pflichtmäßige zu bestimmen bzw. das schon vollzogene Wollen und Handeln ethisch zu beurteilen. Rein und ausschließlich die allgemeine Wertung, aus der die Forderung des kategorischen Imperativs entquillt, bestimmt den Wert des Einzelfalls. Wie „formal“ das Prinzip auch ist, wie sehr es das im Kantischen Sinn Materiale ausschließen mag, es ist ein Prinzip positiver Entscheidung, für den gegebenen und in seiner vollen Konkretion und Individualität genommenen Fall vollkommen zureichend. Diese formale Richtigkeitsgemäßheit dem kategorischen Imperativ läßt also keinen Raum für eine materiale Unrichtigkeit. Ganz im Gegenteil dazu hörten wir: Es präjudiziert in der Erkenntnissphäre die logisch formale Richtigkeit ganz und gar nicht die materiale Richtigkeit.

*Note on the text.* "Diese formale Richtigkeitsgemäßheit dem kategorischen Imperativ" is as the file has it. The dative needs a preposition or an adjective ("Richtigkeit gemäß dem kategorischen Imperativ", formal correctness according to the categorical imperative), and a word may have been run together or lost. The rendering follows the evident sense, and nothing below turns on the compound.

The paragraph's first move is to make the received division of ethics irrelevant. Material and formal ethics, the hedonist's pleasure and Kant's imperative, are all "so-called" (*sogenannte*), and the difference between them is "all one" (*einerlei*) in the respect that matters. Husserl's quotation marks then do the work of a thesis. "Material" and "formal" are Kant's words, put in Kant's mouth ("rejects … as 'material'", "a 'purely formal principle'"). The marks keep the words from being taken in the sense the course gives them. The sentence after them states the open question in so many words: how far the ethical opposition "has analogy with the like-named logical opposition" (*mit dem gleich benannten logischen Gegensatz*). The phrase "like-named" concedes a shared name and nothing more.

The criterion of the formal follows in a pair borrowed from logic: "not merely necessary but sufficient" (*nicht bloß notwendige, sondern zureichende*). Kant is not faulted for having too little content in his principle. He is faulted for asking it to do too much. A sufficient criterion of morality is, by the preceding paragraph's account of ethical "principles", one under which the right act in any case could be found "by mere subsumption" (*durch bloße Subsumption*, Hua XXVIII 42). Logic's principles cannot work that way. They "will not and cannot decide positively what is true in every possible sphere of knowledge"; they can prejudge "only analytic, not synthetic truth" (Hua XXVIII 42). The verb Husserl uses for this, "prejudge" (*präjudizieren*), returns in the last sentence of the passage. It names the relation that formal correctness must lack. A formal principle that prejudges material correctness has ceased to be formal.

"However 'formal'" (*Wie „formal“*) is the turn of the paragraph. The concession grants Kant his exclusion of the material "in the Kantian sense" and denies that the exclusion makes the principle formal in the sense at issue. Husserl's name for what the imperative is, "a principle of positive decision" (*ein Prinzip positiver Entscheidung*), puts the weight on "positive". The consequence is drawn with "therefore" (*also*): the imperative "leaves no room for a material incorrectness". In logic there is such room. A syllogism can be formally valid and materially false, and that gap is what makes form and matter two dimensions of correctness and gives "double truths", formal and material. A principle that closes the gap has only one dimension, whatever it is called.

The inference is quicker than the wording suggests. Husserl takes Kant at his word that the imperative is sufficient, and "he himself instructs us" (*Er leitet uns selbst an*) appeals to Kant's procedure, not to a reading of it. Whether the universalization test really decides the concrete case without any prior valuation is exactly what "the disputes attached to it", which Husserl's sentence mentions, had been about. Husserl does not need to settle this. His claim is conditional on Kant's intention: if the imperative does what Kant meant it to do, it is not formal in the logician's sense.

§8 restates the charge as a dilemma. Kant's "formalistic ethics" could not count as the analogue of formal logic, and "if Kant was right", the analogy between knowing and practical consciousness "went to pieces" (*ging … in die Brüche*, Hua XXVIII 65). The next paragraph removes the dilemma by history. Kant's formal ethics "was not designed at all as an analogue of formal, analytic logic", since Kant did not let himself be led "in a radical way" by the thought of a thoroughgoing parallelism (Hua XXVIII 66). The quarrel is therefore about a word. Kant's "formal" means free of matter, and Husserl's means non-prejudging.

At §19 the charge returns reversed. There a "formal rule-giving of valuing and willing" which supposes that the matter of valuing and willing "could remain out of account" is "a countersense" (*ein Widersinn*): without a material apriori, without kinds of objects that carry value-predicates by their essence, "the concept of objective value would have no hold". "In this respect we must therefore sharply separate our ways from the Kantian doctrine" (*müssen wir also unsere Wege von der Kantischen Lehre scharf sondern*, Hua XXVIII 139; row 21). The two charges fit together. At §5 Kant's principle decides too much to be formal; at §19 a principle that tried to be formal by excluding matter would ground nothing. Husserl's formal *Praktik* avoids both. It decides nothing positively, as §5 requires, and it presupposes in formal generality that material value-laws exist, as §19 requires. The sense of "formal" fixed at §5 thus also fixes the limit §19 sets on formal ethics as a whole: it is a necessary condition of rightness, never a sufficient criterion.

## §5(c), Hua XXVIII 47–48: the good as the existential value

Reads [§5(c), paragraph 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#c-on-the-possibility-of-formal-practical-and-formal-axiological-principles).

In this passage the good (*das Gute*) is the value of something *as existing*. The beautiful is valued without regard to whether its object exists. The good is the beautiful once existence is taken into the valuing, and Husserl defines it through an act: goods (*Güter*) are the correlates of rational valuings "of the genus joy" (*der Gattung Freude*). From that definition follows an apriori chain in which one value passes into the next as the doxic standing of its object changes: beautiful, good if it exists, desire-worthy if it does not, a will-value if it is realizable. The chain is the first sample of a formal axiology. It is also the doctrine that Beilage I calls an "obliquity" (*Schiefheit*), because it identifies valuing something as good with rejoicing in it.

> If we separate off, excluding for the moment the domain of desiring and willing, a domain of non-existential values as a domain of beauty-values in the widest sense; if we thus comprise under it the values for which the existence or non-existence of the value-giving object is irrelevant to the sense and validity of the value-predication; if we set over against it the sphere of existential valuings, in which the reverse holds, and if we call the values that form the correlate of rational existential valuings of the genus joy *goods*, and the corresponding negative values *evils*: this presupposed, it is clear that every beautiful object is at the same time a good one; that is, what proves itself beautiful in rational aesthetic valuing must necessarily prove itself good in rational existential valuing; that is, if it exists, or stands there in conviction as existing, it is necessarily, that is, generally in accordance with reason, the object of a justified joy; likewise in the reverse case of non-existence ⟨it is⟩ the object of justified grief. Again: let us suppose that the character of existence of the object is lacking for consciousness, but that the assumption of existence grounds, in accordance with reason, an assumptive liking. We think ourselves into the existence, then, and in this thought of "supposing it were" there is grounded a quasi-joy that can be rationally justified, namely of the kind: that would be a real joy! Then it is clear that the same object or the same state of affairs would be the justified bearer of a rational wish, and, if it stands there as realizable, of a justified will. If we call the value-correlates of the rational wish desire-values, and those of the rational will will-values or practical values, then it is clear that all these values cohere apriori. *Apriori* it holds: the beautiful is at the same time a good; every good is, if it does not exist, a desire-value; every desire-value is, if it is realizable, a will-value, so that the concept of the good is as a rule extended. What can rightly be desired is also called good, and the practical value is practically good.

The first sentence is a chain of four hortatory subjunctives ("if we separate off … if we comprise … if we set over against … if we call"). The whole chain is a stipulation, and its last clause is a definition by naming: "let us call" (*nennen wir*) the correlates of rational joy "goods". Everything after "this presupposed" (*Dies vorausgesetzt*) depends on that act of naming. The good is introduced as a value-correlate of an emotion of a determinate kind, and the "it is clear" (*ist es klar*) that follows is clear only under the stipulation. When Beilage I asks whether "that A is, is good" is "equivalent" to "that A is, is gladdening" (*erfreulich*), it questions exactly the clause that the presupposition hides.

Two things vary through the chain, and the wording keeps them apart. The first is the doxic standing of the object: "exists, or stands there in conviction as existing" (*existiert es bzw. steht es als existierend in der Überzeugung da*), non-existence, "the assumption of existence" (*die Assumption der Existenz*), "stands there as realizable" (*als realisierbar dasteht*). The second is the act and its correlate: joy or grief, an "assumptive liking" or "quasi-joy", a wish, a will. Each emotive act is matched to one doxic standing. This is the course's model of founding, already at work. The emotive act is "motivated", as §9 puts it, by a value-presupposition and by "the conviction, the judgment, that the beautiful is" (Hua XXVIII 73). The verbs in the chain show the founding: what is beautiful "proves itself" (*sich … ausweist*) good in existential valuing. It does not become good by being judged to exist. It shows itself good to a valuing that takes existence in.

The step to the wish is the most carefully worded. Where existence is lacking, the assumption of existence grounds an "assumptive" liking, and the dash introduces the experience that carries it: "we think ourselves into the existence", and in the thought "supposing it were" there arises a "quasi-joy" of the form "that would be a real joy!" (*Das wäre eine rechte Freude!*). The subjunctive of the exclamation is the wish's own grammar. A wish is a joy held in the mood of supposition. That makes the desire-value a modification of the good rather than a new genus of value, and it is why Husserl can write that "the concept of the good is as a rule extended" (*sich … in der Regel erweitert*). "As a rule" here means "in the usual way of speaking": ordinary language calls the rightly desirable "good", and the practically valuable "practically good". The chain thus explains a usage. One word covers four values because the four are one value under four doxic standings.

The extension of the word hides a gap that the course later finds in the thing. The last link, "every desire-value is, if it is realizable, a will-value", makes the will a further modification of the same joy, a quasi-joy in the realizable. Section III takes this back. At §14 the will is not a wish with a realizability-clause attached: the wish means an "it may be", the will an "it shall be" (row 14). At §15 the will is creative, and its "Let it be!" is not founded on an expectation of what will be (row 15). The chain of §5(c) treats the will as the last of the valuings. The phenomenology of the will treats it as a position of its own kind.

The identification of the good with the joy-value is stated again in the formal axiology itself. In §10 the objective law runs: "If A is a beautiful thing and is in reality, then it is a good one", and "'good' is here understood as the axiological correlate of rational joy, thus the joy-value" (*„Gut“ ist hier verstanden als das axiologische Korrelat der vernünftigen Freude, also das der Freude Werte*, Hua XXVIII 74–75). That is the page to which, together with Hua XXVIII 47 f., Beilage I is keyed. Its correction is explicit: "In the lectures I identified the two. But one must consider carefully whether rightly. I inclined repeatedly to the view (and so again now!) that a distinction is to be made here" (*In den Vorlesungen habe ich beides identifiziert … Ich neigte wiederholt zu der Ansicht (und so auch jetzt wieder!), daß hier zu scheiden sei*, Hua XXVIII 155; row 24). What the Beilage separates is the valuing of the being of A from the joy at it. "I value, taking existence into account, the being: that A is, that is valuable." A valuing that takes existence in need not be a rejoicing. The definition by naming in §5(c) had made it one. The Beilage does not touch the chain's form: the dependence of values on the doxic standing of their objects survives the correction, and so does the formal character Husserl claims for it in the next paragraph, where the connections "seem already to be of a formal nature", since "it does not depend on the 'matter' of beauty and goodness" (Hua XXVIII 48). What the Beilage removes is the act in which §5(c) had grounded the good.

## §6, Hua XXVIII 55: formal but not analytic

Reads [§6, paragraph 7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-formal-relation-between-means-and-end-is-no-logical-relation-the-omni-dominion-of-logical-reason).

The means–end law, "whoever wills the end must rationally will the necessary means", is formal without being analytic. It is formal because it holds whatever the end and the means are. It is not analytic because its terms, will and cause, are not logical concepts: replace them by indeterminate ones and the inference fails. The passage takes Kant's word "analytic" away from this law and so separates two properties that in logic coincide. In the theoretical sphere the formal and the analytic are one. In the practical sphere a law can be independent of the matter of the will and still not follow from the form of judgment. That gap is where the course places its formal *Praktik*.

> In this it is of special importance to bring oneself to the insight that the will-situation is not, say, a mere special case of the intellectual, the logical state of affairs, and so that will-reason is not a special case of logical reason in inferring. Above all it is evident that the rational enclosure of the means in the end, or of the willing of the means in the willing of the end, is not an "analytic" relation, not something like a logical relation of inference. This comes out when we bring the will-situation to predicative expression. From the proposition "I will the end *Z*" and from the proposition "*M* is a necessary means to *Z*" no one can infer by formal-logical principles "I must will *M*, that is, I ought rationally to will *M*", unless he inserts the general proposition: whoever wills an end at all "must" will the necessary means to it. But just this proposition states the principle of the mode of inference generally, and is therefore in question. It is evidently no logical-analytic proposition, none that is evident from the essence of judgment in general, none that would be valid from the form of judgment in general regardless of its particular judgment-matter. If we analyse what this proposition says, and so first what "means and end" says, we are led to a causal relation: the becoming-real of the fact *M* draws, or would draw, after it the becoming-real of the fact *Z*. To this is added (for the mode of inference in question) that the agent cannot realize *Z* immediately, but can immediately realize a causal way to *Z*, and that on the other hand he can realize no other sufficient cause for *Z* than one that implies *M* as a necessary member. Where all this obtains, there we can indeed infer: in such cases we must will *M*. We "must": that says here no psychological necessity, but a necessity of reason, a rational ought. But after this analysis we see all the more, and quite clearly, that this mode of inference from the willing of *Z* to the rational ought-to-will of *M* is no analytic one, none grounded in the mere form of judgment. For then it would have to remain valid if we replaced all the concepts occurring here, cause, effect, will, in short all the not purely logical concepts, by indeterminate ones. Of that there is of course no question. It is no logical contradiction at all, for all the unreason, to will the end and not to will the means.

The paragraph's first claim is negative and is aimed at a reduction: the will-situation is not "a mere special case" (*ein bloßer Sonderfall*) of the logical one. That is the thesis of the section's title ("no logical relation"), and the passage proves it from Kant's own example. The phrase "rational enclosure" (*vernunftgemäßes Eingeschlossensein*) concedes the image on which Kant's "analytic" rested. Something is enclosed in the willing of the end, and Husserl does not deny it. He denies that the enclosure is the containment of a concept in a concept. The quotation marks around "analytic" (*„analytisches“*) cite Kant's word in order to refuse it. Two pages earlier Husserl had quoted the *Groundwork*: the proposition "whoever wills the end wills also the means indispensably necessary to it" is, "as regards the willing, analytic", because "in the willing of an object as my effect my causality as acting cause … is already thought" (Hua XXVIII 51, citing Kant, Ak. IV 417). His objection there is phenomenological: the willing of the end is not "a complex that really implies the willing of the only necessary means", and one can will an end "without being in the least clear about the means" (Hua XXVIII 52). Kant's "already thought" (*schon … gedacht*) is false of the act. The present paragraph adds that it is false of the proposition too.

The proof works through the quotation marks around "must". In the inserted premise, whoever wills an end "must" (*„muß“*) will the means. The marks flag a modal word that cannot be read off the form of judgment, and Husserl's gloss later in the paragraph fixes its sense: "We 'must': that says here no psychological necessity, but a necessity of reason, a rational ought". The syllogism is valid only with the premise, and the premise "states the principle of the mode of inference" itself. A principle cannot be derived by the inference it governs, so the will-inference has a principle of its own.

The decisive sentence is a test of formality borrowed from logic: an analytic inference "would have to remain valid if we replaced all the concepts occurring here, cause, effect, will, in short all the not purely logical concepts, by indeterminate ones". It is the criterion of the Third Logical Investigation: "in an analytic proposition it must be possible to replace every material content, with full preservation of the logical form of the proposition, by the empty form something" (*jede sachhaltige Materie, bei voller Erhaltung der logischen Form des Satzes, durch die leere Form etwas zu ersetzen*, LU III §12, Hua XIX/1 259). Applied to the will-inference, it shows exactly what the inference lacks. Its validity depends on "will" and "cause", so it is not analytic. But the inference is not material either, since nothing in it depends on what *Z* and *M* are. The variables stay variables. The law is formal in respect of the will's matter and contentful in respect of the form "will". The final sentence states the result in the register of the contradiction it denies: willing the end and not the means is "no logical contradiction at all, for all the unreason" (*gar kein logischer Widerspruch, trotz aller Unvernunft*). "Unreason" names a failure that is real and not logical. The concessive phrase holds both claims in one breath.

The next paragraph asks whether the law then belongs to empirical reason, the reason of causal knowledge, since it rests on a causal relation, and answers that it does not: causal facts contain "nothing of means and end and of reason", and the rational ought "is no fact of experience". The law is apriori, "evidently valid in unconditioned generality and necessity" (Hua XXVIII 56–57). Neither analytic logic nor experience accounts for it, and that leaves room for a third reason.

§8 draws the positive consequence and gives Kant's word back in a restricted sense. The task is "not to miss the legitimate sense of this talk of 'analytic'": this "analytic" does not mean, "as Kant meant it", the same as the "analytic" of formal logic, but "a mere analogue of the logical-analytic". "And there now was a point at which we entered the genuine and sought-for parallel sphere to the formal-logical." The means–end laws are formal, "in so far as they include not the least of the matter of what is willed. Only the form, and indeed as will-form, comes into question" (*Nur die Form, und zwar als Willensform, kommt in Frage*, Hua XXVIII 68). The phrase "as will-form" answers the substitution test of §6. In logic the form is the form of judgment, and "will" is matter. In the formal *Praktik* "will" belongs to the form. Every formal practical law will therefore fail the logician's test of analyticity, and the §6 result defines the discipline, it is not a defect in it. The "must" of §1 is fulfilled here for the first time (row 1), and fulfilled as an analogue: the practical sphere has its own formal laws, which are not logical laws.

## §7, Hua XXVIII 64: the emotions mean

Reads [§7, paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-analogies-for-the-judging-position-taking-of-belief-in-the-classes-of-emotive-and-will-acts-as-the-ground-for-the-parallelism-of-the-kinds-of-reason).

The parallelism of the kinds of reason rests on one verb. A liking or a wishing "means" (*vermeint*): it takes a position on its object, holds it for good or bad, and so can be right or wrong, as a judgment can. A mere sensation means nothing. *Vermeinen* here is not the meaning of a word (*bedeuten*) but meaning-*that*: intending, holding-for, the position-taking of an act. Husserl's claim is that the emotions have their own kind of it, an "analogue of judging" that is not a judging. Logical reason can bring values to light only because the emotions have already "decided" before cognition arrives. The passage answers Rickert and Windelband, who read the position-taking of judgment as a feeling, by reversing the direction of their analogy: feeling is not the core of judgment, but feeling has the structure of position-taking that judgment has.

> Of course this critique is directed against the theories of Rickert and Windelband, so popular in our time. The axiological reason disregarded by the tradition breaks through in these theories in a certain way. Its very peculiarity is felt so strongly by these researchers, who lean on Fichte, that they fall into the exaggeration of reinterpreting logical reason itself axiologically. On the other hand, and to return to our starting point, the omni-dominion of logical reason need not make us go astray about the parallelism of the kinds of reason. In it there is expressed a certain essential interweaving of doxic consciousness with emotive consciousness, and so with every consciousness at all, according to which every position-taking, every valuing-as-beautiful or valuing-as-good, can *apriori* be converted into a judging position-taking. […] Bringing out, establishing, determining, objectivating in the specific sense, is the affair of logical reason. Axiological reason with its holdings is, so to speak, hidden from itself. It first becomes manifest through the cognition that is carried out on the ground of the emotive acts. But cognition does not invent; it only draws out what is in a certain way already there. Were the emotions not a domain of meanings, did they not already in themselves, but precisely in the manner of the emotions, make decisions, cast their vote, then cognition would find nothing of values and value-contents before it. It would then find only blind experiences, such as, say, experiences of sensing red and blue. A mere sensing, a mere living-through of a sensuous datum, means nothing; but a liking means, a wishing means, and so on. Meaning is not always judging (in the ordinary sense), a manner of believingly holding-for-something, but it is an analogue of judging.

Verneinen ist nicht immer Urteilen (im gewöhnlichen Sinn), eine Weise des glaubend Für-etwas-Haltens, aber ein Analogon des Urteilens ist es.

Both are taken here as *Vermeinungen* and *Vermeinen* (meanings, meaning), and rendered so. The sense requires it. What the emotions are said to be is the condition of their containing values, and they are opposed to "blind experiences" that "mean nothing"; the sentence between gives "vermeint" three times, correctly spelt; and a negating would be a judging in the ordinary sense, which is what the last sentence denies of the word it glosses. The same confusion runs through §7. In the paragraph before this one the file has "alle Vermengungen, alle intentionalen Stellungnahmen" (all mixtures, all intentional position-takings) and "sofern sie eben verneinen" (in so far as they negate), where the sense needs *Vermeinungen* and *vermeinen*. At Hua XXVIII 59–60 "Erkennen ist ein Vermeinen, verneinen, es sei etwas" and "als verneinendes Verhalten das Analogon zum urteilenden Vermeinen, allgemeiner, zum Seinsverneinen" are probably of the same kind. Genuine *Verneinung* (negation) is treated in §17(d), and none of these places is about negation. The omitted sentence (marked […]) says that the emotive acts, their right and wrong, and the values correlative to them become fields of determinations carried out in logical acts.

The passage moves from a polemic to a thesis, and the hinge is "on the other hand" (*Andererseits*). The polemic grants Rickert and Windelband a discovery and charges them with an overreach. Axiological reason "breaks through" (*bricht … durch*) in their theories, but "these researchers, who lean on Fichte" (*an Fichte sich anlehnenden*) feel its peculiarity so strongly that they "reinterpret" (*umdeuten*) logical reason as valuing. The preceding paragraph names the doctrine: what brings the value-difference of truth and falsity into judgment is supposed to be the addition of "feelings of a transcendental ought, … feelings of the necessity of thought" (Hua XXVIII 63). The naming of Fichte locates the error: the primacy of the practical, carried into the theory of judgment. Husserl's own term for the result is "emotionalism", the mirror image of the intellectualism that dissolves all reason into logical reason (Hua XXVIII 63).

The thesis begins by conceding what the polemic might seem to deny, "the omni-dominion of logical reason" (*Allherrschaft der logischen Vernunft*). Every valuing can be "converted" (*umgewandelt*) into a judgment, and everything that is established about values is established by logical acts. The concession is phrased so that it cannot become a reduction. Logical reason does the "bringing out, establishing, determining", and the series of verbs gives it the work of exhibiting. "Cognition does not invent, it only draws out what is in a certain way already there." What is "already there" is axiological reason, "hidden from itself" (*sich selbst verborgen*). The image divides the labour cleanly. Valuing reason has holdings it cannot see; logical reason sees but owns nothing.

The counterfactual that follows gives the ground of the division, and its verbs are judicial. The emotions "make decisions" (*Entscheidungen treffen*) and "cast their vote" (*ihr Votum abgeben*). Both words are taken from judgment, and both are qualified at once by "but precisely in the manner of the emotions". The next paragraph makes the borrowing explicit: the will does not judge, "and yet in a certain sense and speaking in a likeness it does judge", for "*it itself speaks*, it *casts as it were a vote*" (*er selbst spricht, er gibt gleichsam ein Votum*); "we also call judging a deciding-oneself" (Hua XXVIII 64). The italics there survive in the file. A vote is a position taken by someone who is entitled to take it, and it can be right or wrong. That is all the parallelism needs.

The contrast with sensation fixes what *Vermeinen* adds. Sensing red and blue is a "blind experience", a "mere living-through" (*bloßes Erleben*) that "means nothing". A liking "means". The verb has no object in the sentence, and that is the point: it names the act's own claim, not a content. The final sentence then sets the word against its doxic model. Meaning "is not always judging (in the ordinary sense), a manner of believingly holding-for-something" (*eine Weise des glaubend Für-etwas-Haltens*), "but an analogue of judging". "In the ordinary sense" leaves room for a wider sense of judging in which the emotions do judge. The earlier pages of §7 had prepared that sense: "new basic kinds of position-taking appear here, new kinds of meaning, of holding-for" (*neue Arten des Vermeinens, des Dafürhaltens*), and in every wishing "there lies a holding-for-good" (*ein Für-gut-Halten*, Hua XXVIII 60). Holding-for-good is the emotive analogue of holding-for-true. The hyphenated compounds make the structure visible: *Für-wahr-*, *Für-seiend-*, *Für-gut-Halten*, one form with different predicates.

The English "means" loses a distinction that the argument needs. *Vermeinen* names the act's claim, not a signification (*Bedeutung*): a liking does not signify anything, and Husserl does not say it does. The German verb also keeps the edge of *vermeintlich* (supposed). What the emotion claims may be wrong, which is why its claims fall under a reason of their own.

The word returns at the points where the course decides what the emotions are. §8 defines doxic consciousness as "a meaning that something is" (*ein Vermeinen, es sei etwas*), and splits it into the act, "the judging meaning", the proposition and the state of affairs (Hua XXVIII 66). The emotive analogues of that triad are what Section II studies. §8 also explains why valuing reason is "mute and in a certain way blind" (row 7), and the blindness there is not the blindness of sensation: a meaning act can be right without seeing that it is. At §14 belief is "the meaning of being", and the emotive acts are modalizations of being "of a new dimension" (Hua XXVIII 105; row 14). Beilage I puts the word in quotation marks and asks of the non-rational emotions: "Are the blind acts not 'meanings'?" (*Sind die blinden Akte nicht „Verneinungen“?*, again for *Vermeinungen*, Hua XXVIII 156). "Blind" there means without insight, not without intention. The question shows that the clean division of §7, the meaning emotions against blind sensations, left unsettled whether every emotion, rational or not, contains a valuing. That is the question the Beilage turns into a research programme (row 25).

## §8, Hua XXVIII 68–69: mute and blind reason

Reads [§8, paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#recapitulation-formal-logic-as-the-clue-to-the-discovery-of-the-parallel-structures-in-the-emotive-sphere-the-privilege-of-logical-predicative-reason).

Valuing and practical reason are "mute and in a certain way blind": they can be right without seeing that they are right and without being able to say so. Seeing, including the "seeing" of insight, is a doxic act. So the rightness of a valuing is there before any act has grasped it, and only logical reason can bring it to givenness, form it into concepts and state it as a law. The division of labour is exact. Logical reason contributes the forms in which a right is stated; the valuing acts contribute the rightness stated. This settles the problem of the omni-dominion of logical reason that §§6–7 had raised (row 6). Logic rules everywhere as the reason that formulates, and no other reason is reduced to it.

> Logical reason, however, has the unique privilege that not only in its own field, but in the field of every other genus of meaning, and so in every other sphere of reason, it formulates right, determines rightfulness, predicates and utters laws of right as laws. Valuing and practical reason are, so to speak, mute and in a certain way blind. Even seeing in the narrower and wider sense, and so also in the sense of "insight", is a doxic act. A right can be there without being seen; it can be there without being brought under concepts of right, explicated and thought in the form of judgment, uttered as a law in the form of an assertion. It is there as a right of valuing reason when valuing acts of a certain essential kind and formation are carried out or can be carried out. But merely valuing reason does not see, does not grasp, does not explicate, does not predicate. With it, therefore, acts of the logical sphere in the widest sense of the word, the doxic sphere, must interweave. Only in the carrying out of such acts can acts at all, and ⟨that⟩ which they mean, come to objective givenness for us, and can ⟨we⟩ then see that valuing acts are "meaning" acts, acts that hold for beautiful, for good, and further that they stand under ideal predicates of rightness and wrongness, and so on. So the torch of logical reason must be lit, so that what is hidden of forms and norms in the sphere of the emotions and the will can come into the bright light. But the logical acts only shine in and only make visible what is there. They constitute only the logical forms, not the peculiar contents of reason of the parallel spheres of reason that are grasped in the forms.

German:

> Die logische Vernunft hat nun aber den einzigartigen Vorzug, daß sie nicht nur in ihrem eigenen Feld, sondern im Feld jeder anderen Gattung des Vermeinens, also in jeder anderen Vernunftsphäre Recht formuliert, Rechtmäßigkeit bestimmt, Rechtsgesetze als Gesetze prädiziert und ausspricht. Wertende und praktische Vernunft sind sozusagen stumm und in gewisser Weise blind. Schon das Sehen im engeren und weiteren Sinn, also auch im Sinne des „Einsehens“ ist ein doxischer Akt. Ein Recht kann da sein, ohne gesehen zu sein, es kann da sein, ohne unter Rechtsbegriffe gebracht, in Urteilsform expliziert und gedacht, in Aussageform als Gesetz ausgesprochen zu sein. Es ist da als Recht wertender Vernunft, wenn wertende Akte gewisser Wesensartung und -formung vollzogen bzw. vollziehbar sind. Aber bloß wertende Vernunft sieht nicht, begreift nicht, expliziert nicht, prädiziert nicht. Mit ihr müssen sich also Akte der im weitesten Wortsinn logischen, der doxischen Sphäre verflechten. Nur im Vollzug solcher Akte können uns Akte überhaupt und ⟨das⟩, was sie vermeinen, zur gegenständlichen Gegebenheit kommen und können ⟨wir⟩ dann einsehen, daß wertende Akte „vermeinende“, für schön, für gut haltende sind, dann weiter, daß sie unter idealen Prädikaten der Rechtheit und Unrechtheit stehen usw. Also die Fackel der logischen Vernunft muß aufgesteckt werden, damit, was an Formen und Normen in der Gemüts- und Willenssphäre verborgen ist, an das helle Licht treten kann. Die logischen Akte leuchten aber nur hinein und machen nur sichtbar, was da ist. Sie konstituieren nur die logischen Formen, nicht aber die in den Formen gefaßten eigentümlichen Vernunftgehalte der parallelen Vernunftsphären.

*Note on the text.* This stretch of the file is hard-wrapped with line-break hyphens ("Vermei-nens", "Recht-mäßigkeit", "wei-teren", "prädi-ziert", "hal-tende", "logi-schen" and others); the quotation closes them. The page marker for Hua XXVIII 69 falls between "ein doxischer" and "Akt". Here "Vermeinens" and "vermeinende" are spelt correctly, and they confirm the reading of the damaged forms in §7.

The privilege is described in verbs, and all of them are verbs of saying. Logical reason "formulates right, determines rightfulness, predicates and utters laws of right as laws" (*Recht formuliert, Rechtmäßigkeit bestimmt, Rechtsgesetze als Gesetze prädiziert und ausspricht*). None of them makes anything right. The privilege extends "into the field of every other genus of meaning" (*jeder anderen Gattung des Vermeinens*): the genitive repeats §7's result that the emotions are a genus of meaning, so logic's privilege presupposes the plurality it might seem to threaten. The next sentence supplies the deficit that makes the privilege necessary, in two metaphors of unequal force. Valuing reason is "mute" without qualification, since it cannot assert (§7 had called this the will's "non-self-sufficiency", Hua XXVIII 64). It is blind only "in a certain way" (*in gewisser Weise*).

The reason for the blindness is given in one sentence that the argument depends on: "Even seeing in the narrower and wider sense, and so also in the sense of 'insight', is a doxic act." The quotation marks around *Einsehens* (insight) mark the word as the evidence-term of the theory of knowledge, and the sentence assigns it to belief. If insight is doxic, a valuing cannot be insightful in its own right. Its rightness must then be the kind of thing that "can be there without being seen". The phrase "is there" (*ist da*) carries the ontology of the passage. A right "is there" whenever "valuing acts of a certain essential kind and formation are carried out or can be carried out". The "or can be" (*bzw. vollziehbar*) makes the right independent even of its actual exercise. It belongs to the essence of the act, as truth belongs to the proposition whether or not anyone judges it.

The four negations ("does not see, does not grasp, does not explicate, does not predicate") are answered by an act that the passage then performs on itself. Only in doxic acts can we "see that valuing acts are 'meaning' acts" (*„vermeinende“*). The quotation marks around "meaning" here are a mark of the step from one reason to the other: the predicate is the logician's term for what the valuing does without naming it. The image of the torch closes the step, and it is weighed carefully. The torch must be "lit" (*aufgesteckt*) for what is "hidden" (*verborgen*, the same word as §7's "hidden from itself") to come to light. But the logical acts "only shine in and only make visible what is there" (*leuchten aber nur hinein und machen nur sichtbar*). The double "only" limits what the omni-dominion can claim. Logical acts "constitute only the logical forms, not … the contents of reason". *Konstituieren* is the strongest verb in Husserl's vocabulary, and he allows it to logic only for form.

The inference is weaker than the image. That a right can be there unseen does not by itself show that the valuing act is its source rather than the judgment that first states it. Husserl concedes the gap in the next sentence: to understand "from the deepest grounds" how emotive acts can be "originary sources" of their own values "requires very difficult demonstrations" (Hua XXVIII 69). §8 therefore closes Section I with a division that it has stated but not yet proved.

The division governs the rest of the course. Section II's formal axiology is logic's statement of rights it does not constitute. Its laws are "the objective expression" of motivation-laws among the emotive acts, as §10's title puts it. But the course does not keep the claim that only doxic acts see. At §20 it asks whether a will can be right and "wholly blind": "without any insight, following a blind drive", someone may hit on the best, as a judge "in full insight" can recognize (Hua XXVIII 143). The goal it then sets is "insight into the rightness and therefore not blind but insightful willing" (*nicht blindes, sondern einsichtiges Wollen*, Hua XXVIII 143). The closing paragraph of the course makes the will's own reckoning the condition: only if the one willing "does not cast his will-vote blindly", so that "the rightness of the goal constitutes itself originarily in his willing", is he "in himself consciousness of an ought" (Hua XXVIII 153; row 23). "Blind" there is no longer the blindness of all valuing reason. It is the blindness of a will that lacks insight, and its opposite is a will in which rightness "constitutes itself originarily". By the end, then, the will itself does what §8 allowed only to doxic acts. Whether the insightful will sees by means of an interwoven doxic act, as §8 requires, or in its own way, the course does not say. §8's "in a certain way blind" keeps the question open.

## §9, Hua XXVIII 72: directed upon values

Reads [§9, paragraph 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#apriori-motivation-laws-as-laws-of-rational-consequence-motivational-connections-between-the-logical-and-the-axiological-sphere).

A valuing act is founded on an objectivating act, but it is not directed upon what that act presents. It is directed upon a value. Founding settles what the value is the value *of*: the presented thing, which is or is not. Directedness settles what the valuing takes up: the thing's being good or bad. The two relations run on different axes, and the course's formal axiology needs both. Without founding, valuing would float free of the world; without its own direction, valuing would have no correlate of its own for laws of value to be about, and Section II would be a chapter of the logic of belief. §9 states the distinction in one paragraph, and it states it as a promise.

> This interweaving easily leads astray, since it suggests saying that the valuing acts are intentional only in the sense that they are directed upon the presented objectivities. But we shall still have to carry out that in the proper sense only objectivating acts are directed upon the objective, upon what is or is not, but valuing acts upon values, and more closely, upon positive and negative values. To be sure, the one and the other are essentially fused in the matter, or correspondingly in the acts: values have their object-side and at the same time their specific value-side, the former founding the latter, and when values themselves become objects of judging cognition, the value-side itself is objectivated. But however that may be: with these somewhat difficult relations there hangs together a reason-motivation that connects the intellective and the axiological. For example, whoever is surely convinced that A is cannot will A; whoever is convinced that A is not cannot will that not A is; likewise if instead of "whoever is convinced" we say "whoever surmises with convincing probability". The talk here is of course not of a psychological can or cannot, but of an apriori of reason.

German:

> Diese Verflechtung führt leicht in die Irre, da sie nahelegt zu sagen, daß die wertenden Akte nur in dem Sinn intentionale sind, daß sie auf die vorgestellten Gegenständlichkeiten gerichtet sind. Aber wir werden noch ausführen müssen, daß im eigentlichen Sinn nur objektivierende Akte auf Gegenständliches, auf Seiendes oder Nichtseiendes gerichtet sind, wertende Akte aber auf Werte, und näher, auf positive und negative Werte. Freilich, eins und das andere ist sachlich bzw. in den Akten wesentlich verschmolzen: Werte haben ihre Gegenstandsseite und zugleich ihre spezifische Wertseite, die erstere für die letztere fundierend, und wenn Werte selbst zu Gegenständen der urteilenden Erkenntnis werden, so wird die Wertseite selbst objektiviert. Doch wie immer: Mit diesen etwas schwierigen Verhältnissen hängt eine Vernunftmotivation zusammen, die Intellektives und Axiologisches verknüpft. Zum Beispiel, wer sicher überzeugt ist, daß A ist, kann nicht A wollen; wer überzeugt ist, daß A nicht ist, kann nicht, daß nicht A ist, wollen; ebenso wenn wir, statt „wer überzeugt ist“ sagen „wer mit überzeugender Wahrscheinlichkeit vermutet“. Die Rede ist hier natürlich nicht von einem psychologischen Können oder Nichtkönnen, sondern von einem Apriori der Vernunft.

The misreading and its correction turn on where the word "only" (*nur*) stands. The misreading puts it on the valuing acts: they are intentional "only in the sense" that they are directed upon the presented objectivities, so that their intentionality is borrowed from the presentation beneath them. Husserl's answer moves the "only" to the other side: "in the proper sense only objectivating acts" are directed upon the objective. The restriction is not withdrawn but redistributed. What had limited valuing now limits objectivation, and the limit is drawn by an apposition that defines the objective: "upon the objective, upon what is or is not" (*auf Gegenständliches, auf Seiendes oder Nichtseiendes*). To be an object, in the proper sense, is to be the correlate of a positing of being or non-being. Values fall outside that definition, and so outside objectivity in the proper sense, without falling outside intentionality.

The second clause is elliptical, and the ellipsis is the claim. "But valuing acts upon values" (*wertende Akte aber auf Werte*) has no verb of its own; it borrows "are directed" (*gerichtet sind*) from the clause before. Direction is one relation with two kinds of correlate, not a primary relation of the doxic acts and a derivative one of the emotions. The correlates are then made exactly parallel. Objectivating acts are directed upon "what is or is not"; valuing acts, "more closely" (*näher*), upon "positive and negative values". The polarity of being and non-being has its counterpart in the polarity of plus and minus, and this pairing is what §10 and §11 will formalize, down to the law of excluded fourth (row 12), which adds value-freedom as a third case that being and non-being lack. The hedge "in the proper sense" (*im eigentlichen Sinn*) also concedes something. In an improper sense valuing acts are directed upon the presented object after all, through the presentation that founds them, and the misreading is not false but partial.

"To be sure" (*Freilich*) then gives back what the correction seemed to take. The two directions are "essentially fused", and the paragraph carries the founding relation over from the acts to the correlate. The paragraph before had said that the valuing act is founded in the intellective act "precisely insofar as it constitutes the value-appearance" (*Werterscheinung*, Hua XXVIII 72). Now the value itself has two sides, an "object-side" (*Gegenstandsseite*) and a "specific value-side" (*spezifische Wertseite*), "the former founding the latter". The coinages divide one correlate, not two objects. The last clause adds a third relation: when values become objects of judging cognition, "the value-side itself is objectivated" (*objektiviert*). That is §8's torch of logical reason (row 7), now stated of the correlate. The value-side is first given in valuing and only afterwards made an object.

Here the inference is thinner than the wording. If the value-side can be objectivated, the question is what it was before: a correlate that is not yet an object, given in an act that does not posit being. The paragraph names this a matter of "somewhat difficult relations" and breaks off with "however that may be" (*Doch wie immer*). The distinction rests on a future tense: "we shall still have to carry out" (*wir werden noch ausführen müssen*). It is asserted, not yet shown.

What the paragraph does show is why the axiology needs the distinction now. The sentence after the break draws a sphere-connecting law from the "difficult relations": whoever is surely convinced that A is cannot will A. The law presupposes that belief and will have different correlates. If the will were directed only upon the presented A, the conviction that A is would be no obstacle; it is an obstacle because the will's correlate is something to be made to be, and that conflicts with what is already posited as being. The preceding paragraph had listed sphere-connecting motivations within the intellective domain (certainty excludes doubt, surmise and question); this one carries them across into willing, and the next (Hua XXVIII 73) into joy, grief, hope and fear under a presupposed valuing. Each of these laws joins two acts that keep their own direction. The closing sentence fixes the status of the "cannot": not a psychological inability but "an apriori of reason", as §4 had insisted against psychologism.

The promise is not carried out in a passage of its own. Section III turns to the will instead, and the distinction returns there in the will's terms. In §15 the will's thesis implies belief in future being and yet "is not directed upon it, but precisely upon the willed process" (*Die Willensthesis ist nicht darauf gerichtet, sondern eben auf den gewollten Vorgang*, Hua XXVIII 109): the same refusal to let a founding belief absorb the founded act's direction. In §11(a) the general valuing consciousness, "taken intellectually, i.e. objectivatingly" (*intellektuell d. i. objektivierend gefaßt*), has the givenness of a general value, and Husserl calls this "something wholly new" (Hua XXVIII 86). That is the objectivation of the value-side, which the universal value-laws presuppose. Beilage I goes furthest: it sorts the theses into three classes with three correlates, "the existent, the good (value), the ought-to-be (the practically good)" (*Seiendes, Gutes (Wert), Seinsollendes (praktisch Gutes)*, Hua XXVIII 157; row 25). Value there is the correlate of a thesis of its own kind, as this paragraph required.

The promise was older than 1914. The 1908/09 course had asked whether emotive acts contain "meaning and possibly appearance … not merely through the founding acts but as emotive acts", and answered with a "must": "Somehow the question must admit of an affirmative answer." In valuing "value-objects appear, and indeed not merely the objects that have value, but the values as such" (*nicht bloß die Objekte, die Wert haben, sondern die Werte als solche*, Hua XXVIII 323). The 1914 "value-appearance" takes up this word. Between the two courses, *Ideen I* found terms for the correlate: "The value-objectivity implies its thing; it brings in, as a new objective stratum, valueness" (*Die Wertobjektität impliziert ihre Sache, sie bringt als neue objektive Schicht herein die Wertheit*, §95, Hua III/1 221). There "objective" is extended to the value-stratum, and no objectivating act is needed to make it so. The 1914 paragraph keeps the narrower word: "objective" belongs to what is or is not, and the value-side is objectivated only when judgment takes it up. The course therefore holds the value-correlate in a place its own vocabulary has no name for, and §9 marks that place with a future tense.

## §10, Hua XXVIII 76: the axiological "and"

Reads [§10, paragraph 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#value-laws-as-the-objective-expression-of-the-motivation-laws-the-formal-axiological-laws-of-consequence).

A value-inference has two premisses of different kinds: a valuing (W is a value) and a judgment (if A, then W). What joins them is not the "and" of logic. A logical inference joins two judgments into one theoretical content; here a valuing and a judging, directed upon different correlates (row 8), would give "no unity" if simply set side by side. Husserl's name for what unites them is "bringing into axiological consideration" (*in axiologische Erwägung ziehen*). The judgment stays a judgment, but it takes on a new function: it motivates feeling instead of further belief. The autonomy of the value-inference rests on this change of function, and so does §10's claim that value-laws are laws of their own and not applications of logic to a subject matter.

> One must well distinguish this axiological "and" from the merely logical one that unites the premisses of an intellectual inference. If we go back to the consciousness on either side in inferring, in carrying out consequence, then in the theoretical consciousness of inference there stands before our eyes, in the together of the premisses, a unified theoretical whole. In the case of valuing, however, there is present on the one hand a valuing of W and on the other the judging: if A holds, W holds. That by itself gives as yet no unity. An intellectual situation of unity would be present if we united intellectually the judgment "W is a value" and the judgment "If A holds, W holds"; but we make no intellectual inference at all. The unity between the act of valuing and the judging comes about through what we express as "bringing into axiological consideration". We value W, and we bring into consideration that W would be if A were. This "consideration" is no theoretical act. We bring into axiological consideration, and that is precisely this: the theoretical conviction gains an emotive function and becomes emotion-motivating, not theoretically motivating. The inference therefore runs: if B is a value, then in consideration of the situation "if A were, B would be", A too is a value. Instead of "in consideration" we also say "with regard to this", or simply: if B is a value "and" if there is added that if A were, B would be. It is therefore by no means a simple "and", and no logical "with regard to", "in consideration that". This is to be taken into account in the same way in all similar cases; so with disjunctives.

German:

> Man muß dieses axiologische „und“ wohl unterscheiden von dem bloß logischen, das die Prämissen eines intellektuellen Schlusses vereint. Gehen wir auf das beiderseitige Bewußtsein im Schließen, im Vollzug der Konsequenz zurück, so steht uns beim theoretischen Schlußbewußtsein im Zusammen der Prämisse ein vereinigter theoretischer Bestand vor Augen. Im Fall des Wertens aber liegt vor einmal ein Werten des W und andererseits das Urteilen: Wenn A gilt, gilt W. Das gibt in sich noch keine Einheit. Es liegt eine intellektuelle Einheitslage vor, wenn wir das Urteil „W ist ein Wert“ und wenn wir das Urteil „Wenn A gilt, gilt W“ intellektuell einigten; aber wir machen ja gar keinen intellektuellen Schluß. Die Einheit zwischen dem Akt des Wertens und dem Urteilen kommt zustande durch das, was wir ausdrücken als „in axiologische Erwägung ziehen“. Wir werten W und wir ziehen in Erwägung, daß W wäre, wenn A wäre. Diese „Erwägung“ ist kein theoretischer Akt. Wir ziehen in axiologische Erwägung, und das ist eben: Die theoretische Überzeugung gewinnt eine Gemütsfunktion und wird gemütsmotivierend, nicht theoretisch motivierend. Der Schluß also lautet: Ist B ein Wert, so ist in Erwägung der Sachlage, wenn A wäre, so wäre B, auch A ein Wert. Statt „in Erwägung“ sagen wir auch „in bezug darauf“ oder einfach: Wenn B ein Wert „und“ wenn dazukommt, daß wenn A wäre, B wäre. Es ist also keineswegs ein einfaches „und“ und kein logisches „in bezug auf“, „in Erwägung, daß“. Dies ist bei allen ähnlichen Fällen in gleicher Weise zu berücksichtigen; so bei Disjunktivis.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "im Zusammen der Prämisse" (in the together of the premiss), singular, where the sense requires the plural *Prämissen*; the rendering gives the plural. In the formula the variable changes from W to B ("Ist B ein Wert …"), though the law stated in the paragraph before, and the first half of this one, use W. Whether the change is Husserl's, the editor's or the conversion's cannot be settled from the file; the reading treats B and W as the same term.

The paragraph corrects its own predecessor. The objective law had just been stated with the premisses "taken together … through the form of the 'together', the 'and'" (*durch die Form des „Zusammen“ eben, des „und“*, Hua XXVIII 76). The quotation marks there already held the words at a distance, and this paragraph says why. The "and" written in the formula is a logical form; what it expresses is not a logical uniting. That is §8's division of labour applied to a single word (row 7). Logical reason formulates the value-inference, and its connective appears in the formulation, but the unity it states was made elsewhere.

Husserl's argument proceeds by subtraction. On the theoretical side, "in the together of the premisses" there stands "a unified theoretical whole" (*ein vereinigter theoretischer Bestand*). On the valuing side there is "on the one hand" (*einmal*) a valuing and "on the other" (*andererseits*) a judging, and the paired adverbs keep them apart in the syntax as in the acts. "That by itself gives as yet no unity" (*Das gibt in sich noch keine Einheit*): the "as yet" (*noch*) announces that unity will come, but from somewhere other than juxtaposition. The next sentence names the alternative that would make the inference logical, and puts it in the subjunctive: an intellectual unity "would be present if we united intellectually" the judgment "W is a value" and the hypothetical judgment. Here "W is a value" is the objectivated value-side of row 8, a judgment about value and no longer a valuing. That inference is possible, and it would be a piece of logic applied to value-predicates. Husserl rules it out as a description, not by argument: "but we make no intellectual inference at all" (*aber wir machen ja gar keinen intellektuellen Schluß*). The particle *ja* appeals to what anyone finds who carries out the valuing inference. That is the paragraph's weakest point. The claim is phenomenological, and someone who reports that he does infer intellectually from "W is a value" is not refuted by it, only told that he has done something else.

The positive account is a phrase in quotation marks, "bringing into axiological consideration", and the paragraph glosses it twice. Negatively: "This 'consideration' is no theoretical act." Positively: "the theoretical conviction gains an emotive function" (*gewinnt eine Gemütsfunktion*). The verb is the centre of the paragraph. The conviction is not replaced by a feeling, nor joined to one by a third act; it "gains" a function while remaining a conviction. The second gloss spells the function out as a direction of motivation: the conviction becomes "emotion-motivating, not theoretically motivating" (*gemütsmotivierend, nicht theoretisch motivierend*). This is the language of §9, where every law of consequence was a law of motivation. The axiological unity is a unity of motivation, in which a judgment counts as a premiss because it moves a valuing.

The last sentences return to wording and refuse every candidate. "In consideration" can be replaced by "with regard to this" (*in bezug darauf*), or "simply" by an "and" in quotation marks, followed by "if there is added" (*wenn dazukommt*). Then: "It is therefore by no means a simple 'and', and no logical 'with regard to', 'in consideration that'." The phrases language offers are all logical in form, and none of them is logical in function. The objective law in the paragraph before had already used one of them: "then with regard to this A too is a value" (*so ist mit Rücksicht darauf auch A ein Wertes*). So the "objective expression" that §10's title promises is not free of the act. It keeps a connective whose sense lies in what a valuing subject does with a conviction. Husserl does not hide this; he states that it holds "in all similar cases", disjunctives included, and so for the whole system of value-inferences.

The phrase that names the unity becomes one of the course's working words, and its sense widens. In the next pages it enters the norms for valuing inference: "If one has already held a W to be a value and brings such and such existential connections into consideration (*in Erwägung*), then one must consistently continue to value thus and so" (Hua XXVIII 79). In §17(c) "consideration" becomes the weighing of grounds: when the motivations are laid out, "the deliberation takes the form of a 'consideration'" (*die Überlegung nimmt die Form einer „Erwägung“ an*), and the weights (*Gewichte*) of the possibilities show (Hua XXVIII 121). The quotation marks recur, and the etymology of *Erwägung* in *wägen*, to weigh, now does work that §10 did not ask of it. On the will's side there is "value-consideration" (*Werterwägung*, Hua XXVIII 122), and §18(b) defines its terms over what is considered: a "considered action" (*erwogene Handlung*), or a state of affairs "standing under consideration" (*in Erwägung stehend*), is "demanded" when the will directed upon it would be convenient (Hua XXVIII 135; row 19). Beilage II's practical realm is bounded by the same word: "for my practical consideration only those goods and evils come into question for whose attainability something speaks" (Hua XXVIII 159; row 26). What began as the device that joins one judgment to one valuing becomes the field within which the will chooses.

The connective has a history in the *Logical Investigations*. There Husserl had defended the logical "and" against the "essential error" of logicians who supposed that conjunction rests on "a mere being-together-in-consciousness of the nominal and propositional acts" (*ein bloßes Zusammenbewußtsein der nominalen und propositionalen Akte*). The "and" is a categorial form, given only in "the new act of conjoining (colligating)" (*den neuen Akt des Konjungierens (Kolligierens)*), founded on the acts it unites (LU VI §51, Hua XIX/2 689). The 1914 paragraph has the same two rivals in view: mere juxtaposition ("no unity") and a founded act of synthesis (the intellectual uniting). It takes neither. The axiological "and" is not a new act that constitutes a new object, as colligation constitutes the collective; it is a change of function in an act already there. That is how the parallelism of the course can hold without logical form constituting the value-inference. The price is that the unity of the value-inference has no correlate of its own beside the values it relates. It exists in the motivation, and the formula can only quote it.

## §10, Hua XXVIII 79–80: consequence without objectivity

Reads [§10, paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#value-laws-as-the-objective-expression-of-the-motivation-laws-the-formal-axiological-laws-of-consequence).

The laws of consequence govern how values follow from values. They do not decide whether the first values are objective. A value can be "first" in the order of grounding, underived and valued for its own sake, and still be valued today and its opposite tomorrow, by one person and its opposite by another. Husserl lets a skeptic say this, and the skeptic is a new figure: he accepts the whole consequence-axiology and is refuted by none of it. Being first and being objective, both of which German can say with "in itself" (*in sich*), come apart here, and §11 is written to bring them back together.

> But it must at once be added that one can doubt whether with the necessity of assuming non-derived values the question of the unconditioned objectivity of such values is already decided. Whoever has let himself be convinced by us that consequence in valuing is not merely an empirical peculiarity of the valuing-experiences, but that it stands, just like consequence in judging, under apriori norms to which there correspond apriori laws for the ideal states of value, whoever, I say, has let himself be convinced of this can perhaps still try to defend the following standpoint. If I value A, he may say, then under the given motivation-situations I have to value B, C … in descending direction; reason in consequence demands this, the formal laws of consequence demand it; and certainly I must in doing so, if A is not already valuable in itself but only derivatively, come in the end to a first A that is valuable in itself. But that does not say that A is valuable in itself in the sense of genuine objectivity, of independence from the contingently valuing subject and its contingent mood. There are, precisely, in each case unmotivated valuings, and there must be such; but one time I value A unmotivated and the other time non-A; and just so the one values A and the other non-A. Someone could reject skepticism in the theoretical respect and yet be a skeptic in the axiological ⟨respect⟩, and that although he may acknowledge the consequence-axiology. And that I should have to behave consistently also in the sense that I would have to value, and ought to value, the same A again and again for all eternity, that, he would say, no reason prescribes. What matters here is that the axioms so far do not exclude axiological skepticism. With the clarification of axiological reason in the form of axiological consequence we are not, however, at an end. At any rate this is a first and important field. We shall see how we get further.

German:

> Es muß aber gleich hinzugefügt, daß man zweifeln kann, ob mit der Notwendigkeit der Annahme nicht-derivierter Werte auch schon die Frage der unbedingten Objektivität solcher Werte entschieden ist. Wer sich durch uns hat überzeugen lassen, daß Konsequenz im Werten nicht nur eine empirische Eigentümlichkeit der Wertungserlebnisse ist, sondern daß sie genau so wie die Konsequenz im Urteilen unter apriorischen Normen steht, denen apriorische Gesetze für die idealen Wertverhalte entsprechen, wer, sage ich, davon sich hat überzeugen lassen, kann vielleicht noch folgenden Standpunkt zu vertreten versuchen: Wenn ich, mag er sagen, A werte, dann habe ich unter den gegebenen Motivationslagen in absteigender Richtung B C ... zu werten, das fordert die Vernunft in der Konsequenz, das fordern die formalen Gesetze der Konsequenz; und gewiß muß ich dabei, wenn A nicht schon in sich selbst wert ist, sondern nur abgeleitet, schließlich auf ein erstes, in sich wertes A kommen. Aber damit ist nicht gesagt, daß A in sich wert sei im Sinne der echten Objektivität, der Unabhängigkeit vom zufällig wertenden Subjekt und seiner zufälligen Stimmung. Es gibt eben jeweils unmotivierte Wertungen und es muß solche geben; aber einmal werte ich unmotiviert A und das andere Mal non-A; und ebenso wertet der eine A und der andere non-A. Es könnte einer in theoretischer Beziehung den Skeptizismus ablehnen und doch in der axiologischen ⟨Beziehung⟩ Skeptiker sein, und das, trotzdem er die Konsequenzaxiologie anerkennen mag. Und daß ich mich auch in dem Sinn konsequent verhalten müßte, daß ich immer wieder dasselbe A in alle Ewigkeit werten müßte und werten sollte, das, würde er sagen, schreibe keine Vernunft vor. Worauf es hier ankommt, ist, daß die bisherigen Axiome den axiologischen Skeptizismus nicht ausschließen. Mit der Klarstellung der axiologischen Vernunft in Form der axiologischen Konsequenz sind wir aber auch nicht zu Ende. Jedenfalls ist das ein erstes und wichtiges Gebiet. Wir werden schon sehen, wie wir weiterkommen.

*Note on the text.* The first sentence lacks its auxiliary in the file ("Es muß aber gleich hinzugefügt", for *hinzugefügt werden*); the rendering supplies it. The word *Konsequenz* is split across the page break to Hua XXVIII 80 ("Konse- … quenz"), with a comment of margin line numbers between the halves; the quotation closes the word. The editor's angle brackets around *Beziehung* are given as ⟨…⟩.

The skeptic's foothold is supplied by the law stated just before. Every motivation-connection is finite, so value-grounding must come to "first value-grounds that are no longer, so to speak, 'inferred' values" (*nicht mehr sozusagen „erschlossene“ Werte*, Hua XXVIII 79). The law was meant to secure the system of derivations against regress. The skeptic draws a different consequence from it: "There are, precisely, in each case unmotivated valuings, and there must be such" (*und es muß solche geben*). The "must" is Husserl's own, borrowed from the finitude law. What stops the regress is a valuing that nothing motivates, and of such a valuing the laws of motivation can say nothing. Their whole jurisdiction is the step from a valuing already made to the next one.

The argument is carried by an equivocation in "in itself", and the skeptic names it. The first occurrence is the finitude law's: "a first A that is valuable in itself" (*ein erstes, in sich wertes A*) is simply an A that is not "only derivatively" valuable. The adjectives "first" and "in itself" say the same thing, a position in the order of grounding. The second occurrence adds a qualification that the first lacked: "valuable in itself in the sense of genuine objectivity" (*im Sinne der echten Objektivität*), glossed as "independence from the contingently valuing subject and its contingent mood" (*seiner zufälligen Stimmung*). "Mood" is the skeptic's word for what the finitude law leaves at the root. The word "genuine" (*echten*) concedes that the first sense of "in itself" was a sense of objectivity too, but not the one that matters.

A second word divides in the same way. The skeptic accepts that "reason in consequence demands" (*das fordert die Vernunft in der Konsequenz*) the descending series B, C … once A is valued. He denies that he must behave "consistently" (*konsequent*) "also in the sense" that he would have to value "the same A again and again for all eternity" (*immer wieder dasselbe A in alle Ewigkeit*). *Konsequenz* means inferential coherence, the following of consequent from ground; the skeptic refuses to let it mean constancy over time. The repeated "and the other time non-A", "the one … and the other non-A", extends the refusal from one person's moments to several persons. The theoretical parallel shows what is missing. No one thinks that consequence in judging guarantees the truth of the premisses; the premisses' truth is a matter of truth, not of consequence. Axiology has so far only consequence. It has not yet stated its analogue of truth.

The skeptic is given his position in reported speech, and the mood of the verbs keeps Husserl at a distance from it: "he may say" (*mag er sagen*), "that, he would say, no reason prescribes" (*schreibe keine Vernunft vor*, subjunctive). But Husserl does not answer him. He concedes the point in his own voice: "the axioms so far do not exclude axiological skepticism." This is a different skeptic from the one §4 refuted. §4's skeptic, who denied practical rules, contradicted himself in the form of his own demand (row 2). This skeptic accepts every form; "he may acknowledge the consequence-axiology". The countersense argument cannot reach him, because he has made no formal claim for it to cancel. He also marks where the parallel between the reasons is weakest: "someone could reject skepticism in the theoretical respect and yet be a skeptic in the axiological ⟨respect⟩". The parallelism of §1 claimed that the two reasons stand or fall together, and this sentence allows that they can come apart in a single mind.

The answer comes in §11, and its wording shows how directly it addresses this passage. §11(b) defines "being-valuable-in-itself" (*In-sich-wert-Sein*) as before: value-being "not derived from another value-being", so that no values are presupposed (Hua XXVIII 87). That is the skeptic's first sense, the order of grounding. The step to objectivity is taken by an axiom: for every matter, "one of the three cases is true", positive value in itself, negative value in itself, or value-freedom, the law of excluded fourth, which then "transfers itself to the derived values, which thereby become objective values" (Hua XXVIII 88; row 12). The axiom closes the gap that the skeptic's "one time A, the other time non-A" exploited, because it lets only one of the cases be true of a given matter. But it closes it by stipulation. What follows is stated under a condition: "what stands firm, if the axioms are correct" (*wenn die Axiome richtig sind*) is that value does not dissolve into the subjectivity of valuing, and that "as little as there is a true and false for someone", so little is there a good and bad for someone (Hua XXVIII 89). The defence of the axioms is then declined in so many words. Objectivism "must be fought through" against relativist, psychologist, anthropologist and biologist skepticism, but "that is a chapter of its own. We will not go into it and will treat the fight as one that is decided" (*den Kampf wie einen entschieden behandeln*, Hua XXVIII 89).

So the skeptic of Hua XXVIII 80 is never refuted in the course; he is excluded by an axiom whose ground is left for another occasion. The promise in the passage's last sentence, "we shall see how we get further", is kept in the sense that the course goes further, not in the sense that it answers him. His point also returns in another form at the end of the course. §21 asks how far practical possibilities and the "convenience" of a will are relative to the subject, and finds there a role for subjectivity that has "no analogy" in the sphere of judgment (Hua XXVIII 149, a sentence that breaks off in the file; row 22). The skeptic's "contingent mood" is not readmitted there, but the clean separation of "for someone" from "in itself" that §11 claimed for values does not hold without remainder for the will.

## §11(a), Hua XXVIII 86: bad is not not-good

Reads [§11(a), paragraph 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#a-the-reference-to-the-motivation-situation-in-the-analogue-of-the-law-of-contradiction-in-the-emotive-sphere-value-freedom-and-the-appertaining-axioms).

"Good" and "bad" are contraries, not contradictories. Logic excludes "good" and "not-good" of the same thing, as it excludes any predicate and its negation; but "not-good" covers the bad and also the value-free, so logic says nothing about whether good and bad exclude each other. That they do, under the same presuppositions, is an axiological law. The axiological law of contradiction therefore cannot be derived from the logical one, and the formal axiology is a discipline of its own and not a chapter of logic applied to value-words. The argument turns on the difference between two German forms, *schlecht* or *übel* (bad, evil) and *nicht-gut* (not-good), and on a third, *ungut* (un-good), which ordinary speech uses for the first while its form suggests the second.

> With regard to our laws one must beware of taking them for analytic-logical self-evidences. For example, one could think that an A's being good under certain presuppositions and its being not good under the same presuppositions is analytically-logically self-evidently ⟨impossible⟩. To this, however, two things are to be replied. First: when in our axioms there is talk of positive and negative value, of good and bad, this is by no means the same as if the talk were of good and not-good. In ordinary speech, to be sure, "un-good" and "not-good" are also used for "evil", for "bad". But that is an equivocal distinction. Analytically-logically, predicates such as "good" and "not-good" exclude each other in the same sense as ⟨a⟩ and non-a in general as arbitrary predicates, that is, as the ascribing of a (of "good") and the denying of "good". But analytically-logically "evil" can as little be slipped under "not-good" as "black" under "not-white" ⟨in place of⟩ that which has some other visual quality.

German:

> In Bezug auf unsere Gesetze muß man sich hüten, sie für analytisch-logische Selbstverständlichkeiten zu halten. Zum Beispiel könnte man meinen, daß ein A unter gewissen Voraussetzungen gut sei und daß es unter denselben Voraussetzungen nicht gut sei, das sei analytisch logisch selbstverständlich ⟨unmöglich⟩. Darauf aber ist zweierlei zu entgegnen. Fürs erste: Wenn in unseren Axiomen von positivem und negativem Wert, von gut und schlecht die Rede ist, so ist das keineswegs dasselbe, als ob die Rede sei von gut und nicht-gut. In gewöhnlicher Rede wird allerdings „ungut“ und „nicht-gut“ auch für „übel“, für „schlecht“ gebraucht. Aber das ist eine äquivoke Unterscheidung. Analytisch-logisch schließen sich Prädikate wie „gut“ und „nicht-gut“ in demselben Sinn aus wie und non-a überhaupt als beliebige Prädikate, das heißt, wie Zusprechen des a (des „gut“) und Absprechen des „gut“. Aber analytisch-logisch kann ebensowenig dem „nicht-gut“ das „übel“ unterschoben werden wie dem „nicht-weiß“ das „schwarz“ ⟨statt⟩ das irgendeine andere visuelle Qualität Habende.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "wie und non-a", with the first term missing; the sense and the next clause ("Zusprechen des a") require "wie a und non-a", and the rendering supplies ⟨a⟩. The two words in angle brackets, ⟨unmöglich⟩ and ⟨statt⟩, are the editor's additions, given as LaTeX in the file. The last clause is compressed even with ⟨statt⟩: "black" cannot be put under "not-white" in place of "whatever has some other visual quality", which is what "not-white" means.

The opponent's sentence is built to look like a contradiction. "An A's being good under certain presuppositions and its being not good under the same presuppositions" pairs "good" (*gut*) with "not good" (*nicht gut*), and the pair is impossible "analytically-logically". Husserl grants this and denies that it is the axiom. The axioms speak "of positive and negative value, of good and bad" (*von gut und schlecht*), and the phrase "by no means the same" (*keineswegs dasselbe*) marks the whole difference between the two disciplines. If the axiom were the exclusion of good and not-good, it would be the law of contradiction with "good" put in for "a", and §11's title, "the law of contradiction in the axiological sphere", would name an application of logic. Because the axiom excludes good and bad, it states a relation between two determinate value-qualities, which logic, abstracting from all matter, cannot see.

The sentence on ordinary speech says where the confusion comes from. "Un-good" (*ungut*) and "not-good" are "also" (*auch*) used for "evil" and "bad". The German prefix *un-* is the hinge. In form it negates, as "not" does; in use it often names the opposite quality, as *unschön* names the ugly and not merely the not-beautiful. A language that says *ungut* for *übel* has folded a contrary into the shape of a contradictory. Husserl calls this "an equivocal distinction" (*eine äquivoke Unterscheidung*), an odd but exact phrase: the distinction between "good" and its opposite is drawn with a word that means two things. English is in the same position. "Not good" in ordinary use often means "bad", so a rendering that says "not good" where Husserl writes *schlecht* would reproduce the equivocation he is exposing. That is why the original stays beside the English here.

The comparison with colour makes the logical point and strains the axiological one. "Not-white" is satisfied by anything that has "some other visual quality", and "black" is one such quality among many; it cannot be "slipped under" (*unterschoben*) "not-white" as if it were what "not-white" means. The verb *unterschieben* has a sense of substitution by sleight, and it charges the logicist reading with a substitution of that kind. The analogy shows that "evil" is narrower than "not-good". But it does not show the structure that the axioms need. Colours form a field with many species, so that black is one of indefinitely many non-whites. The value-field, as the surrounding axioms describe it, has exactly three places: positive, negative and value-free. "Not-good" covers just two of them, the bad and the *Adiaphoron* (the value-indifferent). The colour comparison is evidence that the contrary is not the contradictory, and no more than that. The polarity of value, which makes "bad" the opposite of "good" rather than one of its many others, is the thing the axiology asserts, and the comparison neither shows nor needs it.

The paragraph also answers a looseness in the section's own earlier wording. At its opening §11(a) had stated the correlate of the motivation-law by saying that "'contradictorily' opposed values" exclude each other in the same content, "for example that … it be joyful and be unjoyful" (*daß es erfreulich sei und unerfreulich sei*, Hua XXVIII 81). The quotation marks around *kontradiktorisch* already held the logical term at a distance, and the example used the prefix *un-* in exactly the sense this paragraph calls equivocal: *unerfreulich* there means grief-worthy, not merely not-joyful. Just before the passage, Husserl had said that the axiology needs "its own concept 'value'" (*einen eigenen Begriff „Wert“*), as what is general to positive and negative value, and had stated an axiom in which the contradictory of "value" in general is the value-free: "If A is a value, then it is not not a value, i.e. it is no Adiaphoron" (*so ist es nicht nicht ein Wert gleich so ist es kein Adiaphoron*, Hua XXVIII 85). The logical "not" thus has a place in the axiology, but at the level of value in general, where it separates the valuable from the indifferent. It has no place between good and bad. The passage under reading draws that line in the vocabulary.

Its second point (the same paragraph's "Second", which follows the quotation) says why the line matters for the whole discipline. Logical laws transfer to value-predicates, "and first to the general predicate 'value'", only on the presupposition of axiological laws, those that establish "that value can be treated as a predicate" at all (Hua XXVIII 86). The direction of dependence is the reverse of the logicist's. Logic does not ground the axiology by lending it the law of contradiction; the axiology must first make value an objective predicate before logic has anything to work on. The next subsection supplies the law that does this. Because there are three places, not two, the axiological counterpart of excluded middle becomes "a law of excluded fourth" (Hua XXVIII 88; row 12), and the name records what this paragraph established: the value-field cannot be cut in two by a "not".

The prefix returns on the will's side, with the same warning. In §17(d) Husserl analyses will-negation, the "I will not" that crosses out a soliciting but uncompleted "I will", and observes that "we sometimes designate this negativity with the ⟨expression⟩ 'to be unwilling'" (*„unwillig sein“*), "but the ⟨expression⟩ is ambiguous (*zweideutig*), since *Unwille* and 'being unwilling' very often designate the phenomenon of indignation" (Hua XXVIII 125; row 17). *Unwille* is to the will what *ungut* is to value: a word whose prefix promises a negation and whose use names an opposed quality, here an emotion of rejection. In both places the course separates the "not" of grammar from the opposition in the sphere it is describing, and in both it finds ordinary German blurring the two with the same prefix. The new formal disciplines are built on that separation.

## §11(b), Hua XXVIII 87–88: the law of excluded fourth

Reads [§11(b), paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-law-of-excluded-fourth-for-the-basic-values-the-objectivity-of-values).

For any matter, within a given region of values, exactly one of three cases holds: it is in itself positively valuable, in itself negatively valuable, or in itself value-free. This is the law of excluded fourth (*Gesetz vom ausgeschlossenen Vierten*). It is the axiology's counterpart of the logical law of excluded middle, which German calls the law of excluded *third* (*Satz vom ausgeschlossenen Dritten*), and its name counts the cases: where logic has two and excludes a third, the value-sphere has three and excludes a fourth. The law does more than classify. It makes every valuing answerable to a fact about its matter, so that a valuing is valid or invalid as a judgment is true or false. With it the derived values of §10 cease to be values merely "if one lets A stand" and become objective. The axiom is the course's answer to the skeptic who accepted consequence and denied objectivity (row 10).

> We have already said earlier that all value-derivation leads to last basic-values that are not themselves derived. But now it is not merely the case that, once the valuing of an A is factually carried out, it is rational in consequence of it to value B, and that B is therefore relatively valuable if one presupposes A and lets it stand. Rather it is to be claimed as an evidence that one can express this first objectively thus: "If A is a value, then B is a value"; and further that one can say: "Every value-presupposition is to be decided objectively as to validity and non-validity." To this, however, there belongs first that, if M is the matter of the value-positing A, it can be said *apriori*: if M is the matter of a positive value-positing, then, if this positing is valid, the corresponding negative positing or adiaphoric positing is invalid, and conversely, according to all disjunctive combinations. Precisely this we have stated as a law, and indeed as valid for any matter whatever.
>
> Parallel to this we set up the axiom: if M is any matter whatever, then (and always within any axiological region whatever) one of the three cases is true: either M is the matter of a positive value in itself, or of a negative one, or it is in itself value-free. So for the sphere of in-itself-values and value-freedoms we have here an analogue of the law of contradiction and of excluded third, only that the latter is here a law of excluded fourth, which then, as we shall hear presently, transfers itself to the derived values, which thereby become objective values.

German:

> Wir haben früher schon gesagt, daß alle Wertableitung auf letzte Grundwerte führt, die nicht selbst abgeleitet sind. Es ist nun aber nicht bloß so, daß wenn das Werten eines A nun einmal faktisch vollzogen ist, es in Konsequenz davon vernünftig ist, das B zu werten, und daß B somit relativ wert ist, wenn man das A eben voraussetzt und gelten läßt. Vielmehr ist es als eine Evidenz in Anspruch zu nehmen, daß man dies zunächst objektiv so ausdrücken kann: „Wenn A ein Wert ist, so ist B ein Wert“; und weiter, daß man sagen kann: „Jede Wertvoraussetzung ist objektiv nach Geltung und Nichtgeltung zu entscheiden“. Dazu aber gehört fürs erste, daß wenn M die Materie der Wertsetzung A ist, *apriori* gesagt werden kann: Ist M Materie einer positiven Wertsetzung, so ist, wenn diese Setzung gültig ist, die entsprechende negative Setzung oder adiaphorische Setzung ungültig und umgekehrt nach allen disjunktiven Kombinationen. Eben das haben wir gesetzmäßig ausgesprochen, und zwar als gültig für jede beliebige Materie.
>
> Parallel dazu stellen wir das Axiom auf: Ist M eine beliebige Materie, so ist (und immer innerhalb einer beliebigen axiologischen Region) einer der drei Fälle wahr, entweder M ist Materie eines positiven Wertes in sich oder eines negativen oder ist in sich wertfrei. Also für die Sphäre der An-sich-Werte und Wertfreiheiten haben wir hier ein Analogon des Satzes vom Widerspruch und vom ausgeschlossenen Dritten, nur daß letzteres hier ein Gesetz vom ausgeschlossenen Vierten ist, das sich dann, wie wir gleich hören werden, auf die abgeleiteten Werte überträgt, die damit zu objektiven Werten werden.

*Note on the text.* A page break (Hua XXVIII 88) falls between the two paragraphs, marked in the file by a bare "88" comment followed by an unnumbered running head. Neither paragraph is damaged.

The first paragraph moves from fact to claim. Its opening sentence restates the finitude law, and its second describes the position the skeptic of §10 was happy to hold: once a valuing of A "is factually carried out" (*nun einmal faktisch vollzogen ist*), valuing B is rational "in consequence", and B is "relatively" valuable "if one presupposes A and lets it stand" (*wenn man das A eben voraussetzt und gelten läßt*). The phrase "lets it stand" names a permission, not a validity. The word that turns the paragraph is "Rather" (*Vielmehr*), and what follows it is not an argument but a claim staked: "it is to be claimed as an evidence" (*als eine Evidenz in Anspruch zu nehmen*). *In Anspruch nehmen* is to lay claim to something as one's due. Husserl does not show that value-presuppositions can be decided; he asserts that their decidability is self-evident. The claim is then given two formulations, both in quotation marks, as sentences one "can say". The first converts the subject's consequence into an objective hypothetical ("If A is a value, then B is a value"). The second is the stronger: "Every value-presupposition is to be decided objectively as to validity and non-validity." The skeptic of §10 allowed the first and denied the second.

The second formulation needs a law about what is decided, and that is where the count of cases enters. If M is the matter of a positive valuing, and that valuing is valid, "the corresponding negative positing or adiaphoric positing is invalid" (*die entsprechende negative Setzung oder adiaphorische Setzung ungültig*). There are three positings, not two, because the *Adiaphoron* (the value-indifferent) is itself a position taken on the matter, not the absence of one. §11(a) had said so: indifference too is "objectively decided by valuing behaviour and value-consideration" (Hua XXVIII 85). The phrase "according to all disjunctive combinations" (*nach allen disjunktiven Kombinationen*) extends the exclusion to every pair of the three. That is the analogue of the law of contradiction, and it is stated as already proved: "Precisely this we have stated as a law" (*Eben das haben wir gesetzmäßig ausgesprochen*).

The axiom is introduced by a different verb. The law of contradiction was "stated"; the excluded fourth is "set up" (*stellen wir das Axiom auf*). It says that "one of the three cases is true" (*einer der drei Fälle wahr*), and this is the step from exclusion to completeness. Exclusion says that no two cases hold together; completeness says that one always holds. The skeptic's "one time A, the other time non-A" (Hua XXVIII 80) is ruled out only by the second, since he could grant that no single valuing is both positive and negative and still deny that the matter decides between them. The word "true" is the logician's, and Husserl uses it here without quotation marks, because the axiom is a judgment about values and its truth is doxic. Only a page before he had written "axiological validity or 'truth'" with the second word in quotation marks (*einer axiologischen Geltung oder „Wahrheit“*, Hua XXVIII 85), and a footnote to this subsection regrets that "we have here unfortunately no words that correspond exactly to the words truth and falsity" (*Wir haben hier leider keine Worte, die genau den Worten Wahrheit und Falschheit entsprechen*). The value-sphere has validity (*Geltung*) and non-validity; truth belongs to what is said about it.

The axiom has two qualifications, and both fix what objectivity can mean here. It holds "always within any axiological region" (*immer innerhalb einer beliebigen axiologischen Region*). §11(a) had illustrated value-freedom one category at a time: a content may or may not "come into question" as a content of wishing, may or may not concern "the idea of beauty" (Hua XXVIII 85). So "value-free" is not absolute. It is the third value of a determinate category, and the axiom is a law for each category, not for value as such. The second qualification is "in itself" (*in sich*). The paragraph before defined it: value-being "not derived from another value-being", hanging, if on presuppositions at all, only on presuppositions that are "not axiological", such as "a being or non-being" (Hua XXVIII 87). That was the first of the two senses of "in itself" that the skeptic separated (row 10): position in the order of grounding. The axiom joins it to the second, independence from the valuer, by fiat. The basic values are the values that are underived, and of these the axiom says that one case is true.

The name of the law records all this. The logical *Satz vom ausgeschlossenen Dritten* gets its name from what it excludes: between "it is so" and "it is not so" there is no third (Hua XXVIII 82 had stated it in just those terms). Husserl keeps the construction and changes the number. The new law does not exclude a middle between two poles. The Adiaphoron is the middle, and it is admitted as a case. What is excluded is a fourth possibility, a matter that is neither valuable, nor disvaluable, nor indifferent in a given respect, a matter about which there would be no fact for valuing to be right about. The count is deliberate. Where there are two cases, Husserl keeps the old name: in the 1911 course the correlates of realizing will and of omission stand under a law "analogous to the law of contradiction and of excluded third of logic" (*analog ist dem Satz vom Widerspruch und vom ausgeschlossenen Dritten der Logik*, Hua XXVIII 215), since one either realizes or omits. The 1914 coinage marks the place where the value-sphere, unlike the will and unlike belief, has a neutral case of its own (row 11).

The paragraph's last clause announces the payoff: the law "transfers itself to the derived values, which thereby become objective values". The next paragraph draws it: every derived value gains "its objectivity and its not merely hypothetical validity", and value-consequences hold "objectively, in itself, independently of subjectivity" (Hua XXVIII 88). Then the condition already noted under row 10 is added: this stands firm "if the axioms are correct" (Hua XXVIII 89), and the fight against relativism is treated "as one that is decided". The excluded fourth is therefore the course's point of maximal commitment. It is what turns consequence into objectivity, and it is set up, as an axiom, and claimed as evident, rather than derived. Everything that follows in the formal axiology and the *Praktik*, down to the "objectively demanded" of §18 (row 19), presupposes that for every matter in every region there is a fact of the matter about its value.

## §12(b), Hua XXVIII 96: value-production

Reads [§12(b), paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-different-relations-between-the-value-whole-and-the-value-components-value-summation-and-value-production-the-significance-of-time-extension-and-intensity-for-the-determination-of-value).

A whole of values can be valuable in two ways. In a summative whole the parts keep the value they have alone, and the whole is worth what they are worth together. In a productive whole the way the parts are joined founds a new value-unity, which depends on the parts but is not made out of them. There goodness no longer adds up: adding a good can lower the value of the whole, and adding an evil can raise it. This is what *Wertproduktion* (value-production) means. The name sets "product" against "sum", and Husserl's own example, a musical harmony, shows that "composing" can mean either. The distinction confines Brentano's laws of value-summation to the summative case, and with it the formal axiology admits that value-wholes have a structure its laws of consequence do not reach.

> If we consider the relations between value-wholes and their value-components, two things are to be distinguished. It can be that the whole has value only with axiological regard to its possessing precisely those value-composing parts, and indeed in such a way that the merely general possessing of these parts determines the value of the whole exclusively in the manner of value-transfer. So the connection of the value-founding parts is to have no further axiological significance beyond the fact that they are connected; through the connection, as a connection of just this kind, no peculiar axiological product is to arise.
>
> The second is that precisely this excluded thing takes place. Through the peculiar connection of the parts into a whole of this kind, and through the way the own values of the parts work together axiologically, a value-unity is to arise that is more than the collective unity of the value-components: a unity that does not, as in the previous case, simply have the values of the parts in itself and merely link these values, but that, on the basis of the mutual influencing of these values, creates something new, which is indeed dependent on them, and so does not compose them in the proper sense, but composes them somewhat in the way in which we speak of composition in music. In fact every harmony of tones, and likewise every harmony of colours, forms a fitting example. The sensuous and feeling-values of the single elements contribute in a certain sense to the total value, but they do not put it together; they only found it as something essentially new over against them. If we think of the elements as shaken together at random, each still has its content and, considered in itself, its value-character. Every whole so formed has, one can say, with reference to the having of these value-elements, a relative, derivation-value. But over against this, every combination grounds an axiological whole with an axiological unity-character, which is founded in the elements but is by no means built up out of them summatively. A certain combination yields the beauty of harmony, which with a change of the combination turns into disharmony or a confused jumble. Here what is in itself beautiful and ugly, good and bad, can ground an axiological unity, and indeed a positive one, which is not harmed by the co-givenness of the bad but is raised by it. The omission of an element or complex of elements displeasing in itself can lower the positive value of the harmony instead of raising it, and likewise the omission of something beautiful in itself, or its replacement by something less beautiful in itself, can raise the value of the whole instead of lowering it, and so on. Here, then, G + G′ < G, G + U > G instead of < G, etc. can hold.

German:

> Betrachten wir die Verhältnisse zwischen Wertganzen und ihren Wertkomponenten, so ist zweierlei zu unterscheiden. Es kann sein, daß das Ganze nur mit axiologischer Rücksicht darauf Wert hat, als eben jene wertkomponierenden Teile besitzt, und zwar so, daß das bloß allgemeine Besitzen dieser Teile ausschließlich in der Weise der Wertübertragung den Wert des Ganzen bestimmt. Also, die Verbindung der wertfundierenden Teile soll weiter keine axiologische Bedeutung darüber hinaus haben, daß sie eben verbunden sind; es soll durch die Verbindung als Verbindung gerade solcher Art kein eigenartiges axiologisches Produkt erwachsen.
>
> Das zweite ist, daß eben dieses Ausgeschlossene statthat. Durch die eigenartige Verbindung der Teile zu einem Ganzen dieser Art und durch die Weise, wie die eigenen Werte der Teile axiologisch zusammenwirken, soll eine Werteinheit entstehen, die mehr ist als die kollektive Einheit der Wertkomponenten, eine Einheit, die nicht wie im vorigen Fall die Werte der Teile einfach in sich hat und diese Werte bloß verknüpft, sondern die aufgrund der Wechselbeeinflussung dieser Werte ein Neues schafft, das von ihnen zwar abhängig ist, sie also nicht im eigentlichen Sinn komponiert, sondern komponiert etwa in der Weise, wie wir in der Musik von Komposition sprechen. In der Tat bildet jede Tonharmonie und ebenso jede Harmonie von Farben ein passendes Beispiel. Die sinnlichen und Gefühlswerte der einzelnen Elemente tragen zum Gesamtwert in gewissem Sinn bei, aber sie setzen ihn nicht zusammen, sie fundieren ihn nur als ein ihnen gegenüber wesentlich Neues. Denken wir uns die Elemente beliebig durcheinandergewürfelt, so hat jedes immerfort seinen Inhalt und an sich betrachtet seinen Wertcharakter. Jedes so gebildete Ganze hat, kann man sagen, mit Beziehung auf das Haben dieser Wertelemente relativen, Ableitungswert. Aber demgegenüber begründet jede Kombination ein axiologisches Ganzes mit einem axiologischen Einheitscharakter, der in den Elementen fundiert ist, aber sich keineswegs aus ihnen summatorisch aufbaut. Eine gewisse Kombination ergibt die Schönheit der Harmonie, die sich bei Änderung der Kombination in Disharmonie oder wirres Durcheinander umwandelt. Hierbei kann in sich Schönes und Häßliches, Gutes und Schlechtes eine axiologische Einheit begründen, und zwar eine positive Werte, die nicht etwa durch das Mitgegebensein des Schlechten geschädigt, sondern dadurch erhöht ist. Das Weglassen eines in sich mißfälligen Elements oder Elementenkomplexes kann den positiven Wert der Harmonie erniedrigen statt zu erhöhen, und ebenso kann auch das Weglassen eines in sich Schönen oder ein Ersatz durch sich minder Schönes den Wert des Ganzen erhöhen statt zu erniedrigen usw. Hier kann also G + G′ < G, G + U > G statt < G sein etc.

*Note on the text.* Two small faults in the file do not touch the argument. "nur mit axiologischer Rücksicht darauf Wert hat, als eben jene … Teile besitzt" lacks a subject for *besitzt* (presumably *als es eben …*), and "und zwar eine positive Werte" has lost or garbled its noun (presumably *eine positive Werteinheit*, a positive value-unity); the rendering says "a positive one". The formulas are given in the file as LaTeX. The block in the main commentary renders the clause on composition as "not composed by them in the proper sense, but composed rather …". The German makes the new unity the subject and the part-values the object ("das … sie also nicht im eigentlichen Sinn komponiert, sondern komponiert …"): the new does not compose them in the proper sense, but composes them as music does. The thesis is unaffected, since the next sentence says that the parts "do not put it together"; the difference is logged in the key-passage list.

The first paragraph defines summation by what it excludes, and the definition is all negation. The whole has value "only" with regard to its having the parts; the parts' "merely general possessing" (*das bloß allgemeine Besitzen*) determines the whole's value "exclusively" by transfer; the connection has "no further axiological significance beyond the fact that they are connected"; "no peculiar axiological product is to arise". The modal verb *soll* (is to) in the last two sentences marks the definition as a stipulation, a limiting case set up so that the second can be measured against it. The word that the second case will make positive appears first under a negation: *Produkt* (product). The opposition of the two cases is the opposition of the two operations of arithmetic. In a sum the terms survive unchanged and the result is their aggregate; in a product they enter into a result in which they cannot be found as parts. Husserl's formulas at the end keep the plus sign precisely to show where it fails: "G + G′ < G".

The second paragraph opens with a phrase that makes the dependence on the first exact: "that precisely this excluded thing takes place" (*daß eben dieses Ausgeschlossene statthat*). Production is the excluded product, admitted. Its description sets three verbs against three. The summative whole "has" (*hat*) the part-values "in itself" and "merely links" (*bloß verknüpft*) them; the productive unity "creates something new" (*schafft ein Neues*) "on the basis of the mutual influencing" (*Wechselbeeinflussung*) of the values. The compound noun carries the criterion. In the summative case, as the next paragraph says, the components are values "in the whole just as they are for themselves alone" and "do not influence one another" (Hua XXVIII 97). In the productive case each value is what it is in the whole only in relation to the others.

Then comes the word that has to carry both cases at once. The new is dependent on the part-values "and so does not compose them in the proper sense, but composes them somewhat in the way in which we speak of composition in music". *Komponieren* is used twice in one breath with two senses. In the "proper sense" it is the Latin *componere*, to put together, and in that sense the next sentence replaces it with the German equivalent: the elements "do not put it together" (*setzen ihn nicht zusammen*). In the musical sense, to compose is to make a piece in which tones stand in relations that give each its place and the whole its character. The sentence takes back the first sense and keeps the second under a hedge, "somewhat" (*etwa*). The hedge is fair. A musical composition is the work of a composer, and nothing in the value-whole composes; the word imports an agent that the analysis does not need. What it does supply is the right relation of whole to part: the parts are indispensable and yet the value of the whole is not theirs.

The thought-experiment that follows fixes the difference without the metaphor. "Shaken together at random" (*beliebig durcheinandergewürfelt*, the verb of dice), the elements keep "their content and, considered in itself, their value-character", and every such jumble has a "relative, derivation-value" from the values it contains. Over against this, "every combination grounds an axiological whole with an axiological unity-character, which is founded in the elements but is by no means built up out of them summatively". "Founded" (*fundiert*) is the same word that §9 used for the valuing act's relation to its presentation (row 8), and it has the same force here: dependence without composition. The examples then turn the founding into arithmetic that fails. A positive whole can be "raised" by "the co-givenness of the bad"; omitting what displeases can lower the harmony; replacing the beautiful by the less beautiful can raise the whole. The consequence is that "good" and "bad" in a part do not predict the sign of their contribution. For a formal axiology built on laws of transfer (§10), this is a serious limit. The laws of consequence hold for derivation-value, and production is precisely value that is not derived.

The next paragraph draws the limit and turns it against Brentano. "The Brentanian laws are essentially improved by our distinction; only in the restriction to summative values can and must they be maintained" (*nur in der Einschränkung auf summatorische Werte können sie und müssen sie aufrechterhalten werden*, Hua XXVIII 97). The "can and must" keeps the summation-laws as valid within their domain while denying them universality. §12(a) had already prepared this: equals substituted for equals give equals, "but only in the relations of summation (of the summative wholes)" (Hua XXVIII 91). The restriction returns at once in the treatment of time. Duration "works like a summation", yet "the times will not come into consideration simply as mathematical quantities for the summation" (Hua XXVIII 98), because below a certain duration a person can achieve nothing considerable. The will-laws of §18 still use summation where it fits: the negative consequence-values of a good "diminish the good-value through summation" (*mindern durch Summation den Gutwert*, Hua XXVIII 131). Production makes no further appearance under its own name in the course.

The concept has a source in Husserl's own theory of wholes. The third Logical Investigation distinguishes connections that rest on "mere one-sided or mutual foundings" from those in which the being-together of the parts founds "a form-content of its own, a unity-moment of its own" (*ein eigener Forminhalt, ein eigenes Einheitsmoment*). Its examples are the same as the 1914 course's: "tones in the unity of the melody, or piecewise separated colourings in the unity of the colour-configuration" (LU III §22, Hua XIX/1 284). The 1914 "axiological unity-character … founded in the elements" (*axiologischer Einheitscharakter … in den Elementen fundiert*) is that unity-moment carried over into value. In 1901 the unity-moment was a sensuous form that founds nothing beyond itself. In 1914 its axiological counterpart is what decides the value of the whole, and it decides against the arithmetic of the parts. The formal axiology thus takes over a mereology built for intuition, and finds that applied to value it limits the reach of its own laws of consequence.

## §14, Hua XXVIII 105–106: "it may be", "it shall be"

Reads [§14, paragraph 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#wish-and-will).

Wishing and willing are modalizations of being, as believing, surmising and doubting are, but "of a new dimension". Belief means "it is"; surmise means "it is probably"; the wish means "it may be" (*es möge sein*); the will means "it shall be" (*es soll sein*). The difference between the act-genera is carried by the mood of a single verb. The doxic modalities change the adverb attached to "is" (possibly, probably); the emotive ones change the verb's mood, from indicative to optative and to the modal "shall". All acts "go upon being", then, but not all of them in the indicative. The claim lets Husserl treat the will as a position-taking with its own modalities, which §17 will parallel to the doxic ones. It also leaves the will's own word unsettled. The "it shall" is to be taken "in a definite sense", which the passage does not give, and the course replaces it in the next section.

> Wish and will both go in a certain way upon a being, as indeed in a certain way all acts go upon being. Belief is meaning of being, the modalizations of belief are meanings of a modalized being, of a "being-possible", "being-probable", and so on. The emotive acts too are to be grasped as modalizations of being, though of a new dimension: wishing means an "⟨It⟩ may be", willing an "It shall be", whereby the "It shall" is of course to be taken in a definite sense.
>
> The will, one says, goes upon realization. Only in certain cases, on a certain detour, does the will, as we must of course add by way of supplement, go upon something already being in advance and conscious as being: namely as will that something remain as it is.

German:

> Wunsch und Wille gehen beide in gewisser Weise auf ein Sein, wie denn in gewisser Weise alle Akte auf Sein gehen. Der Glaube ist Verneinen des Seins, die Modalisierungen des Glaubens sind Verneinungen eines modalisierten Seins, eines „Möglichseins“, „Wahrscheinlichseins“ usw. Die Gemütsakte sind ebenfalls als Seinsmodalisierungen, obschon neuer Dimension, zu fassen: Das Wünschen vermeint ein „⟨Es⟩ möge sein“, das Wollen ein „Es soll sein“, wobei das „Es soll“ freilich in bestimmtem Sinn zu nehmen ist.
>
> Der Wille, sagt man, geht auf Verwirklichung. Nur in gewissen Fällen, auf einem gewissen Umweg, geht der Wille, wie wir ergänzend freilich beifügen müssen, auf ein im voraus schon Seiendes und als seiend Bewußtes: nämlich als Wille, daß etwas so bleibe, wie es ist.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "Der Glaube ist Verneinen des Seins, die Modalisierungen des Glaubens sind Verneinungen eines modalisierten Seins" (belief is negating of being, the modalizations of belief are negations of a modalized being). This is the conversion's confusion of *Vermeinen* with *Verneinen* noted in the foreword. Belief is not the negation of being, and "being-possible" is not negated by surmise but meant by it. The next sentence has *vermeint* spelt correctly in the same construction ("Das Wünschen vermeint ein …"), and the sentence only makes sense as a series of one verb. The rendering therefore reads *Vermeinen* and *Vermeinungen* (meaning, meanings), as the main commentary does, and the quotation gives what the file has. The print should be checked before anything is built on the noun forms. The ⟨Es⟩ is the editor's addition, given as LaTeX in the file. A page break (Hua XXVIII 106) falls between the two paragraphs.

The first sentence states the thesis twice and hedges it twice. Wish and will go upon being "in a certain way" (*in gewisser Weise*), as "in a certain way" all acts do. The repetition matters. §9 had said that "in the proper sense only objectivating acts" are directed upon "what is or is not" (Hua XXVIII 72; row 8), and the hedge keeps that claim standing: whatever the wish and the will go upon, it is not being in the proper, doxic sense. The second sentence states the proper sense: belief is the meaning of being, and its modalizations mean "a modalized being", "being-possible" (*Möglichsein*), "being-probable" (*Wahrscheinlichsein*). The hyphenless compounds in quotation marks are noun forms of the copula with an adverb fused in. In the doxic modalities the copula stays and its modality is built into it.

The third sentence names the new dimension and does it by switching from adverb to mood. "Wishing means an '⟨It⟩ may be', willing an 'It shall be'." The copula *sein* stays, now as an infinitive, and the modality moves to an auxiliary: *möge*, the optative subjunctive of *mögen*, and *soll*, the modal verb of obligation and of command. The parallel construction, one verb, *vermeint*, with two objects in quotation marks, makes the claim as strongly as grammar can. Wishing and willing are kinds of meaning, as believing is, and their contents are as sayable as "it is". They are not feelings attached to a proposition, and they are not judgments about desirability. The word "dimension" says why they are not further doxic modalities. Possibility and probability lie on one axis with certainty, as weakenings of "is". "May be" and "shall be" do not weaken "is"; they lie off that axis, and each could be combined with the doxic modalities (one can wish what is probable, or will what is only possible).

The sentence ends on a reservation that it does not cash: the "It shall" is "of course to be taken in a definite sense" (*in bestimmtem Sinn*). The "of course" (*freilich*) concedes that *soll* has more than one sense. It is the "shall" of decision, but also the "ought" of norm and the "is to" of an order received. The will's "shall" is meant to be none of these latter, and the passage does not say what it is instead. The second paragraph gives a hint by marking the cases it must exclude. "The will, one says, goes upon realization", and only "on a certain detour" (*auf einem gewissen Umweg*) upon what already is, as the will "that something remain as it is". "It shall be" said of what already is would be no willing; the will's "shall" is directed upon a being that is not yet given as being. The paragraph after it draws the consequence. The will "cannot go upon the ideal but only upon the real, and not upon the past but upon the future", in contrast to joy and wish, which can rest on a mathematical truth or a past event; "the ideal sphere is closed to it, and likewise that of the past" (Hua XXVIII 106).

That consequence depends on the grammar more than the passage admits. "It may be" can be said of anything, past and ideal included, because the optative wishes without claiming to bring about. "It shall be" in the will's sense implies that the one who says it can make it be, and that is why the will is confined to the real and future. But the restriction comes from the sense of realization added in the second paragraph, not from the modal verb itself. A normative "it ought to have been" is perfectly grammatical of the past. So the "definite sense" of *soll* is set by the will's direction upon realization, and the verb alone cannot carry it. Husserl closes the paragraph with a characteristic hedge: the restriction of the will is "probably" (*wohl*) a restriction of reason, and whether a madman might muster a will directed upon the past "we shall not be able to assert" (Hua XXVIII 106).

The next section replaces the formula. §15 says that the will's consciousness is not "it will be, and accordingly I will it" but "because I will it, it will be": "the will speaks its creative 'Let it be!'" (*Es werde!*, Hua XXVIII 107; row 15). *Es werde* is the subjunctive of becoming used as a command, which Beilage I will call the *fiat*, and it supplies what *soll* could not: the will's "shall" is a making-be, not an ought. §17(a) then sets the two formulas side by side: willing "in the original sense" is the analogue of certainty, "I posit simply and practically as ought-to-be: 'Let it be!'" (*ich setze schlechthin und praktisch als seinsollend: „Es werde!“*, Hua XXVIII 114). The adjective *seinsollend* keeps the *soll* of §14 as the name of the correlate, while *Es werde* names the act. Beilage I finally joins them and confesses the lack: the will brings in "something new … the *fiat*, the practical 'It shall be!'" (*das* fiat, *das praktische „Es soll sein!“*, Hua XXVIII 157), and a word is lacking for it (row 25). The exclamation mark that Beilage I adds to §14's formula marks the difference between a "shall" stated and a "shall" enacted.

The normative sense of *soll*, which §14 set aside with "in a definite sense", comes back in the formal *Praktik* and is disowned there. When §18 defines the "objectively demanded" through convenience, Husserl warns that "the talk of the 'ought' (*Sollend*) or of the 'demand' are figurative turns of speech, stemming from commands and orders" (*Gleichnisreden, herstammend von Geboten, Befehlen*, Hua XXVIII 135; row 19). The two warnings belong together. In §14 the will's "shall" must not be heard as an ought; in §18 the axiological ought must not be heard as a command. The course needs both, and it marks both in the same word.

## §15, Hua XXVIII 107: "because I will it, it will be"

Reads [§15, paragraph 1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#action-will-and-the-resolve-will-directed-at-the-future).

The will's certainty about its future is not a prediction. One who wills to travel to Paris is certain that the journey will take place, but that certainty is not given first, by an expectation, and then taken up by the will. It is given by the will itself. The will "makes" its object actual, and the belief in the future that comes with willing is a consequence of the willing, not its foundation. Husserl calls the will "creative" in this sense, and the reading turns on one exchange of connectives: not "it will be, and accordingly I will it", but "because I will it, it will be". The thesis reverses, for the will, the order of founding that §9 laid down for valuing (row 8), and the course's phenomenology of action (§16) and the *fiat* of Beilage I (row 25) both rest on it.

> If the will goes upon something future, it does so in a way of its own, distinct from all other acts concerning the future. For example, joy over something future presupposes the belief in the future being; the expectation of the future lies at its basis as substrate. The joy-valuation, or the good-positing, goes upon what is already posited as future-actual, posited in the underlying belief. The will directed upon something future also implies in a certain way the belief in the future, but it does not presuppose this belief, does not imply it as substrate. Whoever wills, and, let us say more exactly, positively wills (and wills in the mode of will-certainty) that something shall happen, thereby also believes that it will happen. In the resolve to travel to Paris there lies in a certain way, naturally, that the journey to Paris will be actuality, and all the more in the action-will that lives in the acting carrying-out of the journey. But the future journey, or the future stay in Paris, is not in the first place certainly-being and then in addition willed as something certain in advance; on the contrary: were it already certainly-being in advance, then it could not be willed at all. Instead of being certain in advance, it is certain only by virtue of the will-certainty. The will as will-certainty posits the future in a way that first confers on it, for consciousness, the certainty of being. Consciousness does not, as it were, say: "It will be, and accordingly I will it", but: "Because I will it, it will be." In other words, the will speaks its creative "Let it be!". The will-positing is positing of realization. But realization here does not say merely becoming-actual, but making-actual, achievement of realization. That, however, is something wholly its own, which has its source precisely in the peculiarity of will-consciousness and can be understood only there.

German:

> Geht der Wille auf Künftiges, so tut er das in einer eigenen, von allen anderen Künftiges betreffenden Akten unterschiedenen Weise. Zum Beispiel, die Freude über ein Künftiges setzt den Glauben an das künftige Sein voraus, die Erwartung des Künftigen liegt ihm als Unterlage zugrunde. Die Freudenbewertung bzw. die Gutsetzung geht auf das schon als künftig Wirkliches Gesetzte, gesetzt in dem unterliegenden Glauben. Der auf Künftiges gerichtete Wille impliziert in gewisser Weise auch den Glauben an das Künftige, aber er setzt nicht diesen Glauben voraus, impliziert ihn nicht als Unterlage. Wer da will und, sagen wir genauer, positiv will (und im Modus der Willensgewißheit will), daß etwas geschehen solle, glaubt damit auch, daß es geschehen werde. Im Entschluß, nach Paris zu reisen, liegt in gewisser Weise natürlich, daß die Reise nach Paris Wirklichkeit sein wird, und erst recht im Handlungswillen, der im handelnden Vollzug der Reise lebt. Aber die künftige Reise bzw. der künftige Aufenthalt in Paris ist nicht fürs erste gewißseiend und dazu noch als im voraus Gewisses gewollt, sondern im Gegenteil: Wäre es schon im voraus gewißseiend, dann könnte es gar nicht gewollt werden. Es ist, statt im voraus gewiß seiend, allererst vermöge der Willensgewißheit gewiß. Der Wille als Willensgewißheit setzt das Künftige in einer Weise, die ihm für das Bewußtsein Gewißheit des Seins erst erteilt. Das Bewußtsein sagt gewissermaßen nicht: „Es wird sein, und demgemäß will ich es“; sondern: „Weil ich es will, wird es sein.“ Mit anderen Worten, der Wille spricht sein schöpferisches „Es werde!“. Die Willenssetzung ist Setzung der Verwirklichung. Aber Verwirklichung sagt hier nicht bloß Wirklichwerdung, sondern Wirklichmachung, Leistung der Verwirklichung. Das aber ist etwas Ureigenes, das eben in der Eigenheit des Willensbewußtseins seine Quelle hat und sich nur da verstehen läßt.

The passage opens with a contrast case that fixes what "founding" means. Joy over something future "presupposes" (*setzt … voraus*) the belief in it; expectation lies beneath it "as substrate" (*als Unterlage*); the joy "goes upon what is already posited as future-actual". This is the order §9 stated for all valuing: an objectivating act first, a valuing built on it (row 8). The will is then distinguished by a pair of verbs that the sentence keeps apart with care. The will "implies in a certain way" (*impliziert in gewisser Weise*) the belief in the future, but "does not presuppose this belief, does not imply it as substrate". Implication without presupposition is the formal shape of the thesis: the belief is there whenever the will is, but downstream of it.

The middle of the passage argues from the impossibility of the reverse. "Were it already certainly-being in advance, then it could not be willed at all." The coinage *gewißseiend* (certainly-being), written as one word, fuses the doxic modality with being, as §14 had done with *Möglichsein* and *Wahrscheinlichsein* (row 14). The argument repeats the motivation-law of §9: "whoever is surely convinced that A is cannot will A" (Hua XXVIII 72). There the law was stated for what already is; here it is applied to what will be. If the journey were certain apart from the will, the will would have nothing left to do. The certainty must therefore come "only by virtue of the will-certainty" (*allererst vermöge der Willensgewißheit*), and the adverb *allererst* (first of all, only then) gives the will priority in time and in ground at once. The step is weaker than it looks. That a known future cannot be willed shows only that the will's object must not be certain *independently* of the will. It does not yet show that the will *confers* the certainty that accompanies it. Husserl moves from the first claim to the second without a further argument, and the next sentence simply states the second: the will "posits the future in a way that first confers (*erst erteilt*) on it, for consciousness, the certainty of being". The qualification "for consciousness" (*für das Bewußtsein*) keeps the thesis descriptive. It concerns how the future is given to the one who wills, not whether the journey happens.

The two sentences of inner speech carry the thesis in their connectives. Both contain the same future indicative, "it will be" (*es wird sein*), and the same "I will it" (*ich will es*). What changes is the direction of grounding between them. "And accordingly" (*und demgemäß*) makes the willing follow from a prior expectation; "because" (*weil*) makes the expectation follow from the willing. The quotation marks give both sentences to consciousness, and the hedge "as it were" (*gewissermaßen*) admits that consciousness does not literally say either. The order of the clauses is reversed too. In the rejected sentence "it will be" comes first; in the accepted one the clause of willing comes first and the clause of being follows it, as the being follows the willing. Then Husserl replaces both statements with an act of speech: "the will speaks its creative 'Let it be!'" (*Es werde!*). The future indicative "it will be" describes; *Es werde* commands a becoming. The will's certainty is not the content of a statement about the future but the force of an utterance that makes it. This also settles the "definite sense" of §14's "it shall be" (row 14): the will's "shall" is a making-be.

The last sentences fix the point in two coined nouns. "Realization here does not say merely becoming-actual, but making-actual" (*nicht bloß Wirklichwerdung, sondern Wirklichmachung*). The stems are the same; the suffixes divide them. *-werdung*, from *werden*, is intransitive, something that happens; *-machung*, from *machen*, is transitive, something done. The gloss that follows, "achievement of realization" (*Leistung der Verwirklichung*), puts the will's correlate under the heading of a *Leistung*, an accomplishment. That word marks what the will adds to every other act that concerns the future. Joy and expectation receive a future. The will produces one. Husserl's final claim, that this is "something wholly its own" (*etwas Ureigenes*) that "can be understood only there", in will-consciousness, closes off any attempt to explain it from belief or feeling. That is the intended result. It also means the thesis can be checked only by description, not derived.

The next paragraph draws the consequence for founding: "instead of the willing being founded on the belief in the future being, the belief in the future is rather one that wells up out of the willing" (*ein aus dem Wollen hervorquellender*). In action the perception of each realized phase then has "the character of one born out of the willing", "welling forth from creative subjectivity" (*aus der schöpferischen Subjektivität entquollenen*), and its object is "in consequence of the creative 'fiat'" (Hua XXVIII 107). The verbs of welling (*hervorquellen*, *entquellen*) are the ones §16 will use for the will-continuum, in which "willing constantly wells out of willing" (Hua XXVIII 111; row 16). Two pages later Husserl qualifies the reversal: the will's thesis is directed upon the willed process and not upon the belief it implies, "and only reflection teaches that the creative element … necessarily reaches further than the will's proper theme. (These are very difficult relations, which one must think through again and again.)" The future-directed will is "in a certain sense creative intention, and this 'fulfils' itself in the executing action" (Hua XXVIII 109). The quotation marks around "fulfils" register that the will's fulfilment is not the fulfilment of an expectation, since nothing was expected apart from it.

*Ideen II* puts the same connective under quotation marks. In its account of the body as the field of free movement: "I carry out my 'fiat', and the hand moves 'because' I will" (*Ich vollziehe mein „fiat“, und die Hand bewegt sich, „weil“ ich will*, §61, Hua IV 284). The marks on "because" there say what the 1914 passage leaves implicit: the "because" of willing is not the "because" of natural causation. In the 1914 sentence the word stands unmarked inside consciousness's own speech. The two texts make the same claim, that the will grounds the being of its object for the one who wills, and *Ideen II* adds the reservation that the grounding is a "because" of its own kind. The 1914 lectures had already given that kind its name, *Wirklichmachung*.

## §16, Hua XXVIII 110–111: willing wells out of willing

Reads [§16, paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-structure-of-the-action-will).

An action is willed through a continuum of willing, and that continuum generates itself: each phase of the will "wells up" (*hervorquellen*, *entquellen*) out of the one before it and, at the start, out of the *fiat*. Husserl distinguishes this welling from the mere flow of time, in which one now follows another, and he refuses to make it the will's object. The will is directed upon the things being done, not upon the willings that carry it forward. These two claims together give the "creative" will of §15 a temporal form, and they end the regress §15 had threatened. The willing that keeps an action going is produced by willing, but it is not what is willed.

> During the action we have a peculiar structure of willing: belonging to each phase of the action a point of actual creating, the respective now-point with its actual now-achievement, and belonging to it a distinguished will-phase in which the will shows its creative originality. But to each point there also belongs a horizon, in general a double one, of peculiar transformations of will, in which the creatively past and the creative element of the future, what is dispatched and what is still to be dispatched, constitute themselves in consciousness. In addition, the two distinguished points: the starting-point with the first *fiat*, which as it were confers the creative primal impulse, and the end-point with the character "It is accomplished", both points distinguished by correlative one-sidednesses in their horizons. During the process willing constantly wells out of willing, which then passes over into the reproductions, so that in general, in the will-continuities that belong to each time-point, the moments of willing do not lie side by side but stand in continuous relations of welling-up-out-of-one-another. The same holds, in a somewhat different way, no less for the whole will-continua in the sequence of time-points: each new will-continuum, in the passage from the now to the new now, flows forth from the previous one not merely in the way that, in original time-consciousness in general, the now flows forth from the past now; rather it wells out of it by virtue of its own will-creation.
>
> In each now the will-direction and the creative "Let it be!" goes through the continuity of the will-moments; with each new actual point of creation a prior will-intention directed upon its content fulfils itself. The willings are not directed upon the further willings; rather each will is directed upon the things, creatively fulfilled upon the respective now-phase of the process and "intending" the whole rest of the process as to be realized. With this the analysis we gave last time of the will-directions of the forward-looking resolve is also confirmed. In doing we do not do the future will, but precisely the done, although it belongs precisely to this that will-continuities well up out of the initiating will, out of the *fiat*, and each new will-phase out of the previous one.

German:

> Während der Handlung haben wir eine eigentümliche Struktur des Wollens; zu jeder Handlungsphase gehörig einen Punkt des aktuellen Schaffens, den jeweiligen Jetztpunkt mit seiner aktuellen Jetzt-Leistung, zu ihr gehörig eine ausgezeichnete Willensphase, in der der Wille seine schöpferische Originalität zeigt. Zu jedem Punkt gehört aber auch ein im allgemeinen doppelter Horizont von eigentümlichen Willenswandlungen, in denen sich das schöpferisch Vergangene und das Schöpferische der Zukunft, das Erledigte und noch zu Erledigende, bewußtseinsmäßig konstituieren. Zudem die ausgezeichneten zwei Punkte: Anfangspunkt mit dem ersten und gewissermaßen den schöpferischen Uranstoß verleihenden *fiat* und Endpunkt mit dem Charakter „Es ist vollbracht“ — beide Punkte durch korrelative Einseitigkeiten in ihren Horizonten ausgezeichnet. Während des Prozesses entquillt beständig Wollen aus Wollen, was dann in die Reproduktionen übergeht, so daß überhaupt in den Willenskontinuitäten, die zu jedem Zeitpunkt gehören, die Wollensmomente nicht nebeneinander liegen, sondern in kontinuierlichen Relationen des Auseinander-Hervorquellens stehen. Dasselbe gilt in etwas anderer Weise nicht minder für die ganzen Willenskontinua in der Reihenfolge der Zeitpunkte: Jedes neue Willenskontinuum im Übergang vom Jetzt zum neuen Jetzt fließt aus dem vorigen nicht bloß so hervor, wie überhaupt im ursprünglichen Zeitbewußtsein das Jetzt aus dem vergangenen Jetzt hervorfließt, vielmehr entquillt es aus ihm vermöge der eigenen Willensschöpfung.
>
> In jedem Jetzt geht die Willensrichtung und das schöpferische „Es werde!“ durch die Kontinuität der Willensmomente hindurch; mit jedem neuen aktuellen Schöpfungspunkt erfüllt sich eine vorgängige, auf seinen Gehalt gerichtete Willensintention. Die Wollungen richten sich nicht auf die weiteren Wollungen, sondern jeder Wille richtet sich auf die Sachen, schöpferisch erfüllt auf die jeweilige Jetztphase des Vorgangs und „intendierend“ auf den ganzen Rest des Vorgangs als zu realisierenden. Damit bestätigt sich auch die Analyse, die wir letzthin für die Willensrichtungen des vorblickenden Entschlusses gegeben haben. Im Tun tun wir nicht den künftigen Willen, sondern eben das Getane, obschon eben dazu gehört, daß Willenskontinuitäten aus dem Einsatzwillen, aus dem *fiat* hervorquellen und jede neue Willensphase aus der vorigen.

*Note on the text.* The file breaks the first paragraph across hard-wrapped lines and a page break: "aktuel- len" is joined here, the margin line numbers (kept in the file as comments) are dropped, and p. 111 begins inside the sentence on the two distinguished points, at "durch korrelative Einseitigkeiten". Nothing in the wording is in doubt.

The first paragraph lays out the action as a field with a structure, in the vocabulary of time-consciousness: now-point, horizon, phase, continuity. Each term is then marked as the will's. The now-point is a "point of actual creating" (*Punkt des aktuellen Schaffens*) with its "now-achievement" (*Jetzt-Leistung*), the word §15 used for making-actual (row 15). The horizons are horizons of "transformations of will" (*Willenswandlungen*). Past and future are named twice, and the second naming changes their kind. "The creatively past and the creative element of the future" (*das schöpferisch Vergangene und das Schöpferische der Zukunft*) are phases of time with an adjective attached. "What is dispatched and what is still to be dispatched" (*das Erledigte und noch zu Erledigende*) are tasks: the time of the action is measured by the work it has done and has yet to do. The two end-points are given, like the will's positing in §15, as quoted utterances. The *fiat* confers the "creative primal impulse" (*Uranstoß*), and the end-point carries the character "It is accomplished" (*Es ist vollbracht*). The action begins with a command and ends with a report that the command has been carried out.

The decisive claim is about how the phases hang together, and three verbs carry it. "Willing constantly wells out of willing" (*entquillt beständig Wollen aus Wollen*). The moments of willing "do not lie side by side" (*nicht nebeneinander liegen*) but stand in "relations of welling-up-out-of-one-another" (*Relationen des Auseinander-Hervorquellens*), a coinage that turns the verb into a reciprocal relation. Then the contrast that gives the passage its point: each new will-continuum "flows forth" (*fließt … hervor*) from the previous one "not merely" (*nicht bloß*) as the now flows forth from the past now in time-consciousness, "rather it wells out of it by virtue of its own will-creation" (*vielmehr entquillt es aus ihm vermöge der eigenen Willensschöpfung*). The sentence grants the flow and adds the welling. Every will-continuum is in time and passes like any other content of consciousness; what is proper to the will is that it also produces its successor. The difference rests on one pair of verbs, *hervorfließen* against *entquellen*, and on the phrase "by virtue of its own will-creation". The phrase names what has to be shown. It does not show it. The passage gives no description of what would tell a phase that wells out of the last apart from one that merely follows it. The difference is asserted from the side of the will's character as §15 had established it, and the sentence's "rather" (*vielmehr*) claims more than the analysis has given.

The second paragraph draws the consequence that matters for the argument. "The willings are not directed upon the further willings; rather each will is directed upon the things" (*auf die Sachen*). The negation has a precise scope. It does not deny that willings follow from willings: the first paragraph has just said so. It denies that they are the will's object. Each will is "fulfilled" on the present phase and "intending" (*„intendierend“*) the rest of the process. The quotation marks, like those on "fulfils" at the end of §15 (Hua XXVIII 109), hold off the sense of intention taken from perception and expectation. What the will intends is something it will itself make. The closing sentence puts the whole result in one figure: "In doing we do not do the future will, but precisely the done" (*Im Tun tun wir nicht den künftigen Willen, sondern eben das Getane*). Noun, verb and participle all come from *tun*. The object of doing is what has been done, and the participle *das Getane* names the correlate as a result. The concessive clause that follows, "although it belongs precisely to this" (*obschon eben dazu gehört*), keeps the welling of the will in the picture as a condition of the doing, while denying that it is what is done.

This is the confirmation Husserl promises for "the analysis we gave last time". In §15 the resolve to travel seemed to will the future action and, with it, the future willing of that action. Then "if every willing directed upon a future were a willing of a future acting, we should evidently come upon an infinite regress" (Hua XXVIII 108). Husserl's answer there was that the present will "does not posit the future willing or action, but sends the thesis 'Let it be!' through it" (*sendet durch sie die Thesis „Es werde!“ hindurch*): the process to be realized is posited, "and not the will or the action" (Hua XXVIII 108). §16 shows the same structure from inside an action under way. In each now the "Let it be!" "goes through" (*geht … hindurch*) the continuity of will-moments; the preposition repeats the one that answered the regress. What §15 asserted of a resolve, §16 shows in the structure of every action. The regress is stopped by separating two relations that §15 called "confusing": the generative relation of will to will, and the directedness of will upon its matter. The willing passes along the first relation, but it is aimed only along the second.

The neighbours frame this as phenomenology of the act, not yet as theory of its rightness. Before the passage, §16 opens by binding the will to a "belief-substrate referring to real being" (*Glaubensunterlage bezogen auf reales Sein*, Hua XXVIII 109) and traces the executing will from its first phase onward. A resolve that was "not kept livingly through" (*nicht lebendig durchgehalten*) can be renewed and recognized in memory as the same will (Hua XXVIII 110). After it, Husserl separates executing will from simple action and resolve (*Vorsatz*) from decision (*Entschluß*), and §17 turns to the will-modalities. The temporal analysis is not taken up again in the main text. It returns once, as a practical problem, in Beilage II. There the deliberator asks how to count among the goods the resolve "in future I will ever again, as often as new horizons open, seek to gain the insight into what is best" (the file reads *vill ich* for *will ich*), and "what role this resolve plays, to take this resolve again and again in future or to remember it, to keep it up" (*ihn durchzuhalten*, Hua XXVIII 158). That is a will directed upon future willing, the case §15 called possible and §16 kept out of the structure of action. In the Beilage it is no longer a regress to be avoided but a good to be weighed. §16's thesis is a thesis about what the will is directed upon in acting. It leaves open that a will can take willing itself as its theme, and the formal *Praktik* then has to reckon with that will.

The contrast with time-consciousness reads differently against the 1905 lectures. There the now itself is the source and is creative: absolute time constitutes itself "in the flow of the past-modifications and in the constant welling-up of a now, the creative time-point, the source-point of time-positions in general" (*im stetigen Hervorquellen eines Jetzt, des schöpferischen Zeitpunktes, des Quellpunktes der Zeitstellen überhaupt*, Hua X §33, 72). In 1914 the same verb and the same adjective go to the will, and time-consciousness keeps only "flowing forth" (*hervorfließen*), with the direction now running from the past now to the new one. The distinction between the will's welling and time's flow is therefore made in part by redistributing vocabulary. The will is creative in a sense the time lectures had already used for the now, and the passage has to give that sense a new content: what wells up in the action is not only a new now but a new will, produced by the will before it and directed past itself onto the thing being done.

## §17(d), Hua XXVIII 124–125: the plain "I will" is not a "yes"

Reads [§17(d), paragraph 1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#d-affirmation-and-negation-in-the-belief-domain-and-in-the-will-domain).

Affirmation and negation are not the two basic forms of positing. Both are modalizations of a plain positing that comes before them: "yes" and "no" each point back to an "it is" or an "I will" that was offered but not carried out, and they confirm or reject it. The unmodalized "I will" is neither. It is the primal willing (*Urwollen*), and it contains no such pointing-back. The point is made against an "older logic" that counted affirmation and negation as the two "qualities" of judgment and so took plain position for affirmation. Here the point is carried into the will, where it decides what a rejection, and later an omission, is.

> Alongside the modalizations of willing in the narrower sense, of primal willing, as the act to which all other acts are essentially referred back, which we have discussed more closely, many others would still have to be submitted to a deeper analysis. We want to proceed further in one direction only, and take one more basic form into closer view, namely will-negativity, which stands to the primal phenomenon, as positive will-positing, analogously to the way unbelief stands to positive belief in the doxic domain. I add at once a further parallel: alongside the primal phenomenon of positive belief we have in the doxic sphere the modality of belief that is affirmative in the proper sense; alongside the positive judgment "It is so", the affirming judgment "Yes, it is so", so that three forms appear here: the unmodalized ("It is so") and the affirming and the negating ("No, it is not so").
>
> The same holds for the domain of the will. Alongside the simply positive "I will" we have the "Yes, I will" and the "No, I will not". The older logic, to be sure, did not recognize that negation and affirmation are phenomena coordinate with each other but to be set over against primal belief, and indeed modalizations. Under the title "quality" it usually comprehends only two allegedly coordinate forms, affirmation and negation, and in doing so conflates position and affirmation. But one must see that the consciousness of "It is not" expresses, not only linguistically but phenomenologically, a rejection that in itself points back to an "It is", and so to a position, even if one not actually performed, which for its part does not in its structure contain a further pointing-back. And the same holds of affirmation proper, of saying yes proper. As negation as it were crosses out, so affirmation underlines, and accordingly has a complicated intentional structure of the kind that is characteristic precisely of all modalizations. We now convince ourselves of just this state of affairs in the case of will-negation and will-affirmation too. The willing rejection of the "I will not" implies a positive will-claim; it is a willed crossing-out of an "I will" that is not performed but solicits itself. With the ⟨expression⟩ "to be unwilling" we sometimes designate this negativity, but the ⟨expression⟩ is ambiguous, since *Unwille* and "being unwilling" very often designate the phenomenon of indignation.

German:

> Neben den näher besprochenen Modalisierungen des Wollens im engeren Sinn, des Urwollens, als des Aktes, auf den alle anderen Akte wesentlich zurückbezogen sind, wären noch manche anderen einer tieferen Analyse zu unterziehen. Nur in einer Richtung wollen wir noch vorgehen, und eine Grundform wollen wir noch in näheren Augenschein nehmen, nämlich die Willensnegativität, die zum Urphänomen als positiver Willenssetzung analog steht wie im doxischen Gebiet der Unglaube zum positiven Glauben. Ich füge gleich eine weitere Parallele bei: Wir haben neben dem Urphänomen des positiven Glaubens in der doxischen Sphäre die Modalität des im eigentlichen Sinn affirmativen Glaubens; neben dem positiven Urteil „Es ist so“ das bejahende Urteil „Ja, es ist so“, so daß hier drei Formen auftreten: die unmodalisierte („Es ist so“) und die bejahende und die verneinende („Nein, so ist es nicht“).
>
> Dasselbe gilt für das Willensgebiet. Wir haben neben dem schlicht positiven „Ich will“ das „Ja, ich will“ und das „Nein, ich will nicht“. Die ältere Logik hat es allerdings nicht erkannt, daß Verneinung und Bejahung zwar gleichgeordnete, aber dem Urglauben gegenüberzusetzende Phänomene, und zwar Modalisierungen sind. Sie faßt unter dem Titel „Qualität“ in der Regel nur zwei angeblich gleich geordnete Formen Bejahung und Verneinung und vermengt dabei Position und Bejahung. Aber man muß es sehen, daß das Bewußtsein des „Es ist nicht“ nicht nur sprachlich, sondern phänomenologisch eine Ablehnung ausdrückt, die in sich auf ein „Es ist“, also auf eine, wenn auch nicht wirklich vollzogene Position zurückweist, die ihrerseits in ihrer Struktur nicht wieder einen Rückweis enthält. Und dasselbe gilt von der eigentlichen Affirmation, dem eigentlichen Jasagen. Von eben dieser Sachlage überzeugen wir uns nun auch bei der Willensverneinung und Willensbejahung. Das wollende Ablehnen des „Ich will nicht“ impliziert eine positive Willenszumutung; sie ist ein willentliches Durchstreichen eines nicht vollzogenen, aber sich anmutenden „Ich will“. Mit dem ⟨Ausdruck⟩ „unwillig sein“ bezeichnen wir mitunter diese Negativität, doch ist der ⟨Ausdruck⟩ zweideutig, da Unwille und „unwillig sein“ sehr oft das Phänomen der Indignation bezeichnen.

*Note on the text.* Here *Verneinung* is genuine negation, and the sense requires it ("No, it is not so"); this is not the *Vermeinen* confusion. The file gives the opening quotation marks of the first paragraph as ",,", the editor's first ⟨Ausdruck⟩ in LaTeX markup, and "unwil- lig" with a line-break hyphen; these are normalized here. *Willenszumutung* (will-claim, literally an imposition or demand of will) occurs only here in the file, where the course otherwise says *Anmutung* (solicitation), as in *Willensanmutung* (Hua XXVIII 111) and in "sich anmutenden" in the same sentence. Whether it is Husserl's word or a misreading cannot be settled from the file, and nothing below rests on it.

The paragraph sets up a scheme of three and then defends it against a scheme of two. The doxic examples are given as sentences in quotation marks, "It is so", "Yes, it is so", "No, it is not so", and the will's as "I will", "Yes, I will", "No, I will not". The particles carry the analysis. "Yes" and "no" are added to a sentence that can stand without them, and what can stand without them is the "primal phenomenon" (*Urphänomen*). Husserl's account of their order is careful. Affirmation and negation are "coordinate with each other" (*zwar gleichgeordnete*) "but to be set over against primal belief" (*aber dem Urglauben gegenüberzusetzende*). They are on a level with each other, and both stand one level above the plain positing. The older logic's error is a flattening. Its title "quality" admits only two forms, and since every judgment must have one or the other, the plain "It is so" is filed under affirmation, which "conflates position and affirmation" (*vermengt … Position und Bejahung*). The course names no author. The target is a classification, and the correction is to add a level, not a third quality.

The argument for the level is a claim about reference. "It is not" expresses a rejection that "in itself points back" (*in sich … zurückweist*) to an "It is", to "a position, even if one not actually performed". That position "for its part does not in its structure contain a further pointing-back" (*nicht wieder einen Rückweis enthält*). The relative clause does the defining work: the primal phenomenon is what does not point back. Husserl offers this as something "one must see" (*man muß es sehen*), and the phrase "not only linguistically but phenomenologically" (*nicht nur sprachlich, sondern phänomenologisch*) admits that the first evidence is the grammar of "yes" and "no". The step from the particle to the act is where the inference is thinnest. That "no" answers something is plain in speech. That every negating consciousness carries a position it does not perform is a descriptive thesis, and the passage asserts it rather than shows it. For affirmation the thesis is harder still, since the "yes" adds nothing to the content of what it affirms. Husserl gives it the same structure by a figure: "as negation as it were crosses out, so affirmation underlines" (*Wie die Verneinung gleichsam durchstreicht, so unterstreicht die Bejahung*). Both verbs are compounds of *streichen* (to stroke, draw a line), and they differ only in where the line goes. The image puts the affirmed and the negated position under the same operation, a mark made on something already there. This is why affirmation has the "complicated intentional structure" of a modalization. The plain "I will" makes no mark of this kind.

On the will's side the scheme fixes what a rejection is. "The willing rejection of the 'I will not' implies a positive will-claim; it is a willed crossing-out of an 'I will' that is not performed but solicits itself" (*sich anmutenden*). A "no" of the will is therefore not the absence of willing. It is an act directed against a willing that presented itself and was refused. The next paragraph turns this into the distinction between omission and privation. In omission, the will for a journey "instead of being kept through and turned into full deed, is rather crossed out" (*statt durchgehalten … vielmehr durchstrichen*), and we "omit" the journey. But to omit, "though this is often expressed in the words 'I will not'", is not "to perform no willing in the sense of a privation" (Hua XXVIII 125). The same words, "I will not", cover both the crossing-out and the mere absence, and only the first is a modalization of the will. Husserl leaves the privative not-willing to ethics, outside formal *Praktik*.

The closing remark on "unwilling" (*unwillig sein*) shows the same difficulty from the side of the emotions. The word can name will-negativity, but *Unwille* "very often" names indignation. Indignation is a feeling, a negative valuing, not a crossed-out willing. In §11 Husserl had given valuings a "positive or negative quality" and value-freedom as a third case (Hua XXVIII 87). There "quality" names the polarity of liking and disliking, which is no modalization. The ambiguity of the word therefore runs along the line the paragraph has just drawn: between a negation that crosses out a position and a negative quality that is a position of its own. The text marks the ambiguity without drawing this consequence. The consequence matters all the same, because the parallel of logic and ethics depends on keeping a "no" of the will apart from a disvaluing of the feelings.

Formal *Praktik* uses the scheme at once. Among the laws of iterated acts in §18(a), Husserl notes that "the omission of an omission of A implies the doing of A", a double crossing-out that restores the positing. He then separates will-affirmation from the willing of a willing: "if the willing assent is right, then the simple will is right, and conversely. To assent to a will does not mean to will the will" (*Einem Willen zustimmen, heißt nicht, den Willen wollen*, Hua XXVIII 128). The underlining adds no new will. It confirms one, and so it shares the rightness of what it confirms. The same page sets this against belief: "I can also believe in the belief", but a right belief that I believe A does not make the belief in A right. The "yes" has a parallel across the spheres, and the iteration does not.

*Ideen I*, published the year before, has the figure in nearly the same words, for belief only: "Just as negation, metaphorically speaking, crosses out, so affirmation 'underlines'; it 'confirms', 'assenting', a position, instead of 'cancelling' it as negation does" (*Ebenso wie die Negation, bildlich gesprochen, durchstreicht, so „unterstreicht“ die Affirmation*, §106, Hua III/1 244; the passage reads the same in the English translation, "strikes out" and "underscores"). The 1913 sentence quotes "underlines" and calls the whole figure metaphor. In the 1914 sentence the quotation marks are gone and "as it were" (*gleichsam*) qualifies only the crossing-out. *Ideen I* treats the operation noematically, as the character of being crossed out (*Durchstrichenheit*) that the negated acquires. The lecture does not repeat that analysis. It carries the figure into the will, where the crossed-out item is an "I will" that offered itself. The result is a three-part form of the will in which the plain "I will" stands first. The modalities come after it, and the formal laws of §18 attach to them.

## §18(b), Hua XXVIII 130–131: "minderwertig": lower in value becomes bad

Reads [§18(b), paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-will-laws-of-choice-the-absorption-law-the-idea-of-the-practical-realm-and-the-problem-of-the-categorical-imperative).

In a choice, a comparative becomes a polarity. Outside a choice, a lesser good is still a good, and willing it is good. Inside a choice in which a better is available and set aside, the decision for the lesser good is not merely worse than the decision for the better. It is bad. The value it had "considered in and for itself" is not reduced but lost. Husserl calls this a devaluation (*Entwertung*), and he finds it confirmed in a word: *minderwertig* (literally "of lesser value") has come to mean "inferior" in a disparaging sense, and it has done so, he says, because of what happens in choice. The law turns the language of degree into the language of right and wrong. It is the base on which the absorption law and the problem of the categorical imperative are built (row 20).

> We now pass on to the highly important will-laws of choice. A basic law: let any will-subject stand in a choice, referred to any practical possibilities among which the positive values W₁ and W₂ may occur. If now W₁ < W₂, then the practical decision for W₁ alone is not only worse than the practical decision for W₂ alone, but is itself bad; the setting-behind of the better and preference of the worse, both taken in one, is incorrect, and the choice is therefore to be valued as bad.
>
> While, then, considered in and for itself, the willing or doing of a good has a positive value-character, the law shows that this value is not only diminished but entirely lost as soon as the good appears as a practical possibility in a choice beside a better that is at the same time set behind. In this connection what is positive-valued, considered in and for itself, becomes not only lower-valued but negative-valued. The curious devaluation expresses itself, incidentally, very nicely in the linguistic equivocation of the word "minderwertig" [of lower value; inferior], which, precisely with regard to such connections of choice, has as a rule taken on a disparaging meaning.

German:

> Wir gehen nun über zu den höchst wichtigen Willensgesetzen der Wahl. Ein Grundgesetz: Es stehe irgendein Willenssubjekt in einer Wahl, bezogen auf irgendwelche praktischen Möglichkeiten, unter welchen die positiven Werte W₁ und W₂ vorkommen mögen. Ist nun W₁ < W₂, so ist die praktische Entscheidung für W₁ allein nicht nur schlechter als die praktische Entscheidung für W₂ allein, sondern ist sie selbst schlecht; die Hintansetzung des Besseren und Vorziehung des Schlechteren, beides in eins genommen, ist unrichtig, und die Wahl ist also als schlecht zu bewerten.
>
> Während also an und für sich betrachtet Wollen bzw. Tun eines Guten einen positiven Wertcharakter hat, zeigt das Gesetz, daß dieser Wert sich nicht nur mindert, sondern völlig verloren geht, sowie das Gute als praktische Möglichkeit auftritt in einer Wahl neben einem zugleich hintangesetzten Besseren. In diesem Zusammenhang wird das an und für sich betrachtet Positivwertige nicht nur minderwertig, sondern negativwertig. Die merkwürdige Entwertung drückt sich übrigens sehr schön in der sprachlichen Äquivokation des Wortes „minderwertig“ aus, das eben mit Rücksicht auf solche Zusammenhänge der Wahl eine in der Regel abschätzige Bedeutung angenommen hat.

*Note on the text.* The file has these lines hard-wrapped, with line-break hyphens ("unrich- tig", "die- ser", "minder- wertig") that are joined here. The first *minderwertig* is broken at the line end, "minder-" / "wertig"; it is read as one word, since the sentence pairs it with *negativwertig*. The second is split across the page break to p. 131 ("des Wor-" / "tes"). The subscripts are LaTeX in the file ($W_1$) and are printed as W₁ here.

The law is built on a pair of connectives: "not only worse … but itself bad" (*nicht nur schlechter … sondern … selbst schlecht*). "Worse" is a comparative and leaves the decision for W₁ positive, only less so. "Bad" is a positive degree with a negative sign. The law crosses from one to the other, and the paragraph that follows says the same thing three times, each time in sharper words. The value "is not only diminished but entirely lost" (*nicht nur mindert, sondern völlig verloren geht*). The positive-valued "becomes not only lower-valued but negative-valued" (*nicht nur minderwertig, sondern negativwertig*). The grounds are given in the phrase "both taken in one" (*beides in eins genommen*). A choice is one act with two sides, the preference of the one and the "setting-behind" (*Hintansetzung*) of the other. The badness belongs to that unity, not to the willing of W₁ as such. The word "alone" (*allein*) also matters. The decision is for W₁ *alone*, to the exclusion of W₂. A little later Husserl makes explicit that the law "analytically" presupposes that realizing the one possibility and not realizing the better one are compatible (Hua XXVIII 132). Where they can, choosing one also realizes the other, and nothing is set behind.

The phrase "considered in and for itself" (*an und für sich betrachtet*) does the work of a restriction. It names the value the good has when no choice is in view, and it is the value the law takes away. The next paragraph turns the phrase into a principle. Because this law and laws like it hold, all value-laws that ignore choice "constantly carry a restricting phrase with them, this peculiar talk of values 'considered in and for themselves'" (Hua XXVIII 131). Husserl compares this to the mechanics of forces, where a law for one force "at once gains its modification" when new forces appear. The image of an "annulled force, annulled by a counter-force acting at the same point of attack (in the same I and the same time)" explains the sign reversal as a resultant. The setting-behind of the better is a force that outweighs the good willed. Husserl introduces the image with "One can try" and "One can well put it so" (*Man kann versuchen*; *Man kann es wohl so fassen*). It is a construal, and he does not rest the law on it.

The word *minderwertig* is offered as a witness, and the sentence that offers it uses the word in two senses. In "becomes not only lower-valued but negative-valued", *minderwertig* is neutral and comparative: lower in value, still positive. That is the sense the law needs in order to state its contrast. In ordinary German *minderwertig* means inferior, shoddy, worthless, and Husserl says the word "has as a rule taken on a disparaging meaning" (*eine in der Regel abschätzige Bedeutung angenommen hat*). He traces this drift to "such connections of choice". The word itself has made the step from lower to bad that the law describes. The appeal to language is marked as an aside ("incidentally", *übrigens*), and it is not proof. A word can become disparaging for many reasons, and nothing in the history of *minderwertig* shows that its drift runs through choice. The appeal also reverses an earlier one. In §11 Husserl called the everyday use of *ungut* (not-good) for *übel* (evil) "an equivocal distinction" (*eine äquivoke Unterscheidung*, Hua XXVIII 86; row 11), and he warned against it, because ordinary language there blurred contrary and contradictory. Here an equivocation is "very nicely" (*sehr schön*) on the side of the theory. In both places language is read for what it shows about value. Where it supports the law, Husserl accepts its testimony; where it blurs the law, he corrects it.

The law redeems a promise from the theory of value-comparison. In §12, answering an objection that all means to the same end would have to be of equal value, Husserl says that such means can all stand equal in this, "that they are all lower-valued (*minderwertig*) compared with all means to a more valuable final end. That they are positively practically reprehensible then follows from a further law, which will soon occupy us" (Hua XXVIII 100). In §12 *minderwertig* is neutral, and "reprehensible" (*verwerflich*) is held over for the will. §18(b) is that "further law". The axiology of §12 could only rank, and only formal *Praktik* can condemn. Husserl keeps the neutral sense in his own technical use even after the passage. The will and realization of the lower-valued, where a higher value lies within the choice, "would be not merely something lower-valued (*Minderwertiges*) but negative-valued" (Hua XXVIII 132). Every return repeats the passage's "not merely … but". The word stays a comparative in the theory, and the law supplies the step to badness.

The law is then extended to *n* goods, where choosing any good other than the best "is not only of lower value but simply a disvalue" (*nicht nur von minderem Wert, sondern schlechthin Unwert*, Hua XXVIII 133). It is guarded against the misreading that the best of a given disjunction is already the right choice. Finally it becomes the absorption law: "in every choice the better absorbs the good, and the best everything else" (Hua XXVIII 136; row 20). Absorption is the devaluation seen from the side of the ought. What the devaluation law says of the act of choosing, absorption says of what is to be done.

The 1911 course had the absorption but not the sign reversal. In the note analysing a choice between two possibilities (Beilage VII), the conclusion drawn from *minderwertig* stops at the ought: "If I now see that A is lower-valued compared with B, then it is already evident that I ought not" (*Sehe ich nun, daß A minderwertig ist gegenüber B, so ist es schon evident, daß ich nicht soll*, Hua XXVIII 233). The lesser good "drops out of consideration again, since against B it no longer weighs", and it is "eliminated as in any case not-ought". In 1911 the lesser good loses its claim to be chosen. In 1914 the choice of it is bad. The difference is between a not-ought, which is the absence of an ought, and a disvalue, which the will incurs by choosing. The 1914 law gives the will in choice a negative value it can earn, not only a positive value it can miss. *Minderwertig*, with its two senses, holds both stages in one word.

## §18(b), Hua XXVIII 134–135: convenience, and the ought as a figure of speech

Reads [§18(b), paragraph 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-will-laws-of-choice-the-absorption-law-the-idea-of-the-practical-realm-and-the-problem-of-the-categorical-imperative).

The rightness of a will is its fit with a value. A will that directs itself according to something positively valuable is "right", and Husserl calls this fit *Konvenienz* (convenience, in the old sense of befittingness, from Latin *convenire*, to come together, agree). The fit is itself a value. Everything formal *Praktik* says about the ought is then defined from it. A practical possibility "ought to be" if the will directed upon it would be convenient, and "ought not to be" if it would be inconvenient. Husserl says outright that "ought" and "demand" are figures of speech borrowed from commands. The definition is meant to keep the command out. It also makes the ought depend entirely on the will's fit with value, and Husserl later marked the paragraph "No!" and revised it in §21 (row 22).

> We add here some essential explanations. A will directed upon something positively valuable is, as directing itself according to the positive-valued, itself "right". This expresses a special convenience, which is itself to be valued axiologically and is then positive-valued, considered in and for itself. A will directing itself against a positive value (thus rejecting the value), or directing itself in affirmation upon something of disvalue, is incorrect, is "inconvenient", and this inconvenience, this will-contradiction against the positive value, is axiologically a disvalue. The relations are similar for the convenience of a will-negation that directs itself against a disvalue, and for the inconvenience of a will-affirmation that directs itself upon a disvalue.
>
> For this we can also use other expressions, which are to be defined by it. A deliberated action we call "demanded", or a state of affairs under consideration or presented as a practical possibility (a practical *Verhalt*) we call objectively demanded or objectively ought-to-be, if the will directed upon it would be convenient, and not-ought-to-be, negatively demanded, in the case of inconvenience. The negative demand is here equivalent to the demand of rejection. Naturally the talk of the "ought-to-be" or of the "demand" is figurative speech, stemming from injunctions and commands, but of anything of that sort there is in reality no question here. We have to hold strictly to our definition. In the case of the adiaphora both convenience and inconvenience are lacking; there is neither a positive ought there nor an ought of non-being, or a demand of rejection. Whoever chooses something indifferent (on the presupposition that no positive good is also in question) conflicts with no ought, does not behave inconveniently, hence does not proceed incorrectly, but also not rightly in the will-sense, not conveniently. He does nothing owed, but neither is there any ought. But he does not exercise will-reason; this goes precisely only upon convenience.

German:

> Wir fügen hier einige wesentliche Erklärungen bei: Ein auf ein positiv Wertes gerichteter Wille ist als sich nach Positiv-Wertigem richtend selbst „richtig“. Das drückt eine besondere Konvenienz aus, die axiologisch selbst zu werten ist und dann positivwertig ist, an und für sich betrachtet. Ein sich gegen einen Positivwert richtender (also den Wert ablehnender) oder auf ein Unwertes in Bejahung richtender Wille ist unrichtig, ist „inkonvenient“, und diese Inkonvenienz, dieser Willenswiderspruch gegen den Positivwert, ist axiologisch ein Unwert. Ähnlich liegen die Verhältnisse der Konvenienz einer Willensnegation, die sich gegen einen Unwert richtet, und der Inkonvenienz einer Willensaffirmation, die sich auf einen Unwert richtet.
>
> Dafür können wir auch andere Ausdrücke gebrauchen, die sich damit definieren sollen. Eine erwogene Handlung nennen wir „gefordert“ bzw. einen in Erwägung stehenden oder als praktische Möglichkeit vorgestellten Sachverhalt (einen praktischen Verhalt) nennen wir objektiv gefordert oder einen objektiv sein-sollenden, wenn der auf ihn gerichtete Wille konvenient wäre, und nicht sein-sollend, negativ gefordert, im Falle der Inkonvenienz. Die negative Forderung ist dabei gleichwertig der Forderung der Ablehnung. Natürlich sind die Reden vom „Sollend“ oder der „Forderung“ Gleichnisreden, herstammend von Geboten, Befehlen, aber von dergleichen ist hier in Wirklichkeit keine Rede. Wir haben uns streng an unsere Definition zu halten. Im Fall der Adiaphora fehlt sowohl die Konvenienz als die Inkonvenienz, es ist weder ein positives Sollen da noch ein Sollen des Nicht-Seins bzw. eine Forderung der Ablehnung. Wer ein Gleichgültiges wählt — unter Voraussetzung, daß kein positiv Gutes in Mitfrage ist —, streitet mit keinem Sollen, verhält sich nicht inkonvenient, verfährt also nicht unrichtig, aber auch nicht im Willenssinn richtig, konvenient. Er tut nichts Gesolltes, aber es ist auch kein Sollen da. Aber Willensvernunft übt er nicht, diese geht eben nur auf Konvenienz.

*Note on the text.* The page break to p. 135 falls inside the first sentence, between "nach Positiv-Wertigem" and "richtend". The file reads "Gesoltes", corrected here to *Gesolltes*. The second paragraph carries an editor's footnote at "Dafür": "Noted later in the margin of this section: 'No!'" (*Zu diesem Abschnitt nachträglich am Rand vermerkt: „Nein!“*). The file does not say when the note was written or which sentence it targets.

The first sentence defines rightness through a verb. The will is "right" (*„richtig“*) "as directing itself according to the positive-valued" (*als sich nach Positiv-Wertigem richtend*). *Richtig* and *sich richten nach* (to direct oneself according to, to conform to) share a stem, and the definition runs on it: the right will is the one that takes its direction from the value. The English "right" keeps the result and loses the derivation. Husserl puts *richtig* in quotation marks because he is fixing a technical sense, and §18(a) had already set up the vocabulary: founded theses have not only rightness in general, "but they also 'direct' themselves according to their founding theses" (*sondern sie „richten“ sich auch nach ihren Fundament-Thesen*, Hua XXVIII 127). Rightness of will is the limit case of that founding. The will takes its sign from the value it follows. The same verb then carries the negative cases, a will "directing itself against" a positive value or "directing itself in affirmation upon" a disvalue, and the two will-modalities of §17(d) appear in their places. A will-negation against a disvalue is convenient, and a will-affirmation of a disvalue is inconvenient (row 17). Convenience is therefore not a matter of saying yes. It is a matching of sign: positive to positive, rejection to disvalue.

Two glosses stand beside the Latin word, and they pull in different directions. Convenience "is itself to be valued axiologically and is then positive-valued". It is a value of its own, not a borrowed one. Inconvenience is glossed as "this will-contradiction against the positive value" (*dieser Willenswiderspruch*). The second gloss takes the word "contradiction" from logic, where the course had been careful to keep it out. §6 denied that willing the end and not the means is "a logical contradiction at all" (Hua XXVIII 55; row 5). Here "contradiction" is compounded with "will", and the compound marks the analogy without claiming identity. The first gloss has a history the passage does not repeat. In the 1908/09 course Husserl had argued that the value of convenient valuing "lies purely in the convenience as such and not in the transfer of the value-character from the valued value". The valuing of good "is not good because the valued good is a good that radiates its value back onto the convenient valuing, but because it is convenient". The proof is displeasure at an evil: it is good as right valuing, and if its goodness were derived from the evil, "it would otherwise have to be bad" (Hua XXVIII 240; "konvenient" defined, 239). The 1914 phrase "a special convenience, which is itself to be valued axiologically" assumes this result and extends it from valuing to willing.

The second paragraph builds the ought out of convenience by stipulation. The new expressions "are to be defined by it" (*die sich damit definieren sollen*), and Husserl ends the definitions with "We have to hold strictly to our definition". Between the two comes the warning that gives the passage its edge. "Naturally the talk of the 'ought-to-be' or of the 'demand' is figurative speech" (*Gleichnisreden*), "stemming from injunctions and commands, but of anything of that sort there is in reality no question here". The quotation marks around *Sollend* and *Forderung* hold the words at a distance while they are being used. What they borrow from command is the form of address: someone requires something of someone. What they are allowed to mean is only that a will directed upon this possibility would fit the value. The course had drawn this line at the start. In §4 it set "normative propositions, uttered with the sense of rational validity", against "command-sentences, which express suggestive demands": the one who commands "expects that the utterance of the command will be psychologically effective, but he raises no claim to rational validity and its recognition" (Hua XXVIII 34). The figurative ought of §18 is the normative proposition with all trace of the commander removed. The warning comes two pages before Husserl takes Kant's term "categorical imperative" for the open problem of formal *Praktik* (Hua XXVIII 136; row 20). The figure is kept at a distance even as the vocabulary of command is about to return.

The case of the adiaphora shows what the definition leaves out. Whoever chooses something indifferent, where no good is in question, "conflicts with no ought" and does nothing wrong, "but also not rightly in the will-sense". The sentence that closes the passage turns on two balanced negations: "He does nothing owed, but neither is there any ought." The doxic sphere has no such case. A judgment is true or false, but a will can be neither right nor wrong. Husserl draws the consequence in one clause: "But he does not exercise will-reason; this goes precisely only upon convenience" (*diese geht eben nur auf Konvenienz*). The reason of the will is not the avoidance of fault. It is the positive achievement of fit, and where no value calls for a will there is nothing for will-reason to do.

The definition did not stand. The editor records Husserl's later marginal "No!" against this section. In §21 Husserl revises the terminology explicitly: "we may not, as we did last time, already speak of an objective ought in the case of convenience". Convenience is "completely independent of the attainability of what is willed", and the earlier terminology had "tacitly" counted attainability into it (Hua XXVIII 149; row 22). The ought of the passage was too cheap. It followed from fit alone, so a will could fit a value it had no power to realize and still be said to answer a demand. The revision keeps the passage's achievement, convenience as the will's own rightness, valued for itself and decidable "before all deliberation" about what can be attained. It takes away the ought, which now also requires that what is willed be attainable for the subject.

## §18(b), Hua XXVIII 135–137: absorption, and the categorical imperative as what cannot be absorbed

Reads [§18(b), paragraph 7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#b-the-will-laws-of-choice-the-absorption-law-the-idea-of-the-practical-realm-and-the-problem-of-the-categorical-imperative).

In a choice, the ought goes to the best. The lesser goods remain goods "in and for themselves", but the practical ought that each would carry on its own "passes over" to the highest value and is "absorbed" by it. Absorption gives only an ought "under reservation", because the range of a choice is set by whoever asks the will-question and can always be widened. A categorical imperative would be an ought that nothing can absorb. Husserl does not define it by its content or by the form of a maxim. He defines it by an incapacity, "precisely the incapacity to be absorbed". Kant's term thereby becomes the name of a problem: whether, for a willing subject, there is a practical realm closed enough that its best cannot be surpassed.

> In the findings or regularities so far we feel ourselves very restricted. If I stand in a choice, if I put the will-question, shall I A or B and so on, then within the frame of its disjunction the objectively understood practical ought passes over from the lower values to the highest value and is completely "absorbed" by it. You will convince yourselves here that the sense of the disjunctive will-question corresponds exactly to the presuppositions of our law. Namely, "Shall I A or B or C?" means from the outset nothing other than "Is it right to choose A and at the same time not to choose B and C?", and likewise for every other member. If one holds sharply to the sense of this disjunctive will-question, one can state the law simply thus: In every choice the better absorbs the good, and the best absorbs everything else that is to be esteemed, in and for itself, as practically good. This absorption, however, creates no absolute ought but in general only a relative one, or, as we say better, no ought simpliciter but only an ought under reservation. We can also draw on Kantian expressions and say that we do not obtain a "categorical imperative" in this way.
>
> What defines a "categorical imperative"? Well, precisely the incapacity to be absorbed. But do we already know, and know in advance, that for the willing I there is in every given case an ought that cannot be absorbed, a best that cannot be outbid? What alone we know is that in every choice that has a determinately bounded practical realm consisting of a finite number of members there is a best, if a good is found in it at all. Possibly the best is a many-valued one; but with that we could come to terms. It is an axiom that among several equal optima to do none is incorrect, or to choose one of them, no matter which, is correct, practically owed, though only relatively.
>
> Evidently the relativity, the validity under reservation, for any choice lies in this, that it lacks, ⟨so long as⟩ nothing more precise is settled beyond it, a certain finality of bounding. Namely, it is something contingent upon what the chooser directs his will-question and how far he spans the questioning disjunction. He can possibly draw ever-new practical possibilities into the range of the question, and with each expansion of the practical realm the optimum will, generally speaking, change. Members absorbed at the earlier stage may also gain new significance with this very expansion. What is best in a choice and according to the measure of its practical realm is the practically demanded only under reservation, namely in case no better that absorbs it results upon expansion of the practical realm. But for this better the same can hold. It is clear that the possibility of a categorical imperative depends on our being able to find a determinate practical realm belonging to it, a practical field of efficacy objectively belonging to it, which is objectively closed and so no longer capable of expansion, but which ⟨exists⟩ for every time-point in which the I is to put any will-question at all. Is there such a thing possibly for single I's, or is there one *apriori* for every one? Our consideration, carried, as we may well say, throughout by aprioristic and insightful necessities, has led us to the most central problem of ethics, the problem of the categorical imperative.

The first sentence locates absorption precisely. What passes over from the lower values to the highest is "the objectively understood practical ought" (*das objektiv verstandene praktische Sollen*). The range of the question is the chooser's affair, and every widening of it can unseat the optimum and revive members already absorbed.

## §19, Hua XXVIII 139–141: "the better is the enemy of the good": formal only as logic is formal

Reads [§19, paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-idea-of-perfect-will-rightness-the-order-of-the-ethical-disciplines).

Formal *Praktik* is formal in exactly the sense in which formal logic is formal. It says nothing about which things are good, as logic says nothing about which propositions are true. Like logic, it presupposes that there is a material apriori: kinds of objects that carry value-predicates by their essence. A rule-giving for willing that claimed to do without matter would be a countersense, and here Husserl separates himself "sharply" from Kant. The formal principle that remains is a proverb raised to a law, "within the circle of the practically attainable the better is the enemy of the good", and its use is the use of a logical law: it admonishes and corrects, but it decides no case.

> The *formal* Praktik, which alone we had in view, speaks, with laws such as those of consequence and those of the highest practical good, *eo ipso* about nothing material; it contains nothing of determinate material values and goods, exactly as formal logic says nothing of determinate objects, their kinds and genera, of the determinate world with its empirical particularities, and so grounds nothing of materially determinate truths. But that there can be, or are, such truths, and that singular material truths are bound with respect to their matter by essence-laws into which the material content-kinds and content-genera enter, this it presupposes in formal generality, or its own axioms refer to it; and likewise for formal axiology and Praktik. Were there no material *apriori*, were there no kinds and genera of objects distinguishable that by their generic essence would carry value-predicates with them *apriori*, then the concept of objective value would have no hold, and consequently neither would the idea of an objectively prefigured preferability and the idea of a best. A formal rule-giving of valuing and willing, on the presupposition that the matter of valuing and willing, that is, the particularity of content of the value- and will-objects, could be left out of account, is a countersense. In this respect, then, we must sharply separate our ways from the Kantian doctrine.
>
> Our parallelistic considerations, whose feasibility has proved itself everywhere, show us that the analogy of the formal and the material in the practical sphere holds throughout with that in the logical sphere, and accordingly we cannot fall into the confusion of wanting to prescribe, out of merely formal laws and with the mere help of the content-empty categorical imperative, in the respectively given and materially determinate single case, what in it is the practically demanded, the absolutely owed. Formal logic with all its laws cannot put us in a position to derive the least truth of fact. It does not even encompass all apriori truth, but precisely only formal truth. If logic tells us that every proposition contradictory in its matter is false, ⟨it⟩ can still in no single case of propositions of materially determinate matter tell us whether it is contradictory or not. If it tells us that such and such forms of inference are correct and such and such incorrect, it still tells us of no single inference laid before us with materially determinate terms whether it is correct or incorrect. Already the application to the materially determinate is a step that goes beyond logic, and all the more the fixing of the truths and laws belonging to the particular matters by virtue of their particular kind.
>
> Exactly so for formal axiology and Praktik. Within the circle of the practically attainable the better is the enemy of the good; to set the better behind is unconditionally incorrect, to choose the best possible is unconditionally demanded as the only and therefore absolutely correct thing. To keep this formal principle before one's eyes, to state it expressly, can be useful, just as it can be useful to formulate formal-logical laws for oneself and let oneself be admonished by them. But the question of what is good and better and best is not answered for us in this way; and theoretically, too, only a small, if the most fundamental, part of the task of a scientific and, to begin with, apriori ethics is settled.

The first sentence fixes "formal" by its scope. Formal *Praktik* speaks "*eo ipso* about nothing material". The italic on *formale* is Husserl's, and it marks the word against Kant's use of it. In §5 Husserl had held Kant's "formal" at a distance with quotation marks: the categorical imperative, "however 'formal'", is "a principle of positive decision" and leaves no room for a material incorrectness (Hua XXVIII 43; row 3). Here the course's own sense of the word is set out, and it is the logician's sense. Logic "says nothing of determinate objects" and "grounds nothing of materially determinate truths". The sentence that follows reverses the direction. That there are material truths bound by essence-laws, logic "presupposes in formal generality". The formal discipline is not independent of the material. It is the form of a whole whose content it leaves to others, and it needs that content to exist.

The counterfactual then states the stake for value. "Were there no material *apriori* … then the concept of objective value would have no hold", and with it neither "the idea of an objectively prefigured preferability" nor "the idea of a best". This takes the conclusion of §18 and turns it against Kant. The absorption law of §18 needs a best (row 20), and a best needs ranked values grounded in the essence of their objects. "A formal rule-giving (*Regelgebung*) of valuing and willing", on the assumption that the matter "could be left out of account", "is a countersense" (*Widersinn*). *Widersinn* was the name of the skeptic's self-cancellation in §4 (row 2), the sense of a position that contradicts what its own sense presupposes. The second paragraph lays the logical analogy beside the practical case point by point, and the claim is one of limitation. "Content-empty" (*inhaltsleer*) is the epithet of Kant's imperative. The formal principle of the course is, by its own account, no fuller. The difference lies in what each claims. Kant's imperative claims to prescribe, and Husserl's principle claims only to exclude.

The third paragraph states that principle, and the wording shows what Husserl has done to a proverb. "Within the circle of the practically attainable the better is the enemy of the good" (*Im Kreis des praktisch Erreichbaren ist das Bessere der Feind des Guten*). The German saying has two uses. It can warn that striving for the better spoils a good already in hand, or it can say that the better displaces the good. The opening phrase decides between them. Within the circle of what can actually be attained, the better is not a risky ambition that endangers the good. It is a possibility that can be realized, and it defeats the good by the devaluation law (row 18). The restriction removes the prudential sense, in which the better is often out of reach, and leaves the formal one. Two clauses with "unconditionally" (*unbedingt*) follow and spell out the proverb as law. Setting the better behind is "unconditionally incorrect", and choosing the best possible is "unconditionally demanded as the only and therefore absolutely correct thing". The repeated *unbedingt* claims the unconditional for the principle, the property §18 had just made the mark of a categorical imperative (row 20). The claim is made "within the circle of the practically attainable", and whether that circle is closed is the problem §18 left open.

What the principle is good for is stated modestly. Keeping it in view "can be useful, just as it can be useful to formulate formal-logical laws for oneself and let oneself be admonished (*mahnen*) by them". The verb belongs to the vocabulary of practice, not of theory. A formal law reminds and warns. It does not instruct. "But the question of what is good and better and best is not answered for us in this way." The grades of *gut* that made up the absorption law (row 20) now appear as an open question. The settled part of ethics is "small, if the most fundamental". The section goes on to list the material tasks, the classes of goods, sensible and rational, personal and social, and to place formal axiology and *Praktik* first "in the order of the ethical disciplines" (Hua XXVIII 141).

The proverb has a history in the volume, and the 1914 version changes its status. In 1908/09 Husserl cited Brentano's formula, "Do at all times the best among the attainable in the whole sphere subject to your rational influence", and added: "The better is the enemy of the good, that is, popularly speaking, the practical main law that underlies it" (*das ist populär gesprochen das praktische Hauptgesetz*, Hua XXVIII 350–351). In 1911 the principle is put in quotation marks and again called popular: "We could designate the result popularly thus", a principle grounded on "'The better is the enemy of the good'", which "says: Do the best among the attainable!". Brentano's version, Husserl adds, "can only be a preliminary popular expression", and it needs "very careful and penetrating considerations" to set out its "sense, limits and formal presuppositions" (Hua XXVIII 221). In 1914 the quotation marks and the word "popular" are gone. The proverb stands unmarked as "this formal principle", with its limit built into its first phrase. In the lectures it had always been the popular summary of a law, and here it is stated as the law itself. In the same passage Husserl denies that this law, or any formal law, can say what the good is.

## §21, Hua XXVIII 149: "what someone cannot do, he also ought not": the ought gets a subject

Reads [§21, paragraph 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-objectivity-of-the-practical-possibilities-and-their-relativity-to-the-subject-the-convenience-or-inconvenience-grounded-in-the-essence-of-the-will-act-the-components-of-perfect-will-rightness).

The ought is more than convenience. A will is convenient when it fits a value, and this can be decided before anyone asks whether what is willed can be attained. A will is owed (*gesollt*) only if, in addition, what it wills is attainable for the one who wills: "what someone cannot do, he also ought not". With attainability, subjectivity enters the definition of the ought. Practical possibilities are objective, since whether something is possible for me is settled whatever I think about it. But they are possibilities "for" a subject, and no two subjects share the same realm of them. Truth has no such index. At this point the parallel between logic and ethics, which has carried the course since §1, gives way. The analogy that survives is with the rightness of surmise.

> But convenience is not everything. Looked at closely, not even the realizability of the resolve, the fulfillability of the willing, comes into question for it. If we bring it out purely as the one component that belongs to the possible objective validity of the will, then we may not, as we did last time, already speak of an objective ought in the case of convenience. We do well to modify our terminology of last time suitably. A resolve is objectively an owed one, an objectively demanded one: in this there lies, if ⟨we⟩ immerse ourselves in the customary sense of such words, convenience or appropriateness too, but yet more. It is therefore better, as we now do, to distil out the mere convenience and to speak for the corresponding resolve only of appropriateness, but not yet of owedness. That last time we decided terminologically otherwise lay in this, that we tacitly included in convenience at least practical attainability already, which we now, for good reasons, do not do. Convenience is completely independent of the attainability of what is willed, as indeed the former can be decided before all deliberation of the latter. On the other hand, *apriori* nothing is objectively demanded of a subject that is unattainable for it. What someone cannot do, he also ought not. The resolve that has the predicate of the ought has thereby a richer predicate than that of convenience. Now we see, however, that already with this expansion subjectivity plays a role that has no analogy in the domain of judgment or truth.
>
> An objective truth is truth in itself, and this "in itself" says that the judging subject does not relativize the truth: the truth in itself is not truth for this one or that one. Every objective judgment is decided in itself. Whoever judges, if only he judges correctly, judges the same with regard to one and the same propositional matter; he judges precisely the one and same proposition, which is truth. The truth is what it is, even if no one should judge it. On the other hand, what is a practical possibility for me need not on that account be one for everyone else. Practical possibilities are essentially related to the subject capable of will. That a doing is a practical possibility for a subject, or whether it is, is determined in itself; the subject itself may judge incorrectly about it, may hold something to be practically possible that in truth is not. To that extent we have something objective here. But the objectivity of the practical possibility is essentially bound to the subject in question, and the same holds for the subject's entire practical realm for each moment of its possible acting, that is, the realm of all practical possibilities objectively existing for it at the moment in question. If it has practical possibilities at all, and so an entire practical realm, then this is in any case its own, and one will in any case be able to assert this one thing *apriori*: that two I-subjects cannot in principle have an identical entire practical realm, not the identically possible sphere of efficacy. At the same time this realm in general changes from time-point to time-point.

The suspected seam at this point recorded in the key-passage list does not exist in the file as it now stands: nothing is lost before the next paragraph. Smaller slips are quoted as the file has them: "as" for *als*, "wenn uns … vertiefen" without *wir* (supplied in the English), "herauszustillieren" for *herauszudestillieren* (to distil out), and "als das Konvenienz" for *als das der Konvenienz*. None affects the sense.

The first paragraph corrects the previous lecture in plain terms: "we may not, as we did last time, already speak of an objective ought in the case of convenience". In §18 the ought was defined from convenience alone (row 19), and the editor records a later marginal "No!" against that definition. Husserl gives the reason for the error as a hidden premise: "we tacitly included in convenience at least practical attainability already" (*stillschweigend … mit einbezogen*). The cure is a chemical verb, "to distil out" the mere convenience, and a new word for what is left: the convenient resolve is "appropriate" (*angemessen*), "but not yet" owed. The grounds for the change are partly linguistic. In the objectively owed, "if we immerse ourselves in the customary sense of such words (*in den sprachüblichen Sinn*), there lies convenience … but yet more". In the previous lecture the ought was a "figure of speech" to be held strictly to a definition (row 19). Now ordinary usage is consulted, and it is taken to show that the definition was too thin. The ordinary sense of "ought" is used against the stipulation.

The "more" is given in two sentences that differ in register. The first is formal: "*apriori* nothing is objectively demanded of a subject that is unattainable for it". The second is a maxim, without technical vocabulary: "What someone cannot do, he also ought not" (*Was jemand nicht kann, das soll er auch nicht*). It is the contrapositive of "ought implies can", stated in the everyday words of the will's modal verbs *können* and *sollen*, with the indefinite *jemand* (someone) as subject. The *jemand* is the point. The formal law speaks of "a subject"; the maxim makes the ought hold of a person. Attainability, unlike convenience, cannot be decided from the will-act and its object alone. It depends on what this subject, at this moment, can do. That is why "already with this expansion subjectivity plays a role that has no analogy in the domain of judgment or truth". The expansion makes the predicate "richer" and, in the same step, ties it to someone.

The second paragraph shows the disanalogy through the preposition "for" (*für*). Truth "in itself" is "not truth for this one or that one" (*nicht Wahrheit für den oder jenen*); "the truth is what it is, even if no one should judge it" (*ob auch niemand sie urteilet*, with the old subjunctive). Then: "what is a practical possibility for me (*für mich*) need not on that account be one for everyone else". The same preposition that is excluded from truth is admitted to possibility. The course had excluded it from value as well. In §11 Husserl wrote that "as little as there is a true and false for someone (*für jemanden*) … so little is there, and in the same sense, a beautiful and ugly, a good and bad … for someone" (Hua XXVIII 89; row 12). The law of excluded fourth made values objective in the sense of truth. §21 keeps that, since convenience is still decided by the essence of the will-act (Hua XXVIII 147–148). It adds that the ought, unlike value, is "for" someone. The relativity enters at the level of attainability, not at the level of value.

Husserl does not let this relativity become subjectivism, and the paragraph turns on the word "objective". That a doing is possible for a subject "is determined in itself"; the subject "may judge incorrectly about it". "To that extent we have something objective here." The next sentence then qualifies: "But the objectivity of the practical possibility is essentially bound (*wesentlich gebunden*) to the subject in question". The result is an objectivity indexed to a subject, and to a moment, since the realm "changes from time-point to time-point". The final claim is put as an *apriori* truth: "two I-subjects cannot in principle have an identical entire practical realm". The categorical imperative of §18 needed a closed practical realm for each I at each moment (row 20). Here Husserl concedes that this realm, if it exists, is unique to its subject. A categorical ought can then be the same for everyone only in its form. What it demands of each person is that person's own best.

The analogy is not given up, only moved. The paragraph before had already said that objective will-rightness stands as objective judgment-rightness does, "or still better surmise-rightness" (*oder noch besser Vermutungsrichtigkeit*, Hua XXVIII 147). The paragraph after makes the point explicit. Where the analogy with objective truth fails, it "comes forward again in comparison with the cases of surmise-rightness", because the rightness of a surmise is related to "an objectively fixable stock of knowledge and not-knowing", and "this entire realm is bound to the subjects", differing between subjects and for the same subject at different times (Hua XXVIII 149–150). Surmise has what truth lacks: a correctness that is objective and still relative to the position of the one who surmises. The course began by setting logic beside ethics (row 1). By its last section the logic that fits the will is the logic of probability.

The *Prolegomena* show which conception of truth the passage presupposes and leaves standing for judgment. Against specific relativism Husserl wrote there: "What is true is absolutely, 'in itself' true; truth is identically one, whether men or non-men, angels or gods grasp it in judging" (*Was wahr ist, ist absolut, ist „an sich“ wahr*, Prolegomena §36, Hua XVIII 125; the English translation in the corpus agrees). The talk of "truth for this or that species" is there "a countersensical way of speaking". In 1914 the same "in itself" is cited without change, and "truth for this one or that one" is still excluded. What changes is the domain. For the ought, the "for me" that the *Prolegomena* call countersense becomes an essential structure. It is not relativism, because the practical realm of each subject is determined in itself. The step needed no new theory of truth. It needed the recognition that the will's correctness has two components, and that only one of them, convenience, is as free of the subject as truth is.

## §21, Hua XXVIII 153: "if your willing is right, it is not yet valuable": the twofold imperative

Reads [§21, paragraph 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#the-objectivity-of-the-practical-possibilities-and-their-relativity-to-the-subject-the-convenience-or-inconvenience-grounded-in-the-essence-of-the-will-act-the-components-of-perfect-will-rightness).

Rightness and value come apart in the will. A will is right when it aims at the best among the attainable, and anyone can check this from outside, as one can check a calculation. A will has value in the original sense only when the one who wills is determined by reasons, so that the rightness is constituted in the willing itself and not merely matched by it. The course therefore ends with two imperatives. The objective one, "Do the best among the attainable", states what the formal *Praktik* has shown. The noetic one, "Will and act rationally!", states what the formal laws cannot say, because they "say nothing of insight". The sentence that divides them, "if your willing is right, it is not yet valuable on that account", is the last thesis of the lectures.

> Evidently it is the same in every domain of acts, with every kind of meanings, of position-takings, and so specially in the domain of the will too. In the objective attitude we judged with insight about will-rightness. In doing so we carried out an essence-analysis of the components that make up full rightness in the highest sense. The will-goal lies fully and wholly in what we designated as the objectively owed. The highest goal-rightness, a convenience in an expanded sense of the word, belongs to the will of the best among the attainable. But in itself, originarily considered, the value does not lie in the goal-right willing. Here too we have the difference between blind will and reason-will. A will too can be considered objectively; its rightness can, so to speak, be recalculated by any rational subject. But only when the one who wills himself, so to speak in himself, calculates, that is, when he lets himself be determined in his willing by genuine reason-motives, when he does not cast his will-vote blindly but in such a way that the goal-rightness is originarily constituted in his willing, is he in himself consciousness of an ought to which the willing adequately corresponds; only then does he have originary value.
>
> Certainly the objective imperative "Do the best among the attainable" is unassailable, for we see its objective validity with insight. A will that wills otherwise is evidently incorrect, so to speak untrue; it misses the goal prefigured in the general essence of the will. It is just as we establish in the objective consideration of logic: a contradiction may not be judged; which we can also express analogically as an imperative: Commit no contradiction, keep to the logical laws as laws of possible judgment-rightness! But although the logical laws say nothing of insight, we nevertheless demand insightful judging in noetics, and so insightful willing in the noetics of the will. We therefore also have the imperative: Will and act rationally! If your willing is right, it is not yet valuable on that account; only rational willing is valuable.

> Ebenso steht es offenbar in jedem Aktgebiet, bei jederlei Art von Verneinungen, von Stellungnahmen, also speziell auch im Willensgebiet. In der objektiven Einstellung urteilten wir einsichtig über Willensrichtigkeit. Wir vollzogen dabei eine Wesensanalyse der Komponenten, die volle Richtigkeitt im höchsten Sinn ausmachen. Das Willensziel voll und ganz liegt in dem, was wir als objektiv Gesolltes bezeichneten. *Note on the text.* "Bei jederlei Art von Verneinungen, von Stellungnahmen" (with every kind of negations, of position-takings) is the *Vermeinen* confusion. The phrase is a list of act-kinds, which every act-domain, the will's included, possesses. Negations are not such a kind, while *Vermeinungen* (meanings, intendings), set in apposition to *Stellungnahmen* (position-takings), are. It is read and translated as *Vermeinungen*, as the main commentary also reads it. The file also has "Richtigkeitt", "Wortsinns" and "das objektive Imperativ" for *der objektive Imperativ*. They are quoted as found and do not affect the sense.

The paragraph is built on two verbs of calculation, *nachrechnen* and *rechnen*. Any rational subject can "recalculate" (*nachrechnen*) the rightness of a will, check it afterwards as one checks another's sum. The preceding paragraph had used the same word for judgment, a possible "external spectator" who "recalculates the judgment-calculation, so to speak, in place of the one judging" (Hua XXVIII 153). The value lies elsewhere: "only when the one who wills himself, so to speak in himself, calculates" (*in sich selbst sozusagen, rechnet*). The prefix *nach-* (after) is the whole difference. The same operation, done afterwards and by another, establishes rightness. Done in the act by the one who acts, it confers value. "So to speak" (*sozusagen*) is used twice, and it marks the calculation as a figure. What it stands for is given in the next clause: to let oneself "be determined in one's willing by genuine reason-motives". The image of the "will-vote" (*Willensvotum*) returns from §7, where the will, like every position-taking, "speaks" and "gives as it were a vote" (Hua XXVIII 64). A vote can be cast blindly. If the will votes for the best without seeing why, the result is right, and the act still has no merit.

The passage hinges on the adverb "originarily" (*originär*). "In itself, originarily considered, the value does not lie in the goal-right willing." The qualifier is needed because the course has said the opposite of convenient willing without it. In §18 a will that conforms to a value had "a special convenience, which is itself to be valued axiologically and is then positive-valued" (Hua XXVIII 134–135; row 19). "Goal-rightness" (*Zielrichtigkeit*) is here glossed as "a convenience in an expanded sense of the word": convenience, enlarged in §21 by attainability (row 22) and by the requirement of the best. So the right will has a value. It is the value one ascribes to it from outside, and the preceding paragraph explains its status for judgment. Where we value an uninsightful judgment positively, the value lies "only in the ideal possibility of transforming this judgment into an insightful one" (Hua XXVIII 152). The value of a right but blind act is borrowed from the insight it could have had. Husserl states the doxic version in the subjunctive of reported speech: a valuable judging "be" (*sei*) one performed on rational motives, and the rightness "lie" (*liege*) originarily in insightfulness. "We" are said to think so (*meinen wir*). The will's version is in the indicative.

The second paragraph sets the two imperatives side by side, and the logical analogy shows how far each reaches. "A contradiction may not be judged" is a law of logic. "Commit no contradiction!" is the same law turned "analogically" into an imperative. The objective will-imperative is the law of the best turned in the same way. A will that breaks it is "so to speak untrue" (*sozusagen unwahr*): Husserl borrows the predicate of judgment for the will and marks the borrowing. The second imperative has a different source: "although the logical laws say nothing of insight, we nevertheless demand insightful judging in noetics". The concession "although" (*obschon*) shows that the demand is not derived from the laws. It comes from noetics, the theory of the acts, and the "so" (*und so*) that carries it over to the will claims a parallel the course has supported throughout but does not prove here. The "therefore" of "We therefore also have the imperative" (*Wir haben also auch*) gathers up what was said rather than concluding it. Then the closing sentence: "If your willing is right, it is not yet valuable on that account (*darum noch nicht*); only rational willing is valuable." "Not yet" (*noch nicht*) is exact. Rightness is a stage on the way to value, not its opposite and not its equal.

The passage closes what §20 opened. There Husserl stated an "antinomy" that arises "when one passes from formal ethics into noetics". The objective imperative was derived without presupposing "the reason of the one who wills", so a will can hit the best "by chance", "yielding to a blind drive, for example a blind habituation stemming from a conventional upbringing" (Hua XXVIII 143). If insightful willing is then the best, the best of the practical realm is not the best, "an odd way of speaking" (*sonderbare Rede*, Hua XXVIII 143–144). §20 resolved it by placing reason-acts inside the practical realm: I can direct my will upon rational deliberation, and so the law of the best itself requires me to strive for clarity, "whereby I not only secure the otherwise and only relatively best, but add, in the insight itself, a new value" (Hua XXVIII 145). §21 ends on the other side of the same thought. Insight is not one more good to be ranked. It is the condition under which the will has original value at all. The two imperatives are not rivals, because they are about different things: the first about what is willed, the second about how it is willed.

The 1908/09 lectures had the same pair joined in a single command and ranked by degree. In the note on "the value of insightful willing" (Beilage X), to which the editor refers at this point, the will directed upon the best among the attainable "is generally good, the insightful will directed upon this goal is generally better". The supreme commandment reads: "Will insightfully the best among the attainable!" (*Wolle einsichtig das Beste unter dem Erreichbaren!*, Hua XXVIII 356–357). In 1908/09 the right will is good and the insightful will better, on one scale. In 1914 the right will is "not yet valuable", and value belongs "only" to rational willing. The difference is the course's distinction between objective rightness and originary value, which the earlier note had not drawn. The single 1908/09 imperative also becomes two in 1914. This keeps the formal *Praktik*, whose laws "say nothing of insight", apart from the noetics of the will, which says what that *Praktik* cannot.

## Beilage I, Hua XXVIII 155: "in the lectures I identified the two": valuing is not joy

Reads [Beilage I, paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#beilage-i-to-pp-47-f-60-ff-75--1315-obliquities-in-my-doctrine-of-valuing-in-the-lecture-on-formal-axiology-and-praktik).

To value the existence of something as good is not to rejoice in it. The lectures had treated the two as one: goods were defined as the correlates of rational joy, so "that A is, is good" and "that A is, is joyful" said the same. The note takes this back. Valuing, even valuing that takes existence into account, is a position-taking that does not aim at anything. Joy, grief, desire, wish and will are "interested" acts. They aim at an end and come to rest in it, and joy is the end-mode in which a longing is satisfied. The distinction moves joy out of the class of valuings and into the family of desire. Husserl reports that he has leaned this way "repeatedly", and "so again now", and the note ends by asking how the difference is to be clarified.

> How, now, do these content-valuings and existential valuings stand in relation to the acts "interested" in the existence of objects, the acts of joy, grief, regret, desiring and wishing, and even of willing? The question is whether "That A is, is good" (or) "That A is, is un-good" is equivalent to "That A is, is joyful" (or) "That A is, is unjoyful". In the lectures I identified the two. But one must consider carefully whether rightly. I inclined repeatedly to the view (and so again now!) that here a distinction is to be made. […]
>
> Here, surely, there is no talk of desiring, but also none of wishing. The longing that lies in wishing, or even in willing, or that practical "It shall be" which is the correlate of the "I will": of that there is no talk here. Likewise with joy. I have always tried to give joy its coordination with desiring ((with) wishing: "desiring" in ordinary speech has a colouring that points back to the I in the special sense of wishing-for-me, etc.). Joy is the act that satiates, fulfils the longing of desiring; only that it is not essential to it to be the end of a transition from longing tending-toward to fulfilment. Nevertheless, it is the end-mode of desiring. In "desiring" lies unfulfilledness; it is otherwise with joy. Being content in oneself in feeling, either in satisfaction of a desire that is thereby stilled, or being content, glad, without desire. (Being content is ambiguous in this sense.) But valuing is no longing, and no being-content as a mere end-mode of longing in the sense of joy. The "being-interested" of joy and grief, of hope and fear, of wishing in all its forms, and above all of willing, is something quite other than valuing and its valuing-taking-into-account of existence or non-existence. The former acts are in the pregnant sense aiming or terminating acts, but valuing is not. But how is the difference to be clarified?

The hyphen in *Wertend-in-Rechnung-Ziehen* is Husserl's compound. The bracket "((zum) Wünschen" and the parenthetical "(bzw.)" are as in the file. The omitted lines (Hua XXVIII 155) give examples of existential valuing, ending with the being of science as "ought-to-be" (*„Seinsollendes“*). The editor dates the note "probably 1914" (*Wohl 1914*). The whole passage falls on p. 155.

The question is put as a test of equivalence between two sentences in quotation marks: "That A is, is good" and "That A is, is joyful". The sentences have the same subject clause and differ only in the predicate. The word for the test is *gleichwertig* (equivalent, literally "of equal value"), and the lectures had answered it yes. The admission follows in three short sentences that change tense and person: "In the lectures I identified the two. But one must consider carefully whether rightly. I inclined repeatedly to the view (and so again now!) that here a distinction is to be made." The first person reports a past doctrine. The impersonal "one must" opens the question. The past tense of "I inclined repeatedly" records an oscillation, and the parenthesis with its exclamation mark puts the present moment into the series: the inclination returns, without having become a settled view. The note does not announce a correction. It records a recurring doubt about one of the lectures' foundations, at a date the editor puts in the same year as the lectures.

The identification the note withdraws is the one §5(c) made in defining its terms. There Husserl called "the values that form the correlate of rational existential valuings of the genus joy" goods, and the corresponding negative values evils. From this it followed that every beautiful object, if it exists, "is necessarily … object of a justified joy" (Hua XXVIII 47; row 4). The motivation-law of §9 stated the same for the act: whoever likes something as beautiful "must rationally rejoice in the case of the certainty that this 'beautiful thing' is" (Hua XXVIII 73). In those passages "existential valuings of the genus joy" is a single phrase. The note splits it. The genus to which joy belongs is not valuing. The heading of the note keys it to these places, "to pp. 47 f., 60 ff., 75, §§13–15", so the retraction reaches from the definition of goods through the will-phenomenology (rows 4, 6, 8–10, 14–16).

The quotation marks mark where the distinction falls. The acts of joy and desire are "interested" (*„interessierten“*) in existence, and later their "being-interested" (*„Interessiertsein“*) is set against valuing. The opening of the note had already put "valuing" itself in quotation marks when it was used for good-valuing, "existentially interested 'valuing'" (*existenzial interessiertes „Werten“*). The marks there say that the lectures' word did not fit what it named. The interested acts are defined by a word family: *Langen* (longing), *langendes Hintendieren* (longing tending-toward), satiating and fulfilling (*sättigend*, *erfüllend*), the "end-mode" (*Endmodus*). Joy is "the end-mode of desiring". It is what longing becomes when it is fulfilled, and it can also occur where no longing preceded it, as "being content, glad, without desire". The parenthesis "(Being content is ambiguous in this sense)" registers that German *Zufriedensein* covers both the stilling of a desire and a contentment that never desired. Valuing falls outside the family: "valuing is no longing, and no being-content as a mere end-mode of longing in the sense of joy". The defining contrast is put in two adjectives: the interested acts are "in the pregnant sense aiming or terminating" (*abzielende bzw. terminierende*), "but valuing is not". The Latin *terminierend* (ending, coming to rest in a terminus) and the German *abzielend* (aiming) give the same feature from its start and its end. Valuing takes existence "into account" (*in Rechnung ziehen*, a bookkeeping phrase). It reckons with existence and does not aim at it.

The distinction also changes the "ought-to-be". The lines just before the second paragraph conclude that the being of something valuable is itself valuable: "the being of science is not mere being but 'ought-to-be'" (*„Seinsollendes“*). The second paragraph insists that this ought has nothing to do with will: "the practical 'It shall be', which is the correlate of the 'I will': of that there is no talk here". The value-ought is reached by valuing alone, before any desire or will. This prepares the three-class scheme of the note's end, where the correlates of judgment, valuing and willing are "the existent, the good (value), the ought-to-be" and the will's *fiat* is "the practical 'It shall be!'" (Hua XXVIII 157; row 25). The scheme can make valuing a thesis in its own right only because joy has here been removed from it.

The argument stops at a question. "But how is the difference to be clarified?" The next paragraph sets out the alternatives without deciding between them. Does every joy and wish contain a valuing "as a component or substrate", or only the rational ones? And "are the blind acts not 'meanings'?" (Hua XXVIII 156; *Vermeinungen*, row 25). The cost of the change is visible in the lectures it corrects. The law of excluded fourth and the laws of value-comparison were stated for values (rows 12–13), and they survive. What does not survive is the easy passage from value to joy that the motivation-laws of §§9–10 used as their model case (rows 8–10). If good-valuing is not joy, then the rational joy that "must" follow the certainty of a good's existence is a further act. Its rightness depends on the valuing, and it is not identical with it. The note makes the course's act-classification less tidy. In exchange, valuing becomes a "cool" position-taking (Hua XXVIII 156), and Beilage I can later set it beside judgment and will as one of three kinds of thesis.

## Beilage I, Hua XXVIII 157: "a word is lacking here": the *fiat* and the three kinds of thesis

Reads [Beilage I, paragraph 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#beilage-i-to-pp-47-f-60-ff-75--1315-obliquities-in-my-doctrine-of-valuing-in-the-lecture-on-formal-axiology-and-praktik).

The will is not a desire. It presupposes longing, but it adds something that longing does not contain, the *fiat*, the practical "It shall be!". German has no word for the class to which the will then belongs, and Husserl says so: "a word is lacking here". The note therefore regroups the acts. The proper position-takings, the "theses", are three: judgments, which posit being; valuings, which posit being-good; and willings, which posit the willed ought-to-be. Feelings, desires and joys are not theses at all. They are produced by the theses or founded on them. The correlates are three as well: the existent, the good (value), the ought-to-be. This revises the course's founding parallel. In the lectures the emotional and volitional acts were grouped together as analogues of belief (§7, row 6). Here the will stands with valuing and judgment, and the rest of the emotional life drops out of the class of positions.

> Willings, or resolves, deeds and so on, are also evaluated. The will can show differences in regard to "energy"; it can also be more or less passionate. But that then concerns the intensity of underlying desires. I will something and desire it passionately. Will and desire do not cancel each other. I passionately wish for the victory and will the victory. But I need not will passionately, and the will itself probably has no differences of intensity of passion at all. The will does not belong in the series of the desiring acts (joy, wish), the longing ones. But a word is lacking here. The will is also a longing, but it brings in something new, which precisely presupposes longing but is not longing (desiring, wishing): the *fiat*, the practical "It shall be!". It is the question whether this has in itself phenomenological differences of "energy". Have we not to parallelize willing and valuing and set them over against all "emotive acts"? And have we not to say that properly "position-taking" acts, proper theses, are: judgments (positings of being), valuings (positings of being-beautiful and being-good, of being-valuable), willings (practical positings, positings of the willed ought-to-be)? So does not the doctrine of "acknowledgments" and "rejections", approvals and disapprovals (as another word for "position-takings"), have its good sense? Approvals of being, acknowledgments of being, valuing approvals, willing approvals; over against them the receptive experiences (intentional experiences) of presentations (perception, memory, but without position-taking) and the receptive feelings; then the feelings produced by the position-takings, the feelings founded in them. The desires with the joys; these confer on the objects of the joy their joyfulness, which is objective if it itself withstands an objective valuation. Feelings: no position-takings, no theses, no "evaluating" acts in the widest sense. Correlatively we would have three things: the existent, the good (value), the ought-to-be (the practically good). Do the two last belong perhaps in one class? No. I value present, past, future, I value being of every kind as good. I speak my willing "It shall". For the present: it already stands there as good; so I value it. But I can in addition value it as right. It is right so (in case it is in my will-sphere, in the will-attitude). It is so, and as good and best let it remain so; it is right so. For the future: It shall be so, through the will, practically.

German:

> Auch Wollungen bzw. Entschlüsse, Taten etc. werden bewertet. Der Wille kann Unterschiede zeigen in Hinsicht auf die „Energie“; er kann auch mehr oder minder leidenschaftlich sein. Das betrifft dann aber die Intensität unterliegender Begehrungen. Ich will etwas und begehre danach leidenschaftlich. Wille und Begehren heben sich nicht auf. Ich wünsche leidenschaftlich den Sieg und will den Sieg. Ich brauche aber nicht leidenschaftlich zu wollen, und der Wille selbst hat wohl gar keine Intensitätsunterschiede der Leidenschaftlichkeit. Der Wille gehört nicht in die Reihe der begehrenden Akte (Freude, Wunsch), der langenden. Aber es fehlt hier ein Wort. Der Wille ist auch ein Langen, aber er bringt ein Neues herein, das eben Langen voraussetzt, aber nicht Langen ist (Begehren, Wünschen): das *fiat*, das praktische „Es soll sein!“. Es ist die Frage, ob dieses in sich phänomenologische Unterschiede der „Energie“ hat. Haben wir nicht Wollen und Werte zu parallelisieren und allen „Gemütsakten“ gegenüberzustellen? Und haben wir nicht zu sagen, eigentlich „stellungnehmende“ Akte, eigentliche Thesen sind: Urteile (Seinssetzungen), Wertungen (Setzungen des Schön- und Gutseins, des Wertseins), Wollungen (praktische Setzungen, Setzungen des willentlichen Seinsollens). Also hat nicht die Lehre von den „Anerkennungen“ und „Verwerfungen“ – Billigungen und Mißbilligungen (als anderes Wort für „Stellungnahmen“) ihren guten Sinn? Seinsbilligungen, Seinsanerkennungen, bewertende Billigungen, willentliche Billigungen; demgegenüber die rezeptiven Erlebnisse (intentionale Erlebnisse) der Vorstellungen (Wahrnehmung, Erinnerung, aber ohne Stellungnahme) und die rezeptiven Gefühle; dann die durch die Stellungnahmen erzeugten Gefühle, in ihnen fundierten Gefühle. Die Begehrungen mit den Freuden; die erteilen den Gegenständen der Freude die Erfreulichkeit, die objektiv ist, wenn sie selbst vor einer objektiven Wertung standhält. Gefühle: keine Stellungnahmen, keine Thesen, keine im weitesten Sinn „bewertenden“ Akte. Korrelativ hätten wir dreierlei: Seiendes, Gutes (Wert), Seinsollendes (praktisch Gutes). Gehören die beiden letzten etwa in eine Klasse? Nein. Ich werte Gegenwärtiges, Vergangenes, Künftiges, ich werte Sein jeder Art als gut. Ich spreche mein willentliches „Es soll“. Für Gegenwärtiges: Es steht schon als gut da, so werte ich es. Aber ich kann es dazu werten as recht. Es ist recht so (falls es in meiner Willenssphäre ist, in der Willenseinstellung). Es ist so und als Gutes und Bestes bleibe es so, es ist recht so. Bei Zukünftigem: Es soll so sein – durch den Willen, praktisch.

*Note on the text.* "Wollen und Werte zu parallelisieren" (to parallelize willing and values) is read as *Wollen und Werten* (willing and valuing), as the main commentary also reads it. The next sentence lists *Wertungen* (valuings) beside *Wollungen* (willings) as theses, and acts are being compared, not acts with their correlates. The reading depends on this, and the print should be checked. "As recht" is the file's misreading of *als recht* (as right). The preceding paragraph, also on this theme, has "Anegzogensein" for *Angezogensein* (being attracted) and the line "Sind die blinden Akte nicht „Verneinungen“?" (Hua XXVIII 156). That question can only mean "are the blind acts not *Vermeinungen*", that is, meanings or intendings, and it is cited here in that sense.

The paragraph begins by removing intensity from the will. "Energy" (*„Energie“*) and passion seem to belong to willing, but "that then concerns the intensity of underlying desires". The example separates the two acts: "I passionately wish for the victory and will the victory". "Will and desire do not cancel each other", and they are not the same. The previous paragraph had made the same move for valuing. Tiredness weakens joy in a picture gallery, "but the valuings do not change. They have no intensity, they have only differences of 'higher value' and 'lower value'" (Hua XXVIII 156). Intensity marks the feelings, and its absence marks the position-takings. Valuing and willing are alike in having none. The "probably" (*wohl*) in "the will itself probably has no differences of intensity" and the open question whether the *fiat* "has in itself phenomenological differences of 'energy'" leave the point unsettled for the will.

Then the admission: "The will does not belong in the series of the desiring acts (joy, wish), the longing ones. But a word is lacking here." The lack is precise. The will "is also a longing", so it cannot be simply set against the longing acts. But it "brings in something new, which precisely presupposes longing but is not longing". German has *Begehren* and *Wünschen* for the longing and *Wollen* for the whole, but no word for the new element as such. Husserl fills the gap with Latin and a quotation: "the *fiat*, the practical 'It shall be!'" (*das praktische „Es soll sein!“*). In §15 the will spoke "its creative 'Let it be!'" (*Es werde!*, Hua XXVIII 107; row 15), and in §14 willing meant an "It shall be" against wishing's "It may be" (Hua XXVIII 105; row 14). The note keeps the second formula. The *fiat* is named by its correlate, the shall-be, and that correlate becomes the third member of the scheme that follows.

The scheme is set out as two questions with "Have we not" (*Haben wir nicht*), followed by a third question with "So does not … have its good sense?". It is a proposal, not a result. The theses are named with their correlates in parentheses: "judgments (positings of being), valuings (positings of being-beautiful and being-good, of being-valuable), willings (practical positings, positings of the willed ought-to-be)". Every member is a *Setzung* (positing). The word that joined belief to the emotions in §7, *Stellungnahme* (position-taking), is now put in quotation marks: properly "position-taking" acts. The marks restrict it. Not every act that §7 called a position-taking still counts as one. The excluded acts are listed: "the receptive experiences" of presentation, "the receptive feelings", "the feelings produced by the position-takings, founded in them", "the desires with the joys". "Feelings: no position-takings, no theses, no 'evaluating' acts in the widest sense." Joy, which Beilage I.2 had separated from valuing (row 24), here leaves the class of theses entirely. Its object's "joyfulness" is "objective if it itself withstands an objective valuation". Joy borrows its objectivity from a valuing that tests it.

The correlates follow the same order: "the existent, the good (value), the ought-to-be (the practically good)". Husserl asks at once whether the last two are one class, and answers "No". The answer turns on time and on the first person. "I value present, past, future", "being of every kind". The will speaks only "my willing 'It shall'", and for the present it can add only that "it is right so … and as good and best let it remain so", in the subjunctive *bleibe* (let it remain). The ought-to-be of value, "the being of science is … 'ought-to-be'" (row 24), is not the practical ought-to-be. The will's ought has a future, or a present it keeps, and it holds "through the will, practically".

The proposal revises the lectures at their base. In §7 Husserl had argued that a liking and a wishing "mean" (*vermeinen*) just as a judging does. The emotional acts as a whole, and the will with them, were analogues of belief's position-taking, and that was "the ground for the parallelism of the kinds of reason" (Hua XXVIII 64; row 6). The note narrows the parallel. It now runs between three kinds of thesis, and most of the emotional life falls outside it. The phrase "the doctrine of 'acknowledgments' and 'rejections'" (*Anerkennungen*, *Verwerfungen*) names its source only by its vocabulary. In Brentano's *Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkt*, "the difference of acknowledging and rejecting" (*Anerkennen und Verwerfen*) is the qualitative difference within judgment (vol. II, ch. 8 (4), p. 105). The same chapter also argues for what the note denies: "As we separate presentation and judgment, so we unite feeling and will" (*so vereinigen wir Gefühl und Willen*, vol. II, ch. 8 (4) §1, p. 83). The note keeps Brentano's account of judgment as acknowledgment and extends it to valuing and willing as "valuing approvals, willing approvals". It breaks his single class of feeling and will in two. The will goes with judgment as a thesis, and feeling is left as a founded state. A word is lacking because the inherited classification has no place for what the will adds to desire. Husserl names that addition, the *fiat*, and does not yet integrate it.

## Beilage II, Hua XXVIII 157–158: empty possibilities "are to be set as zeros"

Reads [Beilage II, paragraph 1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-28-vorlesungen-uber-ethik-und-wertlehre-commentary.md#beilage-ii-to--21-the-exclusion-of-empty-possibilities-from-practical-consideration).

What I have no reason to think I can attain does not count in my choice. A possible good for which nothing speaks, and against which nothing speaks either, enters the calculation of the best as zero. Only a possibility with a "preponderant chance" of being realizable is a practical possibility "for me". This bounds the practical realm. §18 had made the categorical imperative depend on a realm closed against expansion (row 20), and §21 had made that realm the subject's own (row 22). The note closes it from the subject's knowledge. The horizon of mere possibilities can be treated "as not present", and the realm reaches as far as goods are actually found. The closure is epistemic. It holds for what I know or can reasonably surmise, and the note ends by admitting that it is different for someone who believes he knows more.

> In the practical realm the law is valid: empty possibilities of goods and evils, those for which nothing speaks or against which nothing speaks either, are to be set as zeros. Perhaps better still: in order that a really possible good, and in general something really possible, should come into question as practical for me, some preponderant chance must speak for the possibility of being able to realize it; that is, positive and negative instances must not hold the balance. If it is not present, then it is admittedly not to be said that this is something unattainable, but it does not concern me. It is questionable whether this is precise enough. I could resolve to become a general, a great reformer, a founder of a religion, etc. Are these practical possibilities? At any rate these goals depend on so many contingencies that I must say, on that ground alone, that it would be infinitely improbable to attain them, even if I possessed the positive gifts for it. Whether I have them, I do not know. Nothing speaks for it, perhaps nothing against it either. So it cannot be put into the reckoning at all; probability equal to zero. I have, then, an empty horizon filled with mere possibilities, with arbitrary imaginabilities against which nothing speaks, for which nothing speaks, or with such possibilities for which as good as nothing and against which something infinitely preponderant speaks. This horizon I can treat as not present. I have only to restrict myself to the goods attainable in certainty or with a certain finite probability, and indeed only the positive ones; and in doing so I have to take the horizon only as far as these givennesses reach, as far as I find such goods. For example, if I ascribe eternal life to myself, then this world suffices, for of the beyond I know nothing. Admittedly, for one who believes he knows of it, matters already stand otherwise.

*Note on the text.* P. 158 begins inside the second sentence: "irgend-" ends p. 157 and "eine überwiegende Chance" opens p. 158. The hyphen is joined here. The editor dates the note "probably 1914" (*Wohl 1914*). The file's text of Beilage II ends at Hua 159 (src L2025). Whether it runs on in print cannot be told from the file.

The note opens with a law and immediately offers a better version. The law is arithmetical: empty possibilities "are to be set as zeros" (*sind als Nullen einzusetzen*). *Einsetzen* is the verb for substituting a value into a formula. The calculation of the best from §18 is kept, and the empty possibilities enter it with the value nothing. The image had a precedent. In §18 the adiaphora were "to be regarded within every choice like zeros" (*wie Nullen*, Hua XXVIII 134; row 19). Those were real possibilities with no value. Here the zero applies to goods and evils that do have value but have no claim to be realizable. Then: "Perhaps better still" (*Vielleicht noch besser*), and the law is restated as a condition on what counts "as practical for me" (*als Praktisches für mich*). The second version does not zero a value. It denies the possibility a place in the realm. The "for me" is the preposition of §21 (row 22): the practical is what is possible for this subject, and the condition is now made precise as "some preponderant chance" (*irgendeine überwiegende Chance*). The gloss is an image of scales: "positive and negative instances must not hold the balance" (*dürfen sich nicht die Waage halten*). The language is the language of surmise. §21 had said that the analogue of will-rightness is "still better" surmise-rightness, which is related to "an objectively fixable stock of positive and negative solicitations" (Hua XXVIII 147, 149–150). Beilage II carries the doxic modality into the definition of the practical realm itself.

The next sentence makes the decisive distinction, and the particle *zwar* (admittedly) carries it. "If it is not present, then it is admittedly not to be said that this is something unattainable, but it does not concern me" (*aber es geht mich nichts an*). An empty possibility is not declared impossible. It may be attainable for all I know. It is excluded on other grounds: it does not concern me. The everyday phrase *es geht mich nichts an* belongs to the first person, and it marks the exclusion as a matter of my standpoint, not of the thing. The difference matters against the 1911 course. There the zero was reserved for the unrealizable: "In the context of a choice the realizable disvalues are perhaps only relative zeros, but the non-realizabilities absolute zeros" (*absolute Nullen*, Hua XXVIII 220). In 1911 the realm was bounded by what is in fact attainable. The 1914 note bounds it by what I have grounds to think attainable, and it adds a class of zeros that 1911 did not have. These are the possibilities that may be real and still do not count.

Husserl doubts the formulation at once, "It is questionable whether this is precise enough", and the example shows why. To become "a general, a great reformer, a founder of a religion" is not impossible. But it depends on so many contingencies that it "would be infinitely improbable", and whether I have the gifts for it "I do not know. Nothing speaks for it, perhaps nothing against it either." From this he concludes: "So it cannot be put into the reckoning at all; probability equal to zero." The step joins two different cases. A possibility "for which as good as nothing and against which something infinitely preponderant speaks" has a probability close to zero. A possibility for which "nothing speaks … perhaps nothing against it either" has no assignable probability, since the instances "hold the balance" at nothing. The note counts both as zero. The "So" (*also*) gives the conclusion more force than the example does. What carries it is the practical rule that follows: "This horizon I can treat as not present" (*wie nicht vorhanden*). "As" or "like" (*wie*) marks the treatment as an as-if. The horizon is not shown to be empty of goods. It is set aside for the purposes of choice.

The remaining sentences fix the realm positively. I am "to restrict myself to the goods attainable in certainty or with a certain finite probability, and indeed only the positive ones". Evils drop out, as §18 had required: where goods stand beside evils, "the evils are under all circumstances to be struck" (Hua XXVIII 134). I am to take the horizon "only as far as these givennesses reach, as far as I find such goods". The verb "find" (*finde*) makes the boundary depend on what is found. The practical realm extends as far as my knowledge of attainable goods and stops there. This is the closure §18 asked for, a realm "no longer capable of expansion" for each moment (Hua XXVIII 137; row 20). It is reached by a decision about what to count, not by surveying everything there is.

The last example shows how far the closure depends on the subject. "If I ascribe eternal life to myself, then this world suffices, for of the beyond I know nothing. Admittedly, for one who believes he knows of it, matters already stand otherwise." Even the greatest possible good is excluded if nothing known speaks for its attainability. But the exclusion holds only for the one who does not believe he knows. The believer's realm is different, because his stock of supposed knowledge is different. "Believes he knows" (*zu wissen glaubt*) is carefully chosen: the realm follows the subject's position of knowledge, not the truth of the matter. This is the subject-relativity of §21 at its sharpest. Two subjects "cannot in principle have an identical practical realm" (Hua XXVIII 149; row 22), and here the difference goes as deep as their beliefs about the beyond.

The rest of the note shows what the bounding leaves open. A resolve to seek insight into the best "in future ever again" is itself a good to be placed in the reckoning (Hua XXVIII 158). Deliberation takes time: "while I weigh this, the time passes", and "how far should the consideration extend? What shall I do in a minute, … for all the future?" (Hua XXVIII 158–159). Husserl answers with an ideal I that surveys a horizon of goods, and with a proverb: "What I do not know does not trouble me" (*Was ich nicht weiß, macht mich nicht heiß*, Hua XXVIII 159). It restates the opening rule as a saying. The unknown good "does not concern me", as long as nothing speaks for it. With this rule the course can state its categorical imperative, the best among the attainable, for a realm that is closed in fact. What makes it closed is the limit of what the subject knows. The formal *Praktik* began from the analogy with logic (row 1). It ends with its final boundary drawn by the logic of probability.

# Husserl, *Ideen I*, Parts Three and Four: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md) covers §§63–153 of the First Book, Parts Three and Four. Each reading follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds that meaning, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the book and in his other works. Each heading links to the section of the main commentary that holds the block it reads. Unit references such as "§85.6" name the sixth paragraph of the commentary under § 85. The commentary sets the titles of §79 and §80 as plain paragraphs rather than headings, so readings of §79 link to the nearest heading above, § 78. A separate commentary on §§111–153 has its own [close readings](husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary-close-readings.md); where a passage was read there too, the reading here says so but is written afresh.

The German is quoted from Husserliana III/1, *Ideen zu einer reinen Phänomenologie und phänomenologischen Philosophie, Erstes Buch*, edited by Karl Schuhmann, in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-3-1-ideen-i-allgemeine-einfuhrung-in-die-reine-phanomenologie-recheck.md`). The commentary cites § numbers only. Headings here add the Hua III/1 page, taken from the running heads in the file, and so accurate to about a page. The file also carries the pages of the first edition of 1913 in brackets, and Husserl's own cross-references ("S. 233") use those; where a reading cites one, it says "1913 p.". All English renderings of Husserl are working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows. They are not Kersten's or Boyce Gibson's. Kersten's translation (Collected Works II) is in the corpus, itself badly scanned, and was used to check the sense of every passage quoted; it is cited only where it departs from the German in a way that matters or settles a damaged reading.

The file is an unreviewed conversion, and four cautions hold for every reading. First, Hua prints Husserl's emphasis in spaced type, and the conversion has dropped almost all of it; the italics that survive are incomplete. No reading argues from emphasis or from its absence. Quotation marks survive, in mixed forms (`,,…''`, `„…“`), and are quoted as the file has them. Second, OCR has damaged the text in places, most seriously at a seam in §114 and in §129, where "von" (from) stands for *vor* (before) and reverses the sense. Third, several § headings are demoted, promoted, split or set inline (§§94, 128, 130, 136, 143, 146), and some stretches are hard-wrapped with line-end hyphens and leaked Hua margin line numbers ("Sei- 25 ten"); the readings cite sections by Husserl's numbering, not by the file's heading levels, and leave leaked numbers out of the quotations, saying so. Fourth, footnotes are mislinked, split or given repeated labels; they are identified by order and content, not by label. Each reading says where damage touches its passage, and none repairs the wording.

## §65, Hua III/1 139: phenomenology under its own norms

Reads [§65.6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#the-back-reference-of-phenomenology-to-itself).

The relatedness of phenomenology to itself (*Bezogenheit auf sich selbst*) is, in §65, not the plain fact that the phenomenologist's own acts are experiences and so fall within the field he studies. Husserl grants that fact and dismisses it in a few sentences. The claim of the passage is normative. Statements of method are themselves eidetic statements about consciousness, so they stand under the norms they state, clarity, insight, faithful expression, and they must remain checkable against those norms by renewed reflection. The claim has the form of a requirement, not a finished result, and the conclusion that the whole book "is through and through phenomenology" holds of its content, not of the form its concepts have yet reached.

> The essential relatedness of phenomenology to itself shows itself here in this: that what is weighed and established in methodical reflection under the titles clarity, insight, expression and the like belongs, for its part, itself to the phenomenological domain; that all the reflexive analyses are phenomenological essence-analyses; and that the methodological insights won stand, as regards their establishment, themselves under the norms that they formulate. In new reflections one must therefore be able at any time to convince oneself that the states of affairs stated in the methodological statements are to be given in perfect clarity, that the concepts used really adapt themselves faithfully to what is given, etc.
>
> What has been said holds evidently for all methodological investigations relating to phenomenology, however wide we may span their frame, and so it is understood that this whole writing, [which] wants to prepare the way for phenomenology, is according to its content itself through and through phenomenology.

German:

> Die wesentliche Bezogenheit der Phänomenologie auf sich selbst zeigt sich hier darin, daß, was da in der methodischen Reflexion unter den Titeln Klarheit, Einsicht, Ausdruck u. dgl. erwogen und festgestellt wird, seinerseits selbst zur phänomenologischen Domäne gehört, daß all die reflexiven Analysen phänomenologische Wesensanalysen sind und die gewonnenen methodologischen Einsichten hinsichtlich ihrer Feststellung selbst unter den Normen stehen, die sie formulieren. In neuen Reflexionen muß man sich [124] davon also jederzeit überzeugen können, daß die in den methodologischen Aussagen ausgesagten Sachverhalte in vollkommener Klarheit zu geben seien, daß die benutzten Begriffe sich dem Gegebenen wirklich treu anpassen usw.
>
> Das Gesagte gilt offenbar für alle auf die Phänomenologie bezüglichen methodologischen Untersuchungen, wie weit wir ihren Rahmen auch spannen mögen, und so versteht es sich, daß diese ganze Schrift, der Phänomenologie den Weg bereiten will, ihrem Inhalte nach selbst durch und durch Phänomenologie ist.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "diese ganze Schrift, der Phänomenologie den Weg bereiten will" with no relative pronoun; a "die" (which) has dropped out, and Kersten's "this whole essay, which aims at preparing the way" confirms the sense. The bracketed "[124]" is the 1913 page.

The three clauses with "that" (*daß*) climb from domain to status to norm. What method weighs "belongs, for its part, itself" (*seinerseits selbst*) to the phenomenological domain: the doubled reflexive marks that the topics of method, clarity and insight and expression, are modes of consciousness and so objects like any other. The analyses that treat them "are" essence-analyses. And the insights won "stand … under the norms that they formulate". The restriction "as regards their establishment" (*hinsichtlich ihrer Feststellung*) is exact. It does not say the norms are true because they apply to themselves; it says that the act of fixing them, and the statements that fix them, are held to the standard those statements set. A methodological statement is one more phenomenological statement, and it can fail as one.

The second sentence turns the status into a duty, and its modality is the point. "One must … be able at any time to convince oneself" (*muß man sich … jederzeit überzeugen können*): a standing possibility of checking, not a check already done. The subjunctive "are to be given" (*zu geben seien*) belongs to reported content and keeps the claim at the level of what the statements commit one to. "Therefore" (*also*) draws the duty from the status, and the inference holds: if method-statements are eidetic statements, they owe the clarity that eidetic statements owe. The final sentence is looser. "Evidently" (*offenbar*) and "so it is understood" (*so versteht es sich*) present the claim about "this whole writing" as a corollary, but the corollary needs more than the premise gives: that everything the book contains is either method-reflection or eidetic description of pure consciousness. Husserl secures it with a qualifier, "according to its content" (*ihrem Inhalte nach*). The book is phenomenology in what it treats, not yet in the scientific form of its concepts.

The paragraph before the passage supplies that distinction, and it is what makes the self-application coherent. Every self-related discipline, Husserl concedes, must begin "with methodical aids that it has only afterwards to form scientifically and definitively"; the concepts of an incipient phenomenology are already phenomenological but "gain their scientific stamp only in the system of the already grounded science" (Hua III/1 138). The method is first practised "naively" (*naiv betätigt*), to get one's bearings in the new field; reflection on its essence then takes over "the function of a general and logically strict grounding of the method", and, "consciously followed", the method gains the rank of a scientific method that permits "limiting and improving criticism" (Hua III/1 139). Self-application is thus the second stage of a two-stage structure. It is what allows the method to correct its own earlier practice, and it presupposes that practice. The circle Husserl's opponent sees is a sequence.

The passage turns the opening objection of §65 from a question about domain into a question about norms. The objection was that the experiences of the research itself would belong to what is researched. Husserl's first reply took the model of psychology and logic: "the thinking of the logician [is] a logical thing, namely itself belonging within the range of the logical norms", and self-relatedness would be worrying only if knowledge of everything else depended on knowledge of the thinker's own thinking, "a visibly absurd presupposition" (Hua III/1 138). The logician's case already joined domain and norm, and the passage carries both over to phenomenology. The book's index pairs the two cases: it lists a "back-reference of logic to itself" at 1913 p. 122 beside that of phenomenology at pp. 122 ff. (file L8645, L8793). Just before, §64 had settled the analogous worry about the phenomenologist as natural man by the geometer: "the mathematizing subject does not belong to the eidetic content of the mathematical propositions themselves". §65 adds what the geometer lacks. The geometer's thinking does not belong to geometry; the phenomenologist's thinking, purified, does belong to phenomenology.

What follows is the execution of the passage's list. §66 takes the third title first, expression: words drawn from common speech take on a fixed sense as they "cover" (*,,decken''*) the intuitively given, and can then be fixed scientifically (Hua III/1 140). §67 takes clarity and builds the scale of givenness (row 2 below), and §§68–70 give the method of clarification. Each is a case of the norms "formulated" in §65 and, by §65's claim, subject to them. The demand behind the passage was set two sections earlier. In §63 phenomenology "must by its essence raise the claim to be 'first' philosophy", and therefore "demands the most perfect freedom from presuppositions and, in relation to itself, absolute reflective insight"; "its own essence is to realize the most perfect clarity about its own essence and so also about the principles of its method" (Hua III/1 136, file L2930). §65 shows that the demand is not circular. The concrete test comes in §79, where the psychologists' doubts about self-observation are met as doubts about reflection, and the reply has to be given in reflection (rows 6 and 7 below). In §77, Husserl calls the essence-study of reflections of "fundamental methodological significance" for phenomenology, because "all modes of immanent essence-grasping" fall under the concept of reflection (Hua III/1 166). Method-reflection is reflection, and so the theory of reflection is the place where self-application becomes a doctrine.

In 1913 the self-relatedness is "no difficulty" and raises "no serious misgivings" (*keine ernstlichen Bedenken*). The *Cartesian Meditations* keep the phrase and harden the norm. At the close of the Fifth Meditation (§63, Hua I 178), the critique of transcendental-phenomenological knowledge is "the further and last problematic" of phenomenology, its "self-critique" (*Selbstkritik*) with a view to the extent, limits and modes of apodicticity, and the first phenomenology is "in its kind itself still affected by a naivety (apodictic naivety)". Then: "given the essential back-reference of phenomenology to itself, this critique too demands a critique" (*bei der wesensmäßigen Rückbeziehung der Phänomenologie auf sich selbst fordert auch diese Kritik eine Kritik*), though without a troublesome regress, "despite the evident possibility of iterable transcendental reflections and critiques". The 1913 structure is there, naive practice and then a grounding reflection, but the norm has changed from clarity to apodicticity, and the second stage is no longer within reach of the book that names it. §65 already makes the claim later works keep: method stands under its own norms. What it leaves open is how far the book meets them.

## §67, Hua III/1 142: clarity as givenness, from zero to one

Reads [§67.8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#method-of-clarification-nearness-to-givenness-and-farness-from-givenness).

In §67 "givenness in the pregnant sense" is one dimension with intuitiveness and clarity. A consciousness gives its object exactly so far as it intuits it, and intuits it exactly so far as the object is clear to it, and the degrees of the three are one scale, bounded below by darkness and above by "pure self-givenness". Two restrictions make the identification work. Givenness here is not originary givenness: a phantasy can stand at the top of the scale. And darkness is not the lowest degree of givenness but its absence, so the scale's lower bound lies outside it. The first restriction is what lets §70 privilege free phantasy; the second sits uneasily with what §68 says next.

> In the case of full unclarity, the counter-pole of full clarity, nothing at all has come to a givenness; the consciousness is a "dark" one, no longer intuiting at all, in the proper sense no longer "giving" at all. We have accordingly to say:
>
> Giving consciousness in the pregnant sense and intuitive, as against non-intuitive, clear as against dark: that coincides. Likewise: levels of givenness, of intuitiveness, of clarity. The zero-limit is darkness, the one-limit is full clarity, intuitiveness, givenness.
>
> Here, however, givenness is not to be understood as originary givenness, and so not as perceptual. The "self-given" we do not identify with the "originarily given", the "bodily" [given].

German:

> Im Falle der vollen Unklarheit, dem Gegenpol der vollen Klarheit, ist gar nichts zu einer Gegebenheit gekommen, das Bewußtsein ist ein ,,dunkles", gar nicht mehr anschauendes, im eigentlichen Sinne überhaupt nicht mehr ,,gebendes". Wir haben danach zu sagen:
>
> Gebendes Bewußtsein im prägnanten Sinne und anschauliches, gegenüber unanschaulichem, klares gegenüber dunklem, das deckt sich. Desgleichen: Stufen der Gegebenheit, der Anschaulichkeit, der Klarheit. Die Nullgrenze ist die Dunkelheit, die Einsgrenze ist die volle Klarheit, Anschaulichkeit, Gegebenheit.
>
> Dabei ist aber Gegebenheit nicht zu verstehen als originäre Gegebenheit, somit nicht als wahrnehmungsmäßige. Das ,,selbstgegeben" identifizieren wir nicht mit dem ,,originärgegeben", dem ,,leibhaft".

The middle paragraph is set off as a definition and has almost no verbs. Its one finite verb is "that coincides" (*das deckt sich*), and the demonstrative gathers three pairs into a single subject: giving, intuitive as against non-intuitive, clear as against dark. The three words come from different places. "Giving" (*gebend*) names a character of the act; "intuitive" (*anschaulich*) a mode of presenting; "clear" (*klar*) belongs first to what is presented, since the paragraph before said that "correlatively the grasped is in its sense 'unclear'" (Hua III/1 141). The coincidence joins an act-predicate to an object-predicate, so the scale is at once noetic and noematic before §88 has the terms for it. The verb itself is the one §66 had just used for the word that "covers" (*,,decken''*) the intuitively given (Hua III/1 140). Clarity and givenness coincide as an expression coincides with what it expresses: in extension, at every point.

"Likewise" (*Desgleichen*) carries the step from coincidence to scale without a verb at all, and it claims more than "that coincides". Three concepts can coincide in extension without coinciding in degree. Husserl asserts the second and gives it no separate argument; the description of the preceding paragraph is meant to have shown it, in the image of an "absolute nearness" where givenness is "absolute, that is, pure self-givenness", and of a remainder of unclarity that "overshadows" moments of what is "itself" given, so that they do not reach "the circle of light of the purely given" (Hua III/1 142). The section title's "nearness to givenness" and "farness from givenness" (*„Gegebenheitsnähe“ und „Gegebenheitsferne“*) supply the spatial image; the sentence about the limits supplies the numbers. "Zero-limit" and "one-limit" (*Nullgrenze*, *Einsgrenze*) are a mathematician's words for the bounds of the unit interval, and they make clarity a magnitude.

The paragraph before the definition hedges the lower bound. In full unclarity "nothing at all has come to a givenness", and the consciousness is "in the proper sense no longer 'giving' at all". The quotation marks around "dark" and "giving" mark the words as used of a limit case. Darkness is then not a low value of givenness but its absence, and the "zero-limit" is a bound the scale approaches but whose occupant is not on it. The next section strains this. §68 opens by distinguishing "genuine gradual levels of clarity, to which one may also add gradual levels within darkness" (Hua III/1 143). If darkness has degrees, it is not a point, and the zero-limit marks the edge of a second scale rather than the end of one. The revision of the Sixth Investigation that Husserl took to galley proofs in the summer of 1913 (Hua XX/1, Text Nr. 3) had faced the asymmetry head on. There perfect clarity is "an unshiftable, though ideal limit-point, which in mathematical symbolism we should have to call the one-limit", while the zero-limit, "absolute 'dark'", has "an essentially other character": it is "not an ideal point of the clarity-series itself" but "an occurrence lying outside it, that of pure empty re-presentation", and so "not quite like the zero point of an intensity-series" (Hua XX/1 130, Text Nr. 3 §30). *Ideas I* keeps the vocabulary and drops the caution. §68 even says that clarity admits "continuous intensity-like differences, as intensities starting with zero, but closing above with a fixed limit" (Hua III/1 143), which is the very comparison XX/1 had qualified.

The third paragraph of the quotation holds the restriction the method needs. "Self-given" is not "originarily given", not given "bodily" (*leibhaft*). The definition is about the fullness with which something stands before consciousness, not about the mode, perceptual or re-presentational, in which it stands there. A remembered or phantasied colour can be given at the one-limit. Without this clause §70 could not say what it says, that free phantasy has a privileged position in essence-clarification (row 3): the eidetician needs exemplars at full clarity, and phantasy can supply them at will. The scale of §67 is what makes "clarification" one operation, bringing what floats before us "to normal nearness, to perfect clarity" (Hua III/1 141), whatever kind of intuition it is performed in.

Kersten blurs the chain of words on which the identification rests. He renders the "dark" consciousness as "blind", then the "dark" of the definition as "unclear" and the zero-limit as "complete obscurity". The German says *dunkel* three times and *Dunkelheit* once, so that the negative pole of the scale has one name throughout; the variation in English loses the point that the consciousness which is "no longer giving" is the same darkness that bounds the scale.

The understanding returns twice in the book, each time restricted. In §125 Husserl says that "our earlier discussions of the method of clarification", with a footnote to §67 (1913 p. 125), "need essential supplements" with respect to the statement, "the vital element of science" (Hua III/1 290). Clarity is now one task among two: confused "logical" acts must be brought to "perfect logical distinctness", and in the founding layer "all non-intuitiveness" turned into intuitiveness, and only then does "the method described earlier" come into action, with the concept of intuition "of clear consciousness" carried over from monothetic to synthetic acts. Distinctness is a dimension §67's scale does not measure. In Part Four the clause on originarity comes back as the axis of reason. The first basic form of reason-consciousness is the "originarily giving 'seeing'" of §136, and the evidence of §137 is divided into "originary" and "pure". The distinction §67 set aside so that phantasy could reach the one-limit is the distinction on which rational positing depends: a phantasy may give its object with full clarity and give it no right to be posited. §67 fixes clarity as the measure of method; Part Four will need a second measure for reason.

## §70, Hua III/1 148: "fiction" as the vital element of eidetic science

Reads [§70.11](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#the-role-of-perception-in-the-method-of-essence-clarification-the-privileged-position-of-free-phantasy).

"Fiction" in the closing sentence of §70 means the free forming of examples in phantasy, one's own or a poet's, and not untruth. Husserl's claim is that this forming is the medium in which eidetic knowledge lives and the store it feeds on. It is not the ground of that knowledge. The ground is the seeing of essences, for which fiction supplies exemplars, as §4 had said. The sentence is paradoxical only if "fiction" is heard in its other sense, as what is false, and Husserl names the ambiguity in the sentence itself.

> Extraordinarily much profit is to be drawn from the offerings of history, in still richer measure from those of art and especially of poetry, which are indeed imaginings, but which, as regards the originality of their new formations, the fullness of their single features and the gaplessness of their motivation, tower high above the achievements of our own phantasy, and which, moreover, through the suggestive power of artistic means of presentation, convert with particular ease into perfectly clear phantasies when they are apprehended understandingly.
>
> So one can really, if one loves paradoxical speeches, say, and, if one understands the ambiguous sense well, say in strict truth, that "fiction" makes up the vital element of phenomenology, as of all eidetic science, that fiction is the source from which the knowledge of "eternal truths" draws its nourishment.

German:

> Außerordentlich viel Nutzen ist zu ziehen aus den Darbietungen der Geschichte, in noch reicherem Maße aus denen der Kunst und insbesondere der Dichtung, die zwar Einbildungen sind, aber hinsichtlich der Originalität der Neugestaltungen, der Fülle der Einzelzüge, der Lückenlosigkeit der Motivation über die Leistungen unserer eigenen Phantasie hoch emporragen und zudem durch die suggestive Kraft künstlerischer Darstellungsmittel sich bei verstehendem Auffassen mit besonderer Leichtigkeit in vollkommen klare Phantasien umsetzen.
>
> So kann man denn wirklich, wenn man paradoxe Reden liebt, sagen und, wenn man den vieldeutigen Sinn wohl versteht, in strikter Wahrheit sagen, daß die „Fiktion“ das Lebenselement der Phänomenologie, wie aller eidetischen Wissenschaft, ausmacht, daß Fiktion die Quelle ist, aus der die Erkenntnis der „ewigen Wahrheiten“ ihre Nahrung zieht.

*Note on the text.* This stretch is hard-wrapped in the file with line-end hyphens ("Lebensele-/ment", "Wis-/senschaft", "vollkom-/men"); the lines are joined here and nothing else is changed. The file attaches to the sentence a note reading "Cf. § 4, p. 12 f." (1913 pages) and prints Husserl's own note, "A sentence that should be especially suited, as a quotation, to mocking the eidetic mode of knowledge in naturalistic fashion" (*Ein Satz, der sich als Zitat besonders eignen dürfte, die eidetische Erkenntnisweise naturalistisch zu verhöhnen*), only after the opening of §71, under the label `[^89]`. The reading takes the mockery note as the sentence's own, by its content; the file cannot show whether the reference to §4 belongs here or to an earlier sentence of §70.

The sentence says "say" twice, and the two sayings are not the same claim. The first is licensed by a taste, "if one loves paradoxical speeches"; the second by an understanding, "if one understands the ambiguous sense well", and only the second earns "in strict truth" (*in strikter Wahrheit*). Husserl does not resolve the paradox; he states it so that it can be heard both ways and tells the reader which way is true. The quotation marks do the same work. "Fiction" is in quotation marks once, at the moment the word risks being heard as "falsehood", and then drops them: the second "fiction" is the term in the sense now fixed. "Eternal truths" keeps its marks. It is borrowed vocabulary, the rationalists' name for necessary truths, held at a distance because Husserl's "essence-truths" need no eternity, only independence of fact. The paradox is the pairing of the two marked words. What posits nothing feeds the knowledge of what holds without exception.

Neither metaphor makes fiction a ground. A vital element (*Lebenselement*) is a medium, what a science lives in as a fish lives in water; a source from which knowledge "draws its nourishment" (*ihre Nahrung zieht*) is a supply. Both describe what eidetic knowledge works on and with. Neither says what makes it valid. The paragraph before had drawn the same line for perception: the eidetician must "fertilize" phantasy with rich observation in originary intuition, "whereby this fertilization of course does not mean that experience as such has a validity-grounding function" (Hua III/1 148). The sentence applies that restriction to fiction as well, without repeating it. The grounding basis of essence-thinking was named in §4: "the thinking about pure essences … needs as grounding bases the seeing of essences" (*bedarf … als begründende Unterlagen der Wesensschauung*, Hua III/1 17). In the strict sense, then, the sentence claims that the seeing of essences finds its exemplars in fiction and needs nothing from fact. The paradox lives in the metaphors and disappears in the doctrine.

The art paragraph shows how wide "fiction" is. Poetry's offerings "are indeed imaginings" (*die zwar Einbildungen sind*), yet they surpass "our own phantasy" in originality, fullness of detail and "gaplessness of motivation", and they turn into "perfectly clear phantasies" when "apprehended understandingly". Fiction is not the phenomenologist's private invention. It includes what he takes over from others and makes clear by understanding, and the criterion is §67's scale: what counts is that the exemplar reach the one-limit of clarity (row 2). History stands first in the list, and history is not fiction. The list ranks sources by the richness of the exemplars they offer, and fiction takes first place because it offers more, not because it offers unreality.

The word "ambiguous" (*vieldeutig*) has a second side within the book. A fiction is both the act of feigning and the figment it produces, and only the figment is unreal. §46 had used the difference against the sceptic: an ego whose stream held only phantasies is "evidently countersense", because "what floats before us may be a mere figment (*Fiktum*); the floating-before itself, the feigning consciousness, is not itself feigned" (Hua III/1 97). The act of fiction is an experience like any other, open to perceiving reflection. That is why fiction can be the vital element of phenomenology in particular. Its exemplars are feigned experiences, and the feigning of an experience is itself an experience. Kersten renders *Fiktion* here as "feigning", which keeps the act and loses the figment. The German holds both and so keeps the ambiguity the sentence names. The English chooses one of the two senses that the sentence asks the reader to keep together.

The geometer frames the passage, and §71 at once turns the frame into a problem. Husserl's case for phantasy was the geometer, who "operates in his research thinking incomparably more in phantasy than in perception of the figure or the model" and gains in phantasy "the incomparable freedom in the arbitrary reshaping of the feigned figures" that first opens "the expanses of essential possibilities" (Hua III/1 146–147). The phenomenologist, "in the most general respect", is no different. §71 then asks whether a descriptive eidetics is possible at all, given that the only effective eidetic sciences are mathematical, and §§72–75 answer that phenomenology cannot be a mathematics of experiences (rows 4 and 5). The methodological kinship stops at freedom in phantasy. What the free variation yields differs in kind: exact ideal shapes for the geometer, vague morphological types for the phenomenologist.

The freedom of fiction comes back in Part Four, bounded. In §149 Husserl varies a centaur in phantasy and finds that "in the progress of this ever more perfectly illustrating and more closely determining phantasy-process we are free to a large extent", "but completely free we are not", if the process is to stay concordant. "We are bound, for example, by a lawful space as a frame that the Idea of a possible thing in general firmly prescribes to us"; however we deform what is phantasied, "spatial shapes pass over again into spatial shapes" (Hua III/1 346). The index of 1913 lists the place as "fiction bound by essence-laws of what is feigned" (1913 p. 311). That binding is what the paradox of §70 left implicit. Fiction can feed the knowledge of necessary truths because it is free within limits it does not make, and the limits are the essences it shows. §149's centaur is §70's fiction with its limits made explicit: varied as far as concordance allows, it shows the region thing it cannot leave.

## §72, Hua III/1 152: the "definite" manifold

Reads [§72.8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#concrete-abstract-and-mathematical-essence-sciences).

A manifold is "definite" (*definit*) in §72 when a finite stock of concepts and axioms decides everything about it: every possible formation of the domain is fixed "in the manner of pure analytic necessity", so that "nothing more remains open". Husserl gives the concept twice, once as a property of the domain and once as a property of propositions about it, and declares the two equivalent. In the second form, "true" and "formal-logical consequence of the axioms" mean the same in such a domain. The concept is introduced to be denied of consciousness. §73 asks whether the stream of experience is a definite manifold, and the answer, that it is not, fixes phenomenology as a descriptive science.

> In other words, the manifold of spatial formations in general has a noteworthy logical fundamental property, for which we introduce the name "definite" manifold or "mathematical manifold in the pregnant sense".
>
> It is characterized by this: that a finite number of concepts and propositions, to be drawn as the case may be from the essence of the domain in question, completely and unambiguously determines the totality of all possible formations of the domain in the manner of pure analytic necessity, so that in principle nothing more remains open in it.
>
> […]
>
> An equivalent of the concept of a definite manifold lies also in the following propositions:
>
> Every proposition to be formed from the distinguished axiomatic concepts, in whatever logical forms, is either a pure formal-logical consequence of the axioms or a likewise pure counter-consequence, that is, formally contradicting the axioms, so that the contradictory opposite would then be a formal-logical consequence of the axioms. In a mathematically definite manifold the concepts "true" and "formal-logical consequence of the axioms" are equivalent, and likewise the concepts "false" and "formal-logical counter-consequence of the axioms".

German:

> Mit anderen Worten, die Mannigfaltigkeit der Raumgestaltungen überhaupt hat eine merkliche logische Fundamentaleigenschaft, für die wir den Namen ,,definite" Mannigfaltigkeit oder ,,mathematische Mannigfaltigkeit im prägnanten Sinne" einführen.
>
> Sie ist dadurch charakterisiert, daß eine endliche Anzahl, gegebenenfalls aus dem Wesen des jeweiligen Gebietes zu schöpfender Begriffe und Sätze die Gesamtheit aller möglichen Gestaltungen des Gebietes in der Weise rein analytischer Notwendigkeit vollständig und eindeutig bestimmt, so daß also in ihm prinzipiell nichts mehr offen bleibt.
>
> […]
>
> Ein Äquivalent des Begriffes einer definiten Mannigfaltigkeit liegt auch in folgenden Sätzen:
>
> Jeder aus den ausgezeichneten axiomatischen Begriffen, nach welchen logischen Formen immer zu bildende Satz ist entweder eine pure formallogische Folge der Axiome, oder eine ebensolche [136] Widerfolge, d.h. den Axiomen formal widersprechend; so daß dann das kontradiktorische Gegenteil eine formallogische Folge der Axiome wäre. In einer mathematisch-definiten Mannigfaltigkeit sind die Begriffe ,,wahr" und ,,formallogische Folge der Axiome" äquivalent, und ebenso die Begriffe ,,falsch" und ,,formallogische Widerfolge der Axiome".

The name arrives in quotation marks, ",,definite" Mannigfaltigkeit", and beside a second name that glosses it, "mathematical manifold in the pregnant sense". The marks flag a term being introduced (*einführen*), not a term doubted. What is striking is where Husserl puts the property. It belongs to "the manifold of spatial formations", that is, to the domain, and it is "logical": a property of the domain that shows only in how propositions about it hang together. The first definition keeps both sides. Concepts and propositions do the determining, but they are "to be drawn … from the essence of the domain", and what they determine is "the totality of all possible formations of the domain". The axioms are not stipulated. They are drawn from an essence and owe their completeness to it.

Three adverbs carry the property: "completely and unambiguously" (*vollständig und eindeutig*), "in the manner of pure analytic necessity". Completeness says no formation escapes; unambiguity says each is fixed one way; analytic necessity says the fixing needs nothing beyond logic once the axioms are in place. The clause that follows sums them up in spatial terms, "so that in principle nothing more remains open in it". A paragraph later Husserl recasts the same point in the vocabulary of definition: such a manifold is "mathematically exhaustively definable", the "definition" lies in the axiom system, and the axioms carry "the greatest conceivable prejudgment" (*das denkbar größte Präjudiz*), so that "nothing more remains undetermined". "Open" and "undetermined" are the words §§74–75 will use of consciousness, and their negation here is what consciousness lacks.

The "equivalent" moves the property from the domain to its propositions. Every proposition formable from the axiomatic concepts is either a consequence of the axioms or a "counter-consequence" (*Widerfolge*), a coinage modelled on *Folge* that names derivable contradiction. Then the decisive sentence: "true" and "formal-logical consequence of the axioms" are "equivalent". The quotation marks on "true" matter. They mark the word as a concept being compared, and the equivalence is restricted, "in a mathematically definite manifold". Husserl does not identify truth with derivability. He says that in one kind of domain the two coincide in extension. Truth remains the wider concept; definiteness is the case in which deduction exhausts it. The connective "also" (*auch*) presents the second formulation as an equivalent "lying in" the first, and Husserl does not show the equivalence. The objectual version ("nothing remains open") and the propositional version (every proposition decidable) coincide only if every determination of a formation is expressible by a proposition built from the axiomatic concepts. The passage assumes that, and the assumption is exactly what fails for a domain whose essences cannot be fixed in exact concepts.

The passage also asserts that space is definite. "In other words" (*Mit anderen Worten*) ties the name to the preceding sentence: "the generic essence of the geometrical domain, or the pure essence of space, is so constituted that geometry can be fully certain of really mastering, by its method, all possibilities, and exactly" (Hua III/1 152). The property is a reason for geometry's certainty and is itself certified by it. Husserl does not ask how one could know that a given set of axioms is complete. The next paragraphs generalize instead. The definitions "remain in force" under "formalizing generalization": the axiom system becomes a system of axiom-forms, the manifold a manifold-form (Hua III/1 153). Definiteness is a formal-ontological concept that a material domain can instantiate.

§73 applies the concept and sets its condition. The question is put twice, the second time "in a correlative turn": "Is the stream of consciousness a genuine mathematical manifold?" And does it resemble physical nature, which, if the physicist's guiding ideal holds, "would be designated a concrete definite manifold" (Hua III/1 154)? The condition follows at once. Exact concept-formation "is by no means a matter of our free choice and logical art"; for the axiomatic concepts, which "must be demonstrable in immediate intuition", it "presupposes exactness in the grasped essences themselves". Definiteness rests on exact essences, and whether a domain has them "depends entirely on the peculiarity of the domain". The question about consciousness is thus passed down to the question that §74 answers with the distinction of exact and morphological essences (row 5). §75 draws the conclusion. Consciousness is "a fluctuating that runs in different dimensions", so that "there can be no talk of a conceptually exact fixing of any eidetic concreta" (Hua III/1 157). The words from §72 come back reversed. A phenomenological concretum is taken "exactly in the determinacy and indeterminacy" with which it shows its thing, "in the wavering clarity and intermittent darkness" that belongs to it. A definite manifold leaves nothing open; the stream of experience is described in its openness.

*Formal and Transcendental Logic* (1929) restates the concept and asks the question §72 passed over. Its §31 repeats the propositional form, every proposition constructible from the axiomatic concepts is either "true", an analytic consequence, or "false", an analytic contradiction, "*tertium non datur*", and equates "definite axiom system" with the domain of a "nomological science". Then it adds: "How can one know a priori that a domain is a nomological one, e.g. space in its spatial shapes, and that the series of immediately evident spatial axioms one has set up comprehends the essence of space completely?" (Hua XVII 100). In 1913 the completeness of space was the premise from which the concept was read off. In 1929 it is a problem. The tie to Hilbert is not new: §72's own note had already said that "the close relation of the concept of definiteness to the 'completeness axiom' introduced by D. Hilbert for the foundation of arithmetic will be obvious to every mathematician", and had traced the concepts to Husserl's work of the early 1890s on the imaginary (§72, note 91 in the file). What is new in 1929 is that Husserl uses Hilbert's word "complete" (*vollständig*), "originally foreign to me", for his own concept (Hua XVII 101), and asks how completeness could be known. The file of Hua XVII is an unreviewed conversion, but the passage reads continuously. The change bears on *Ideas I*. The definite manifold was always an ideal of mastery that consciousness fails to meet; the later text adds that whether space itself meets it is a question the mathematician cannot settle by method alone.

## §74, Hua III/1 155–156: exact and morphological essences

Reads [§74.4–5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#descriptive-and-exact-sciences).

§74 divides essences by how they are reached. Morphological essences, the vague types that descriptive concepts express ("notched", "lens-shaped"), are taken "immediately from simple intuition" by abstraction, and they are vague in principle. Exact essences, the correlates of geometry's concepts, are not seen at all; they are "ideal limits" that morphological essences approach without reaching, and Husserl gives them "the character of 'Ideas' in the Kantian sense". The distinction is one of origin before it is one of content, and the passage says so. It is also what allows phenomenology, which has only morphological essences of experience to work with, to be rigorous without being exact.

> The geometrical concepts are "ideal" concepts; they express something that one cannot "see"; their "origin", and with it also their content, is an essentially other one than that of the concepts of description, as concepts that bring to expression essences taken immediately from simple intuition, and no "ideals". Exact concepts have their correlates in essences that have the character of "Ideas" in the Kantian sense. Over against these Ideas or ideal essences stand the morphological essences, as correlates of the descriptive concepts.
>
> That ideation which yields the ideal essences as ideal "limits", which in principle are to be found in no sensuous intuition, and which morphological essences each time more or less "approach" without ever reaching them, this ideation is something fundamentally other than essence-grasping through simple "abstraction", in which a salient "moment" is raised into the region of essences as something in principle vague, as something typical. The firmness and clean distinguishability of the generic concepts, or generic essences, that have their extension in the flowing, must not be confused with the exactness of the ideal concepts and of the genera that have nothing but ideal things in their extension.

German:

> Die geometrischen Begriffe sind „Ideal”begriffe, sie drücken etwas aus, was man nicht „sehen” kann; ihr „Ursprung” und damit auch ihr Inhalt ist ein wesentlich anderer als derjenige der Beschreibungsbegriffe als Begriffe, die unmittelbar der schlichten Anschauung entnommene Wesen und keine „Ideale” zum Ausdruck bringen. Exakte Begriffe haben ihre Korrelate in Wesen, die den Charakter von „Ideen” im Kantischen Sinne haben. Diesen Ideen oder Idealwesen stehen gegenüber die morphologischen Wesen, als Korrelate der deskriptiven Begriffe.
>
> Diejenige Ideation, welche die Idealwesen ergibt als ideale „Grenzen”, die prinzipiell in keiner sinnlichen Anschauung vorfindlich sind, denen sich jeweils morphologische Wesen mehr oder minder „annähern”, ohne sie je zu erreichen, diese Ideation ist [139] etwas grundwesentlich anderes als die Wesenserfassung durch schlichte „Abstraktion”, in welcher ein abgehobenes „Moment” in die Region der Wesen erhoben wird als ein prinzipiell Vages, als ein Typisches. Die Festigkeit und reinliche Unterscheidbarkeit der Gattungsbegriffe, bzw. Gattungswesen, die ihren Umfang im Fließenden haben, darf nicht mit der Exaktheit der Idealbegriffe verwechselt werden und der Gattungen, die durchaus Ideales in ihrem Umfange haben.

Nine words in two paragraphs stand in quotation marks, and they fall into two groups. "Ideal" (in the hybrid „Ideal”begriffe, where the marks close inside the compound), "Ideals", "Ideas", "limits" and "approach" are the vocabulary of exactness. "See", "origin", "abstraction" and "moment" are words Husserl uses of his own method elsewhere. The marks on the first group signal borrowing, from mathematics and from Kant; those on the second signal a restricted use. Exact concepts express what one cannot "see": the marks keep the word in the sense of §§3–4, where seeing is the intuiting of essences, so that the claim is precise. Ideal essences are not intuited at all, not even eidetically. Their "origin" is "an essentially other one", and "with it also their content". This is the order of the argument. The difference in what the concepts mean is derived from a difference in how they arise.

The second paragraph names the two operations, and the names are uneven. The exact essences come from "ideation" (*Ideation*), with no marks; the morphological from "simple 'abstraction'". In §4 "Ideation", in quotation marks, was the general name for seeing essences in free phantasy (Hua III/1 16). Here the word is narrowed to the operation that yields ideal limits, and the ordinary essence-seeing of descriptive science, and so of phenomenology, is called "abstraction", in marks that keep off the empiricist doctrine Part One had rejected. Phenomenology's own access to essences thus sits on the side of the pair that has the plainer name. The name is plainer, but its grasp is no weaker. An abstracted "moment" is "raised into the region of essences": it becomes an essence in the full sense, though "as something in principle vague, as something typical". "In principle" (*prinzipiell*) is the point. The vagueness is not a defect of grasp that more care would remove. The paragraph before had said so of the naturalist's words "notched, lens-shaped, umbelliform": they are "essentially and not accidentally inexact, and therefore also unmathematical" (Hua III/1 155).

The structure of the ideal is given by two verbs in quotation marks. Ideal essences are "limits" that morphological essences "approach" (*„annähern”*), "without ever reaching them". This is what "in the Kantian sense" means here, and it is less than Kant meant. For Kant an Idea is "a necessary concept of reason, to which no congruent object can be given in the senses" (*Critique of Pure Reason*, 1787, "Von den transzendentalen Ideen", file L1412). Husserl keeps the non-givenness in sense and adds the series that approaches without end. The concepts of geometry, which for Kant are not Ideas at all, become Ideas because no sensuous shape is a perfect circle. The marks around „Ideen” concede that the sense is borrowed and bent.

The last sentence of the quotation protects phenomenology. The "firmness and clean distinguishability" of generic concepts "that have their extension in the flowing" must not be confused with exactness. A morphological concept can be fixed, used consistently and told apart from its neighbours, all without being exact. Rigour and exactness come apart. That is the claim §75 needs when it calls phenomenology "a descriptive essence-doctrine", which, "like every descriptive, non-substructing and non-idealizing discipline, has its right in itself" (Hua III/1 156). §73 had made definiteness depend on "exactness in the grasped essences themselves" (row 4), and §74 shows where exactness comes from: from idealization, not from seeing. Consciousness offers its essences to seeing only, so a definite theory of it is excluded. The exact sciences, Husserl adds, "operate with ideal substructions" and cannot replace "the original and justified tasks of pure description" (Hua III/1 156). "Substruction" names what idealization does. It builds under the given something that is not given in it.

The Kantian Idea has three uses in the book, and §74 is the first. In §83 the whole stream of experience is grasped "in the manner of an Idea in the Kantian sense" (row 9), and in §143 the perfect givenness of a thing is prefigured as "Idea" in the Kantian sense (row 27). The later uses keep the structure fixed here, something never given in one intuition but prescribed as the limit of an endless process, and change what it is applied to: first ideal shapes, then the stream, then the thing. They also change one feature. In §74 the Idea is not seen; in §83 and §143 the Idea itself is grasped with insight, though what it prescribes is never given. §149 will call that insight "'Idea'-beholding" (row 30). The distinction of §74 is therefore not the last word on Ideas. It shows that the limits cannot be found in intuition, and the later uses show that they can be seen as limits.

The quoted "origin" is a question Husserl later answered at length. In the *Crisis* (§9a), the limit-shapes are traced to the practice of perfecting: from "the practice of perfecting, in free penetration into the horizons of conceivable perfecting in the 'again and again', limit-shapes are prefigured everywhere, toward which, as invariant poles never to be reached, each series of perfecting runs" (Hua VI 23). The structure is still §74's, poles approached and never reached, but the origin is now a history: a method of idealization "formed historically long ago" and practised "in intersubjective community". The Hua VI file has lost pages elsewhere, not here, and the passage reads continuously. In 1913 the origin of exact concepts was "an essentially other one" and stayed in quotation marks. In 1936 it is an achievement of a culture. The later text does not change the distinction. It unpacks what the earlier one had marked as needing explanation and did not explain.

## §79, Hua III/1 172–173: Watt's doubt restated as an essence-doubt

Reads [§79.8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#the-phenomenological-study-of-experience-reflections) (the commentary sets the §79 title as a plain paragraph, so the link goes to the § 78 heading above it).

The objection that §79 answers is not Watt's. Watt doubted, as a psychologist, that self-observation yields knowledge of experiencing as it is lived: observing always comes after, and what it knows is an object, not the lived state. Husserl first sets that doubt aside, since phenomenology makes no claims about the existence of experiences. He then rebuilds it as a doubt about essences. Phenomenology owes its essence-statements about unreflected experiences to reflection; if reflection changes what it grasps, the essence it seizes is only the essence of the reflected experience. That rebuilt objection, put largely in Watt's own sentences, is the one phenomenology must meet, and Husserl makes it by changing the grammar of Watt's sentences.

> If now phenomenology also has to make no existence-establishments about experiences, and so no "experiences" and "observations" in the natural sense, the sense in which a fact-science must rely on such things, it nonetheless makes, as a principial condition of its possibility, essence-establishments about unreflected experiences. These, however, it owes to reflection, more closely to reflective essence-intuition. Consequently the sceptical misgivings regarding self-observation come into consideration for phenomenology too, and do so insofar as these misgivings can be extended in an obvious way from immanent experiencing reflection to every reflection whatever.
>
> Indeed, what would become of phenomenology if "it were not to be seen how a report on the experiencing of experiencing, even when it is there, gets onto paper"? What would become of it if it might make statements about the essences of "known", reflected experiences, but not about essences of experiences simpliciter? What would become of it if "hardly even conjectures were to be made about how one comes to knowledge of immediate experiencing" — or to knowledge of its essence? It may be that the phenomenologist has no existence-establishments to carry out regarding the experiences that float before him as exemplary for his ideations. Yet in these ideations, one could object, he beholds only Ideas of what he has before his eyes in the example at the time. As soon as his look turns to the experience, it becomes for the first time that as which it now presents itself to him; as soon as he turns his look away, it becomes another. The essence grasped is only essence of the reflected experience, and the opinion that through reflection one can win absolutely valid knowledge that holds for experiences in general, reflected or unreflected, is wholly unfounded. "How is one to be able to posit states", be it even as essential possibilities, "of which one can [have] no knowledge?"

German:

> Wenn nun die Phänomenologie auch keine Daseinsfeststellungen über Erlebnisse zu machen hat, also keine „Erfahrungen“ und „Beobachtungen“ in dem natürlichen Sinne, in demjenigen, in dem eine Tatsachenwissenschaft auf dergleichen sich stützen muß, so macht sie doch, als prinzipielle Bedingung ihrer Möglichkeit, über unreflektierte Erlebnisse Wesensfeststellungen. Diese verdankt sie aber der Reflexion, näher der reflektiven Wesensintuition. Folglich kommen die skeptischen Bedenken hinsichtlich [154] der Selbstbeobachtung auch für die Phänomenologie, und zwar insofern in Betracht, als sich diese Bedenken in naheliegender Weise von der immanenten erfahrenden Reflexion auf jede Reflexion überhaupt erstrecken lassen.
>
> In der Tat, was sollte aus der Phänomenologie werden, wenn „es nicht einzusehen wäre, wie ein Bericht über Erleben des Erlebens, auch wenn es da ist, zu Papier kommt“? Was sollte aus ihr werden, wenn sie Aussagen machen dürfte über die Wesen „gewußten“, reflektierten Erlebnissen, nicht aber über Wesen von Erlebnissen schlechthin? Was sollte werden, wenn „kaum einmal Vermutungen darüber anzustellen wären, wie man zur Erkenntnis unmittelbaren Erlebens kommt“ — bzw. zur Erkenntnis seines Wesens? Mag sein, daß der Phänomenologe keine Daseinsfeststellungen bezüglich der Erlebnisse zu vollziehen hat, die ihm als exemplarische für seine Ideationen vorschweben. Er erschaut doch, so könnte man einwenden, in diesen Ideationen nur Ideen von solchem, was er jeweils eben im Exempel vor Augen hat. So wie sein Blick sich dem Erlebnis zuwendet, wird es erst zu dem, als was es sich ihm nun darbietet, sowie er den Blick abwendet, wird es zu einem anderen. Das erfaßte Wesen ist nur Wesen von dem reflektierten Erlebnis, und die Meinung, durch Reflexion absolut gültige Erkenntnisse gewinnen zu können, die für Erlebnisse überhaupt, ob reflektierte oder unreflektierte, Geltung haben, ist völlig unbegründet. „Wie soll man Zustände statuieren können“, sei es auch als Wesensmöglichkeiten, „von denen man kein Wissen kann?“

*Note on the text.* The last quoted question lacks a verb in the file ("von denen man kein Wissen kann"); where Watt's sentence is first quoted, it reads "von denen man kein Wissen haben kann" (Hua III/1 171). The rendering supplies "[have]". The first quotation of Watt also has "zu Papiere", the reuse "zu Papier"; the file cannot show whether the variation is Husserl's or the conversion's, and nothing turns on it. Husserl's notes on Watt are all labelled `[^93]` in the file and are matched here by order.

Watt is quoted a page earlier, in the indicative: "It is not to be seen how a report on the experiencing of experiencing, even when it is there, gets onto paper", and "One can hardly even make conjectures about how one comes to knowledge of immediate experiencing" (Hua III/1 170). In the passage the same words return inside conditionals: "if 'it were not to be seen …'", "if 'hardly even conjectures were to be made …'". The quotation marks keep Watt's wording; the subjunctive (*wäre*, *anzustellen wären*) removes his assertion. What was a report of a difficulty becomes the antecedent of a hypothetical whose consequent is the ruin of phenomenology. Husserl does not yet deny the antecedent. He states what would follow from it, at full strength, so that the reply in §79.11–17 can deny it.

The interpolations do the rest. Watt asked how one comes to knowledge of immediate experiencing; Husserl adds, outside the marks, "or to knowledge of its essence" (*bzw. zur Erkenntnis seines Wesens*). Watt asked how one can posit states of which one can have no knowledge; Husserl inserts "be it even as essential possibilities" (*sei es auch als Wesensmöglichkeiten*) into the middle of the question. Each insertion lifts a question about facts into a question about essences, and each is made in Husserl's voice inside Watt's sentence. The middle question has no quotation from Watt at all. It is Husserl's own formulation of the strongest form of the doubt: that phenomenology "might make statements about the essences of 'known', reflected experiences, but not about essences of experiences simpliciter". The marks on "known" (*„gewußten“*) take up Watt's report of Lipps, where the "known" reality of self-observation's objects was set against the merely lived reality of present experiences, and where Husserl had already glossed "lived" with a bracket: "namely merely lived, not 'known', that is, reflectively grasped" (Hua III/1 170). The gloss translates Watt's psychology into the vocabulary of reflection, and the passage completes the translation.

The paragraph before the passage gives the logic of the restatement. Phenomenology has "no 'experiences' and 'observations' in the natural sense", and the marks on both words mark the fact-science's use of them, the use phenomenology does without. "Nonetheless" (*doch*) it makes essence-statements about unreflected experiences, and it "owes" them to reflection. "Consequently" (*Folglich*) the sceptical misgivings apply, insofar as they can be extended "from immanent experiencing reflection to every reflection whatever". The extension is what makes the doubt dangerous. A doubt about experiencing reflection, about inner perception as a source of facts, would leave the phenomenologist untouched, since he can use clear fictions in place of perceived experiences (the first reply, with its note back to §70, row 3). A doubt about reflective essence-intuition reaches the fiction too. The objection's last sentences press the point with a phantasied centaur: having the centaur in view, "I do not have its modes of appearing … in view", and the reflective turn that would bring them into view "brings the whole experience into flux and modifies it", so that in the new ideation "I have something new before my eyes" (Hua III/1 173). Even the doctrine of adumbration, the essence-claim that things appear only in adumbrations, would be unwarranted.

The restatement is what the reply needs. Husserl's answer is that "all genuine scepticism" is marked by "the principial countersense that in its argumentations it implicitly presupposes, as conditions of the possibility of their validity, exactly what it denies in its theses" (Hua III/1 174). Only the rebuilt objection has this form. Watt's psychological doubt was a doubt about method and could be met by better method, or, as Watt hoped, by experiment. The essence-doubt says that reflection alters what it grasps, and to say that is to claim knowledge, reflective knowledge, of the unreflected experience and of what reflection does to it. "Constantly a knowledge of unreflected experiences, among them of unreflected reflections, is presupposed, while at the same time the possibility of such knowledge is put in question" (Hua III/1 174). By putting the doubt in its strongest form, Husserl also puts it in the only form that refutes itself. A reader may ask whether the doubt Watt raised has been answered or replaced. Husserl's reply holds against the doubt as he restated it, and against Watt only so far as Watt's sentences commit him to the restatement.

The excursus also tests §65's claim that method stands under its own norms (row 1). The doubt concerns reflection, and reflection is the method; the reply, that scepticism loses its force by "return from verbal argumentations to essence-intuition, to the originarily giving intuition and its very own right" (Hua III/1 175), is given in the method that was doubted. Husserl accepts the circle and turns it against the sceptic: whoever "only says: I doubt the cognitive significance of reflection" is already reflecting. The restatement made the argument possible, and the concession at its end shows its limit. "Everything depends", Husserl adds, on actually carrying out the intuition, and on being able to take up the analyses of §78 as intuitively as they were offered. What the reply establishes in the end is that the doubt cannot be stated without reflection. Whether a given reflection grasped its object faithfully is left to practice.

## §79, Hua III/1 176: reflection as a necessity that binds even God

Reads [§79.17](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#the-phenomenological-study-of-experience-reflections) (the link goes to the § 78 heading, as above).

The conclusion of the excursus turns a method into a law of essence. That consciousness is knowable only through reflection is not a fact about human beings, their "psychophysical constitution", or the limits of finite minds; it belongs to what consciousness is. Husserl marks the status by naming the one knower who could not be limited by fact: "even God is bound" to it, as to an arithmetical identity. God here is no object of theology but, as the note says, a "limit-concept" of the theory of knowledge, the index of perfect knowledge. Its work is to separate the essential from the factual. Whatever binds God is essence, and so no essential feature of a way of knowing can count as a defect of that knowing.

> The phenomena of reflection are in fact a sphere of pure and possibly perfectly clear givennesses. It is an essential insight attainable at any time, because immediate, that from what is objectively given as such a reflection is possible upon the giving consciousness and its subject; from the perceived, the bodily "there", upon the perceiving; from the remembered, as it "floats before us" as such, as "having been", upon the remembering; from the statement in its unfolding being-given upon the stating, etc.; whereby the perceiving comes to givenness as perceiving of just this perceived, the consciousness in each case as consciousness of what is in each case conscious. It is evident that essentially — and so not from merely accidental grounds, still less merely "for us" and our accidental "psychophysical constitution" — only through reflections of this kind is something like consciousness and consciousness-content (in the reell or the intentional sense) knowable. To this absolute and insightful necessity, then, God too is bound, just as to the insight that 2 + 1 = 1 + 2. He too could win knowledge of his consciousness and consciousness-content only reflectively. With this it is at the same time said that reflection can be involved in no antinomic conflict with the ideal of perfect knowledge.

German:

> Die Phänomene der Reflexion sind in der Tat eine Sphäre reiner und evtl. vollkommen klarer Gegebenheiten. Husserl's note to the sentence on God:

> We are not here playing the dispute over into the domain of theology: the Idea God is a necessary limit-concept in epistemological considerations, or an indispensable index for the construction of certain limit-concepts, which even the philosophizing atheist could not do without.

*Note on the text.* The file gives the arithmetic in LaTeX (`$2 + 1 = 1 + 2$`); the note is one of the many labelled `[^93]` (L3689) and is matched to this sentence by its content.

The dash-enclosed clause names what the necessity is not. Three negatives step down from the general to the specific: "not from merely accidental grounds", "still less merely 'for us'" (*etwa gar bloß ,,für uns"*), and "our accidental 'psychophysical constitution'". The quotation marks on "for us" and "psychophysical constitution" mark the vocabulary of an anthropological relativism, the view that a way of knowing belongs to the human apparatus and might differ for another one. Watt had ended on that note, hoping that experimental psychology, with its "inductive method", would settle what self-observation can do (Hua III/1 171). Against him the claim is placed outside every apparatus. The adverb "essentially" (*wesensmäßig*) and the triple negation carry it, and "only" (*nur*) restricts the access: reflection is not the best way to know consciousness but the only one.

"Then" (*also*) introduces God as a consequence, and the consequence is a test. If the necessity is one of essence, a knower free of every factual limit must still be subject to it. The arithmetical comparison places the test: God is bound to it "just as" (*ebenso wie*) to the insight that 2 + 1 = 1 + 2. The comparison claims more than it shows. Commutativity is a formal truth that holds of any domain. The reflection-law is a material essence-law about one region, consciousness. Husserl's "just as" equates their necessity, and that is his doctrine: material eidetic laws are as unconditional as formal ones, though grounded in a regional essence rather than in the form of objects as such. The sentence does not argue for this. It assumes the parity of formal and material a priori that Part One had set up, and lets God's being bound display it.

The next sentence draws the moral, and it is the reason God appears at all. Reflection "can be involved in no antinomic conflict with the ideal of perfect knowledge". The sceptic's suspicion was that reflection, by changing what it grasps, falls short of an ideal it can never meet, so that only a knower who grasped experiences without reflecting would know them as they are. The God-sentence removes that knower. The ideal of perfect knowledge, personified, knows its consciousness by reflection too. What follows generalizes: "Every kind of being has essentially its modes of givenness and with them its modes of the method of knowledge. To treat essential peculiarities of these as defects, even to put them down in the manner of accidental, factual defects to 'our human' knowledge, is countersense" (Hua III/1 176). The quotation marks return on "our human", in the same function as "for us".

The note carries the same logic one level up. God is not posited; the Idea God is "a necessary limit-concept in epistemological considerations". The "or" (*bzw.*) offers a second, more cautious description, "an indispensable index for the construction of certain limit-concepts". On the first, God is himself a limit; on the second, God marks the place where limits are constructed, the ideal of "perfect knowledge" toward which every way of knowing is measured. Either way the atheist needs it, since every argument of the form "even a perfect knower would have to…" uses it. The word "limit-concept" (*Grenzbegriff*) ties the note to §74, where exact essences were ideal "limits" that morphological essences approach (row 5). God is the ideal limit of knowing, as the circle is the ideal limit of round shapes, and in the same way it is not found among the things it is the limit of.

The figure is older in the book, and there it served the opposite case. In §43, against the view that perception does not reach the thing in itself, Husserl had imagined the objector's God, "the subject of absolutely perfect knowledge and so also of all possible adequate perception", who would perceive the thing without appearances. That view is "countersense" (*widersinnig*), because it would make the spatial thing a reell constituent "of the divine stream of consciousness and experience" (Hua III/1 89; the file is badly damaged at this page, with leaked line numbers and garbled letters, and is paraphrased here). In §43 God cannot do what is excluded by the essence of things; in §79 he cannot do what is excluded by the essence of consciousness. The two uses together give the structure: perfect knowledge is not knowledge freed from modes of givenness but knowledge of each kind of being in the mode proper to it. Things are known through adumbrations, consciousness through reflection.

The limit-concept returns in Part Four for things. In §150 the necessities of orientation and perspectival appearance hold "not merely for us humans, but also for God, as the ideal representative of absolute knowledge" (row 31). The formula there makes explicit what the §79 note left in a disjunction: God is the "ideal representative" of absolute knowledge, a limit-concept in the first of the note's two senses. Between §79 and §150 the argument runs the same way in both regions. A necessity is shown to be essential by showing that the ideal knower is bound by it, and once it is essential, the finitude of human knowledge is no objection to it. §79 is the first place where this holds for consciousness itself, and it is what closes the excursus. The sceptic's case depended on measuring reflection against a knowledge that does without it, and that standard has turned out to be countersense.

# Husserl, *Ideen I*, Parts Three and Four: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md) covers §§63–153 of the First Book, Parts Three and Four. Each reading follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds that meaning, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the book and in his other works. Each heading links to the section of the main commentary that holds the block it reads. Unit references such as "§85.6" name the sixth paragraph of the commentary under § 85. The commentary sets the titles of §79 and §80 as plain paragraphs rather than headings, so readings of §79 link to the nearest heading above, § 78. A separate commentary on §§111–153 has its own [close readings](husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary-close-readings.md); where a passage was read there too, the reading here says so but is written afresh.

The German is quoted from Husserliana III/1, *Ideen zu einer reinen Phänomenologie und phänomenologischen Philosophie, Erstes Buch*, edited by Karl Schuhmann, in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-3-1-ideen-i-allgemeine-einfuhrung-in-die-reine-phanomenologie-recheck.md`). The commentary cites § numbers only. Headings here add the Hua III/1 page, taken from the running heads in the file, and so accurate to about a page. The file also carries the pages of the first edition of 1913 in brackets, and Husserl's own cross-references ("S. 233") use those; where a reading cites one, it says "1913 p.". All English renderings of Husserl are working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows. They are not Kersten's or Boyce Gibson's. Kersten's translation (Collected Works II) is in the corpus, itself badly scanned, and was used to check the sense of every passage quoted; it is cited only where it departs from the German in a way that matters or settles a damaged reading.

The file is an unreviewed conversion, and four cautions hold for every reading. First, Hua prints Husserl's emphasis in spaced type, and the conversion has dropped almost all of it; the italics that survive are incomplete. No reading argues from emphasis or from its absence. Quotation marks survive, in mixed forms (`,,…''`, `„…“`), and are quoted as the file has them. Second, OCR has damaged the text in places, most seriously at a seam in §114 and in §129, where "von" (from) stands for *vor* (before) and reverses the sense. Third, several § headings are demoted, promoted, split or set inline (§§94, 128, 130, 136, 143, 146), and some stretches are hard-wrapped with line-end hyphens and leaked Hua margin line numbers ("Sei- 25 ten"); the readings cite sections by Husserl's numbering, not by the file's heading levels, and leave leaked numbers out of the quotations, saying so. Fourth, footnotes are mislinked, split or given repeated labels; they are identified by order and content, not by label. Each reading says where damage touches its passage, and none repairs the wording.

## §65, Hua III/1 139: phenomenology under its own norms

Reads [§65.6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#the-back-reference-of-phenomenology-to-itself).

The relatedness of phenomenology to itself (*Bezogenheit auf sich selbst*) is, in §65, not the plain fact that the phenomenologist's own acts are experiences and so fall within the field he studies. Husserl grants that fact and dismisses it in a few sentences. The claim of the passage is normative. Statements of method are themselves eidetic statements about consciousness, so they stand under the norms they state, clarity, insight, faithful expression, and they must remain checkable against those norms by renewed reflection. The claim has the form of a requirement, not a finished result, and the conclusion that the whole book "is through and through phenomenology" holds of its content, not of the form its concepts have yet reached.

> The essential relatedness of phenomenology to itself shows itself here in this: that what is weighed and established in methodical reflection under the titles clarity, insight, expression and the like belongs, for its part, itself to the phenomenological domain; that all the reflexive analyses are phenomenological essence-analyses; and that the methodological insights won stand, as regards their establishment, themselves under the norms that they formulate. In new reflections one must therefore be able at any time to convince oneself that the states of affairs stated in the methodological statements are to be given in perfect clarity, that the concepts used really adapt themselves faithfully to what is given, etc.
>
> What has been said holds evidently for all methodological investigations relating to phenomenology, however wide we may span their frame, and so it is understood that this whole writing, [which] wants to prepare the way for phenomenology, is according to its content itself through and through phenomenology.

German:

> Die wesentliche Bezogenheit der Phänomenologie auf sich selbst zeigt sich hier darin, daß, was da in der methodischen Reflexion unter den Titeln Klarheit, Einsicht, Ausdruck u. dgl. erwogen und festgestellt wird, seinerseits selbst zur phänomenologischen Domäne gehört, daß all die reflexiven Analysen phänomenologische Wesensanalysen sind und die gewonnenen methodologischen Einsichten hinsichtlich ihrer Feststellung selbst unter den Normen stehen, die sie formulieren. In neuen Reflexionen muß man sich [124] davon also jederzeit überzeugen können, daß die in den methodologischen Aussagen ausgesagten Sachverhalte in vollkommener Klarheit zu geben seien, daß die benutzten Begriffe sich dem Gegebenen wirklich treu anpassen usw.
>
> Das Gesagte gilt offenbar für alle auf die Phänomenologie bezüglichen methodologischen Untersuchungen, wie weit wir ihren Rahmen auch spannen mögen, und so versteht es sich, daß diese ganze Schrift, der Phänomenologie den Weg bereiten will, ihrem Inhalte nach selbst durch und durch Phänomenologie ist.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "diese ganze Schrift, der Phänomenologie den Weg bereiten will" with no relative pronoun; a "die" (which) has dropped out, and Kersten's "this whole essay, which aims at preparing the way" confirms the sense. The bracketed "[124]" is the 1913 page.

The three clauses with "that" (*daß*) climb from domain to status to norm. What method weighs "belongs, for its part, itself" (*seinerseits selbst*) to the phenomenological domain: the doubled reflexive marks that the topics of method, clarity and insight and expression, are modes of consciousness and so objects like any other. The analyses that treat them "are" essence-analyses. And the insights won "stand … under the norms that they formulate". The restriction "as regards their establishment" (*hinsichtlich ihrer Feststellung*) is exact. It does not say the norms are true because they apply to themselves; it says that the act of fixing them, and the statements that fix them, are held to the standard those statements set. A methodological statement is one more phenomenological statement, and it can fail as one.

The second sentence turns the status into a duty, and its modality is the point. "One must … be able at any time to convince oneself" (*muß man sich … jederzeit überzeugen können*): a standing possibility of checking, not a check already done. The subjunctive "are to be given" (*zu geben seien*) belongs to reported content and keeps the claim at the level of what the statements commit one to. "Therefore" (*also*) draws the duty from the status, and the inference holds: if method-statements are eidetic statements, they owe the clarity that eidetic statements owe. The final sentence is looser. "Evidently" (*offenbar*) and "so it is understood" (*so versteht es sich*) present the claim about "this whole writing" as a corollary, but the corollary needs more than the premise gives: that everything the book contains is either method-reflection or eidetic description of pure consciousness. Husserl secures it with a qualifier, "according to its content" (*ihrem Inhalte nach*). The book is phenomenology in what it treats, not yet in the scientific form of its concepts.

The paragraph before the passage supplies that distinction, and it is what makes the self-application coherent. Every self-related discipline, Husserl concedes, must begin "with methodical aids that it has only afterwards to form scientifically and definitively"; the concepts of an incipient phenomenology are already phenomenological but "gain their scientific stamp only in the system of the already grounded science" (Hua III/1 138). The method is first practised "naively" (*naiv betätigt*), to get one's bearings in the new field; reflection on its essence then takes over "the function of a general and logically strict grounding of the method", and, "consciously followed", the method gains the rank of a scientific method that permits "limiting and improving criticism" (Hua III/1 139). Self-application is thus the second stage of a two-stage structure. It is what allows the method to correct its own earlier practice, and it presupposes that practice. The circle Husserl's opponent sees is a sequence.

The passage turns the opening objection of §65 from a question about domain into a question about norms. The objection was that the experiences of the research itself would belong to what is researched. Husserl's first reply took the model of psychology and logic: "the thinking of the logician [is] a logical thing, namely itself belonging within the range of the logical norms", and self-relatedness would be worrying only if knowledge of everything else depended on knowledge of the thinker's own thinking, "a visibly absurd presupposition" (Hua III/1 138). The logician's case already joined domain and norm, and the passage carries both over to phenomenology. The book's index pairs the two cases: it lists a "back-reference of logic to itself" at 1913 p. 122 beside that of phenomenology at pp. 122 ff. (file L8645, L8793). Just before, §64 had settled the analogous worry about the phenomenologist as natural man by the geometer: "the mathematizing subject does not belong to the eidetic content of the mathematical propositions themselves". §65 adds what the geometer lacks. The geometer's thinking does not belong to geometry; the phenomenologist's thinking, purified, does belong to phenomenology.

What follows is the execution of the passage's list. §66 takes the third title first, expression: words drawn from common speech take on a fixed sense as they "cover" (*,,decken''*) the intuitively given, and can then be fixed scientifically (Hua III/1 140). §67 takes clarity and builds the scale of givenness (row 2 below), and §§68–70 give the method of clarification. Each is a case of the norms "formulated" in §65 and, by §65's claim, subject to them. The demand behind the passage was set two sections earlier. In §63 phenomenology "must by its essence raise the claim to be 'first' philosophy", and therefore "demands the most perfect freedom from presuppositions and, in relation to itself, absolute reflective insight"; "its own essence is to realize the most perfect clarity about its own essence and so also about the principles of its method" (Hua III/1 136, file L2930). §65 shows that the demand is not circular. The concrete test comes in §79, where the psychologists' doubts about self-observation are met as doubts about reflection, and the reply has to be given in reflection (rows 6 and 7 below). In §77, Husserl calls the essence-study of reflections of "fundamental methodological significance" for phenomenology, because "all modes of immanent essence-grasping" fall under the concept of reflection (Hua III/1 166). Method-reflection is reflection, and so the theory of reflection is the place where self-application becomes a doctrine.

In 1913 the self-relatedness is "no difficulty" and raises "no serious misgivings" (*keine ernstlichen Bedenken*). The *Cartesian Meditations* keep the phrase and harden the norm. At the close of the Fifth Meditation (§63, Hua I 178), the critique of transcendental-phenomenological knowledge is "the further and last problematic" of phenomenology, its "self-critique" (*Selbstkritik*) with a view to the extent, limits and modes of apodicticity, and the first phenomenology is "in its kind itself still affected by a naivety (apodictic naivety)". Then: "given the essential back-reference of phenomenology to itself, this critique too demands a critique" (*bei der wesensmäßigen Rückbeziehung der Phänomenologie auf sich selbst fordert auch diese Kritik eine Kritik*), though without a troublesome regress, "despite the evident possibility of iterable transcendental reflections and critiques". The 1913 structure is there, naive practice and then a grounding reflection, but the norm has changed from clarity to apodicticity, and the second stage is no longer within reach of the book that names it. §65 already makes the claim later works keep: method stands under its own norms. What it leaves open is how far the book meets them.

## §67, Hua III/1 142: clarity as givenness, from zero to one

Reads [§67.8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-parts-3-4-commentary.md#method-of-clarification-nearness-to-givenness-and-farness-from-givenness).

In §67 "givenness in the pregnant sense" is one dimension with intuitiveness and clarity. A consciousness gives its object exactly so far as it intuits it, and intuits it exactly so far as the object is clear to it, and the degrees of the three are one scale, bounded below by darkness and above by "pure self-givenness". Two restrictions make the identification work. Givenness here is not originary givenness: a phantasy can stand at the top of the scale. And darkness is not the lowest degree of givenness but its absence, so the scale's lower bound lies outside it. The first restriction is what lets §70 privilege free phantasy; the second sits uneasily with what §68 says next.

> In the case of full unclarity, the counter-pole of full clarity, nothing at all has come to a givenness; the consciousness is a "dark" one, no longer intuiting at all, in the proper sense no longer "giving" at all. We have accordingly to say:
>
> Giving consciousness in the pregnant sense and intuitive, as against non-intuitive, clear as against dark: that coincides. Likewise: levels of givenness, of intuitiveness, of clarity. The zero-limit is darkness, the one-limit is full clarity, intuitiveness, givenness.
>
> Here, however, givenness is not to be understood as originary givenness, and so not as perceptual. The "self-given" we do not identify with the "originarily given", the "bodily" [given].

German:

> Im Falle der vollen Unklarheit, dem Gegenpol der vollen Klarheit, ist gar nichts zu einer Gegebenheit gekommen, das Bewußtsein ist ein ,,dunkles", gar nicht mehr anschauendes, im eigentlichen Sinne überhaupt nicht mehr ,,gebendes". Wir haben danach zu sagen:
>
> Gebendes Bewußtsein im prägnanten Sinne und anschauliches, gegenüber unanschaulichem, klares gegenüber dunklem, das deckt sich. Desgleichen: Stufen der Gegebenheit, der Anschaulichkeit, der Klarheit. Die Nullgrenze ist die Dunkelheit, die Einsgrenze ist die volle Klarheit, Anschaulichkeit, Gegebenheit.
>
> Dabei ist aber Gegebenheit nicht zu verstehen als originäre Gegebenheit, somit nicht als wahrnehmungsmäßige. Das ,,selbstgegeben" identifizieren wir nicht mit dem ,,originärgegeben", dem ,,leibhaft".

The middle paragraph is set off as a definition and has almost no verbs. Its one finite verb is "that coincides" (*das deckt sich*), and the demonstrative gathers three pairs into a single subject: giving, intuitive as against non-intuitive, clear as against dark. The three words come from different places. "Giving" (*gebend*) names a character of the act; "intuitive" (*anschaulich*) a mode of presenting; "clear" (*klar*) belongs first to what is presented, since the paragraph before said that "correlatively the grasped is in its sense 'unclear'" (Hua III/1 141). The coincidence joins an act-predicate to an object-predicate, so the scale is at once noetic and noematic before §88 has the terms for it. The verb itself is the one §66 had just used for the word that "covers" (*,,decken''*) the intuitively given (Hua III/1 140). Clarity and givenness coincide as an expression coincides with what it expresses: in extension, at every point.

"Likewise" (*Desgleichen*) carries the step from coincidence to scale without a verb at all, and it claims more than "that coincides". Three concepts can coincide in extension without coinciding in degree. Husserl asserts the second and gives it no separate argument; the description of the preceding paragraph is meant to have shown it, in the image of an "absolute nearness" where givenness is "absolute, that is, pure self-givenness", and of a remainder of unclarity that "overshadows" moments of what is "itself" given, so that they do not reach "the circle of light of the purely given" (Hua III/1 142). The section title's "nearness to givenness" and "farness from givenness" (*„Gegebenheitsnähe“ und „Gegebenheitsferne“*) supply the spatial image; the sentence about the limits supplies the numbers. "Zero-limit" and "one-limit" (*Nullgrenze*, *Einsgrenze*) are a mathematician's words for the bounds of the unit interval, and they make clarity a magnitude.

The paragraph before the definition hedges the lower bound. In full unclarity "nothing at all has come to a givenness", and the consciousness is "in the proper sense no longer 'giving' at all". The quotation marks around "dark" and "giving" mark the words as used of a limit case. Darkness is then not a low value of givenness but its absence, and the "zero-limit" is a bound the scale approaches but whose occupant is not on it. The next section strains this. §68 opens by distinguishing "genuine gradual levels of clarity, to which one may also add gradual levels within darkness" (Hua III/1 143). If darkness has degrees, it is not a point, and the zero-limit marks the edge of a second scale rather than the end of one. The revision of the Sixth Investigation that Husserl took to galley proofs in the summer of 1913 (Hua XX/1, Text Nr. 3) had faced the asymmetry head on. There perfect clarity is "an unshiftable, though ideal limit-point, which in mathematical symbolism we should have to call the one-limit", while the zero-limit, "absolute 'dark'", has "an essentially other character": it is "not an ideal point of the clarity-series itself" but "an occurrence lying outside it, that of pure empty re-presentation", and so "not quite like the zero point of an intensity-series" (Hua XX/1 130, Text Nr. 3 §30). *Ideas I* keeps the vocabulary and drops the caution. §68 even says that clarity admits "continuous intensity-like differences, as intensities starting with zero, but closing above with a fixed limit" (Hua III/1 143), which is the very comparison XX/1 had qualified.

The third paragraph of the quotation holds the restriction the method needs. "Self-given" is not "originarily given", not given "bodily" (*leibhaft*). The definition is about the fullness with which something stands before consciousness, not about the mode, perceptual or re-presentational, in which it stands there. A remembered or phantasied colour can be given at the one-limit. Without this clause §70 could not say what it says, that free phantasy has a privileged position in essence-clarification (row 3): the eidetician needs exemplars at full clarity, and phantasy can supply them at will. The scale of §67 is what makes "clarification" one operation, bringing what floats before us "to normal nearness, to perfect clarity" (Hua III/1 141), whatever kind of intuition it is performed in.

Kersten blurs the chain of words on which the identification rests. He renders the "dark" consciousness as "blind", then the "dark" of the definition as "unclear" and the zero-limit as "complete obscurity". The German says *dunkel* three times and *Dunkelheit* once, so that the negative pole of the scale has one name throughout; the variation in English loses the point that the consciousness which is "no longer giving" is the same darkness that bounds the scale.

The understanding returns twice in the book, each time restricted. In §125 Husserl says that "our earlier discussions of the method of clarification", with a footnote to §67 (1913 p. 125), "need essential supplements" with respect to the statement, "the vital element of science" (Hua III/1 290). Clarity is now one task among two: confused "logical" acts must be brought to "perfect logical distinctness", and in the founding layer "all non-intuitiveness" turned into intuitiveness, and only then does "the method described earlier" come into action, with the concept of intuition "of clear consciousness" carried over from monothetic to synthetic acts. Distinctness is a dimension §67's scale does not measure. In Part Four the clause on originarity comes back as the axis of reason. The first basic form of reason-consciousness is the "originarily giving 'seeing'" of §136, and the evidence of §137 is divided into "originary" and "pure". The distinction §67 set aside so that phantasy could reach the one-limit is the distinction on which rational positing depends: a phantasy may give its object with full clarity and give it no right to be posited. §67 fixes clarity as the measure of method; Part Four will need a second measure for reason.

# Jean-Luc Marion, *Réduction et donation*, read against Husserl, *Ideen I* (Husserliana III): a comparative commentary

## Foreword

Marion (R) is cited by division, section, paragraph and note (R I.1 ¶4, R I n. 15), since the edition used has no page numbers. Husserl's *Ideen I* (P) is cited by section and by the pages of Husserliana III/1 (Schuhmann, 1976), which reproduces the text of 1913. Marion cites the earlier Husserliana III (Biemel, 1950), whose pagination differs and whose main text weaves in Husserl's later handwritten additions. Each of his references is therefore located by section and wording, given once in his form, and checked against the 1913 text and the apparatus of Hua III/2. Six traps govern this pair. Marion's Ideen I contains words Husserl wrote after 1913. Husserl's giving is the act's ("originarily giving intuition", "giving consciousness"), while Marion's is the phenomenon's own ("what gives itself"), and the French *donation* covers both. The principle of all principles (§24) belongs to the critique of empiricism in Part I, ahead of the reduction, and carries a clause of limits. Ideen I's talk of being (consciousness and reality as regions, absolute being) is regional and eidetic and must not be read through Heidegger. Ideen I's "object" is formal and wide, and its ego is the pure I, not the transcendental I of the *Cartesian Meditations*. Finally, "metaphysics of presence" is Heidegger's and Derrida's category, not Husserl's. Ideen I has its own reserve against total presence in the inadequacy of thing-givenness and in adequate givenness as an Idea in the Kantian sense (§§138, 143).

The Foreword's single sentence puts givenness "au centre de la réduction, donc de la phénoménologie" (at the centre of the reduction, and therefore of phenomenology; R Foreword ¶1) and names the goal: to determine "ce qui, sans l'être, vient après le sujet" (what, without being it, comes after the subject). Neither half is Ideen I's. Ideen I introduces the reduction as a switching off and bracketing of the general thesis of the world (§§31–32) and names its result a "residue": transcendental consciousness (§33), "absolute consciousness as the residue of the annihilation of the world" (§49). Givenness enters Ideen I at the edges of that doctrine: immanent perception gives its object absolutely (§46), and every region has its own mode of originary givenness (§138, Hua III/1 321). Marion's "therefore" makes givenness the centre of a method Husserl organized around the being of consciousness. That reordering is the book's thesis, and Marion states it as a programme, not as exegesis. The second clause prepares chapter V, where the Ideen I pure I, "a peculiar, not constituted transcendence in immanence" (eine eigenartige — nicht konstituierte — Transzendenz in der Immanenz, §57, Hua III/1 124), is read as an I "outside being". Marion's double sense of *sans l'être* (without being the subject, without being at all) will be laid on a text in which Husserl exempts the pure I from switching off only "as far as" its evident co-givenness with pure consciousness reaches (§57).

The acknowledgements name the restored Paris Husserl Archives (Courtine, Franck), the Leuven Husserl-Archiv (Ijsseling, Bernet) and Michel Henry (R Foreword ¶2). Leuven matters for this pair in a way Commentary R does not register. The Husserliana are edited there under Ijsseling, and it was under that direction that Schuhmann's Hua III/1–2 (1976) separated the 1913 text of Ideen I from Husserl's later additions. Marion thanks the Archive whose own edition he does not cite: his Ideen I references are to Biemel's text of 1950 (see the next division). Franck's *Chair et corps* (1981), to which Marion owes his reading of Ideen I §46 (R II n. 44), is a likely channel for the vocabulary of "flesh" that colours his French for Husserl's *leibhaft* (R I.3 ¶2, below). The date, 24 February 1989, sets the book thirteen years after the critical edition.

## Bibliographical note

Marion cites Husserl "d'après les *Husserliana*" (from the *Husserliana*), with one stated exception: the *Logical Investigations*, cited from the second edition of 1913 (R Bibliographical note ¶1). The note implies that the Husserliana text of Ideen I is a single, settled text. It is not. Marion's page numbers (§24 at "Hua, III, p. 52", R I n. 15) are Biemel's, and Biemel's main text is a "new edition enlarged on the basis of the author's handwritten additions" (neue, auf Grund der handschriftlichen Zusätze des Verfassers erweiterte Auflage; Hua III/1, Vorbemerkung). Schuhmann's edition restored the 1913 text and moved the additions into an apparatus. The consequence shows at once in chapter I. Marion quotes §19 as "Le 'voir' (νοεῖν) immédiat" (the immediate "seeing" (νοεῖν), R I n. 83). The 1913 text reads "Das unmittelbare »Sehen«" (the immediate "seeing", Hua III/1 43), and the Greek word is a later blue-pencil note in Husserl's copy D: "NB νοεῖν" (Hua III/2, to Hua III/1 43, line 1). The note does not record which edition was used for Ideen I. Its silence lets a later Husserl speak as the Husserl of 1913.

The note on Heidegger's editions (the Gesamtausgabe, and *Sein und Zeit* in the tenth edition with Martineau's translation; R Bibliographical note ¶2) bears on this pair only through its last sentence: Marion uses the usual translations "quitte à les modifier" (modifying them where needed). For Ideen I the usual translation is Ricœur's *Idées directrices* (1950), cited once by name (R II n. 37). Where Marion's French for a term of Ideen I carries his thesis, it cannot be settled from this corpus whether the rendering is Ricœur's, modified or not. The rendering is counted against Marion only where his argument rests on it.

The note lists earlier versions: chapter I appeared as "La percée et l'élargissement" in *Philosophie* in 1984, chapter III in 1987, chapter IV in 1987 (R Bibliographical note ¶3). For this pair the dates mean that the argument of chapter I, which sets Ideen I's principle of all principles in line with the breakthrough of 1900–1901, was formed in the early 1980s, against the Logical Investigations and their 1913 self-interpretations, eight years after the critical edition of Ideen I was available.

## Phenomenology as Such

The epigraph is Heidegger's (GA 58, 5): what does "given", "givenness" mean, "this magic word of phenomenology and the stumbling block for the others"? Ideen I is the text that made the word magic. *Gegebenheit* and its compounds run through the book: originary givenness, mode of givenness (*Gegebenheitsweise*), "a mode of givenness in which essences are originarily given as objects" (Gegebenheitsart, in der Wesen als Gegenstände originär gegeben sind, §21, Hua III/1 47). Ideen I never makes givenness a theme of its own. It uses the word to qualify acts (giving intuition, giving consciousness) and the ways objects stand before them. Heidegger's 1919/20 remark already aims at that use, and Marion takes over the aim. The epigraph thus states the book's relation to Ideen I in one line: Husserl's most frequent operative word is to be turned into a question.

Phenomenology takes up the role of philosophy after Nietzsche has exhausted the possibilities of metaphysics, and it undertakes "un nouveau commencement" (a new beginning; R Phenomenology as such ¶1). Ideen I also claims newness, in other terms. Its Introduction demands "eine neue, gegenüber den natürlichen Erfahrungs- und Denkeinstellungen völlig geänderte Weise der Einstellung" (a new attitude, wholly changed against the natural attitudes of experience and thought, Hua III/1 5) and a science of a "new dimension" (§62). Ideen I's history of philosophy is §62's line from Descartes through Locke and Hume to Kant, each of whom came near the field of transcendental consciousness and missed it. Nietzsche does not appear in it. Marion's periodization (the end of metaphysics as its completion, then a new beginning) is Heidegger's, and it places Husserl in a history whose terms Husserl did not use. This is a re-inscription of Ideen I's claim to begin. The claim is kept, and its ground is changed from a change of attitude to a turn in the history of being.

The second paragraph supplies the frame Marion will impose on every Ideen I locus in the book. Both Nietzsche's last advance and Husserl's first arrival turn on one question: can the conditions of presence be stretched until every being reaches presence, "sans aucune condition ni réserve" (without any condition or reserve; R Phenomenology as such ¶2)? Phenomenology would then fulfil "la nostalgie de toute la philosophie moderne" (the nostalgia of all modern philosophy). The phrase is Ideen I §62: "So begreift es sich, daß die Phänomenologie gleichsam die geheime Sehnsucht der ganzen neuzeitlichen Philosophie ist" (so it is understood that phenomenology is, as it were, the secret longing of all modern philosophy; R n. 2 / Hua III/1 134, R's "Hua, t. III, p. 148"). Marion's paraphrase drops "gleichsam" (as it were) and "geheime" (secret; restored at R I.3 ¶2), and *nostalgie* turns a longing toward what is to come into a regret for what was. The departure lies in what the longing is for. In §62 phenomenology is the one science that performs for all sciences, and for itself, the critique they cannot perform, and so gives "die letzte Sinnesbestimmung des Seins ihrer Gegenstände" (the final determination of the sense of the being of their objects). What modern philosophy longed for, in Husserl's account, is a field (transcendental consciousness) and a critical science of it. Marion makes it the liberation of presence from every condition. The phrase "sans aucune condition ni réserve" runs against Ideen I's own principle, which admits what gives itself "aber auch nur in den Schranken, in denen es sich da gibt" (but also only within the limits in which it gives itself there, §24). This is selection: Marion keeps the claim that phenomenology fulfils modern philosophy and drops the critical function that made it the fulfilment. §62's "final determination of the sense of being" is passed over here. Chapter V will make it load-bearing, as phenomenology's claim to be the true ontology.

Husserl's breakthrough is ambiguous because it extends presence past all limits and thereby reproduces presence's metaphysical determination, "l'objectivité" (objectivity; R Phenomenology as such ¶3). The return to the matters at issue is compromised if they give themselves only "sous la figure de leur objectivation constituée" (in the figure of their constituted objectivation). No locus is cited, but the charge is aimed at Ideen I more than at the *Investigations*. Ideen I does say that "»Gegenstand« ist überall ein Titel für Wesenszusammenhänge des Bewußtseins" ("object" is everywhere a title for essential connections of consciousness, §145, Hua III/1 336), and it makes the region thing the "transcendental guiding thread" of constitution (§150). That supports Marion. Two things in P qualify it. Ideen I's "object" is the formal "something in general", widened in correlation with intuition: "Auch Wesenserschauung ist eben Anschauung, wie eidetischer Gegenstand eben Gegenstand ist" (seeing of essences too is intuition, just as an eidetic object is an object, §3, Hua III/1 14). And Husserl widens formal ontology to values and practical objects (§148). What Marion calls objectivity is in Ideen I a formal title, not a privileged region. Marion will grant this in chapter V ("La formalité de l'objectivité") and make the formality itself the charge. Here the charge stands undifferentiated, and the reader must not take "objectivity" for Ideen I's word: Ideen I's word is *Gegenstand*, and its width is the point of §3.

Heidegger's starting point is that phenomenology's objective does not coincide with objectivity, and the conflict now reads: does the return lead back to objectivity or to being, and does "ce qui opère la re(con)duction" (what performs the re(con)duction) act "en tant que *Je* transcendantal ou en tant que *Dasein*" (as transcendental I or as Dasein; R Phenomenology as such ¶4)? Ideen I's vocabulary differs at both ends. Its reduction is a switching off (*Ausschaltung*) and bracketing (*Einklammerung*), and what remains is a residue, "transzendentales Bewußtsein" (transcendental consciousness, §33), not an operating I. Its ego is the "pure I" (§§57, 80), retained because it is co-given with every cogitatio, and Husserl leaves "the questions of the pure I *in suspenso*" for many inquiries (§57, Hua III/1 124). The transcendental I as the agent of the reduction is the I of the *Cartesian Meditations*. Marion's parenthesis "re(con)duction" adds an agent who leads back. That is Marion's reading of the reduction as an act with a performer, the axis on which the whole book compares Husserl, Heidegger and the third reduction. It is a re-inscription of Ideen I. Applied to Ideen I, the question "transcendental I or Dasein" asks of a text whose reduction yields a region of being, and whose I is a co-given pole, which agent performs it. Husserl did not pose that question in 1913.

Heidegger himself failed: Dasein remains haunted by the I, the phenomenon of being never shows itself, and the phenomenology of the inapparent stays a programme. Phenomenology must either disappear or grant that its method depends no more on the question of being than it could on objectivity alone (R Phenomenology as such ¶5, citing SZ §83, 437). No Ideen I locus is engaged, but the dilemma presupposes one. Ideen I already makes phenomenology independent of every ontology. The extended reduction switches off formal logic as *mathesis universalis* and the material eidetic disciplines (§§59–60), and §61 forbids taking "kein einziger Lehrsatz, ja nicht einmal ein Axiom" (not a single theorem, not even an axiom) from the sciences that fall under the reduction. Marion's "second way" (a phenomenology that rests on its own protocols, before or beyond both the constituted object and the being in its being) has a Husserlian precedent in this switching off of ontologies. Chapter II (n. 4, Ideen I §153) and chapter V will cite it. The list of those who took the way (Levinas, Ricœur, Derrida, Henry) puts Ricœur, Ideen I's French translator, among them.

The closing paragraph states the method: since "in phenomenology possibility stands higher than actuality" (SZ §7, 38), the principle must be pushed to its end, even against phenomenology as it has been carried out, because a genuine thought is surpassed "en la répétant" (by repeating it), "alors même l'échec réussit" (then even failure succeeds; R Phenomenology as such ¶6). Ideen I is the "phénoménologie déjà effectuée" (phenomenology already carried out) against which the repetition will be most often exercised: the reduction of §§31–62 will be repeated in chapter II through Heidegger and in chapter VI into a third reduction. The principle of repetition is Heidegger's, and Marion turns it on Heidegger as well. Ideen I knows a weaker form of the thought. It calls itself an introduction and a beginning, and Husserl's later notes in his own copies (Hua III/2) show him already repeating the book against itself. Marion's repetition differs in kind: it keeps the method and changes what the method is for.

## I. The Breakthrough and the Enlargement

### 1 - Two Interpretations and an Enlargement

Marion opens with the year 1913. Husserl salutes the *Logical Investigations* as "a work of breakthrough" in the preface to their second edition, and in the same year he publishes Ideen I, so that the salute is a farewell (R I.1 ¶1, n. 1). Every later reading, he argues, understands 1900–1901 by what it does not yet say, so the breakthrough must be read on the sole discourse of the *Investigations*. Of the two readings strong enough to do this, Heidegger's starts from the Sixth Investigation and the givenness of being, and Derrida's from the First and the primacy of presence, leading to "le « principe des principes » de 1913" (the "principle of principles" of 1913). Marion cites the 1913 Foreword to the LU and the draft preface published by Fink, not Ideen I. Ideen I gives the strongest evidence for his "depreciation beneath the praise". Its Introduction names the pure phenomenology it opens as "dieselbe, die in den »Logischen Untersuchungen« zu einem ersten Durchbruch kam, und deren Sinn sich mir in der Fortarbeit des letzten Jahrzehnts immer tiefer und reicher erschloß" (the same that came to a first breakthrough in the *Logical Investigations*, and whose sense disclosed itself to me ever more deeply and richly over the last decade's work; Hua III/1 4). The same page denies that this phenomenology is the descriptive psychology readers took from the LU, "in vermeintlicher Übereinstimmung" (in supposed agreement) with them. Ideen I thus does what Marion describes: it grants the breakthrough and claims for itself the authority to say what it meant. Marion's method is a refusal of that authority. His reading of P on the fact is right, and the refusal is his.

Read from the Sixth, the LU lead to ontological difference; read from the First, to *différance*. Marion draws two questions from the conflict: in what, for Husserl himself and "abstraction faite du prolongement des *Ideen*" (setting aside the prolongation in the *Ideas*), does the breakthrough consist; and does the givenness Heidegger takes from the Sixth coincide with the intuition Derrida indicts in the First (R I.1 ¶2)? The first question brackets Ideen I, and the bracketing does not hold. Two paragraphs later Marion uses §24 as the "accomplishment and truth" of the LU's return to the things (¶4). In §6 the principle of all principles supplies the decisive text for givenness's priority over intuition (n. 83). Ideen I is set aside as a lens and then brought back as a witness. This is not inconsistency for its own sake. Marion's thesis is that 1913 states what 1900–1901 had done without saying so, which is Ideen I's own thesis about the LU (Hua III/1 4). The bracket concerns Ideen I's doctrines (noema, reduction, pure I); its principle is allowed to testify.

Already in 1900 Husserl names the difficulty of pure phenomenological analysis: an "orientation antinaturelle" (anti-natural orientation) of thought, a "habitus anti-naturel de la réflexion" (anti-natural habit of reflection), which takes acts rather than objects as the matter at issue. The return to "the things themselves" leads concepts back to "fully developed intuitions" (R I.1 ¶3, nn. 6–7). The 1913 draft preface says the LU method is "radically intuitive in my enlarged sense", and the 1901 *Selbstanzeige* claims "a fundamental and hitherto unaccomplished enlargement of the concepts of perception and intuition" (nn. 11–12). Ideen I presupposes this and recasts it. Its anti-natural turn is no longer reflection on acts but the suspension of the general thesis of the natural attitude (§§27–32). Its Introduction demands "a new attitude, wholly changed against the natural" (Hua III/1 5), and its acts are eidetic and, after the reduction, transcendental (Hua III/1 4–5). Ideen I also restates the anti-empiricist core of the return to the things in its own terms: "Vernünftig oder wissenschaftlich über Sachen urteilen, das heißt aber, sich nach den Sachen selbst richten" (to judge rationally or scientifically about things means to direct oneself by the things themselves, §19). Marion's reconstruction stays at the pre-reductive level that Ideen I's Introduction warns readers of the LU against, where the breakthrough is a turn from objects to acts. That is the level he chose by bracketing the *Ideas*. His conclusion (the breakthrough raises intuition, as the worker of evidence, to the sole justification of every statement) is one Ideen I would sign. Ideen I would add that intuition justifies only within the limits of what it gives (§24).

The first confirmation addresses the objection that the principle of all principles of 1913 sets phenomenology on a new course. The 1913 draft preface credits the LU with "radically professing" that principle, and for Husserl the principle "constitue l'accomplissement et la vérité" (constitutes the accomplishment and truth) of the return to the things: "que l'intuition originairement donatrice soit une source de droit de la connaissance" (that originarily giving intuition is a source of right for knowledge; R I.1 ¶4, n. 15 / Hua III/1 51, R's "Hua, III, p. 52"). The quotation is faithful to "daß jede originär gebende Anschauung eine Rechtsquelle der Erkenntnis sei" (§24) and drops only "jede" (every). The function supports Marion. §24 opens "Doch genug der verkehrten Theorien" (but enough of perverse theories) and closes the critique of empiricism (§§18–23). Its work is the one Marion assigns the breakthrough: to refuse the identification of giving acts with sensuous experience. "Also der Erfahrung substituieren wir das Allgemeinere »Anschauung«" (so for experience we substitute the more general "intuition", §20, Hua III/1 44), and "alle Anschauungsarten" (all kinds of intuition) count as "gleichwertige Rechtsquellen der Erkenntnis" (equally valid sources of right for knowledge, §20, Hua III/1 45). Two departures remain. First, §24 states the principle for every assertion, not for phenomenology alone: the natural scientist follows a "parallel principle", and the principle stands in Part I, before the epoché. Marion makes it the principle of phenomenology as such. Second, he closes the paragraph with Aristotle's νοῦς at the principle of principles. Ideen I's 1913 text gives that link no support, but Husserl's later hand does: the marginal "NB νοεῖν" on "Sehen" in §19 (Hua III/2, to Hua III/1 43), which Marion's Biemel text prints as Husserl's own (n. 83). The connection is Husserl's after 1913, not in 1913.

The second confirmation is that intuition can serve as the principle only if it is first enlarged, as the Second Investigation announces and the Sixth carries out in §45. The First Investigation is preparatory, the Sixth "the most mature", and the order of exposition runs "en zigzag" (R I.1 ¶5, nn. 18–23). Ideen I confirms the enlargement as a standing doctrine and states it in its most general form. "Auch Wesenserschauung ist eben Anschauung, wie eidetischer Gegenstand eben Gegenstand ist" (seeing of essences too is intuition, just as an eidetic object is an object), and the generalization of the two correlative concepts is "nicht ein beliebiger Einfall, sondern durch die Natur der Sachen zwingend gefordert" (not an arbitrary notion but compellingly required by the nature of things; §3, Hua III/1 14). Its note replaces the LU's "Ideation" by a "freer concept" that includes even "dark", no longer intuiting, consciousness of an essence. Another note defends "meine Lehre von der kategorialen Anschauung" (my doctrine of categorial intuition) against Külpe (§3 n., Hua III/1 16). §21 adds that "jedes urteilende Einsehen" (every judging insight) falls "unter den Begriff gebender Intuition" (under the concept of giving intuition), whose differentiations run "parallel to the logical categories" (Hua III/1 47). Marion's reconstruction of 1900–1901 is confirmed by 1913, and more strongly than he shows. One point should be marked for later. In §3 the enlargement of intuition is correlative with an enlargement of the object, so Ideen I's answer to "what does the enlarged intuition give?" is "an object in the widened sense". That answer is the "objectivity" Marion will indict.

### 2 - The Domains of Intuition

What does enlarged intuition give? The draft preface answers: "something objective which is, without being in the manner of the real", so that ideas "are, and count as objects". In 1900–1901 the seven regular solids are seven objects as truly as the seven Sages. The acts themselves must become objects, and "objectness" (*Gegenständlichkeit*) is preferred to "object" because it covers states of affairs and categorial forms. The two extensions, of intuition beyond the sensuous and of objectness beyond the real, reach "le même horizon — catégorial" (the same horizon, the categorial; R I.2 ¶1, nn. 24–28). Ideen I states the parallel as a single correlation (§3, Hua III/1 14, above), and it answers the reproach of "platonizing realism" in the same terms. Essences are objects with true being, graspable by intuition, without being real things, once object and real are kept apart "wie es in den »Logischen Untersuchungen« geschehen ist" (as was done in the *Logical Investigations*), with "Gegenstand definiert als irgend etwas" (object defined as anything whatever; §22, Hua III/1 47). The horizon Marion calls categorial is, in Ideen I, formal ontology, the eidetic science of "Gegenstand überhaupt" (object in general), whose categories are those of the "empty form" of the object (§10). This is reconstruction. Marion's "categorial horizon" is Ideen I's formal region, read back into the LU. Ideen I insists that the formal region "is not coordinate with the material regions" (§10). That qualification will matter at the next step.

Against Kant's restriction of intuition to the sensuous, Marion finds three moments, each a new sense of the categorial, and concludes that "toute intuition est, pour Husserl, à un degré différent, catégoriale" (every intuition is, for Husserl, categorial in some degree; R I.2 ¶2). The moments are sensuous intuition fulfilling a categorial form; ideation founded on sensuous intuition, giving "*the* red"; and categorial intuition proper. Heidegger's 1925 gloss ("I see first, universally: there is a house") makes the "as" of the house precede and enable the seeing of a house (n. 37). No Ideen I locus is cited. The founding of the seeing of essences on individual intuition is restated in §3 (Hua III/1 14–15). The conclusion runs against Ideen I's distinctions. Red and house are material essences; categorial forms are formal. Ideen I separates the two strictly: logical categories belong to the formal region, red to the material (§§9–10), and the formal region "ist also doch nicht etwas den materialen Regionen Koordiniertes" (is not something coordinate with the material regions, §10, Hua III/1 26). LU VI makes the same cut between sensuous and categorial concepts. Marion's "every intuition is categorial" folds the material essence into the categorial and Ideen I's formal region into its material regions. It is a radicalization of the LU's enlargement against the distinction 1913 draws, and Heidegger's "as" is the mediator through which it passes. The thesis prepares chapter V's charge that Husserl's object is formal through and through. That charge needs the material essences to drop out, and here they are already absorbed.

The last step is categorial intuition proper (LU VI §45). From the homogeneity of the fulfilling function, the "is" of "gold is yellow" demands an intuition of its own, and its absence is felt when the "is" goes unfulfilled. Categorial intuition is then a case of "the great class of acts in which something appears as 'actual' and as 'given in person'", so that "l'intuition résulte de la donation sans exception" (intuition results from givenness without exception; R I.2 ¶3, nn. 38–40). Ideen I keeps the doctrine (§3 n., Hua III/1 16) but no longer lets the "is" wait on a categorial intuition. The "is" of a positing becomes a noematic being-character (*Seinscharakter*), the correlate of the doxic thesis: what appears perceptually has "den Charakter des »wirklich« seienden schlechthin" (the character of "actually" being simply; §103, Hua III/1 239). What fulfils is now the noematic sense in the mode of its fullness (§132; Commentary P on §132 agrees). Marion's sentence "intuition results from givenness" reads the LU through his thesis. The text he cites says only that categorial intuition belongs to the acts in which something appears as given. In the LU the givenness is the act's achievement, the appearing of an object "as given in person". Marion's inversion, by which the act is what givenness makes possible, is announced here before it is argued (§6).

The Kantian limit is transgressed in several ways "pour un seul motif : l'exigence phénoménologique de la donation en présence de tout phénomène, sans reste ni réserve" (for a single motive: the phenomenological requirement that every phenomenon be given in presence, without remainder or reserve; R I.2 ¶4). The transition sets up the verdict of §3. Ideen I has its own Kant, used to set a limit rather than to break one. Adequate givenness of a thing is in principle impossible "in abgeschlossener Erscheinung" (in a closed appearance), but its perfect givenness is prefigured "als »Idee« (im Kantischen Sinn)" (as an "Idea" in the Kantian sense), an infinite continuum of appearings whose infinity "prinzipiell nicht gegeben sein kann" (in principle cannot be given; §143, Hua III/1 331). Commentary P reports this correctly. The remainder Marion denies is in Ideen I an essential law of thing-givenness. Marion is right about the motive of the enlargement and wrong, for 1913, about its result. Ideen I extends givenness to every region (§138, Hua III/1 321) and keeps in every transcendent region an unfillable horizon.

### 3 - The Accomplishment of Presence

If metaphysics is the uninterrogated primacy of bringing beings into presence, and that bringing is done by intuition, metaphysics is never so accomplished as in the LU, which open "universal intuition", "wooden iron" to many ears (R I.3 ¶1, n. 41). The Göttingen lectures of 1907 list the modes of authentic givenness and conclude "überall ist die Gegebenheit" (givenness is everywhere; n. 42, *Idee der Phänomenologie*, Hua II 74). Husserl calls for teams and generations of workers. A 1925 text demands a "Gesamtanschauung der Welt" (overall intuition of the world; n. 44, Hua IX 88). Intuition "ne respecte rien" (respects nothing) and pours the world into presence "sans retrait, sans reste, sans retenue" (without withdrawal, without remainder, without restraint). Ideen I is the systematic form of the 1907 list: "Jeder Region und Kategorie prätendierter Gegenstände entspricht ... eine Grundart von originär gebendem Bewußtsein" (to every region and category of putative objects there corresponds a basic kind of originarily giving consciousness, §138, Hua III/1 321). On universality P supports Marion. On "sans reste" it does not. §138 makes inadequacy and the possibility of "explosion" of perception essential to the thing (Commentary P on §138), §143 makes total givenness an Idea, and §44 makes the being of consciousness given precisely without appearing: "Das Erlebnis stellt sich ... nicht dar" (the experience does not present itself in appearances, Hua III/1 93). Marion's "Gesamtanschauung" comes from 1925, and his "barbarism" of presence is an image of the telos §143 describes. Read against Ideen I, the paragraph names a telos as an accomplishment. Marion concedes as much when he calls the enterprise "programmatic" and the task "almost truly infinite": that is §143's infinity under another name.

The breakthrough opens only onto the metaphysical accomplishment of presence, and Marion rejoins Derrida's conclusion by way of Nietzsche. The fragment "How I recognize my own kind" reveals philosophy's "hidden" history, as Husserl makes phenomenology "la « nostalgie secrète *(geheime)* de toute la philosophie moderne »" (the "secret (*geheime*) nostalgia of all modern philosophy"; R I.3 ¶2, n. 46 / §62, Hua III/1 134). Nietzsche's Dionysian Yes to the world "without subtracting, excepting or selecting" answers the principle of all principles, which accepts what is offered "originairement dans l'« intuition » (pour ainsi dire dans son effectivité charnelle), ... simplement comme ce qu'il se donne, mais aussi seulement dans les bornes dans lesquelles il se donne là" (originarily in "intuition" (so to speak in its fleshly actuality), ... simply as what it gives itself as, but also only within the limits in which it gives itself there; n. 48 / Hua III/1 51). Husserl says "not more", Nietzsche "not less": "Rien n'est qui n'apparaisse, rien n'apparaît qui ne soit" (nothing is that does not appear, nothing appears that is not). The quotation of §24 is exact. "Charnelle" for "leibhaft" (in bodily presence, Husserl's standard word for perceptual self-presence, softened by "sozusagen", so to speak) draws the principle toward the flesh and the Dionysian body; the argument does not rest on it. The departure is in the limits clause. In §24 it restricts the right that intuition confers: an assertion may claim nothing beyond what is given, and as given. It is the anti-speculative half of the principle, and its target is theory (§24 ¶1: no theory can mislead us, since every theory draws its truth from the originary givens). Marion makes it the measure of a totality, the "ni plus" that, joined to Nietzsche's "ni moins", gives exact and complete presence. That is a re-inscription. The formula "nothing is that does not appear" also fails as a statement of Ideen I. There the absolute being, consciousness, does not appear in the sense of presenting itself through appearances (§44, Hua III/1 93), and the real is "unity of sense" for consciousness (§55, Hua III/1 120), not appearance as such. The further text Marion adds, that "all ontologies fall under the reduction" and pure phenomenology "contains" them (n. 50), is Ideen III (Hua V 76–77), not Ideen I.

Marion's answer to the first question stands in outline: the breakthrough accomplishes the metaphysics of presence by enlarging intuition until, like Nietzsche's great Amen, it brings all beings into presence (R I.3 ¶3). Ideen I has entered the argument at two points, the secret longing of §62 and the principle of §24, and at both Marion has kept the wording and changed the function. The longing for a critical science has become a longing for unconditional presence. The limit on the right of intuition has become the measure of total presence. The verdict holds for the *Investigations* as Marion reads them through their 1913 and 1925 self-interpretations. Ideen I does not confirm it, since the text has already moved the limit of presence into the essence of the thing (§143) and taken the being of consciousness out of appearing (§44).

### 4 - A Misconstrual of Signification?

Is the accomplishment of the primacy of intuition enough to fold the breakthrough back into metaphysics? Marion makes two remarks. The category "metaphysics of presence" is Heidegger's, and Derrida uses it while rejecting Heidegger's reading of the LU without justifying the split use (R I.4 ¶1, n. 51). Derrida's LU go to the edge of presence by freeing signification from intuition, then retreat. Derrida needs two decisions: to reduce signification to the sign, and to suppose that intuition alone brings into presence. No Ideen I locus is cited. One is conspicuously absent. Derrida's case for the subordination of meaning to sense and vision rests in large part on Ideen I §124: the layer of expression "ist ... nicht produktiv" (is not productive), its productivity "exhausts itself in expressing" a sense already constituted in pre-expressive acts (Hua III/1 287; Commentary P on §124 agrees). In 1913, signification (*Bedeutung*) is a stratum that mirrors a noematic sense (*Sinn*) belonging to every act, intuitive acts first of all. Marion confines the dispute to the LU and leaves aside the text in which Husserl himself made signification secondary. The silence matters. Ideen I is the strongest witness for Derrida's "telos of vision", and Marion's defence of the LU against Derrida does not reach it.

The second decision yields the aporia. If signification signifies without intuition, and intuition, enlarged to a *Gesamtanschauung*, exhausts presence, what presence is left to signification? Either Husserl contradicts himself, or he had already exceeded intuition as the last name of presence, and signification "pourrait, même sans être vue par intuition, encore *être*" (could, even without being seen by intuition, still *be*; R I.4 ¶2). Marion takes the second way. Ideen I has a third answer, which Marion's dilemma excludes. It neither subordinates sense to intuition nor sets them apart: it makes sense the noematic core common to the fulfilled and the empty act. "Diese Unterschiede gehen nicht den puren Sinn, bzw. Satz an" (these differences do not concern the pure sense or proposition), which is identical in both members; it concerns "the manner in which" the sense is fulfilled or unfulfilled (§136, Hua III/1 315–316; Commentary P on §136). Signification's presence without intuition is, for 1913, the being of the noema. The noema is neither an intuition nor a non-presence, and Husserl calls it "Sinn im Wie seiner Gegebenheitsweise" (sense in the how of its mode of givenness, §94, Hua III/1 217). Marion's second way will arrive at "givenness" as the term above both (§6). Ideen I already has a term above both, and it is the noema. Selection and silence again: the dilemma is real for the LU and was already answered differently in 1913.

### 5 - Presence without Intuition

However universal intuition becomes, it admits an exteriority, the "phenomenologically irreducible difference" that leads by a "total separation" to the opposition of intuition and signification. This opposition precedes the oppositions within intuition (sensuous and categorial, adequate and inadequate, individual and general), and signification marks off *a priori* the spaces intuition fills (R I.5 ¶1, nn. 55–57). The formula *i* + *s* = 1 must not suggest an inverse proportion, since intuition can give only to what it is not, the always prior signification. Ideen I keeps the priority and changes its bearer. The fulfilled and the empty act share one sense, and fulfilment adds "a specific reason-character" to the positing, not a new sense (§136, Hua III/1 315–316). Marion's "intuition can give only to what it is not" holds in 1913 of the noematic sense, which intuitive acts have as well. The difference he needs, signification against intuition, becomes in Ideen I a difference of modes of one sense: "Sinn im Modus seiner Fülle" (sense in the mode of its fullness, §132). Marion reconstructs the LU faithfully. His "always prior signification" is in Ideen I the sense, and its priority is not a priority over intuition.

Because an act of signification constitutes itself "without the least fulfilling or illustrating intuition having to intervene", signification is autonomous and intuition an "eventual" supplement. Mathematical idealities show that inadequacy is the rule, and the ideal object "π is transcendental" is seen "with evidence" (*ich sehe ein*, I see with insight) to be identical whether or not anyone thinks it. So "l'évidence phénoménologique s'accomplit, ici du moins, sans intuition de remplissement" (phenomenological evidence is accomplished, here at least, without fulfilling intuition; R I.5 ¶2, nn. 58–62). Ideen I contests the conclusion directly. In 1913, insight is a seeing: "jedes urteilende Einsehen, wie insbesondere das unbedingt allgemeiner Wahrheiten, [fällt] unter den Begriff gebender Intuition" (every judging insight, in particular that of unconditionally general truths, falls under the concept of giving intuition; §21, Hua III/1 47). Evidence is defined as the genus of all "seeing" referred to originary givenness, "Sehen (im extrem erweiterten Sinne)" (seeing in an extremely enlarged sense; §137, Hua III/1 318). The "blind" predication that 2 + 1 = 1 + 2 becomes evident when the state of affairs is "originär gegeben" (originarily given, §136, Hua III/1 315). Ideen I also anticipates the mathematical case: for essence-positings "die anschauliche Vergegenwärtigung ihrer vollkommenen Erfüllung [ist] gleichwertig mit der Erfüllung selbst" (the intuitive re-presentation of their perfect fulfilment is equivalent to the fulfilment itself; §140, Hua III/1 326). The insight Marion calls evidence without intuition is, for 1913, an intuition of essence. Marion reads the LU's "ich sehe ein" as evidence that does without seeing. Ideen I reads the same words as seeing in the enlarged sense. On the text of the LU Marion's reading is defensible. Against Ideen I it is a contestation that Ideen I can answer, since 1913 removed the opposition on which the argument rests.

Derrida's proof text, LU I §11, says that without "possibility" or "truth" the statement's intention is accomplished only "symbolically" and lacks, "as one usually says" (*wie man zu sagen pflegt*), the "true", "authentic" *Bedeutung*. Marion shows that the thesis is natural consciousness's, marked by the incise and the quotation marks, and that the sentences Derrida omits state Husserl's own: every statement, cognitive or not, "has its meaning and constitutes in it the signification" (R I.5 ¶3, nn. 63–64). The point concerns the LU, and Marion's reading of §11 is careful. Ideen I bears on it as a check on both sides. It keeps Marion's point: the sense is identical in the blind and the insightful act (§136). It also gives Derrida a ground the LU do not give him: the reason-character, and with it truth, belongs to a positing "dann und nur dann" (then and only then) when it rests on a fulfilled, originarily giving sense (§136, Hua III/1 316; Commentary P agrees), and "truth" is the correlate of the perfect reason-character of the primal doxa (§139). In 1913 a meaning without intuition keeps its sense and lacks its right. Derrida's "true meaning-to-say" is in Ideen I the rational right of the positing, and on that point Ideen I sides with the "on a coutume de dire" that Marion assigns to natural consciousness. Both readers find their thesis in 1913, each in one layer of the distinction.

Derrida's misreading has its motive in LU I §26: essentially occasional expressions (*I*, *this*) take their signification from the living speech and its intuitive circumstances. Marion answers that such expressions are exceptions, essentially non-expressive; that the LU's I is a mere "complex of experiences" with no ideal signification; and that LU VI §5 Addendum partly restores the "indicated signification" (R I.5 ¶4, nn. 65–69). The second argument rests on a premise Husserl withdrew in 1913. Ideen I posits a pure I as "a peculiar, not constituted transcendence in immanence", co-given with every cogitatio and exempt from switching off (§57, Hua III/1 124), and §80 relates every experience to it. The second edition of the LU, which Marion cites, appeared the same year. Marion reads the First Investigation's "I" in the 1901 sense while citing the 1913 text. From 1913 the *I* that LU I §26 treats as indexical stands over against a pure I, which apart from its "modes of relation" is "völlig leer an Wesenskomponenten" (wholly empty of essential components; §80, Hua III/1 178). Marion's first and third arguments stand. The second holds for 1901 only, and it does not affect the conclusion that the occasional expression is not the paradigm of expression.

Marion's thesis is that signification in the LU gives itself evidently without depending on fulfilling intuition: "we find it there". It owes nothing to the sign either, and it has a "content", "ideal content", "intentional sense". "Quel mode de la présence *sui generis* se déploie lorsque la signification, d'elle-même et d'elle seule, se présente ?" (what mode of presence *sui generis* unfolds when signification, of itself and by itself alone, presents itself?; R I.5 ¶5, nn. 70–71). Ideen I's answer to this question is the noema. Husserl takes the LU's "intentional content" and "sense" into the noematic sense, the "Vermeinte" (what is meant) with its determinate content (§132, Hua III/1 305), and he names its mode: not a real (*reell*) moment of the experience but an intentional one (§§88, 97), which is "in the how of its mode of givenness" (§94). Marion asks the question as though no answer existed in Husserl. In §6 he will answer it with "givenness", having first said that the noesis-noema correlation, "dominante dans les *Ideen*" (dominant in the *Ideas*), must not be hastily found in the LU (R I.6 ¶3). The noema is set aside because it is the doctrine of the *Ideas*, but it is also the closest Husserlian candidate for the presence Marion seeks. This is the chapter's main silence about P.

### 6 - The Evidence of Givenness

The Sixth Investigation confirms the First: every signification "can be thought completely without the least correlative intuition"; the signifying intention "is given first, and given for itself"; there are "countless significations that can never come to expression". Signification appears in evidence "as one and the same", and since "phénoménologiquement, l'être se réduit à l'évidence" (phenomenologically, being reduces to evidence), independent significations are "wirklich seiende Bedeutungen" (actually existing significations; R I.6 ¶1, nn. 72–76). The middle premise is Marion's, offered as phenomenology's. Its nearest Husserlian statement is Ideen I §142, which Marion does not cite: "wahrhaft seiender Gegenstand" (truly existing object) and an object to be posited "in einer ursprünglichen vollkommenen Vernunftthesis" (in an originary perfect reason-thesis) are equivalent correlates (Hua III/1 329; Commentary P on §142). That equivalence is not a reduction of being to evidence. It is a correlation of essence between the idea of true being and the idea of a possible adequate givenness, and in the case of things Husserl makes the givenness an Idea (§143). Marion's "being reduces to evidence" radicalizes §142 into an identity. The step lets signification "exist" because it is evident, and that existence is what §6 needs. Ideen I gives the correlation, not the identity.

Marion now envisages a second enlargement: signification is enlarged until it exists as an autonomous being, "the domain of signification is far wider than that of intuition" (n. 77). The two surpluses collide, and evidence seems to flow from two sources. The late self-interpretation sharpens the conflict: in the *Krisis* the LU are said to have made "evidence" a problem, "freed it from the pre-eminence of scientific evidence and enlarged it to universal originary self-giving" (zur allgemeinen originalen Selbstgebung erweitert; R I.6 ¶2, n. 79, Hua VI 237). Is the enlargement of evidence that of intuition or that of signification? Marion finds it undecided. Ideen I decides. Faced with naming the highest genus of reason-consciousness, Husserl says one may extend either "Sehen" (seeing) or "Einsehen", "Evidenz" (insight, evidence), and takes "Evidenz" for "the most general concept", defined as "das auf originäre Gegebenheit bezogene »Sehen« (im extrem erweiterten Sinne)" (the "seeing" referred to originary givenness, in an extremely enlarged sense; §137, Hua III/1 318). "Evidenz — das Wort in unserem erweiterten Sinne verstanden" (evidence, the word taken in our enlarged sense) is then distributed over every region (§138, Hua III/1 321). The 1913 enlargement of evidence is an enlargement of seeing to everything originarily given, and nothing in Ideen I gives signification a separate source of evidence. Marion's undecidability is real for the LU. The *Krisis* sentence reflects the decision of 1913, which Marion's bracketing of the *Ideas* keeps out of view.

The way out comes from the *Krisis* note: the breakthrough of 1898 was "this universal correlational *a priori* of the object of experience and the modes of givenness". Marion urges that it be identified neither with noesis-noema ("dominant in the *Ideen*") nor with intuition-intention, but with "the correlation between appearing and what appears as such" (n. 80). Givenness precedes intuition and signification, which are its modes. In 1913 the principle "ne privilégie l'intuition qu'en tant qu'il l'interprète d'abord comme « originellement donatrice »" (privileges intuition only insofar as it first interprets it as "originarily giving") and even admits a formulation by givenness: "Qu'il nous soit évident, que toute théorie ne pourrait à son tour tirer sa vérité que de données (ou : donations ? *Gegebenheiten*) originaires" (let it be evident to us that every theory could in turn draw its truth only from originary data (or: givennesses? *Gegebenheiten*); R I.6 ¶3, n. 83 / Hua III/1 51). The first claim is §19 almost word for word: seeing "hat rechtgebende Funktion nur, weil und soweit sie originär gebende ist" (has a right-giving function only because and insofar as it is originarily giving; Hua III/1 43). That is P's strongest support for Marion's priority of giving over intuition. The second claim rests on a rendering. "Gegebenheiten" in the plural, the sources from which a theory draws (*schöpfen*) its truth, are givens; Ideen I uses the plural so, as in "ganze Klassen von echten Gegebenheiten" (whole classes of genuine givens, §20, Hua III/1 45). "Donations" turns the givens into the event of giving, and the question mark shows that Marion knows it. In §19 the giving belongs to the seeing ("originär gebendes Bewußtsein", originarily giving consciousness), not to what is seen. Marion's conclusion, that givenness gives intuition and signification as its modes, transfers the participle from the act to the phenomenon. The noema is set aside for the same reason. In Ideen I the modes of givenness are noematic, "Sinn im Wie seiner Gegebenheitsweise" (§94, Hua III/1 217), so the "more essential" correlation Marion draws from the *Krisis* is, in 1913, the noesis-noema correlation itself. Marion's correlation of appearing and what appears is Ideen I's noema, read without the noesis.

A text of the Sixth Investigation shows the object constituted "as 'given'" in fulfilling acts, given "on the same mode as it is meant by the signification", and the play of quotation marks marks two givennesses, one in signification and one in intuition. "Es gibt also … Bedeutungen" (there are significations). Presence is therefore givenness, and Derrida's conclusion stands while his argument falls: presence triumphs in signification as in intuition, and Derrida is "too little radical". Why then did Heidegger see a beginning in the LU (R I.6 ¶4, nn. 85–89)? Ideen I says what the paragraph says, in its own terms. The object meant and the object given are one object with one sense, given in different modes. Fulfilment is a "covering" (*Deckung*) between acts of the same sense and different reason-value, "die unvollkommene Gegebenheitsweise verwandelt sich in die vollkommene" (the imperfect mode of givenness turns into the perfect one; §140, Hua III/1 324). For Husserl, "given" applies to every mode of having the object, the empty included. Marion's reading of the quotation marks is borne out by 1913. His "unique originary givenness" is a name for what Ideen I calls the identical sense across modes of givenness. The question it leaves open is whether that name adds anything. Marion's answer, in §7 and chapter II, is that it adds the question Husserl did not ask.

### 7 - Givenness as Question

Heidegger twice acknowledged the breakthrough as the condition of *Sein und Zeit*: in 1925, where categorial intuition "gives a ground", and in 1973 at Zähringen, where Husserl's *tour de force* put being into presence "phenomenally present in the category", and "being" was no longer a concept (R I.7 ¶1, nn. 90–92). Marion's reading is that the 1973 text calls categorial intuition an *analogy* with sensuous intuition, reached "by the path of analogy" because the categorial is "just as given" as the sensuous. Being is given, and categorial intuition is only a crude name, "la finalement très fruste analogie" (the finally very crude analogy), for an abyss of givenness that Husserl names without thinking. Heidegger destroys categorial intuition and never mentions it again in *Sein und Zeit*. Ideen I has a sentence on the analogy, and it denies what the 1973 seminar asserts. The seeing of essences is "das Analogon des sinnlichen Wahrnehmens und nicht des Einbildens" (the analogue of sensuous perception and not of imagining; §23, Hua III/1 50), and yet "hier liegt nicht eine bloß äußerliche Analogie vor, sondern radikale Gemeinsamkeit" (here there is no merely external analogy but a radical community; §3, Hua III/1 14), since the generalization of intuition and object is "compellingly required by the nature of things". For Husserl in 1913, the giving that sensuous and essential seeing share is what grounds the analogy, so the analogy cannot be a crude cover for the giving. Heidegger's 1973 reconstruction ("on the path of analogy") is mediation. Marion receives the LU through it and does not test it against Ideen I. Read against §3, the "abyss" Marion finds under the analogy is the "radical community" Husserl names above it. Husserl names it as the community of giving acts and their objects, not as a question. On that point Marion's charge stands: Husserl names the giving without asking what giving is.

The 1925 course confirms it. Categorial intuition is intuition, the "plain grasp of what is given in bodily presence (*von leibhaftig Gegebenem*) as it shows itself", and bodily presence is "a distinguished mode of the self-givenness of a being". Categorial acts "give objectness" (*sie geben Gegenständlichkeit*), and objectivity in the widest sense is "far richer than the reality of a thing". The breakthrough is "the requirement of a freeing of the ground" (R I.7 ¶2, nn. 93–96). The vocabulary through which Heidegger reads the Sixth Investigation in GA 20 is largely that of Ideen I: "leibhaftig" is the "leibhaften Wirklichkeit" of §24 (Hua III/1 51) and the bodily givenness of the perceived thing in §§39–43. The thesis that objectness is wider than reality, and that reality becomes intelligible only from it, restates §3 (Hua III/1 14) and the formal ontology of §10. This is a second mediation. Marion's "givenness" in 1925 is Ideen I's giving intuition as Heidegger restated it, applied to the LU. His conclusion, that the breakthrough is the enlargement of presence understood as objectivity "selon la mesure excessive de la donation" (by the excessive measure of givenness), rests partly on 1913's correlation of intuition and object. Chapter V will charge the same correlation with confining givenness to objectness. Here Marion takes it from Heidegger as liberation; in chapter V he will find it in Husserl as limitation.

Heidegger's privileging of givenness saves his reading from Derrida and, to a degree, saves Husserl. Husserl never says being is intuited by an "intuition of being": in fulfilment, "being in the sense of truth" is realized and the little word *is* "is itself given, or at least presumed given", in the object's "actual being-given (*aktuelles Gegebensein*)". And Husserl always infers categorial intuition by analogy, as "an act that gives it, the analogue of ordinary sensuous intuition" (R I.7 ¶3, nn. 97–98). Husserl, dazzled, "manages" the excess of givenness without questioning it, perhaps from fright. Ideen I has a more determinate doctrine of the *is* than the Sixth Investigation, and Marion does not consider it. The *is* of a positing is a noematic being-character (§103), and "being in the sense of truth" becomes the equivalence of "truly existing object" with what can be posited in an originary perfect reason-thesis (§142, Hua III/1 329). Ideen I does not leave the givenness of the *is* anonymous. It assigns it to the doxic thesis and its rational right, and it ties true being to the idea of adequate givenness. Marion's "anonymous givenness of the *is*" holds for the LU, which Marion reads through Heidegger. For 1913 it overlooks a thesis, the being-character as noematic correlate of belief, which Marion's chapter II will meet.

Husserl could not answer the question "quoi donc se donne ?" (what then gives itself?), because he never heard it as a question: "Que signifie donner ... comment donc penser que tout ce qui est ne soit qu'autant qu'il est donné ?" (what does giving mean ... how are we to think that everything that is, is only insofar as it is given?). Heidegger, seeing that the breakthrough is the enlargement of givenness, asks what givenness means and so does not reduce it to presence too early. Two prerequisites follow: a step back that makes givenness worthy of question (a reduction), and recognition that in givenness the being of what appears is at stake. The single question is whether the reduction leads phenomenology to see being as a phenomenon (R I.7 ¶4). Marion restricts the silence to the *Investigations* ("du moins dans les *Recherches logiques*", at least in the *Logical Investigations*), and the restriction is exact. Ideen I does not ask what giving means, but it asks the second half of Marion's question and answers it. How is everything that is only insofar as it is given? "Alle realen Einheiten sind »Einheiten des Sinnes«" (all real unities are "unities of sense"), and sense-unities presuppose sense-giving consciousness (§55, Hua III/1 120); the being of the transcendent is "bloß phänomenales Sein" (merely phenomenal being), that of the immanent "absolutes Sein" (§44). Ideen I does not leave the relation of being to givenness unthought. It thinks it as the relativity of reality to absolute consciousness and calls the result transcendental idealism. Marion's two prerequisites, the reduction and the question of being, are Ideen I's two themes (Part II and §§42–55). Chapter II will ask whether Husserl's answer is a question of being at all.

The chapter has used Ideen I less often than it relies on it. Three loci carry Marion's argument: §62's secret longing, which frames phenomenology as the fulfilment of metaphysics; §24's principle, made both the LU's own principle and, through the clause of limits, the measure of total presence; and §§19 and 24 again, read as giving priority to givenness over intuition. The first two keep Husserl's words and change their function. The longing for a critical science becomes the longing for presence without condition, and the restriction on the right of intuition becomes the measure of a totality. The third is Marion's strongest Husserlian ground ("rechtgebende Funktion nur, weil und soweit sie originär gebende ist", §19). Even there, his rendering of the plural givens as "donations" and his transfer of giving from the act to the phenomenon are his own moves, and the Greek gloss that links the principle to Aristotle's νοῦς is Husserl's later hand, printed by Biemel as 1913. Where Ideen I could have answered Marion's questions, the chapter sets it aside: the noema answers his question about the sui generis presence of signification, §137 decides the enlargement of evidence he finds undecided, and §3 meets the "crude analogy" with a "radical community". This silence is method, not oversight. By bracketing the *Ideas*, Marion keeps open in the LU a question that Ideen I had closed by its doctrines of noematic sense and absolute consciousness. Givenness can be shown as the unasked question of 1900–1901 because the answer of 1913 is held back until chapter II. His reading of the *Investigations* holds against their own text, and his account of what Husserl "never asked" holds for them alone. Against Ideen I, the chapter's verdict on Husserl, that he manages givenness without thinking it, still has to be earned.

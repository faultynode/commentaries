# Jean-Luc Marion, *Réduction et donation*, read against Husserl, *Ideen I* (Husserliana III): a comparative commentary

## Foreword

Marion (R) is cited by division, section, paragraph and note (R I.1 ¶4, R I n. 15), since the edition used has no page numbers. Husserl's *Ideen I* (P) is cited by section and by the pages of Husserliana III/1 (Schuhmann, 1976), which reproduces the text of 1913. Marion cites the earlier Husserliana III (Biemel, 1950), whose pagination differs and whose main text weaves in Husserl's later handwritten additions. Each of his references is therefore located by section and wording, given once in his form, and checked against the 1913 text and the apparatus of Hua III/2. Six traps govern this pair. Marion's Ideen I contains words Husserl wrote after 1913. Husserl's giving is the act's ("originarily giving intuition", "giving consciousness"), while Marion's is the phenomenon's own ("what gives itself"), and the French *donation* covers both. The principle of all principles (§24) belongs to the critique of empiricism in Part I, ahead of the reduction, and carries a clause of limits. Ideen I's talk of being (consciousness and reality as regions, absolute being) is regional and eidetic and must not be read through Heidegger. Ideen I's "object" is formal and wide, and its ego is the pure I, not the transcendental I of the *Cartesian Meditations*. Finally, "metaphysics of presence" is Heidegger's and Derrida's category, not Husserl's. Ideen I has its own reserve against total presence in the inadequacy of thing-givenness and in adequate givenness as an Idea in the Kantian sense (§§138, 143).

The Foreword's single sentence puts givenness "au centre de la réduction, donc de la phénoménologie" (at the centre of the reduction, and therefore of phenomenology; R Foreword ¶1) and names the goal: to determine "ce qui, sans l'être, vient après le sujet" (what, without being it, comes after the subject). Neither half is Ideen I's. Ideen I introduces the reduction as a switching off and bracketing of the general thesis of the world (§§31–32) and names its result a "residue": transcendental consciousness (§33), "absolute consciousness as the residue of the annihilation of the world" (§49). Givenness enters Ideen I at the edges of that doctrine: immanent perception gives its object absolutely (§46), and every region has its own mode of originary givenness (§138, Hua III/1 321). Marion's "therefore" makes givenness the centre of a method Husserl organized around the being of consciousness. That reordering is the book's thesis, and Marion states it as a programme, not as exegesis. The second clause prepares chapter V, where the Ideen I pure I, "a peculiar, not constituted transcendence in immanence" (eine eigenartige — nicht konstituierte — Transzendenz in der Immanenz, §57, Hua III/1 124), is read as an I "outside being". Marion's double sense of *sans l'être* (without being the subject, without being at all) will be laid on a text in which Husserl exempts the pure I from switching off only "as far as" its evident co-givenness with pure consciousness reaches (§57).

The acknowledgements name the restored Paris Husserl Archives (Courtine, Franck), the Leuven Husserl-Archiv (Ijsseling, Bernet) and Michel Henry (R Foreword ¶2). Leuven matters for this pair in a way Commentary R does not register. The Husserliana are edited there under Ijsseling, and it was under that direction that Schuhmann's Hua III/1–2 (1976) separated the 1913 text of Ideen I from Husserl's later additions. Marion thanks the Archive whose own edition he does not cite: his Ideen I references are to Biemel's text of 1950 (see the next division). Franck's *Chair et corps* (1981), to which Marion owes his reading of Ideen I §46 (R II n. 44), is a likely channel for the vocabulary of "flesh" that colours his French for Husserl's *leibhaft* (R I.3 ¶2, below). The date, 24 February 1989, sets the book thirteen years after the critical edition.

## Bibliographical note

Marion cites Husserl "d'après les *Husserliana*" (from the *Husserliana*), with one stated exception: the *Logical Investigations*, cited from the second edition of 1913 (R Bibliographical note ¶1). The note implies that the Husserliana text of Ideen I is a single, settled text. It is not. Marion's page numbers (§24 at "Hua, III, p. 52", R I n. 15) are Biemel's, and Biemel's main text is a "new edition enlarged on the basis of the author's handwritten additions" (neue, auf Grund der handschriftlichen Zusätze des Verfassers erweiterte Auflage; Hua III/1, Vorbemerkung). Schuhmann's edition restored the 1913 text and moved the additions into an apparatus. The consequence shows at once in chapter I. Marion quotes §19 as "Le 'voir' (νοεῖν) immédiat" (the immediate "seeing" (νοεῖν), R I n. 83). The 1913 text reads "Das unmittelbare »Sehen«" (the immediate "seeing", Hua III/1 43), and the Greek word is a later blue-pencil note in Husserl's copy D: "NB νοεῖν" (Hua III/2, to Hua III/1 43, line 1). The note does not record which edition was used for Ideen I. Its silence lets a later Husserl speak as the Husserl of 1913.

The note on Heidegger's editions (the Gesamtausgabe, and *Sein und Zeit* in the tenth edition with Martineau's translation; R Bibliographical note ¶2) bears on this pair only through its last sentence: Marion uses the usual translations "quitte à les modifier" (modifying them where needed). For Ideen I the usual translation is Ricœur's *Idées directrices* (1950), cited once by name (R II n. 37). Where Marion's French for a term of Ideen I carries his thesis, it cannot be settled from this corpus whether the rendering is Ricœur's, modified or not. The rendering is counted against Marion only where his argument rests on it.

The note lists earlier versions: chapter I appeared as "La percée et l'élargissement" in *Philosophie* in 1984, chapter III in 1987, chapter IV in 1987 (R Bibliographical note ¶3). For this pair the dates mean that the argument of chapter I, which sets Ideen I's principle of all principles in line with the breakthrough of 1900–1901, was formed in the early 1980s, against the Logical Investigations and their 1913 self-interpretations, eight years after the critical edition of Ideen I was available.

## Phenomenology as Such

The epigraph is Heidegger's (GA 58, 5): what does "given", "givenness" mean, "this magic word of phenomenology and the stumbling block for the others"? Ideen I is the text that made the word magic. *Gegebenheit* and its compounds run through the book: originary givenness, mode of givenness (*Gegebenheitsweise*), "a mode of givenness in which essences are originarily given as objects" (Gegebenheitsart, in der Wesen als Gegenstände originär gegeben sind, §21, Hua III/1 47). Ideen I never makes givenness a theme of its own. It uses the word to qualify acts (giving intuition, giving consciousness) and the ways objects stand before them. Heidegger's 1919/20 remark already aims at that use, and Marion takes over the aim. The epigraph thus states the book's relation to Ideen I in one line: Husserl's most frequent operative word is to be turned into a question.

Phenomenology takes up the role of philosophy after Nietzsche has exhausted the possibilities of metaphysics, and it undertakes "un nouveau commencement" (a new beginning; R Phenomenology as such ¶1). Ideen I also claims newness, in other terms. Its Introduction demands "eine neue, gegenüber den natürlichen Erfahrungs- und Denkeinstellungen völlig geänderte Weise der Einstellung" (a new attitude, wholly changed against the natural attitudes of experience and thought, Hua III/1 5) and a science of a "new dimension" (§62). Ideen I's history of philosophy is §62's line from Descartes through Locke and Hume to Kant, each of whom came near the field of transcendental consciousness and missed it. Nietzsche does not appear in it. Marion's periodization (the end of metaphysics as its completion, then a new beginning) is Heidegger's, and it places Husserl in a history whose terms Husserl did not use. This is a re-inscription of Ideen I's claim to begin. The claim is kept, and its ground is changed from a change of attitude to a turn in the history of being.

The second paragraph supplies the frame Marion will impose on every Ideen I locus in the book. Both Nietzsche's last advance and Husserl's first arrival turn on one question: can the conditions of presence be stretched until every being reaches presence, "sans aucune condition ni réserve" (without any condition or reserve; R Phenomenology as such ¶2)? Phenomenology would then fulfil "la nostalgie de toute la philosophie moderne" (the nostalgia of all modern philosophy). The phrase is Ideen I §62: "So begreift es sich, daß die Phänomenologie gleichsam die geheime Sehnsucht der ganzen neuzeitlichen Philosophie ist" (so it is understood that phenomenology is, as it were, the secret longing of all modern philosophy; R n. 2 / Hua III/1 134, R's "Hua, t. III, p. 148"). Marion's paraphrase drops "gleichsam" (as it were) and "geheime" (secret; restored at R I.3 ¶2), and *nostalgie* turns a longing toward what is to come into a regret for what was. The departure lies in what the longing is for. In §62 phenomenology is the one science that performs for all sciences, and for itself, the critique they cannot perform, and so gives "die letzte Sinnesbestimmung des Seins ihrer Gegenstände" (the final determination of the sense of the being of their objects). What modern philosophy longed for, in Husserl's account, is a field (transcendental consciousness) and a critical science of it. Marion makes it the liberation of presence from every condition. The phrase "sans aucune condition ni réserve" runs against Ideen I's own principle, which admits what gives itself "aber auch nur in den Schranken, in denen es sich da gibt" (but also only within the limits in which it gives itself there, §24). This is selection: Marion keeps the claim that phenomenology fulfils modern philosophy and drops the critical function that made it the fulfilment. §62's "final determination of the sense of being" is passed over here. Chapter V will make it load-bearing, as phenomenology's claim to be the true ontology.

Husserl's breakthrough is ambiguous because it extends presence past all limits and thereby reproduces presence's metaphysical determination, "l'objectivité" (objectivity; R Phenomenology as such ¶3). The return to the matters at issue is compromised if they give themselves only "sous la figure de leur objectivation constituée" (in the figure of their constituted objectivation). No locus is cited, but the charge is aimed at Ideen I more than at the *Investigations*. Ideen I does say that "»Gegenstand« ist überall ein Titel für Wesenszusammenhänge des Bewußtseins" ("object" is everywhere a title for essential connections of consciousness, §145, Hua III/1 336), and it makes the region thing the "transcendental guiding thread" of constitution (§150). That supports Marion. Two things in P qualify it. Ideen I's "object" is the formal "something in general", widened in correlation with intuition: "Auch Wesenserschauung ist eben Anschauung, wie eidetischer Gegenstand eben Gegenstand ist" (seeing of essences too is intuition, just as an eidetic object is an object, §3, Hua III/1 14). And Husserl widens formal ontology to values and practical objects (§148). What Marion calls objectivity is in Ideen I a formal title, not a privileged region. Marion will grant this in chapter V ("La formalité de l'objectivité") and make the formality itself the charge. Here the charge stands undifferentiated, and the reader must not take "objectivity" for Ideen I's word: Ideen I's word is *Gegenstand*, and its width is the point of §3.

Heidegger's starting point is that phenomenology's objective does not coincide with objectivity, and the conflict now reads: does the return lead back to objectivity or to being, and does "ce qui opère la re(con)duction" (what performs the re(con)duction) act "en tant que *Je* transcendantal ou en tant que *Dasein*" (as transcendental I or as Dasein; R Phenomenology as such ¶4)? Ideen I's vocabulary differs at both ends. Its reduction is a switching off (*Ausschaltung*) and bracketing (*Einklammerung*), and what remains is a residue, "transzendentales Bewußtsein" (transcendental consciousness, §33), not an operating I. Its ego is the "pure I" (§§57, 80), retained because it is co-given with every cogitatio, and Husserl leaves "the questions of the pure I *in suspenso*" for many inquiries (§57, Hua III/1 124). The transcendental I as the agent of the reduction is the I of the *Cartesian Meditations*. Marion's parenthesis "re(con)duction" adds an agent who leads back. That is Marion's reading of the reduction as an act with a performer, the axis on which the whole book compares Husserl, Heidegger and the third reduction. It is a re-inscription of Ideen I. Applied to Ideen I, the question "transcendental I or Dasein" asks of a text whose reduction yields a region of being, and whose I is a co-given pole, which agent performs it. Husserl did not pose that question in 1913.

Heidegger himself failed: Dasein remains haunted by the I, the phenomenon of being never shows itself, and the phenomenology of the inapparent stays a programme. Phenomenology must either disappear or grant that its method depends no more on the question of being than it could on objectivity alone (R Phenomenology as such ¶5, citing SZ §83, 437). No Ideen I locus is engaged, but the dilemma presupposes one. Ideen I already makes phenomenology independent of every ontology. The extended reduction switches off formal logic as *mathesis universalis* and the material eidetic disciplines (§§59–60), and §61 forbids taking "kein einziger Lehrsatz, ja nicht einmal ein Axiom" (not a single theorem, not even an axiom) from the sciences that fall under the reduction. Marion's "second way" (a phenomenology that rests on its own protocols, before or beyond both the constituted object and the being in its being) has a Husserlian precedent in this switching off of ontologies. Chapter II (n. 4, Ideen I §153) and chapter V will cite it. The list of those who took the way (Levinas, Ricœur, Derrida, Henry) puts Ricœur, Ideen I's French translator, among them.

The closing paragraph states the method: since "in phenomenology possibility stands higher than actuality" (SZ §7, 38), the principle must be pushed to its end, even against phenomenology as it has been carried out, because a genuine thought is surpassed "en la répétant" (by repeating it), "alors même l'échec réussit" (then even failure succeeds; R Phenomenology as such ¶6). Ideen I is the "phénoménologie déjà effectuée" (phenomenology already carried out) against which the repetition will be most often exercised: the reduction of §§31–62 will be repeated in chapter II through Heidegger and in chapter VI into a third reduction. The principle of repetition is Heidegger's, and Marion turns it on Heidegger as well. Ideen I knows a weaker form of the thought. It calls itself an introduction and a beginning, and Husserl's later notes in his own copies (Hua III/2) show him already repeating the book against itself. Marion's repetition differs in kind: it keeps the method and changes what the method is for.

## I. The Breakthrough and the Enlargement

### 1 - Two Interpretations and an Enlargement

Marion opens with the year 1913. Husserl salutes the *Logical Investigations* as "a work of breakthrough" in the preface to their second edition, and in the same year he publishes Ideen I, so that the salute is a farewell (R I.1 ¶1, n. 1). Every later reading, he argues, understands 1900–1901 by what it does not yet say, so the breakthrough must be read on the sole discourse of the *Investigations*. Of the two readings strong enough to do this, Heidegger's starts from the Sixth Investigation and the givenness of being, and Derrida's from the First and the primacy of presence, leading to "le « principe des principes » de 1913" (the "principle of principles" of 1913). Marion cites the 1913 Foreword to the LU and the draft preface published by Fink, not Ideen I. Ideen I gives the strongest evidence for his "depreciation beneath the praise". Its Introduction names the pure phenomenology it opens as "dieselbe, die in den »Logischen Untersuchungen« zu einem ersten Durchbruch kam, und deren Sinn sich mir in der Fortarbeit des letzten Jahrzehnts immer tiefer und reicher erschloß" (the same that came to a first breakthrough in the *Logical Investigations*, and whose sense disclosed itself to me ever more deeply and richly over the last decade's work; Hua III/1 4). The same page denies that this phenomenology is the descriptive psychology readers took from the LU, "in vermeintlicher Übereinstimmung" (in supposed agreement) with them. Ideen I thus does what Marion describes: it grants the breakthrough and claims for itself the authority to say what it meant. Marion's method is a refusal of that authority. His reading of P on the fact is right, and the refusal is his.

Read from the Sixth, the LU lead to ontological difference; read from the First, to *différance*. Marion draws two questions from the conflict: in what, for Husserl himself and "abstraction faite du prolongement des *Ideen*" (setting aside the prolongation in the *Ideas*), does the breakthrough consist; and does the givenness Heidegger takes from the Sixth coincide with the intuition Derrida indicts in the First (R I.1 ¶2)? The first question brackets Ideen I, and the bracketing does not hold. Two paragraphs later Marion uses §24 as the "accomplishment and truth" of the LU's return to the things (¶4). In §6 the principle of all principles supplies the decisive text for givenness's priority over intuition (n. 83). Ideen I is set aside as a lens and then brought back as a witness. This is not inconsistency for its own sake. Marion's thesis is that 1913 states what 1900–1901 had done without saying so, which is Ideen I's own thesis about the LU (Hua III/1 4). The bracket concerns Ideen I's doctrines (noema, reduction, pure I); its principle is allowed to testify.

Already in 1900 Husserl names the difficulty of pure phenomenological analysis: an "orientation antinaturelle" (anti-natural orientation) of thought, a "habitus anti-naturel de la réflexion" (anti-natural habit of reflection), which takes acts rather than objects as the matter at issue. The return to "the things themselves" leads concepts back to "fully developed intuitions" (R I.1 ¶3, nn. 6–7). The 1913 draft preface says the LU method is "radically intuitive in my enlarged sense", and the 1901 *Selbstanzeige* claims "a fundamental and hitherto unaccomplished enlargement of the concepts of perception and intuition" (nn. 11–12). Ideen I presupposes this and recasts it. Its anti-natural turn is no longer reflection on acts but the suspension of the general thesis of the natural attitude (§§27–32). Its Introduction demands "a new attitude, wholly changed against the natural" (Hua III/1 5), and its acts are eidetic and, after the reduction, transcendental (Hua III/1 4–5). Ideen I also restates the anti-empiricist core of the return to the things in its own terms: "Vernünftig oder wissenschaftlich über Sachen urteilen, das heißt aber, sich nach den Sachen selbst richten" (to judge rationally or scientifically about things means to direct oneself by the things themselves, §19). Marion's reconstruction stays at the pre-reductive level that Ideen I's Introduction warns readers of the LU against, where the breakthrough is a turn from objects to acts. That is the level he chose by bracketing the *Ideas*. His conclusion (the breakthrough raises intuition, as the worker of evidence, to the sole justification of every statement) is one Ideen I would sign. Ideen I would add that intuition justifies only within the limits of what it gives (§24).

The first confirmation addresses the objection that the principle of all principles of 1913 sets phenomenology on a new course. The 1913 draft preface credits the LU with "radically professing" that principle, and for Husserl the principle "constitue l'accomplissement et la vérité" (constitutes the accomplishment and truth) of the return to the things: "que l'intuition originairement donatrice soit une source de droit de la connaissance" (that originarily giving intuition is a source of right for knowledge; R I.1 ¶4, n. 15 / Hua III/1 51, R's "Hua, III, p. 52"). The quotation is faithful to "daß jede originär gebende Anschauung eine Rechtsquelle der Erkenntnis sei" (§24) and drops only "jede" (every). The function supports Marion. §24 opens "Doch genug der verkehrten Theorien" (but enough of perverse theories) and closes the critique of empiricism (§§18–23). Its work is the one Marion assigns the breakthrough: to refuse the identification of giving acts with sensuous experience. "Also der Erfahrung substituieren wir das Allgemeinere »Anschauung«" (so for experience we substitute the more general "intuition", §20, Hua III/1 44), and "alle Anschauungsarten" (all kinds of intuition) count as "gleichwertige Rechtsquellen der Erkenntnis" (equally valid sources of right for knowledge, §20, Hua III/1 45). Two departures remain. First, §24 states the principle for every assertion, not for phenomenology alone: the natural scientist follows a "parallel principle", and the principle stands in Part I, before the epoché. Marion makes it the principle of phenomenology as such. Second, he closes the paragraph with Aristotle's νοῦς at the principle of principles. Ideen I's 1913 text gives that link no support, but Husserl's later hand does: the marginal "NB νοεῖν" on "Sehen" in §19 (Hua III/2, to Hua III/1 43), which Marion's Biemel text prints as Husserl's own (n. 83). The connection is Husserl's after 1913, not in 1913.

The second confirmation is that intuition can serve as the principle only if it is first enlarged, as the Second Investigation announces and the Sixth carries out in §45. The First Investigation is preparatory, the Sixth "the most mature", and the order of exposition runs "en zigzag" (R I.1 ¶5, nn. 18–23). Ideen I confirms the enlargement as a standing doctrine and states it in its most general form. "Auch Wesenserschauung ist eben Anschauung, wie eidetischer Gegenstand eben Gegenstand ist" (seeing of essences too is intuition, just as an eidetic object is an object), and the generalization of the two correlative concepts is "nicht ein beliebiger Einfall, sondern durch die Natur der Sachen zwingend gefordert" (not an arbitrary notion but compellingly required by the nature of things; §3, Hua III/1 14). Its note replaces the LU's "Ideation" by a "freer concept" that includes even "dark", no longer intuiting, consciousness of an essence. Another note defends "meine Lehre von der kategorialen Anschauung" (my doctrine of categorial intuition) against Külpe (§3 n., Hua III/1 16). §21 adds that "jedes urteilende Einsehen" (every judging insight) falls "unter den Begriff gebender Intuition" (under the concept of giving intuition), whose differentiations run "parallel to the logical categories" (Hua III/1 47). Marion's reconstruction of 1900–1901 is confirmed by 1913, and more strongly than he shows. One point should be marked for later. In §3 the enlargement of intuition is correlative with an enlargement of the object, so Ideen I's answer to "what does the enlarged intuition give?" is "an object in the widened sense". That answer is the "objectivity" Marion will indict.

### 2 - The Domains of Intuition

What does enlarged intuition give? The draft preface answers: "something objective which is, without being in the manner of the real", so that ideas "are, and count as objects". In 1900–1901 the seven regular solids are seven objects as truly as the seven Sages. The acts themselves must become objects, and "objectness" (*Gegenständlichkeit*) is preferred to "object" because it covers states of affairs and categorial forms. The two extensions, of intuition beyond the sensuous and of objectness beyond the real, reach "le même horizon — catégorial" (the same horizon, the categorial; R I.2 ¶1, nn. 24–28). Ideen I states the parallel as a single correlation (§3, Hua III/1 14, above), and it answers the reproach of "platonizing realism" in the same terms. Essences are objects with true being, graspable by intuition, without being real things, once object and real are kept apart "wie es in den »Logischen Untersuchungen« geschehen ist" (as was done in the *Logical Investigations*), with "Gegenstand definiert als irgend etwas" (object defined as anything whatever; §22, Hua III/1 47). The horizon Marion calls categorial is, in Ideen I, formal ontology, the eidetic science of "Gegenstand überhaupt" (object in general), whose categories are those of the "empty form" of the object (§10). This is reconstruction. Marion's "categorial horizon" is Ideen I's formal region, read back into the LU. Ideen I insists that the formal region "is not coordinate with the material regions" (§10). That qualification will matter at the next step.

Against Kant's restriction of intuition to the sensuous, Marion finds three moments, each a new sense of the categorial, and concludes that "toute intuition est, pour Husserl, à un degré différent, catégoriale" (every intuition is, for Husserl, categorial in some degree; R I.2 ¶2). The moments are sensuous intuition fulfilling a categorial form; ideation founded on sensuous intuition, giving "*the* red"; and categorial intuition proper. Heidegger's 1925 gloss ("I see first, universally: there is a house") makes the "as" of the house precede and enable the seeing of a house (n. 37). No Ideen I locus is cited. The founding of the seeing of essences on individual intuition is restated in §3 (Hua III/1 14–15). The conclusion runs against Ideen I's distinctions. Red and house are material essences; categorial forms are formal. Ideen I separates the two strictly: logical categories belong to the formal region, red to the material (§§9–10), and the formal region "ist also doch nicht etwas den materialen Regionen Koordiniertes" (is not something coordinate with the material regions, §10, Hua III/1 26). LU VI makes the same cut between sensuous and categorial concepts. Marion's "every intuition is categorial" folds the material essence into the categorial and Ideen I's formal region into its material regions. It is a radicalization of the LU's enlargement against the distinction 1913 draws, and Heidegger's "as" is the mediator through which it passes. The thesis prepares chapter V's charge that Husserl's object is formal through and through. That charge needs the material essences to drop out, and here they are already absorbed.

The last step is categorial intuition proper (LU VI §45). From the homogeneity of the fulfilling function, the "is" of "gold is yellow" demands an intuition of its own, and its absence is felt when the "is" goes unfulfilled. Categorial intuition is then a case of "the great class of acts in which something appears as 'actual' and as 'given in person'", so that "l'intuition résulte de la donation sans exception" (intuition results from givenness without exception; R I.2 ¶3, nn. 38–40). Ideen I keeps the doctrine (§3 n., Hua III/1 16) but no longer lets the "is" wait on a categorial intuition. The "is" of a positing becomes a noematic being-character (*Seinscharakter*), the correlate of the doxic thesis: what appears perceptually has "den Charakter des »wirklich« seienden schlechthin" (the character of "actually" being simply; §103, Hua III/1 239). What fulfils is now the noematic sense in the mode of its fullness (§132; Commentary P on §132 agrees). Marion's sentence "intuition results from givenness" reads the LU through his thesis. The text he cites says only that categorial intuition belongs to the acts in which something appears as given. In the LU the givenness is the act's achievement, the appearing of an object "as given in person". Marion's inversion, by which the act is what givenness makes possible, is announced here before it is argued (§6).

The Kantian limit is transgressed in several ways "pour un seul motif : l'exigence phénoménologique de la donation en présence de tout phénomène, sans reste ni réserve" (for a single motive: the phenomenological requirement that every phenomenon be given in presence, without remainder or reserve; R I.2 ¶4). The transition sets up the verdict of §3. Ideen I has its own Kant, used to set a limit rather than to break one. Adequate givenness of a thing is in principle impossible "in abgeschlossener Erscheinung" (in a closed appearance), but its perfect givenness is prefigured "als »Idee« (im Kantischen Sinn)" (as an "Idea" in the Kantian sense), an infinite continuum of appearings whose infinity "prinzipiell nicht gegeben sein kann" (in principle cannot be given; §143, Hua III/1 331). Commentary P reports this correctly. The remainder Marion denies is in Ideen I an essential law of thing-givenness. Marion is right about the motive of the enlargement and wrong, for 1913, about its result. Ideen I extends givenness to every region (§138, Hua III/1 321) and keeps in every transcendent region an unfillable horizon.

### 3 - The Accomplishment of Presence

If metaphysics is the uninterrogated primacy of bringing beings into presence, and that bringing is done by intuition, metaphysics is never so accomplished as in the LU, which open "universal intuition", "wooden iron" to many ears (R I.3 ¶1, n. 41). The Göttingen lectures of 1907 list the modes of authentic givenness and conclude "überall ist die Gegebenheit" (givenness is everywhere; n. 42, *Idee der Phänomenologie*, Hua II 74). Husserl calls for teams and generations of workers. A 1925 text demands a "Gesamtanschauung der Welt" (overall intuition of the world; n. 44, Hua IX 88). Intuition "ne respecte rien" (respects nothing) and pours the world into presence "sans retrait, sans reste, sans retenue" (without withdrawal, without remainder, without restraint). Ideen I is the systematic form of the 1907 list: "Jeder Region und Kategorie prätendierter Gegenstände entspricht ... eine Grundart von originär gebendem Bewußtsein" (to every region and category of putative objects there corresponds a basic kind of originarily giving consciousness, §138, Hua III/1 321). On universality P supports Marion. On "sans reste" it does not. §138 makes inadequacy and the possibility of "explosion" of perception essential to the thing (Commentary P on §138), §143 makes total givenness an Idea, and §44 makes the being of consciousness given precisely without appearing: "Das Erlebnis stellt sich ... nicht dar" (the experience does not present itself in appearances, Hua III/1 93). Marion's "Gesamtanschauung" comes from 1925, and his "barbarism" of presence is an image of the telos §143 describes. Read against Ideen I, the paragraph names a telos as an accomplishment. Marion concedes as much when he calls the enterprise "programmatic" and the task "almost truly infinite": that is §143's infinity under another name.

The breakthrough opens only onto the metaphysical accomplishment of presence, and Marion rejoins Derrida's conclusion by way of Nietzsche. The fragment "How I recognize my own kind" reveals philosophy's "hidden" history, as Husserl makes phenomenology "la « nostalgie secrète *(geheime)* de toute la philosophie moderne »" (the "secret (*geheime*) nostalgia of all modern philosophy"; R I.3 ¶2, n. 46 / §62, Hua III/1 134). Nietzsche's Dionysian Yes to the world "without subtracting, excepting or selecting" answers the principle of all principles, which accepts what is offered "originairement dans l'« intuition » (pour ainsi dire dans son effectivité charnelle), ... simplement comme ce qu'il se donne, mais aussi seulement dans les bornes dans lesquelles il se donne là" (originarily in "intuition" (so to speak in its fleshly actuality), ... simply as what it gives itself as, but also only within the limits in which it gives itself there; n. 48 / Hua III/1 51). Husserl says "not more", Nietzsche "not less": "Rien n'est qui n'apparaisse, rien n'apparaît qui ne soit" (nothing is that does not appear, nothing appears that is not). The quotation of §24 is exact. "Charnelle" for "leibhaft" (in bodily presence, Husserl's standard word for perceptual self-presence, softened by "sozusagen", so to speak) draws the principle toward the flesh and the Dionysian body; the argument does not rest on it. The departure is in the limits clause. In §24 it restricts the right that intuition confers: an assertion may claim nothing beyond what is given, and as given. It is the anti-speculative half of the principle, and its target is theory (§24 ¶1: no theory can mislead us, since every theory draws its truth from the originary givens). Marion makes it the measure of a totality, the "ni plus" that, joined to Nietzsche's "ni moins", gives exact and complete presence. That is a re-inscription. The formula "nothing is that does not appear" also fails as a statement of Ideen I. There the absolute being, consciousness, does not appear in the sense of presenting itself through appearances (§44, Hua III/1 93), and the real is "unity of sense" for consciousness (§55, Hua III/1 120), not appearance as such. The further text Marion adds, that "all ontologies fall under the reduction" and pure phenomenology "contains" them (n. 50), is Ideen III (Hua V 76–77), not Ideen I.

Marion's answer to the first question stands in outline: the breakthrough accomplishes the metaphysics of presence by enlarging intuition until, like Nietzsche's great Amen, it brings all beings into presence (R I.3 ¶3). Ideen I has entered the argument at two points, the secret longing of §62 and the principle of §24, and at both Marion has kept the wording and changed the function. The longing for a critical science has become a longing for unconditional presence. The limit on the right of intuition has become the measure of total presence. The verdict holds for the *Investigations* as Marion reads them through their 1913 and 1925 self-interpretations. Ideen I does not confirm it, since the text has already moved the limit of presence into the essence of the thing (§143) and taken the being of consciousness out of appearing (§44).

### 4 - A Misconstrual of Signification?

Is the accomplishment of the primacy of intuition enough to fold the breakthrough back into metaphysics? Marion makes two remarks. The category "metaphysics of presence" is Heidegger's, and Derrida uses it while rejecting Heidegger's reading of the LU without justifying the split use (R I.4 ¶1, n. 51). Derrida's LU go to the edge of presence by freeing signification from intuition, then retreat. Derrida needs two decisions: to reduce signification to the sign, and to suppose that intuition alone brings into presence. No Ideen I locus is cited. One is conspicuously absent. Derrida's case for the subordination of meaning to sense and vision rests in large part on Ideen I §124: the layer of expression "ist ... nicht produktiv" (is not productive), its productivity "exhausts itself in expressing" a sense already constituted in pre-expressive acts (Hua III/1 287; Commentary P on §124 agrees). In 1913, signification (*Bedeutung*) is a stratum that mirrors a noematic sense (*Sinn*) belonging to every act, intuitive acts first of all. Marion confines the dispute to the LU and leaves aside the text in which Husserl himself made signification secondary. The silence matters. Ideen I is the strongest witness for Derrida's "telos of vision", and Marion's defence of the LU against Derrida does not reach it.

The second decision yields the aporia. If signification signifies without intuition, and intuition, enlarged to a *Gesamtanschauung*, exhausts presence, what presence is left to signification? Either Husserl contradicts himself, or he had already exceeded intuition as the last name of presence, and signification "pourrait, même sans être vue par intuition, encore *être*" (could, even without being seen by intuition, still *be*; R I.4 ¶2). Marion takes the second way. Ideen I has a third answer, which Marion's dilemma excludes. It neither subordinates sense to intuition nor sets them apart: it makes sense the noematic core common to the fulfilled and the empty act. "Diese Unterschiede gehen nicht den puren Sinn, bzw. Satz an" (these differences do not concern the pure sense or proposition), which is identical in both members; it concerns "the manner in which" the sense is fulfilled or unfulfilled (§136, Hua III/1 315–316; Commentary P on §136). Signification's presence without intuition is, for 1913, the being of the noema. The noema is neither an intuition nor a non-presence, and Husserl calls it "Sinn im Wie seiner Gegebenheitsweise" (sense in the how of its mode of givenness, §94, Hua III/1 217). Marion's second way will arrive at "givenness" as the term above both (§6). Ideen I already has a term above both, and it is the noema. Selection and silence again: the dilemma is real for the LU and was already answered differently in 1913.

### 5 - Presence without Intuition

However universal intuition becomes, it admits an exteriority, the "phenomenologically irreducible difference" that leads by a "total separation" to the opposition of intuition and signification. This opposition precedes the oppositions within intuition (sensuous and categorial, adequate and inadequate, individual and general), and signification marks off *a priori* the spaces intuition fills (R I.5 ¶1, nn. 55–57). The formula *i* + *s* = 1 must not suggest an inverse proportion, since intuition can give only to what it is not, the always prior signification. Ideen I keeps the priority and changes its bearer. The fulfilled and the empty act share one sense, and fulfilment adds "a specific reason-character" to the positing, not a new sense (§136, Hua III/1 315–316). Marion's "intuition can give only to what it is not" holds in 1913 of the noematic sense, which intuitive acts have as well. The difference he needs, signification against intuition, becomes in Ideen I a difference of modes of one sense: "Sinn im Modus seiner Fülle" (sense in the mode of its fullness, §132). Marion reconstructs the LU faithfully. His "always prior signification" is in Ideen I the sense, and its priority is not a priority over intuition.

Because an act of signification constitutes itself "without the least fulfilling or illustrating intuition having to intervene", signification is autonomous and intuition an "eventual" supplement. Mathematical idealities show that inadequacy is the rule, and the ideal object "π is transcendental" is seen "with evidence" (*ich sehe ein*, I see with insight) to be identical whether or not anyone thinks it. So "l'évidence phénoménologique s'accomplit, ici du moins, sans intuition de remplissement" (phenomenological evidence is accomplished, here at least, without fulfilling intuition; R I.5 ¶2, nn. 58–62). Ideen I contests the conclusion directly. In 1913, insight is a seeing: "jedes urteilende Einsehen, wie insbesondere das unbedingt allgemeiner Wahrheiten, [fällt] unter den Begriff gebender Intuition" (every judging insight, in particular that of unconditionally general truths, falls under the concept of giving intuition; §21, Hua III/1 47). Evidence is defined as the genus of all "seeing" referred to originary givenness, "Sehen (im extrem erweiterten Sinne)" (seeing in an extremely enlarged sense; §137, Hua III/1 318). The "blind" predication that 2 + 1 = 1 + 2 becomes evident when the state of affairs is "originär gegeben" (originarily given, §136, Hua III/1 315). Ideen I also anticipates the mathematical case: for essence-positings "die anschauliche Vergegenwärtigung ihrer vollkommenen Erfüllung [ist] gleichwertig mit der Erfüllung selbst" (the intuitive re-presentation of their perfect fulfilment is equivalent to the fulfilment itself; §140, Hua III/1 326). The insight Marion calls evidence without intuition is, for 1913, an intuition of essence. Marion reads the LU's "ich sehe ein" as evidence that does without seeing. Ideen I reads the same words as seeing in the enlarged sense. On the text of the LU Marion's reading is defensible. Against Ideen I it is a contestation that Ideen I can answer, since 1913 removed the opposition on which the argument rests.

Derrida's proof text, LU I §11, says that without "possibility" or "truth" the statement's intention is accomplished only "symbolically" and lacks, "as one usually says" (*wie man zu sagen pflegt*), the "true", "authentic" *Bedeutung*. Marion shows that the thesis is natural consciousness's, marked by the incise and the quotation marks, and that the sentences Derrida omits state Husserl's own: every statement, cognitive or not, "has its meaning and constitutes in it the signification" (R I.5 ¶3, nn. 63–64). The point concerns the LU, and Marion's reading of §11 is careful. Ideen I bears on it as a check on both sides. It keeps Marion's point: the sense is identical in the blind and the insightful act (§136). It also gives Derrida a ground the LU do not give him: the reason-character, and with it truth, belongs to a positing "dann und nur dann" (then and only then) when it rests on a fulfilled, originarily giving sense (§136, Hua III/1 316; Commentary P agrees), and "truth" is the correlate of the perfect reason-character of the primal doxa (§139). In 1913 a meaning without intuition keeps its sense and lacks its right. Derrida's "true meaning-to-say" is in Ideen I the rational right of the positing, and on that point Ideen I sides with the "on a coutume de dire" that Marion assigns to natural consciousness. Both readers find their thesis in 1913, each in one layer of the distinction.

Derrida's misreading has its motive in LU I §26: essentially occasional expressions (*I*, *this*) take their signification from the living speech and its intuitive circumstances. Marion answers that such expressions are exceptions, essentially non-expressive; that the LU's I is a mere "complex of experiences" with no ideal signification; and that LU VI §5 Addendum partly restores the "indicated signification" (R I.5 ¶4, nn. 65–69). The second argument rests on a premise Husserl withdrew in 1913. Ideen I posits a pure I as "a peculiar, not constituted transcendence in immanence", co-given with every cogitatio and exempt from switching off (§57, Hua III/1 124), and §80 relates every experience to it. The second edition of the LU, which Marion cites, appeared the same year. Marion reads the First Investigation's "I" in the 1901 sense while citing the 1913 text. From 1913 the *I* that LU I §26 treats as indexical stands over against a pure I, which apart from its "modes of relation" is "völlig leer an Wesenskomponenten" (wholly empty of essential components; §80, Hua III/1 178). Marion's first and third arguments stand. The second holds for 1901 only, and it does not affect the conclusion that the occasional expression is not the paradigm of expression.

Marion's thesis is that signification in the LU gives itself evidently without depending on fulfilling intuition: "we find it there". It owes nothing to the sign either, and it has a "content", "ideal content", "intentional sense". "Quel mode de la présence *sui generis* se déploie lorsque la signification, d'elle-même et d'elle seule, se présente ?" (what mode of presence *sui generis* unfolds when signification, of itself and by itself alone, presents itself?; R I.5 ¶5, nn. 70–71). Ideen I's answer to this question is the noema. Husserl takes the LU's "intentional content" and "sense" into the noematic sense, the "Vermeinte" (what is meant) with its determinate content (§132, Hua III/1 305), and he names its mode: not a real (*reell*) moment of the experience but an intentional one (§§88, 97), which is "in the how of its mode of givenness" (§94). Marion asks the question as though no answer existed in Husserl. In §6 he will answer it with "givenness", having first said that the noesis-noema correlation, "dominante dans les *Ideen*" (dominant in the *Ideas*), must not be hastily found in the LU (R I.6 ¶3). The noema is set aside because it is the doctrine of the *Ideas*, but it is also the closest Husserlian candidate for the presence Marion seeks. This is the chapter's main silence about P.

### 6 - The Evidence of Givenness

The Sixth Investigation confirms the First: every signification "can be thought completely without the least correlative intuition"; the signifying intention "is given first, and given for itself"; there are "countless significations that can never come to expression". Signification appears in evidence "as one and the same", and since "phénoménologiquement, l'être se réduit à l'évidence" (phenomenologically, being reduces to evidence), independent significations are "wirklich seiende Bedeutungen" (actually existing significations; R I.6 ¶1, nn. 72–76). The middle premise is Marion's, offered as phenomenology's. Its nearest Husserlian statement is Ideen I §142, which Marion does not cite: "wahrhaft seiender Gegenstand" (truly existing object) and an object to be posited "in einer ursprünglichen vollkommenen Vernunftthesis" (in an originary perfect reason-thesis) are equivalent correlates (Hua III/1 329; Commentary P on §142). That equivalence is not a reduction of being to evidence. It is a correlation of essence between the idea of true being and the idea of a possible adequate givenness, and in the case of things Husserl makes the givenness an Idea (§143). Marion's "being reduces to evidence" radicalizes §142 into an identity. The step lets signification "exist" because it is evident, and that existence is what §6 needs. Ideen I gives the correlation, not the identity.

Marion now envisages a second enlargement: signification is enlarged until it exists as an autonomous being, "the domain of signification is far wider than that of intuition" (n. 77). The two surpluses collide, and evidence seems to flow from two sources. The late self-interpretation sharpens the conflict: in the *Krisis* the LU are said to have made "evidence" a problem, "freed it from the pre-eminence of scientific evidence and enlarged it to universal originary self-giving" (zur allgemeinen originalen Selbstgebung erweitert; R I.6 ¶2, n. 79, Hua VI 237). Is the enlargement of evidence that of intuition or that of signification? Marion finds it undecided. Ideen I decides. Faced with naming the highest genus of reason-consciousness, Husserl says one may extend either "Sehen" (seeing) or "Einsehen", "Evidenz" (insight, evidence), and takes "Evidenz" for "the most general concept", defined as "das auf originäre Gegebenheit bezogene »Sehen« (im extrem erweiterten Sinne)" (the "seeing" referred to originary givenness, in an extremely enlarged sense; §137, Hua III/1 318). "Evidenz — das Wort in unserem erweiterten Sinne verstanden" (evidence, the word taken in our enlarged sense) is then distributed over every region (§138, Hua III/1 321). The 1913 enlargement of evidence is an enlargement of seeing to everything originarily given, and nothing in Ideen I gives signification a separate source of evidence. Marion's undecidability is real for the LU. The *Krisis* sentence reflects the decision of 1913, which Marion's bracketing of the *Ideas* keeps out of view.

The way out comes from the *Krisis* note: the breakthrough of 1898 was "this universal correlational *a priori* of the object of experience and the modes of givenness". Marion urges that it be identified neither with noesis-noema ("dominant in the *Ideen*") nor with intuition-intention, but with "the correlation between appearing and what appears as such" (n. 80). Givenness precedes intuition and signification, which are its modes. In 1913 the principle "ne privilégie l'intuition qu'en tant qu'il l'interprète d'abord comme « originellement donatrice »" (privileges intuition only insofar as it first interprets it as "originarily giving") and even admits a formulation by givenness: "Qu'il nous soit évident, que toute théorie ne pourrait à son tour tirer sa vérité que de données (ou : donations ? *Gegebenheiten*) originaires" (let it be evident to us that every theory could in turn draw its truth only from originary data (or: givennesses? *Gegebenheiten*); R I.6 ¶3, n. 83 / Hua III/1 51). The first claim is §19 almost word for word: seeing "hat rechtgebende Funktion nur, weil und soweit sie originär gebende ist" (has a right-giving function only because and insofar as it is originarily giving; Hua III/1 43). That is P's strongest support for Marion's priority of giving over intuition. The second claim rests on a rendering. "Gegebenheiten" in the plural, the sources from which a theory draws (*schöpfen*) its truth, are givens; Ideen I uses the plural so, as in "ganze Klassen von echten Gegebenheiten" (whole classes of genuine givens, §20, Hua III/1 45). "Donations" turns the givens into the event of giving, and the question mark shows that Marion knows it. In §19 the giving belongs to the seeing ("originär gebendes Bewußtsein", originarily giving consciousness), not to what is seen. Marion's conclusion, that givenness gives intuition and signification as its modes, transfers the participle from the act to the phenomenon. The noema is set aside for the same reason. In Ideen I the modes of givenness are noematic, "Sinn im Wie seiner Gegebenheitsweise" (§94, Hua III/1 217), so the "more essential" correlation Marion draws from the *Krisis* is, in 1913, the noesis-noema correlation itself. Marion's correlation of appearing and what appears is Ideen I's noema, read without the noesis.

A text of the Sixth Investigation shows the object constituted "as 'given'" in fulfilling acts, given "on the same mode as it is meant by the signification", and the play of quotation marks marks two givennesses, one in signification and one in intuition. "Es gibt also … Bedeutungen" (there are significations). Presence is therefore givenness, and Derrida's conclusion stands while his argument falls: presence triumphs in signification as in intuition, and Derrida is "too little radical". Why then did Heidegger see a beginning in the LU (R I.6 ¶4, nn. 85–89)? Ideen I says what the paragraph says, in its own terms. The object meant and the object given are one object with one sense, given in different modes. Fulfilment is a "covering" (*Deckung*) between acts of the same sense and different reason-value, "die unvollkommene Gegebenheitsweise verwandelt sich in die vollkommene" (the imperfect mode of givenness turns into the perfect one; §140, Hua III/1 324). For Husserl, "given" applies to every mode of having the object, the empty included. Marion's reading of the quotation marks is borne out by 1913. His "unique originary givenness" is a name for what Ideen I calls the identical sense across modes of givenness. The question it leaves open is whether that name adds anything. Marion's answer, in §7 and chapter II, is that it adds the question Husserl did not ask.

### 7 - Givenness as Question

Heidegger twice acknowledged the breakthrough as the condition of *Sein und Zeit*: in 1925, where categorial intuition "gives a ground", and in 1973 at Zähringen, where Husserl's *tour de force* put being into presence "phenomenally present in the category", and "being" was no longer a concept (R I.7 ¶1, nn. 90–92). Marion's reading is that the 1973 text calls categorial intuition an *analogy* with sensuous intuition, reached "by the path of analogy" because the categorial is "just as given" as the sensuous. Being is given, and categorial intuition is only a crude name, "la finalement très fruste analogie" (the finally very crude analogy), for an abyss of givenness that Husserl names without thinking. Heidegger destroys categorial intuition and never mentions it again in *Sein und Zeit*. Ideen I has a sentence on the analogy, and it denies what the 1973 seminar asserts. The seeing of essences is "das Analogon des sinnlichen Wahrnehmens und nicht des Einbildens" (the analogue of sensuous perception and not of imagining; §23, Hua III/1 50), and yet "hier liegt nicht eine bloß äußerliche Analogie vor, sondern radikale Gemeinsamkeit" (here there is no merely external analogy but a radical community; §3, Hua III/1 14), since the generalization of intuition and object is "compellingly required by the nature of things". For Husserl in 1913, the giving that sensuous and essential seeing share is what grounds the analogy, so the analogy cannot be a crude cover for the giving. Heidegger's 1973 reconstruction ("on the path of analogy") is mediation. Marion receives the LU through it and does not test it against Ideen I. Read against §3, the "abyss" Marion finds under the analogy is the "radical community" Husserl names above it. Husserl names it as the community of giving acts and their objects, not as a question. On that point Marion's charge stands: Husserl names the giving without asking what giving is.

The 1925 course confirms it. Categorial intuition is intuition, the "plain grasp of what is given in bodily presence (*von leibhaftig Gegebenem*) as it shows itself", and bodily presence is "a distinguished mode of the self-givenness of a being". Categorial acts "give objectness" (*sie geben Gegenständlichkeit*), and objectivity in the widest sense is "far richer than the reality of a thing". The breakthrough is "the requirement of a freeing of the ground" (R I.7 ¶2, nn. 93–96). The vocabulary through which Heidegger reads the Sixth Investigation in GA 20 is largely that of Ideen I: "leibhaftig" is the "leibhaften Wirklichkeit" of §24 (Hua III/1 51) and the bodily givenness of the perceived thing in §§39–43. The thesis that objectness is wider than reality, and that reality becomes intelligible only from it, restates §3 (Hua III/1 14) and the formal ontology of §10. This is a second mediation. Marion's "givenness" in 1925 is Ideen I's giving intuition as Heidegger restated it, applied to the LU. His conclusion, that the breakthrough is the enlargement of presence understood as objectivity "selon la mesure excessive de la donation" (by the excessive measure of givenness), rests partly on 1913's correlation of intuition and object. Chapter V will charge the same correlation with confining givenness to objectness. Here Marion takes it from Heidegger as liberation; in chapter V he will find it in Husserl as limitation.

Heidegger's privileging of givenness saves his reading from Derrida and, to a degree, saves Husserl. Husserl never says being is intuited by an "intuition of being": in fulfilment, "being in the sense of truth" is realized and the little word *is* "is itself given, or at least presumed given", in the object's "actual being-given (*aktuelles Gegebensein*)". And Husserl always infers categorial intuition by analogy, as "an act that gives it, the analogue of ordinary sensuous intuition" (R I.7 ¶3, nn. 97–98). Husserl, dazzled, "manages" the excess of givenness without questioning it, perhaps from fright. Ideen I has a more determinate doctrine of the *is* than the Sixth Investigation, and Marion does not consider it. The *is* of a positing is a noematic being-character (§103), and "being in the sense of truth" becomes the equivalence of "truly existing object" with what can be posited in an originary perfect reason-thesis (§142, Hua III/1 329). Ideen I does not leave the givenness of the *is* anonymous. It assigns it to the doxic thesis and its rational right, and it ties true being to the idea of adequate givenness. Marion's "anonymous givenness of the *is*" holds for the LU, which Marion reads through Heidegger. For 1913 it overlooks a thesis, the being-character as noematic correlate of belief, which Marion's chapter II will meet.

Husserl could not answer the question "quoi donc se donne ?" (what then gives itself?), because he never heard it as a question: "Que signifie donner ... comment donc penser que tout ce qui est ne soit qu'autant qu'il est donné ?" (what does giving mean ... how are we to think that everything that is, is only insofar as it is given?). Heidegger, seeing that the breakthrough is the enlargement of givenness, asks what givenness means and so does not reduce it to presence too early. Two prerequisites follow: a step back that makes givenness worthy of question (a reduction), and recognition that in givenness the being of what appears is at stake. The single question is whether the reduction leads phenomenology to see being as a phenomenon (R I.7 ¶4). Marion restricts the silence to the *Investigations* ("du moins dans les *Recherches logiques*", at least in the *Logical Investigations*), and the restriction is exact. Ideen I does not ask what giving means, but it asks the second half of Marion's question and answers it. How is everything that is only insofar as it is given? "Alle realen Einheiten sind »Einheiten des Sinnes«" (all real unities are "unities of sense"), and sense-unities presuppose sense-giving consciousness (§55, Hua III/1 120); the being of the transcendent is "bloß phänomenales Sein" (merely phenomenal being), that of the immanent "absolutes Sein" (§44). Ideen I does not leave the relation of being to givenness unthought. It thinks it as the relativity of reality to absolute consciousness and calls the result transcendental idealism. Marion's two prerequisites, the reduction and the question of being, are Ideen I's two themes (Part II and §§42–55). Chapter II will ask whether Husserl's answer is a question of being at all.

The chapter has used Ideen I less often than it relies on it. Three loci carry Marion's argument: §62's secret longing, which frames phenomenology as the fulfilment of metaphysics; §24's principle, made both the LU's own principle and, through the clause of limits, the measure of total presence; and §§19 and 24 again, read as giving priority to givenness over intuition. The first two keep Husserl's words and change their function. The longing for a critical science becomes the longing for presence without condition, and the restriction on the right of intuition becomes the measure of a totality. The third is Marion's strongest Husserlian ground ("rechtgebende Funktion nur, weil und soweit sie originär gebende ist", §19). Even there, his rendering of the plural givens as "donations" and his transfer of giving from the act to the phenomenon are his own moves, and the Greek gloss that links the principle to Aristotle's νοῦς is Husserl's later hand, printed by Biemel as 1913. Where Ideen I could have answered Marion's questions, the chapter sets it aside: the noema answers his question about the sui generis presence of signification, §137 decides the enlargement of evidence he finds undecided, and §3 meets the "crude analogy" with a "radical community". This silence is method, not oversight. By bracketing the *Ideas*, Marion keeps open in the LU a question that Ideen I had closed by its doctrines of noematic sense and absolute consciousness. Givenness can be shown as the unasked question of 1900–1901 because the answer of 1913 is held back until chapter II. His reading of the *Investigations* holds against their own text, and his account of what Husserl "never asked" holds for them alone. Against Ideen I, the chapter's verdict on Husserl, that he manages givenness without thinking it, still has to be earned.

## II. The Entity and the Phenomenon

### 1 - Putting Ontology Out of Play

The chapter opens on two sentences: Heidegger in 1925, "scientific ontology is nothing other than phenomenology", against Husserl in 1912, "an sich ... ist Ontologie nicht Phänomenologie" (in itself ontology is not phenomenology; R II.1 ¶1, n. 1). Marion will ask, of phenomenology, of the phenomenon and of the entity, whether each reaches or depends on an understanding of being. Husserl's sentence is from the 1912 draft published as Ideen III, Beilage I (Hua V 129), not from Ideen I. Ideen I holds a more divided position, and Marion's flat opposition simplifies it. On one side, Ideen I separates phenomenology from every ontology. The ontologies are eidetic sciences of objects, formal (§10) or regional (§9). Phenomenology is the descriptive eidetics of transcendentally pure experiences (§75), and the extended reduction switches off formal logic and the material eidetics (§§59–60). On the other side, phenomenology performs for every science "die letzte Sinnesbestimmung des Seins ihrer Gegenstände" (the final determination of the sense of the being of their objects, §62, Hua III/1 134), and its "Herrschaftsbereich" (sphere of dominion) extends "über alle die anderen Wissenschaften ..., die sie doch ausschaltet" (over all the other sciences, which it nonetheless switches off; §76, Hua III/1 159). Ideen I's phenomenology is not ontology, and it claims authority over the sense of being of every ontology's objects. Marion's first question (does phenomenology reach an understanding of being?) has in Ideen I the answer "yes, as the clarification of the sense of being", the answer chapter V will exploit. Here Marion sets the scene with the half that suits Heidegger's opposition.

Phenomenology's overstepping of ontology belongs with its final self-determination as transcendental idealism (*Cartesian Meditations* §41, the 1930 *Nachwort*). Ideen I's last section confirms it: "les connexions entre les phénoménologies constitutives et les ontologies formelles et matérielles correspondantes *n'impliquent aucunement que les premières fondent les secondes*" (the connections between constitutive phenomenologies and the corresponding formal and material ontologies *in no way imply that the former ground the latter*), and "the phenomenologist does not judge ontologically" when he recognizes in an ontological concept the index of a constitutive connection (R II.1 ¶2, n. 4 / Hua III/1 359, R's "Hua, III, p. 379"). Under the reduction, "toutes les ontologies ... tombent sous le coup de la réduction" (all ontologies fall under the reduction; Ideen III), the belief in being is put out of action (*Nachwort*), and "de l'être, en régime de réduction, il n'est plus question" (under the regime of the reduction, being is no longer in question). The quotation from §153 inverts Husserl's sentence. The 1913 text reads "daß in diesen Zusammenhängen ... nichts von einer Begründung der ersteren durch die letzteren liegt" (that in these connections there lies nothing of a grounding of the former, the phenomenologies, *by* the latter, the ontologies; Hua III/1 359). Boyce Gibson's 1931 translation agrees: "no hint of a grounding of the former on the latter". Husserl is saying that phenomenology does not borrow its ground from ontology. The ontological concept is only a "Leitfaden" (guiding thread) for intuitive demonstrations "die ihr Recht und ihre Geltung rein in sich selbst tragen" (which carry their right and validity purely in themselves). Marion's version says that phenomenology does not ground ontology. His paragraph rests on that reading, since ontological right and validity "ne rejaillissent en aucune manière sur l'ontologie elle-même" (in no way reflect back on ontology itself) and the meeting "reste un croisement sans jamais devenir une alliance" (remains a crossing without ever becoming an alliance). Ideen I says the reverse of that conclusion: phenomenology gives every ontology its final sense-determination (§62), and constitutive phenomenology treats every ontological concept as the index of constitutive connections of consciousness (§§149–153; Commentary P agrees). Whether the inversion is Ricœur's or Marion's cannot be settled from the corpus; Marion's argument is the one that uses it. The rest of the paragraph meets Ideen I too. Ideen I does not say that being "is no longer in question" under the reduction. It says that the epoché negates nothing (§32, Hua III/1 65), that "dem vollgültigen Sein der Welt ... [ist] so wenig etwas abgezogen" (nothing is subtracted from the fully valid being of the world; §55, Hua III/1 121), and that what is bracketed "ist nicht von der phänomenologischen Tafel weggewischt" (is not wiped from the phenomenological slate) but marked with an index and taken back by "eine umwertende Vorzeichenänderung" (a revaluing change of sign; §76, Hua III/1 159). Marion's "other world, without vestige or restoration of the old" is built from Ideen III and the 1930 *Nachwort*. The 1913 text he cites first, §153, says something else even before the inversion.

### 2 - The Method of Ontology

Heidegger's first turn contests the prohibition and thereby admits it. *Sein und Zeit* §7 and the 1927 course make phenomenology the method of ontology, and ontology the scientific form of philosophy. Phenomenology no longer constitutes itself as the science that philosophy awaits, as for Husserl, for whom it is "la nostalgie cachée *(geheime Sehnsucht)* de toute la philosophie moderne" (the hidden nostalgia of all modern philosophy; R II.2 ¶1, n. 14 / §62, Hua III/1 134). It becomes ancillary, a method that serves the restoration of ontology. The §62 sentence appears here in its third French form: "nostalgie" in the introductory chapter, "nostalgie secrète" in I.3, now "nostalgie cachée". The function Marion assigns it here is closer to §62 than in the introductory chapter. Phenomenology as the final science, critical for all others "und zugleich für sich selbst" (and at the same time for itself, Hua III/1 134), is Husserl's claim. Marion's contrast between autonomous science (Husserl) and method (Heidegger) is a fair reconstruction of the two positions. One qualification: Ideen I also treats phenomenology as methodologically reflexive, a science that must think through its own method as part of its subject matter (§§63–65; the auxiliary Commentary on Parts III–IV covers §65, "the back-reference of phenomenology to itself"). Heidegger's "method" is a reversal of Husserl's priority. Husserl already had the concept of a reflexive method.

The violence done to Husserl's definition should not hide the prior question: where does Heidegger see the ontological charge of phenomenology decided? In 1925: phenomenological research is "interpretation of the entity in the direction of its being", and Husserl has missed the question of the being of the intentional. The way of being of acts "remains undetermined", because Husserl's guiding idea is that of an absolute science, a return "to the traditional idea of philosophy" and not to the things themselves (R II.2 ¶2, nn. 15–23). Marion forestalls an objection with Ideen I. Husserl does pose "the unbridgeable essential difference" between the phenomenal being of the transcendent and the absolute being of the immanent (§§42–44; the phrase itself is §43's and names the difference between perception and image or sign representation, Hua III/1 89–90), but he defines the being of consciousness as "eine Sphäre absoluter Position" (a sphere of absolute position) and "absolute Wirklichkeit" (absolute actuality; §46, nn. 24–25). That is, from position, permanent presence and objective subsistence, "celle même qui permettait à Kant de définir l'être du monde" (the very notion by which Kant defined the being of the world). The two quotations are 1913 text (§46, Hua III/1 98), and the departure lies in "position". In §46 the word means a *positing*, the thesis of reflection. In the absolute sphere "hat Widerstreit, Schein, Anderssein keinen Raum" (conflict, semblance, being-otherwise have no room), and my lived actuality is given "durch eine unbedingte, schlechthin unaufhebliche Setzung" (by an unconditioned, utterly uncancellable positing). The necessity in question is "die Notwendigkeit eines Faktums" (the necessity of a fact, Hua III/1 98), the factual being of the flowing present experience, not permanence. Marion's Kant, for whom being is the absolute position of a thing, supplies a sense the sentence does not have. A second qualification touches the charge that a region of being must be a position, hence an objectivity. Ideen I anticipates that charge and answers it. Consciousness and the real are both called "seiend" and "Gegenstand", but "nur nach den leeren logischen Kategorien gleich benannt" (named alike only according to the empty logical categories). "Zwischen Bewußtsein und Realität gähnt ein wahrer Abgrund des Sinnes" (between consciousness and reality gapes a veritable abyss of sense; §49, Hua III/1 105). Marion's own note 24 cites the clause that settles the matter: the difference of modes of being is "ein prinzipieller Unterschied der Gegebenheitsart" (a difference in principle of the mode of givenness, §42). Ideen I's difference of being is drawn from givenness, from shading against non-shading appearance (§§42, 44). That is closer to Marion's own programme than to Heidegger's charge of objectivity, and the paragraph, which voices Heidegger, does not note it. Heidegger's verdict ("unphänomenologisch") is reported with assent. As a reading of Ideen I it is mediation: Marion takes §§42–49 through GA 20 §§11–13, which cites §76 (Hua III/1 159) for the "radikalste aller Seinsunterscheidungen" (the most radical of all distinctions of being) and then denies that it is one.

The turn shows in one index, phenomenology seeking ontology by becoming method, and in one displacement: the phenomenological concerns no longer the knowledge of phenomena but the knowledge of their mode of exhibition, phenomenality (R II.2 ¶3). How phenomenality differs from the phenomenon "sinon verbalement" (other than verbally) requires a debate between Husserl's and Heidegger's definitions of the phenomenon. Ideen I's definition is not single. "Phenomenon" there names the transcendentally purified experience, an "irreal" object (Introduction, Hua III/1 6), and also the thing as appearing (the "bloß phänomenales Sein" of the transcendent, §44). Ideen I already has a word for something like phenomenality: the noematic "Sinn im Wie seiner Gegebenheitsweise" (sense in the how of its mode of givenness, §94, Hua III/1 217), or the noematic "Erscheinungsweisen" (modes of appearing). Marion's "mode of exhibition" (*Art der Aufweisung*) is Heidegger's. The question he poses is one Ideen I answered with the noema, as at R I.5 ¶5. The next section sets that answer aside a second time, by determining the Husserlian phenomenon from the *Logical Investigations*' "Erlebnis".

### 3 - The "Reduced Phenomenon," Reduced to Present Objectivity

Heidegger repeats that phenomenology's principle is the return to the things themselves, and that Husserl does not keep it. In the *Ideas* it competes with another, "the principle of all principles", posed "avant même la réduction (§ 31-32)" (before the reduction itself, §§31–32). Marion quotes §24 again, now ending "seulement dans les limites, à l'intérieur desquelles il se donne [*comme étant*] là" (only within the limits inside which it gives itself [*as being*] there), together with its "negative formulation", the "norm" of §59: "ne rien revendiquer *(in Anspruch zu nehmen)*, que nous ne puissions nous rendre essentiellement évident à la conscience même, dans la pure immanence" (to claim nothing that we cannot make essentially evident to ourselves in consciousness itself, in pure immanence; R II.3 ¶1, n. 28 / Hua III/1 51, 126). With Heidegger's 1962 commentary, the principle draws its unshakable legitimacy "from transcendental subjectivity, already presupposed as the matter of philosophy". Three points bear on P. First, the bracket "[*comme étant*]" is Marion's. The German has "in denen es sich da gibt" (in which it gives itself there), and the gloss writes the being of the given into a principle that speaks only of its givenness. Second, the §59 norm concerns premises. In its context (the switching off of pure logic as *mathesis universalis*), "Nichts in Anspruch zu nehmen" means to make use of, to rely on, nothing but what can be made evident in pure immanence. Marion's "revendication", the claim that what gives itself makes upon consciousness, turns a rule for the phenomenologist into a claim exercised by the phenomenon. This is the first appearance of *Anspruch* as "revendication", the word that names the call in chapter VI. Third, the principle precedes the reduction in Ideen I, as Marion says, and §24 holds as much for the natural scientist's "parallel principle". Heidegger's claim that its legitimacy comes from presupposed transcendental subjectivity is therefore retrospective. In 1913 the principle stands before transcendental consciousness has been introduced (§33). Marion grants that Husserl sees no difference between the two principles (n. 31) and that the split appears only "from an already Heideggerian point of view". He then accepts the split. His conclusion, that the principle defines the phenomenality of phenomena as givenness but interprets givenness as effective presence to consciousness, is his own, argued in what follows.

Three remarks establish the rule of consciousness over phenomenality. (a) Phenomena are given in the present as *leibhafte Wirklichkeit* (bodily actuality). "It belongs to the mode of being of lived experience that a gaze of intuitive perception can be directed quite immediately upon every actual experience living as originary present" (§45). The pre-eminence of the present comes from consciousness itself: "*Mon* intropathie *(Einfühlen)* et *ma* conscience en général est, à titre de présent qui coule *(strömende Gegenwart)*, donnée originairement et absolument, non seulement selon l'essence, mais aussi selon l'existence" (*My* empathy and *my* consciousness in general is, *as flowing present*, given originarily and absolutely, not only according to essence but also according to existence; §46). (b) Intuition, "originally giving", is the tribunal before which appearing is authenticated (§§19, 20, 78, 79). (c) The phenomenon is defined from the *Erlebnis*, and "evidence is the *Erlebnis* of truth" (R II.3 ¶2, nn. 32–38). Remark (a) rests in part on words Husserl did not print in 1913. The §46 sentence reads "Aber mein Einfühlen und mein Bewußtsein überhaupt ist originär und absolut gegeben, nicht nur nach Essenz, sondern nach Existenz" (but my empathy and my consciousness in general is originarily and absolutely given, not only according to essence but according to existence; Hua III/1 97). "Als strömende Gegenwart" (as flowing present) is a later addition in Husserl's copy D (Hua III/2, to Hua III/1 97, line 21), which Biemel's text prints and Marion cites as Ideen I. Commentary R reproduces it as Husserl's. The step that matters, from the present of the *experience* (§45, 1913) to consciousness itself *as* present, is the interpolated step. The 1913 §45 also qualifies the present it names. Reflection grasps the experience as something "das nicht nur ist und innerhalb des wahrnehmenden Blickes dauert, sondern schon war, ehe dieser Blick sich ihm zuwendete" (which not only is and endures within the perceiving gaze but already was before the gaze turned to it; Hua III/1 95). The present of the experience is a flowing one with a retentional past that reflection always arrives after. In remark (b) Marion answers the question he left open in chapter I, "qui donne, de l'intuition ou de l'apparition ?" (which gives, intuition or appearing?), with "l'intuition sans doute" (intuition, no doubt). For Husserl that is right: §19 ties the right of seeing to its being "originär gebende" (Hua III/1 43), and §79 speaks of "die originär gebende Anschauung und ihr ureigenes Recht" (originarily giving intuition and its very own right; Hua III/1 175). It reverses the use Marion made of the same adjective at R I.6 ¶3, where "originarily giving" was read as givenness's priority over intuition. Both readings are Marion's; only the second is Husserl's. Remark (c) concerns the *Logical Investigations*. Ideen I resolves the equivocation of "Erscheinung" (appearance, as experience and as what appears) by a third term: the appearing as such belongs to the noema, which is no *reell* (really inherent) component of the experience (§97 title, §88). The phenomenon of 1913 is no longer defined "from the *Erlebnis*" in the sense Marion draws from the LU.

Consciousness thus imposes on phenomenality the reflectivity of presence, the absolute of intuition, and the ordeal of lived experience. The return to the things becomes a "return to the sources of intuition" (§147), and the phenomenon is "reduced" in two senses. "Every lived experience is a 'presently being' experience" (§110, n. 42). The high point of Husserl's meditation, the opposition of immanent and transcendent, opens only in terms of bodily givenness: "Toute choséité donnée en personne *(leibhaft gegebene)* peut, malgré cette donation en personne, aussi n'être pas, tandis que nul vécu donné en personne ... ne peut aussi n'être pas" (every thinghood given in person can, *despite this givenness in person*, also not be, while no experience given in person can also not be; §46, n. 44). Consciousness is reduced to the Cartesian substance (§49, "nulla 're' indiget ad existendum"); immanent and transcendent being "are both called 'being', 'object'" (§49, n. 47); perception "does not presentify something non-present" but grasps a self "in its bodily present" (§43, n. 48). The Husserlian phenomenon is "un phénomène plat" (a flat phenomenon; R II.3 ¶3). Four of these loci do not say in 1913 what Marion makes them say. (1) "Malgré cette donation en personne" renders "trotz dieser leibhaften Gegebenheit", another addition in copy D (Hua III/2, to Hua III/1 98, line 18). The 1913 sentence is "Alles leibhaft gegebene Dingliche kann auch nicht sein, kein leibhaft gegebenes Erlebnis kann auch nicht sein" (Hua III/1 98), as Boyce Gibson's translation confirms ("All corporeally given thing-like entities can also not be"). The interpolated "despite" sets givenness against being, which is the very contrast Marion draws from it. (2) The "presently being" sentence is not in §110 but in §111 (Hua III/1 251; Commentary P on §111 places it there), and it is introduced to the opposite effect. Because every experience can be reflected on as present, "entspricht jedem Erlebnis ... eine Serie von ideal möglichen Erinnerungsmodifikationen" (there corresponds to every experience a series of ideally possible memory modifications), each with its neutrality modification, phantasy. The sentence grounds the non-present modifications of every experience. (3) §49's "zwar beides" (certainly both) is a concession that continues "evident ist aber, daß ... nur nach den leeren logischen Kategorien gleich benannt ist" (but it is evident that what is so called on both sides is named alike only according to the empty logical categories, Hua III/1 105). Marion stops at the concession and asks whether "objectivity" must be the only face of the entity, the very question Husserl's next clause answers in the negative. (4) §43's sentence on perception refutes the image and sign theories, the claim that perception presentifies an absent thing-in-itself through a sign (Hua III/1 91). The same section and the next make the thing given only by shading, one-sidedly, with an open horizon of the unseen (§44). The non-present is built into Ideen I's perception as horizon. What §43 excludes is that perception should be a *re*-presentation of something absent. The "flat phenomenon" is a defensible verdict on the immanent sphere, where "Widerstreit, Schein, Anderssein keinen Raum" have. As a verdict on Ideen I's phenomenon of the thing it holds only for a text with §§44, 111 and 138 removed. Ideen I also says that the absolute won by the reductions "ist in Wahrheit nicht das Letzte" (is in truth not the last), but constitutes itself in a deeper sense whose "Rätsel des Zeitbewußtseins" (riddles of time-consciousness) Husserl brackets on purpose (§81, Hua III/1 182). The flatness is in part a declared methodological bracketing, not a doctrine of the phenomenon.

### 4 - From the Inapparent Phenomenon to the "Phenomenon of Being"

Can the phenomenon be understood otherwise than as permanent presence to consciousness? Heidegger's phenomenon in SZ §7, "what shows itself in itself", seems to repeat Husserl, but it differs twice. It shows itself on its own initiative, without reference to consciousness, and its counter-concept is coveredness, so that phenomenology has to let be seen what does not show itself at first (R II.4 ¶1, nn. 49–50). Marion's contrast is drawn against the "flat phenomenon" of II.3, which is the immanent sphere. Ideen I has its own counter-concept, but places it on the side of the thing. The transcendent is given through shading, with possibilities of "Auffassungsänderung, Umschlagen einer Erscheinung" (change of apprehension, reversal of an appearance) and of semblance, all of which "in der Erlebnissphäre wesensmäßig ausgeschlossen sind" (are essentially excluded in the sphere of experience; §46, Hua III/1 98). For Husserl coveredness and semblance belong to appearing as such, and absoluteness belongs to what does not appear, consciousness. Heidegger's self-showing that hides is, in Ideen I's terms, the transcendent phenomenon. The difference is real but lies elsewhere than Marion puts it. Heidegger does not admit an exception where nothing hides. Husserl's absolute sphere is that exception, and Husserl grounds it in reflection, not in a presupposed consciousness.

Marion adds Heidegger's 1925 sentences: phenomenological questioning begins in what is most self-evident, phenomena are not lying open in daylight, every phenomenological proposition can harden and conceal, and the phenomenon is an enigma. The Husserlian phenomenon is defined by evidence and stays within it. The Heideggerian one implies, in principle, the inapparent of appearing (R II.4 ¶2, nn. 51–52). No Ideen I locus is engaged. The contrast "evidence reduces appearing to presence" presupposes the reading of II.3. Ideen I's phenomenology of reason makes non-evidence a theme within evidence: the absurd, the "explosion" of perception, the motivated but empty possibility (§§138, 140, 145; Commentary P). Husserl's evidence is a mode of positing with degrees and counter-modes, not a single brightness. That does not make Husserl's phenomenon enigmatic in Heidegger's sense, but the antithesis "evidence or enigma" passes over the place where Ideen I treats the hidden as a matter for the theory of reason.

"Soviel Schein — soviel Sein" (as much semblance, so much being; GA 20, SZ §7): where the enigma of the phenomenon appears, being appears, since what is most properly hidden is "the being of the entity". "Ce phénomène 'l'être' ..., il vaut de l'élaborer" (this phenomenon "being" must be worked out). "Elaborons-le, donc, avec Heidegger et sans Husserl" (let us work it out, then, with Heidegger and without Husserl; R II.4 ¶3, nn. 53–55). Marion says outright here that he is leaving P. In Ideen I, "Schein" is what the absolute sphere excludes (§46), and the charge that the reduction turns the world into "subjektiven Schein" (subjective semblance) is rejected (§55, Hua III/1 121). Being in Ideen I is not a phenomenon. It is the correlate of a rational positing, "wahrhaft seiender Gegenstand" (truly existing object) as equivalent to what can be posited in an originary perfect reason-thesis (§142, Hua III/1 329). The phrase "phenomenon of being" has no counterpart in P, and Marion does not claim one.

The phenomenology of the inapparent (Zähringen, 1973) is required because being "is not perceivable", "nothing of an entity". Only entities can be uncovered (*Entdecktheit*); being is disclosed (*Erschlossenheit*) beforehand, and a phenomenon of being would have to be one that does not exhaust itself in presence, since it is defined by its power to refuse itself (R II.4 ¶4, nn. 56–60). Ideen I offers a counter-model to the equation Marion relies on, that what appears is an entity and what does not appear is being. Its absolute being, consciousness, is "prinzipiell nicht durch Abschattung und Erscheinung zu geben" (in principle not to be given through shading and appearance; §49, Hua III/1 105). "Das Erlebnis stellt sich ... nicht dar" (the experience does not present itself, §44, Hua III/1 93), yet it is given absolutely. In Ideen I, then, there is a being given without appearing, and it is the being on which all appearing depends. Marion's later move in chapter V, the I "hors d'être" (outside being), will use this very feature of the pure I and of consciousness, and Henry had built a phenomenology on it. Here the parallel goes unremarked, and the opposition of the flat present phenomenon to the inapparent being leaves out the term of Ideen I that is neither.

### 5 - The Two Senses of the Reduction

Against the flat phenomenon stands the "depth" of the Heideggerian one: being is to be read off the entity (*ablesen*), in the fore-sight (*Vor-sicht*) of interpretation, and the phenomenon is seen *as* entity entering presence. Phenomenon and phenomenality, entity and being are crossed by one transgression: "Sein ist das transcendens schlechthin" (being is the transcendens pure and simple; R II.5 ¶1, nn. 61–64). The paragraph is Heidegger's, read closely. Its bearing on P lies in the word "depth". Ideen I declares a depth beneath its own field: the transcendental absolute "ist ... nicht das Letzte; es ist etwas, das sich selbst in einem gewissen tiefliegenden und völlig eigenartigen Sinn konstituiert" (is not the last; it is something that constitutes itself in a certain deep-lying and wholly peculiar sense; §81, Hua III/1 182), and Ideen I sets that depth aside. The depth Husserl brackets is time-consciousness, not being. Marion's contrast between Husserl's surface and Heidegger's depth would hold if Husserl's surface were his last word. In 1913 Husserl says it is not.

Heidegger keeps the reduction and displaces it. For Husserl it is the "Rückführung" (leading back) from the natural attitude to transcendental life and its noetic-noematic experiences, in which objects are constituted as correlates (GA 24 §5). For Heidegger it is the leading back from the entity to the understanding of its being. Bracketing "takes nothing from the entity" but makes its being-character present (GA 20). Husserl's charge, made to Ingarden, that Heidegger misunderstood the reduction rests on Husserl's own blindness: he sees two terms (world-thesis and reduced phenomenon) where Heidegger sees three (world-thesis, phenomenon, sense of being), because Husserl never reaches the phenomenon "comme à un étant" (as an entity; R II.5 ¶2, nn. 65–67). Heidegger's two descriptions are good readings of Ideen I. The first restates §§86 and 135 (constitution of objects as correlates of consciousness), and the second agrees with §§32, 55 and 76: the epoché negates nothing, takes nothing from the being of the world, and returns the bracketed with an index. That second agreement tells against Marion's own account in II.1, where under the reduction "being is no longer in question". Marion's argument that Husserl sees only two terms meets in Ideen I a third that Marion does not discuss. The reduced world returns as sense: "Alle realen Einheiten sind »Einheiten des Sinnes«" (all real unities are "unities of sense"), and "Realität und Welt sind hier eben Titel für gewisse gültige Sinneseinheiten" (reality and world are here titles for certain valid unities of sense; §55, Hua III/1 120). The object returns as the noematic X, "der Gegenstand schlechthin" (the object simpliciter) as against the object "im Wie" (in the how, §131). Husserl too leads the entity back to the sense of its being. The real difference between the two reductions is what the third term is: for Husserl, sense constituted by an absolute consciousness that is "nicht selbst wieder durch Sinngebung" (not itself in turn by sense-giving, §55); for Heidegger, the sense of being on which Dasein projects. Marion's "two against three" schema is a re-inscription of that difference, and it makes Husserl's answer look like a failure to pose the question.

### 6 - The Redoubled Reduction — Dasein

The analytic of Dasein escapes the suspicion of anthropology, because it answers the demand that the question of being "be constructed" (*gestellt*). Constructing an apparatus that oversteps every entity excludes any regression behind the phenomenological attitude, and requires "une *certaine* mise entre parenthèses" (a *certain* bracketing; R II.6 ¶1, n. 68). This is Heidegger read through the reduction, and the reduction invoked is a generalized one, defined only as a step back from the entity. Ideen I's reduction is specific: the suspension of the general thesis of the natural attitude (§§30–32), whose residue is a region. Marion's "certaine" marks the distance. From here on, "reduction" in R means any leading back that suspends one level for another, and Ideen I's sense survives only as the first and narrowest case.

The question has three terms: what is asked about (*Gefragtes*, the being of entities), what is interrogated (*Befragtes*, the entity), and what is to be found out (*Erfragtes*, the sense of being). The reduction is redoubled: from the entity to its being, then from the being of the entity to the sense of being, "jusqu'à l'être sans égard pour l'étant" (to being without regard for the entity; R II.6 ¶2, nn. 69–71). Ideen I has no theory of the question. Its nearest counterpart is §65: phenomenology refers back to itself, since its own acts of reflection and description belong to the field it describes (auxiliary Commentary on §65). SZ's thesis that "the questioning is itself an entity given with the question" has that formal shape. The difference is the one Marion is after. In Ideen I the inquirer's acts are brought under the eidetics of consciousness as further experiences. In SZ the inquirer's being becomes the entry to the question of being. Marion's "redoubling" has no Husserlian analogue, and nothing in his account depends on one.

Why Dasein? Because the questioner is an entity given with the question, because Dasein is ontically distinguished by being ontological, and because "intentionality is the *ratio cognoscendi* of transcendence". Dasein's privilege is phenomenological, not anthropological: it alone performs the redoubled reduction (R II.6 ¶3, nn. 72–76). The P-locus implied is intentionality as "das phänomenologische Hauptthema" (the principal theme of phenomenology, Ideen I §84) and the "wunderbare Eigenheit" (wonderful peculiarity) of being "Bewußtsein von etwas" (consciousness of something), "auf die alle vernunfttheoretischen und metaphysischen Rätsel zurückführen" (to which all the riddles of the theory of reason and of metaphysics lead back; §84, Hua III/1 189). Heidegger takes intentionality as Husserl's theme and makes it the index of a transcendence Husserl places elsewhere, in the thing over against consciousness (§§42–44). Marion's paragraph rests on this re-inscription of Ideen I's principal theme and states it through Heidegger, without a claim about Ideen I.

### 7 - The Redoubled Reduction — the Nothing

The second tactic is *What Is Metaphysics?* (1929): the question about the Nothing is "elaborated", and anxiety, after boredom has revealed "das Seiende im Ganzen" (beings as a whole), carries out a reduction on the whole of beings. In the "abweisende Verweisung" (repelling referral), beings slip away and the referral points to being. Being does not appear as the last wreck of the shipwreck of beings, "ni, pour parler comme Husserl, comme un « résidu »" (nor, to speak like Husserl, as a "residue"; R II.7 ¶1, nn. 77–82). Here Marion sets Heidegger's reduction against Ideen I's own experiment of total loss, Husserl's "Vernichtung der Dingwelt" (annihilation of the thing-world, §49). Husserl supposes that the whole of beings in the world dissolves, and finds that consciousness "würde ... zwar notwendig modifiziert, aber in seiner eigenen Existenz nicht berührt" (would necessarily be modified but would not be touched in its own existence; §49, Hua III/1 104). What remains is the "residue" of §§33 and 49. The two experiments share their starting point, the slipping away of beings in their totality, and part at the end: Husserl's yields a region that remains, Heidegger's a referral that points away from every remainder. Marion's word "résidu" takes Husserl's term and denies it of being, so that Heidegger's Nothing becomes a radicalization of §49 in which the residue gives way to a movement. The juxtaposition holds against the text of §49. Marion leaves implicit that Husserl's annihilation is a thought experiment about the possibility of non-being in the transcendent (the "Möglichkeit des Nichtseins", §49), and Heidegger's anxiety a fundamental mood. The resemblance is one of structure, and Marion does not claim influence.

The objection is that the reduction of 1929 ends in the Nothing, not in "the being of beings", and lacks the third term. Marion answers that the Nothing belongs to the being of beings ("le Rien ... se découvre comme appartenant à l'être de l'étant", the Nothing reveals itself as belonging to the being of beings, 1929), so that the first step leads from beings in totality to their being. He then proposes "à titre de simple hypothèse" (as a mere hypothesis) that the *Erfragte* of 1929 is ontological difference, which Heidegger's later additions to the text (dated by Marion to 1931) name four times (R II.7 ¶2, nn. 83–85). This is Heidegger read with his later notes, a mediation Marion states openly. No P-locus is engaged. The paragraph's bearing on Ideen I is negative and precise: the three-term structure that makes Heidegger's reduction "redoubled" is the structure Marion says Husserl lacked in II.5. Ideen I has, in sense-giving and the noematic X (§§55, 131), a third term. It is not a difference between being and beings, and Husserl never asks for one.

The two retrievals of the reduction converge: to receive being itself in givenness in person, as phenomenon. "Sein west als Erscheinen" (being comes to presence as appearing; *Einführung in die Metaphysik*, n. 86). Heidegger can still call himself Husserl's disciple, since he carries the reduction further by asking of its possibility what its actuality no longer gave (R II.7 ¶3, nn. 86–88). The sentence "being comes to presence as appearing" has a determinate place in Ideen I, and Marion does not cite it: it is Ideen I's doctrine of the transcendent. The real is "bloß phänomenales Sein" (merely phenomenal being, §44), given through appearing, against the absolute being of consciousness, "prinzipiell nicht durch ... Erscheinung zu geben" (in principle not to be given through appearance, §49). Heidegger's 1935 formula makes universal what Ideen I confined to one side of "the most radical of all distinctions of being" (§76). That is why Ideen I can be read both as the furthest point of a metaphysics of presence (chapter I) and as the text that holds back a being that does not appear (chapter V). The present chapter keeps only the first of these.

Chapter II is where Ideen I does the most work in R, and where Marion's text of it is least reliable. Two of his quotations from §46 carry Husserl's later additions, printed by Biemel and repeated by Commentary R: "as flowing present" and "despite this givenness in person". In each case the added words bear the argument: the first makes consciousness itself a present, the second sets givenness against being. One sentence of §153 is rendered in reverse, turning Husserl's refusal to ground phenomenology on ontology into a refusal of phenomenology to ground ontology. Other loci are cut where Husserl qualifies them: §49's "both called being" stops before "only according to the empty logical categories", and §111's "presently being" experience is quoted without the memory and phantasy modifications it grounds. The doctrines Marion assigns to Husserl are partly Husserl's after 1913 (the 1930 *Nachwort*, Ideen III, the *Cartesian Meditations*), and the epoché of II.1 that leaves "no question of being" is not Ideen I's, which subtracts nothing from the world's being (§§32, 55, 76). Two of Marion's claims hold against P. Ideen I does make the immanent sphere one of uncancellable positing and evident presence, in which semblance has no room. And Ideen I never makes being a phenomenon, since it treats being as the correlate of reason (§142). The chapter's lasting thesis, that Husserl's reduction does not ask about being as such, is borne out. The route to it, a "flat" phenomenon reduced to present objectivity, is built partly from a later Husserl and partly from cut quotations. Ideen I's own reserves (horizon and shading for the thing, a constitution "not the last" for consciousness, a being given without appearing) are the resources chapters V and VI will need, and here they go unread.

## III. The Ego and Dasein

### 1 - The Figure of Descartes in Heidegger's Itinerary

Heidegger's relation to Descartes looks secondary next to his dealings with Nietzsche, Hegel, Kant or Aristotle, yet the record runs from 1921 to 1974 (R III.1 ¶1). The chapter is about Heidegger and Descartes, and Ideen I enters it as the third party that makes their quarrel phenomenological. The paragraph engages no P-locus. It sets the frame in which Ideen I will be read for the rest of the chapter: as a Cartesian text. Ideen I invites that reading. It names Descartes at the decisive turns: the "Cartesian attempt at universal doubt" as the model the epoché replaces (§31), the "one Cartesian expression *cogito*" for all acts (§28, Hua III/1 59), the "Höhepunkt" (high point) at which "ein ... Kern der ... Meditationen des Descartes endlich zu seinem Rechte kommt" (a kernel of Descartes' *Meditations* finally comes into its own; §46, Hua III/1 99), and the "wunderbar tiefsinnige Cartesische Fundamentalbetrachtung" (wonderfully profound Cartesian fundamental meditation) as the first approach to phenomenology (§62, Hua III/1 134). Marion's chapter can treat any criticism of Descartes as a criticism of Husserl because Ideen I itself claims the Cartesian descent.

In 1921/22 Heidegger examines the "metaphysics of the I" in its Kantian and phenomenological forms before reaching Descartes, whose failing is to have taken the *sum* in an "indifferent", "formally objective" sense and put the weight of the question on the *ego* (R III.1 ¶2, n. 1). "Phenomenological" here means Husserl's, and the "metaphysics of the I" includes the pure I that Ideen I had reinstated (§§57, 80). Ideen I gives Heidegger's charge a foothold. Its "Ich bin" enters first as "ich, der wirkliche Mensch, ein reales Objekt wie andere in der natürlichen Welt" (I, the actual human being, a real object like others in the natural world; §33, Hua III/1 68), and the "allgemeine natürliche Faktum des »Ich bin«, »Ich denke«" (general natural fact of "I am", "I think") is taken up only for its essential content, its *Wesensgehalt* (§34, Hua III/1 69). Husserl's *sum* is a fact to be reduced to an essence. That is an indifference to the being of the *sum*, in Heidegger's sense, which Husserl adopts as method. Marion does not cite these sections. They show that Heidegger's 1921 diagnosis fits Ideen I's procedure as well as Descartes'.

The Marburg courses open and close with Descartes (1923/24, announced in 1928 as the source of SZ §§19–21) and pair him with Leibniz, for whom the *Ich* is the dimension from which metaphysical concepts are drawn without its mode of being being grasped. Heidegger finds Descartes' importance not in the *ego* as transcendental principle but in what the *ego* hides, the indeterminacy of its *esse*. That hiding calls for a phenomenological examination, since phenomenology concerns what does not show itself (R III.1 ¶3, n. 2). The last sentence applies SZ §7's concept of phenomenon to the history of philosophy, the move II.4 drew from SZ. No P-locus is engaged. The contrast "the *ego* as principle" against "the *ego* as concealment" maps onto Ideen I: Husserl reads Descartes for the first, as the seed of the epoché and of the absolute sphere (§§31, 46), and Heidegger for the second.

The late texts (Le Thor 1969, Zähringen 1973, "Der Fehl heiliger Namen" 1974) keep Descartes at the threshold of modern thought: subjectivity, unquestioned in its being since it is the *fundamentum inconcussum*, "constitutes the barrier" to the question of being (R III.1 ¶4, nn. 3–5). For P the relevant term is "barrier". Ideen I's absolute sphere, in which "Widerstreit, Schein, Anderssein keinen Raum" have and whose thesis is "schlechthin zweifellos" (utterly indubitable, §46, Hua III/1 98), is the twentieth-century form of the *inconcussum*. Zähringen's barrier stands, for Heidegger, where §46 places its high point. Marion's question (what concept makes Descartes necessary to Heidegger?) receives its answer in the next section, through Husserl.

### 2 - The Phenomenological Motive of the Original Confrontation

While Heidegger criticized Descartes at Marburg, Husserl approved him at Freiburg (*Erste Philosophie*, 1923/24), as he had since 1907, "bien avant les *Ideen*" (well before the *Ideas*). Descartes reaches Heidegger already invested by Husserl with phenomenological dignity, so that every discussion of Descartes is a discussion of Husserl, and Descartes becomes "le motif non phénoménologique de Husserl" (Husserl's non-phenomenological motive; R III.2 ¶1, nn. 6–7). The mediation Marion names is exact, and Ideen I is its central document (the loci listed under III.1 ¶1). One distinction in Ideen I itself is passed over. Husserl does not take over Descartes' doubt. He replaces it with the epoché, because doubt implies a modified thesis (the "Antithese", §31) and the epoché does not. He also limits the epoché, "mit gutem Grunde" (with good reason), to the natural world rather than making it universal (§32, Hua III/1 65). Husserl's Descartes is corrected at the point of method. The descent Marion draws, through the *ego*'s certainty and the being of consciousness, is the one Husserl kept. The other, through doubt, Husserl had revised in 1913.

In 1925 Heidegger asks whether Husserl's working out of the thematic field poses "the question of the being of this region, of the being of consciousness". He addresses to Husserl the critique he addressed to Descartes in 1921. Husserl did note "un unüberbrückbarer Wesensunterschied" (an unbridgeable essential difference), "ein wahrer Abgrund des Sinnes" (a veritable abyss of sense), and "die prinzipielle Unterschiedenheit der Seinsweisen, die kardinalste, die es überhaupt gibt" (the difference in principle of modes of being, the most cardinal there is; R III.2 ¶2, n. 9 / Hua III/1 89–90, 105, 88). The first of these phrases does not name the difference between consciousness and reality. In §43 the unbridgeable essential difference lies "zwischen Wahrnehmung einerseits und bildlich-symbolischer oder signitiv-symbolischer Vorstellung andererseits" (between perception on one side and image-symbolic or sign-symbolic representation on the other). It is a difference between kinds of intuiting, used against the image theory of perception. Marion borrows it for the cardinal difference of modes of being, as he did at R II n. 24. Chapter IV will quote §43 in its own context. But he reasons within an epistemic pair (certainty, contingency) that belongs to the being of the world, and he defines consciousness by citing Descartes: "nulla 're' indiget ad existendum" (§49, n. 10 / Hua III/1 104). The quotations are exact 1913 text. The charge that Husserl reasons within certainty and contingency is supported by §46's title, "Zweifellosigkeit der immanenten, Zweifelhaftigkeit der transzendenten Wahrnehmung" (indubitability of immanent, dubitability of transcendent perception), and by the "notwendige" and "zufällige" theses of Hua III/1 98. Marion leaves out the ground Husserl gives for the cardinal difference in §42 itself: "ein prinzipieller Unterschied der Gegebenheitsart" (a difference in principle of the mode of givenness, Hua III/1 89), shading against non-shading (§44). For Husserl the difference of being rests on a difference of givenness, not on degrees of certainty, and certainty follows from the mode of givenness. Heidegger's objection holds if givenness is itself an epistemic category. Marion, whose own thesis is that givenness is not one, here voices Heidegger without saying where he stands.

Five remarks on §49. (a) Husserl claims to define the being of consciousness. (b) He cites Descartes, *Principia* I §51. (c) He alters the formula, dropping *alia* and putting "re" in quotation marks, "au mépris de toute probité philologique" (in defiance of all philological probity), because *alia res* would make consciousness a *res*. (d) The Cartesian definition fits badly, since substance covers extended things too and every finite substance needs God's concurrence. (e) The deep convergence outweighs these differences (R III.2 ¶3, n. 11). Remarks (b) and (c) treat as a quotation what Ideen I does not present as one. Husserl does not name Descartes at §49; the edition's index of names does not list the page under Descartes (Hua III/1, index, "Descartes" at 1913 pp. 50, 53, 55, 56, 61, 63, 87, 118, 300; §49 is 1913 p. 92); and the sentence is a Latin phrase worked into German prose, not a citation. The next sentence fixes the sense of the quotation marks: "Andererseits ist die Welt der transzendenten »res« durchaus auf Bewußtsein ... angewiesen" (on the other hand, the world of transcendent "res" is thoroughly dependent on consciousness; Hua III/1 104). "Res" in quotation marks is the transcendent thing, and the phrase says that consciousness needs no thing of the world in order to exist. Husserl's adaptation is pointed and intelligible as an allusion, and "philological probity" is a standard for citation that the sentence does not claim to meet. Marion's substantive point survives. Husserl does define the absoluteness of consciousness by non-dependence, a substance-concept, and Marion is right that Descartes' own concept would cover extension and require God (remark d). Ideen I does not ignore the second point. Husserl takes up the "Transzendenz Gottes" just after the pure I and switches it off (§58, Hua III/1 125), so the dependence of finite substance on God is suspended, not overlooked. Marion's note dismisses Boehm's restrictive reading of *res*. On the text of Hua III/1 104 that reading is the one the next sentence supports.

Heidegger noticed the formula in 1925 and identified it as Descartes': absolute being does not determine the entity in its being but places the region within the order of constitution, as formally prior to all objectivity. Husserl's leading question is "how can consciousness become the object of an absolute science?", an idea taken over from modern philosophy since Descartes and won "not phenomenologically, by return to the things themselves, but by return to a traditional idea of philosophy". Descartes, the *mathesis universalis* of the *Regulae*, is the obstacle that held Husserl back. The "affinity" with Descartes that Heidegger names in GA 20 refers to the place where "this meditation has reached its summit" (R III.2 ¶4, nn. 12–16). That place is Ideen I §46, Hua III/1 99, and Heidegger's "summit" is Husserl's "Höhepunkt". Heidegger's reading of the high point is accurate: Husserl says there that a kernel of Descartes' *Meditations* "endlich zu seinem Rechte kommt". The charge that pure consciousness is won by return to an idea of absolute science has a textual ground in Ideen I. Husserl calls phenomenology a science of "a wholly different dimension" (§62), and he defends its claim to be "erste" Philosophie (first philosophy, §63). The charge also passes over the route Ideen I actually takes. The absolute sphere is reached through a descriptive contrast between modes of givenness (§§42–46), and the "idea of absolute science" is not invoked there. Heidegger's genealogy places the origin of pure consciousness in an inherited idea. Ideen I places it in the eidetic description of immanent and transcendent perception. Marion adopts Heidegger's genealogy. Heidegger's own note, "Kritik der Konstruktion der »Ontologien« Husserls" (critique of the construction of Husserl's "ontologies", n. 16), confirms that the target is Ideen I's regional ontologies.

Descartes is therefore the obstacle par excellence to phenomenology's ontological accomplishment, and to destroy the *ego* is to free the access to Dasein (R III.2 ¶5). Through Husserl, Ideen I has become the means of that conclusion. Destroying Descartes is destroying the Cartesian kernel of §46. The verdict on Husserl, "less a phenomenologist the more he remains Cartesian", rests on accepting Heidegger's genealogy of the absolute sphere, which the section has adopted rather than tested against §§42–44.

### 3 - The First Failure: The Indeterminacy of the "ego sum"

In SZ Descartes is the "extreme counter-case" of the problem of worldhood, and the "phenomenological destruction of the *cogito sum*" is announced for the unpublished Part II. Marion stresses that the charge first concerns the *sum* (SZ §6), and that the two failures, toward the world and toward the *ego*, anticipate "exactement" (exactly) Husserl's two regions: absolute consciousness and relative reality. "De même que Descartes manque à les penser comme telles, Husserl manque à penser leurs sens d'être respectifs" (just as Descartes fails to think them as such, Husserl fails to think their respective senses of being; R III.3 ¶1 (c), nn. 17–19). Remark (c) carries the chapter's thesis about P. The mapping holds at the level of the regions: consciousness as the region of absolute being and the real as relative (§§44, 49, 76). It does not hold at the level of Marion's claim that Husserl fails to think "their respective senses of being". Ideen I thinks both under the title of sense. The real is "Einheit des Sinnes" (unity of sense), relative to sense-giving consciousness (§55). Consciousness is what is "nicht selbst wieder durch Sinngebung" (not itself in turn by sense-giving, §55, Hua III/1 120) and is not given by appearing (§49). Whether these are "senses of being" in Heidegger's sense is the real question. Marion, following SZ, answers no, and does not discuss Husserl's answer.

Descartes, usually called the thinker of the *cogito sum*, could not think the *sum*. He reduces *sum* to *cogito* and *cogito* to *ego*, and postulates the univocity of certainty from the known object up to the knowing subject. "Nota est omnibus essentiae ab existentia distinctio" (the distinction of essence and existence is known to all). The failure hides itself (R III.3 ¶2, nn. 20–21). The paragraph concerns Descartes. Its charge of a univocal certainty, transferred to Husserl through III.3 ¶1 (c), meets in Ideen I a doctrine built against univocity. Evidence differs by type, adequate or inadequate, assertoric or apodictic, pure or impure, and every region has "ein Grundtypus originärer Evidenz" (a basic type of originary evidence) whose perfection "in anderen Sphären ... zu verlangen, ist Widersinn" (it is absurd to demand in other spheres; §138, Hua III/1 321; Commentary P on §§137–138). The certainty of the immanent and the presumptive certainty of the thing are, for Ideen I, two kinds. If Husserl's two regions repeat Descartes' failures, they do not repeat the univocity of certainty Marion finds in Descartes.

The failure decides the *ego*'s being by default: Descartes thinks it on the model of the intraworldly being, by a "reflection" (*Rückstrahlung*) of the understanding of the world onto Dasein's self-interpretation. The Cartesian *ego* and all its substitutes, "jusqu'à son avatar husserlien compris" (up to and including its Husserlian avatar), fall onto the intraworldly entity (R III.3 ¶3, n. 22). Ideen I describes exactly such a reflection and treats it as the error the reduction removes. Through the body, consciousness is "apperceived" as part of nature, the psychological ego as a real object in the world (§§33, 53), and the reduction switches off that apperception so that the pure I is not a real object (§§33, 49, 57). Heidegger's charge is that Husserl's pure I still borrows the being of the worldly thing in another form, as absolute *position*. That charge is the one weighed at II.2 ¶2. Marion's "avatar husserlien" cites no text. In Ideen I, the falling of the *ego* onto the world is not the cause of Husserl's failure. It is Husserl's own description of the natural attitude, which the reduction is designed to undo.

### 4 - The Second Failure: The Permanence of the Intraworldly Entity

Descartes misses worldhood as well as the *ego*, putting in place of the ready-to-hand (*Zuhandenheit*) the bare persistence of the present-at-hand (*Vorhandenheit*): "Sein = ständige Vorhandenheit" (being = constant presence-at-hand), dictated by the certainty that mathematics affords (R III.4 ¶1, nn. 23–24). Ideen I is the near source of Heidegger's vocabulary here, and it holds a resource against the thesis. In its description of the natural attitude, things are "für mich einfach da, im wörtlichen oder bildlichen Sinne »vorhanden«" (for me simply there, in the literal or figurative sense "present-at-hand"; §27, Hua III/1 55). In the same section: "Unmittelbar stehen Dinge als Gebrauchsobjekte da, der »Tisch« mit seinen »Büchern«, das »Trinkglas«" (things stand there immediately as objects of use, the "table" with its "books", the "drinking glass"), and "diese Wertcharaktere und praktischen Charaktere gehören konstitutiv zu den »vorhandenen« Objekten" (these value-characters and practical characters belong constitutively to the "present-at-hand" objects; Hua III/1 58). The 1913 world of the natural attitude is a world of use, value and practice, and its "vorhanden" includes them. Heidegger's step was to split Husserl's single word and to make the use-character prior. Marion does not apply the second failure to Husserl here; III.5 ¶2 (b) will. The connection with Ideen I passes through this section of §27, which Marion does not cite.

As a historian of Descartes, Marion makes three remarks. (a) Heidegger cites the *remanet* of the wax but not the earlier *remaneat* of the *ego* in the Second Meditation, which would have confirmed his thesis. (b) Descartes' doctrine of substance confuses the ontological and the ontic, and Heidegger's marginal note "ontologische Differenz" reveals the aporia in which Husserl would be caught, "distinguishing substances (or 'regions') solely by ontic criteria". (c) Proving the existence of the world presupposes that worldhood has been missed (R III.4 ¶2, nn. 25–33). Remark (b) is the one that concerns P, and on Ideen I's text it is wrong. Husserl's regions are not distinguished by ontic criteria. A region is "eine oberste materiale Gattung" (a highest material genus) of essences, determined eidetically and *a priori* (§§9–10), and the "radikalste aller Seinsunterscheidungen" (most radical of all distinctions of being) is drawn from modes of givenness (§§42, 76). Heidegger's objection is that an eidetic distinction of regions is still a distinction between beings, not between being and beings. In that form it is defensible, but "ontic" is the wrong word for Husserl's criterion, and Marion's "uniquement par critères ontiques" (solely by ontic criteria) attributes to Ideen I a naïveté it lacks. Remark (c) touches Ideen I as well. Husserl does not prove the world's existence; he shows that no proof from experience could give it absolute certainty and that the question does not arise for the reduced phenomenon (§46, Hua III/1 99). That is close to Heidegger's "scandal" turned around.

The two failures are one: Descartes grasped the being of Dasein only as the being of the *res extensa*, as substance, and passed this on to Kant, for whom "consciousness of my Dasein" means "my presence-at-hand in Descartes' sense" (R III.4 ¶3, n. 34). Kant's "Dasein" is the term Ideen I also uses for factual existence ("Dasein" of the world, §32; "Daseinsthesis", §46). Heidegger's re-appropriation of the word takes it from the vocabulary Husserl shared with Kant. Marion does not remark on this, and nothing in his argument depends on it.

### 5 - Dasein as "Destruction" of the "ego"

The two failures lead back to the misconstrual of ontological difference: "being is never explicable by beings". The *ego*, made to account for all other beings, remains undetermined in its own being, an "ontologische Bodenlosigkeit" (ontological groundlessness) from Descartes to Hegel. An idealism that reduces everything to a subject undetermined in its being and characterized only negatively as a "non-thing" is "no less naïve methodologically than the crudest realism" (SZ §43). Dasein "coincides neither ontically nor ontologically with the *res cogitans*" (R III.5 ¶1, nn. 35–37). SZ §43's sentence on idealism is aimed at transcendental idealism, and Ideen I is the text in which consciousness is characterized "negatively as a non-thing": "nulla »re« indiget" (§49), "prinzipiell nicht durch Abschattung und Erscheinung zu geben" (§49), not reality (§§33, 49). The negative characterization Heidegger attacks is Ideen I's own, and Marion reports the attack as Heidegger's. The phrase "undetermined in its being" is the point at which Ideen I is most open to the charge. Husserl says of the pure I that "it is wholly empty of essential components ... in itself indescribable: pure I and nothing further" (§80, Hua III/1 178).

Three ontic oppositions, which Heidegger never states but which can be reconstructed. (a) The *ego* is a *res* and shares the reality of intraworldly beings, which leads to "l'impossibilité husserlienne de distinguer effectivement la région-conscience de la région-monde" (the Husserlian impossibility of effectively distinguishing the region consciousness from the region world). Dasein, by contrast, is "abgegrenzt gegen ... Realität" (delimited against reality). (b) The *ego* is born of theoretical doubt "curis omnibus exsolutus" (freed of all cares), whereas for Dasein knowing presupposes "a *deficiency* of concernful dealing". (c) The *res cogitans* is unextended, Dasein spatial (R III.5 ¶2, nn. 38–40). Each opposition can be set against Ideen I. (a) Ideen I does not let consciousness share the reality of the world. It says the two are "nichts weniger als gleichgeordnete Seinsarten" (anything but coordinate modes of being), and that what they share is "nur nach den leeren logischen Kategorien" (only according to the empty logical categories; §49, Hua III/1 105). Ideen I's pure consciousness is, in so many words, delimited against reality. Marion's "impossibility" is Heidegger's verdict that Husserl's delimitation is only formal. Stated as Husserl's inability to distinguish the regions, it contradicts the text Marion quotes most often. (b) The world of the natural attitude in Ideen I is practical and evaluative (§27, above), and its consciousness includes "fühlend, wollend" (feeling, willing) along with considering. Husserl's own primacy of the theoretical is that a doxic positionality "lies" in every act of feeling and willing as well (§117; Commentary P on §117). That is a primacy of positing, not of a disengaged theoretical attitude. Remark (b) fits Descartes better than Husserl. (c) Ideen I's pure consciousness is non-spatial, and its body belongs to nature (§53), so here the opposition holds.

Ontologically the opposition becomes irreducible. (a) In Dasein its being is at stake, as play and uncertainty, against the *fundamentum inconcussum*, the "quid firmum et mansurum" (something firm and lasting) and "fonds qui soit tout à moi" (a foundation wholly my own) of Descartes. (b) Dasein's existence is possibility, not *existentia* as presence-at-hand, which Descartes leaves undefined as self-evident. (c) Dasein is essentially in a world, whereas the *ego* is won on the hypothesis that the world may be lost, a "weltlose Ich" (worldless I), "à propos de Husserl et de Kant, donc aussi de Descartes" (said of Husserl and of Kant, hence of Descartes too). The *ego* is "the entity for which its being is *not* at issue" (R III.5 ¶3, nn. 41–45). On (c) Ideen I offers the clearest support anywhere in R. §49's annihilation of the world is the experiment of a worldless consciousness: the being of consciousness "würde durch eine Vernichtung der Dingwelt zwar notwendig modifiziert, aber in seiner eigenen Existenz nicht berührt" (would be modified by an annihilation of the thing-world but not touched in its own existence; Hua III/1 104). "Weltloses Subjekt" is a fair description of what §49 claims is possible. Marion's "donc aussi Descartes" runs the genealogy backwards from Husserl: Descartes' doubt supposes the world in question, Husserl's §49 supposes it gone. On (a), Ideen I's "notwendige, schlechthin zweifellose" thesis of "meines reinen Ich und Ichlebens" (my pure I and the life of my I, §46, Hua III/1 98) is the 1913 *inconcussum*. Husserl qualifies it as "die Notwendigkeit eines Faktums" (the necessity of a fact). That makes the certainty factual and flowing, though no less certain. Marion's opposition between play and certainty holds against it.

### 6 - Dasein as Confirmation of the "ego"

Destruction would be meaningless unless the *ego* held an ontology, and unless it kept, for the new beginning, "une réserve et potentialité d'être" (a reserve and potentiality of being; R III.6 ¶1). From here on the chapter reverses its course, and Ideen I recedes. The reversal affects Marion's relation to P indirectly. If the *ego* keeps a potentiality that Dasein does not exhaust, the Husserlian pure I, which chapter V will take up, becomes a candidate for that reserve.

Dasein recognizes itself in the *ego* along four traits. The first is finitude: Dasein exists finitely, and the *ego* knows itself "cum sim finitus" (since I am finite), a finitude that grounds doubt and so the *cogitatio* that sets beings up as *cogitata*. Heidegger does not see the parallel, since he reads Cartesian finitude through the "anthropologie antico-chrétienne" (ancient-Christian anthropology; R III.6 ¶2, nn. 46–47). No P-locus. Ideen I's pure consciousness is not finite in this sense. Its stream is "beiderseitig endlos" (endless on both sides, §49, Hua III/1 104), and its idea is given as an "Idee im Kantischen Sinne" (§83). Finitude is the trait by which Descartes' *ego* resembles Dasein and differs from Husserl's. Marion's convergence of *ego* and Dasein bypasses Husserl, who here falls outside both.

(b) Mineness: Dasein's being is "je meines" (in each case mine), and its address must use the personal pronoun "ich bin", so Dasein speaks at least once "comme l'*ego cogito*" (like the *ego cogito*), and Descartes, by performing the *ego* in the first person, approaches Dasein's insubstitutability. (c) The possibility of impossibility: death for Dasein, free will facing divine omnipotence for the *ego* (R III.6 ¶3, nn. 48–51). Ideen I has the first-person form of the *ego* without its performance. The "Ich bin" is a "natural fact" whose essence is to be studied (§34, Hua III/1 69). The pure I is "für jeden Erlebnisstrom ein prinzipiell verschiedenes" (for each stream of experience a different one in principle, §57). Its mineness is a structural feature of every stream, not an act of saying "I". Marion's emphasis on the performative "Ego" ("hoc pronuntiatum", this statement) distinguishes Descartes from his successors (Spinoza's "homo cogitat", Malebranche, Leibniz). By that criterion Ideen I belongs with the successors, since its "Ich bin" is eidetically generalized.

Is the convergence specious, since the *ego* is ontologically undetermined and Dasein determined? (d) Dasein too is undetermined, provisionally and also positively: the "Wovor der Angst ist völlig unbestimmt" (what anxiety is anxious about is completely indeterminate), death is "certain and as such indeterminate", and the caller of conscience has "Unbestimmtheit und Unbestimmbarkeit" (indeterminacy and indeterminability) as a positive character. The debate is between an ontological indeterminacy (*ego*) and an ontic one (Dasein), and the *ego* may mime Dasein as the One (*das Man*) does (R III.6 ¶4, nn. 52–55). The paragraph prepares chapter V, and Ideen I is its silent third. Husserl's pure I is indeterminate by definition: "völlig leer an Wesenskomponenten ..., an und für sich unbeschreiblich" (wholly empty of essential components, in and for itself indescribable; §80, Hua III/1 178). Like Marion's Dasein, it is determined only by its relations, its "Beziehungsweisen" and "Verhaltensweisen" (modes of relation and of comportment). Husserl's emptiness is not Heidegger's existential indeterminacy. It is a phenomenological finding, not a mood or a decision. But it is the third case Marion's dichotomy lacks: an I that is neither ontologically undetermined by negligence, like Descartes', nor ontically undetermined in its existence, like Dasein, and that is undetermined because it is not an object of description at all. Chapter V's "Je hors d'être" (I outside being) will rest on §80. The present paragraph builds the frame in which that reading becomes possible.

*Ego* and Dasein meet along finitude, mineness, the possibility of impossibility and indeterminacy. Authenticity and inauthenticity do not estrange them, since that opposition belongs to Dasein's existence. "Quel mime les réunit donc encore ?" (what mime still unites them?; R III.6 ¶5). No P-locus. Of the four convergences, Ideen I's pure I shares only the last, and in a form Marion has not yet named.

### 7 - The Repetition of the "ego"

The urgency of the destruction shows that Dasein recognized itself in the *ego*, a mimetic rivalry in which "the victor seems at times vanquished by the vanquished". SZ §43 even sketches a repetition: "*sum*" in the sense of "I-am-in-a-world", in which the *cogitationes* become modes of being among intraworldly beings. Descartes, by contrast, says that "*cogitationes* are present-at-hand, and in them an *ego* is also present-at-hand as a worldless *res cogitans*". The late seminars speak of thinking the Greek ἐγώ (R III.7 ¶1, nn. 56–57). Heidegger's sentence on Descartes ("cogitationes sind vorhanden, und darin ist ein ego als weltlose res cogitans mitvorhanden") describes in Cartesian terms the structure Ideen I calls the stream of experiences with its pure I. Marion says the repetition must make the *ego cogito sum* appear as another phenomenon than the one to which "Descartes, donc aussi Kant et Husserl" (Descartes, hence also Kant and Husserl) confined themselves. The "donc" again runs Husserl into Descartes. Ideen I's reduction of the "Ich bin" to its essence (§34) is itself a repetition of the *cogito*, eidetic where Heidegger's is existential. Marion's chapter recognizes only Heidegger's.

Then the *ego* does not disappear in SZ but is born "à son authentique figure phénoménologique" (to its authentic phenomenological figure), and the new beginning would begin with the *ego* declined according to existential demands (R III.7 ¶2). The claim inverts the chapter's opening thesis, and its stake for P is the word "phénoménologique". In chapter II Husserl's pure consciousness was called non-phenomenological, after Heidegger. Here the *ego* achieves phenomenological legitimacy, but as Dasein's self, not as Husserl's pure I. Ideen I's *ego* has dropped out of the comparison it began.

Dasein's mineness requires the personal pronoun. The I is legitimate only as existential selfhood (*Selbstheit*), disclosed in care and resolute anticipation, not as the identity and constancy of substance (*Selbigkeit*, *Beständigkeit*). The same I can be repeated inauthentically, as *res cogitans*, or authentically, as resolute self. The *ego cogito sum* is "un territoire à occuper ... une œuvre à refaire" (a territory to occupy, a work to be redone; R III.7 ¶3, nn. 58–63). The distinction between *Selbigkeit* (sameness) and *Selbstheit* (selfhood) has a near analogue in Ideen I. The pure I is an identical pole related to every experience of its stream (§§57, 80), and it is not a substance with "Wesenskomponenten" (§80). Husserl's I is an identical without content, Heidegger's an existing without substance. Both refuse the substantial constancy SZ §64 attacks, by different routes. Marion's two possible repetitions (Cartesian, existential) leave out Husserl's, in which the I is identity without substance. That is the one he will use in chapter V.

Between *ego* and Dasein the struggle is over the interpretation of one phenomenon, "I think", "I am". Two questions remain: (a) whether the I is exhausted by selfhood and care, and (b) whether it is exhausted in its function of "I am", or whether something "other than being" is at stake in it (R III.7 ¶4, n. 64). Question (b) is the book's turn toward its own answer, the "interloqué" of chapter VI. Its Husserlian presupposition is Ideen I's pure I: "nicht konstituierte" (not constituted, §57), "unbeschreiblich" (indescribable, §80), and exempt from the reduction only within the limits of its evident co-givenness. An I that is not determined by being is the I Husserl described in 1913 under the title of phenomenological datum. Marion asks his question "malgré le silence de *Sein und Zeit*" (despite the silence of *Being and Time*) and does not name Ideen I as the text in which an I without being was already posed.

The chapter's Husserl is Heidegger's: the Cartesian of GA 20, whose absolute consciousness repeats the *ego*'s failure to think its being. Ideen I supplies the evidence for that Husserl. §46 says that a kernel of Descartes comes into its own. §49 defines consciousness by a reworking of the definition of substance and imagines a worldless consciousness. §80 leaves the I empty. These loci are quoted exactly. Two of Marion's own steps go beyond them. He reads the unattributed "nulla 're' indiget" as a falsified quotation, though the next sentence of §49 gives its sense. He charges Husserl with distinguishing regions "by ontic criteria" and with being unable to separate consciousness from the world, where Ideen I draws the distinction eidetically, from modes of givenness, and as an "abyss of sense". Where Ideen I could have qualified Heidegger's genealogy, the chapter does not consult it. The epoché is a correction of Cartesian doubt (§§31–32). Husserl's natural world is already a world of use and value, "vorhanden" in a sense that includes the practical (§27). His evidence is plural by region, not a univocal certainty (§138). The chapter's own turn, toward an I in which something other than being may be at stake, reaches its Husserlian source in §§57 and 80. Marion will use that source in chapter V without connecting it to the destruction he has followed here.

## IV. The Question of Being or Ontological Difference

### 1 - The Breakthrough and the Difference: *Sein und Zeit*

Phenomenology made a first breakthrough with the *Logical Investigations*, the interplay of intuition and intention, and a second when Heidegger turned intentionality toward the being of beings. Ontological difference defines the second breakthrough, since it alone separates metaphysics from the thinking of being and makes the destruction of the history of ontology possible. Any hesitation in its emergence would therefore be a symptom of "une incohérence foncière de toute l'entreprise" (a fundamental incoherence in the whole enterprise; R IV.1 ¶1, n. 1). The two breakthroughs repeat the frame of chapter I, now in sequence: Husserl's is the enlargement of intuition, Heidegger's the difference of being and beings. Ideen I has no place in this scheme. In Marion's account it is neither the LU's breakthrough nor Heidegger's, and it reappears in IV.4 as the place where a "difference" was named and left unthought.

Ontological difference must emerge from its own latency, since metaphysics thinks within the difference "unthought as such" (*Identität und Differenz*). Two difficulties follow: a breakthrough born of indecision, and the absence of the phrase from SZ §6, where the destruction is announced (R IV.1 ¶2, nn. 2–3). No P-locus. The terms "latent" and "patent" anticipate IV.4's claim that the difference was latent in Ideen I, and "unthought as such" is the formula IV.4 will apply to Husserl.

### 2 - Emergence and Delay

By Heidegger's own 1949 account, *Vom Wesen des Grundes* (1928, published in the 1929 Festschrift for Husserl) "names the ontological difference", and SZ would be the major text that ignores it (R IV.2 ¶1, nn. 4–5). The place of first naming has a Husserlian setting Marion records without comment: the essay appeared in the volume of homage for Husserl's seventieth birthday. That is the only link to P in the paragraph.

Naming is not thinking, and the 1927 course (*Grundprobleme* §22) already derives ontological difference from the duality of truth (uncoveredness of beings, disclosedness of being), which SZ §44 also works out. Heidegger's 1929 note calls the 1927 course "the first public communication" and assigns it to SZ I.3, *Zeit und Sein*. How can SZ distinguish two kinds of truth without inferring the difference (R IV.2 ¶2, nn. 6–12)? No P-locus. The bifurcation of truth has a Husserlian counterpart Marion leaves aside: Ideen I's "truth" is the correlate of the perfect reason-character of the primal doxa (§139), and "true being" the correlate of an originary perfect reason-thesis (§142). Ideen I has one concept of truth, distributed over regions by types of evidence (§138), and no difference of the truth of being from the truth of beings.

In *Grundprobleme* §22 the difference (*Unterschied*) is "there" latently in Dasein's existence and becomes explicit, as *ontologische Differenz*, through Dasein's temporality. The single surviving chapter of Part II stops at §22a, and the course's shrinking toward its goal suggests an aporia rather than a breakthrough. Its continuity with SZ suggests that the analytic of Dasein served, from the start, the explication of ontological difference (R IV.2 ¶3, nn. 13–15). No P-locus. The latent/explicit pair (*Unterschied*/*Differenz*) is the structure IV.4 will use to read Husserl's "Unterschied" as latently ontological.

### 3 - The Occurrences and the Notes

Commentators either deny that SZ names the difference (Vail) or hold that it moves within it without naming it (Salus, Granel, Beaufret), and Beaufret's paradox, "the book of the difference" that never says the word, is "paradoxale jusqu'à l'incohérence" (paradoxical to the point of incoherence; R IV.3 ¶1, nn. 16–19). No P-locus. Marion's two theses to be refuted, that the phrase is absent and that the difference is thought, frame a question to which Husserl will be the answer in IV.4.

SZ does use the phrase: §12, "den ontologischen Unterschied" (the ontological difference) between being-in as existential and the "insideness" of present-at-hand things as category; and §63, "ontologische Unterscheidung" (ontological distinction) between existence and reality, repeated without the adjective on the last page as the "Unterschied" of the being of Dasein from beings not of Dasein's kind, and of "consciousness" from "thing" (R IV.3 ¶2, nn. 20–25). The pair of the last page, "»Bewußtsein« und »Ding«" in quotation marks, is Husserl's vocabulary, and Marion will make it his chief evidence in IV.4. SZ §63's "existence and reality" pairs Heidegger's term for Dasein with Ideen I's term for the thing (*Realität*, §§42–49). The quotation marks in §83 mark Husserl's words as Husserl's.

Two verbal occurrences carry the work: SZ §2, where being as what is asked about "sich ... wesenhaft unterscheidet" (differs essentially) from the uncovering of beings, and the end of Division I (§44), where being must "von allem Seienden unterschieden sein" (be distinguished from every being), which Heidegger's marginal note glosses "Ontologische Differenz" (R IV.3 ¶3–¶4, nn. 26–28). These two paragraphs concern SZ only. Their bearing on P is negative but exact: the difference SZ §2 marks between two modes of showing (*Aufweisungsart*) is the kind of difference Ideen I §42 grounds in "Gegebenheitsart" (mode of givenness, Hua III/1 89). Husserl distinguishes modes of givenness of two regions of beings. SZ §2 distinguishes the showing of being from the uncovering of beings. Marion's IV.4 will bring the two together.

Four more marginal notes (§§7, 20, 39 and one on "being is never explicable by beings") read SZ through ontological difference, and Heidegger did not need to over-read his text: SZ "moves within" the difference, which it names (R IV.3 ¶5, nn. 29–32). The note on §20 ("ontologische Differenz", against the Cartesian *substantia*) was used in III.4 against Husserl's regions. The note on §39, "being *qua* difference 'in' Da-sein", returns in IV.4 beside a "coincidence" with Husserl's LU. No P-locus here.

### 4 - The Most Cardinal Unthought

A "certain" ontological difference runs through SZ from §2 to §83, and the thread that led SZ into it "prend son origine chez Husserl" (originates in Husserl; R IV.4 ¶1, n. 33). This is the hypothesis that makes Ideen I the source of Heidegger's breakthrough. The chapter's dependence on P is concentrated in this section.

Husserl too uses "ontological difference", from 1913, in the second edition of LU III: "der ontologische Unterschied" between abstract and concrete contents, three times. More importantly, SZ §63's "ontologische Unterscheidung der Existenz und Realität" (ontological distinction of existence and reality) repeats a distinction "fixée canoniquement" (canonically fixed) in Ideen I §42. Marion quotes: "L'être comme conscience et l'être comme réalité. La différence *(Unterschied)* principielle entre les modes d'intuition. (…) Une différence *(Unterschied)* fondamentale et d'essence s'introduit entre l'être comme conscience et l'être comme chose. (…) la différence principielle *(prinzipielle Unterschiedenheit)* des manières d'être, la plus cardinale (…) une *différence (Unterschied) principielle* des modes de donation" (being as consciousness and being as reality; the difference in principle between modes of intuition; a fundamental essential difference between being as consciousness and being as thing; the most cardinal difference of modes of being; a difference in principle of modes of givenness; R IV.4 ¶2, n. 37 / Hua III/1 86–89). Further: §43's "Wesensunterschied" no bridge spans; §49's "abyss of sense" between two acceptations of "being" that are alike "only according to the empty logical categories"; and "§79" (that is, §76): consciousness as "Urkategorie des Seins überhaupt" (primal category of being in general), "Urregion" (primal region), and "this most radical of all distinctions of being". "Un abîme de sens entre deux acceptions de « étant », qu'est-ce sinon une différence des *manières* d'être, donc une différence déjà ontologique ?" (an abyss of sense between two acceptations of "being", what is it if not a difference of *manners* of being, hence a difference already ontological?; n. 39 / Hua III/1 89–90, 105, 159). This is the strongest use of Ideen I in the book, and most of it holds. The quotations of §§42, 49 and 76 are exact 1913 text. §42's title is "Sein als Bewußtsein und Sein als Realität. Prinzipieller Unterschied der Anschauungsweisen" (being as consciousness and being as reality; difference in principle of modes of intuition), and its sentences say what Marion says they say. He now quotes §49 to the end of its period, including "only according to the empty logical categories", which he cut in chapter II (R II n. 47). Read entire, that clause gives his argument its best premise: the two are called "being" by a merely formal title, so the difference between them is not one of beings under a common concept. Three details need correction. (1) §42's middle sentence reads "zwischen Sein als *Erlebnis* und Sein als Ding" (between being as *experience* and being as thing, Hua III/1 87), and its next words assign the difference to "dem regionalen Wesen Erlebnis" (the regional essence experience). Husserl's own frame is regional essence, which Marion's ellipsis drops. That frame is what Heidegger's critique will charge with stopping short. (2) §43's unbridgeable difference is between perception and image or sign representation (Hua III/1 89–90), not between consciousness and reality. It is a difference of modes of intuiting and does not belong in a list of differences of modes of being. Marion's §43 sentence here states its terms correctly, but he counts it among the proofs. (3) "§79" in Marion's text is §76, as his note gives it. LU III's "ontologischer Unterschied" is not Ideen I, and its adjective has Husserl's 1913 sense: "ontological" means belonging to the eidetic science of objects, here the formal ontology of whole and part (Ideen I §§9–10 give the frame). Marion's "the locution without the concept" therefore understates. Husserl had the locution with his own concept of the ontological, the regional and formal eidetics of objects, and that is the concept Heidegger had to break with. On the main thesis, that Ideen I gave Heidegger the two beings and modes of being whose difference SZ calls "ontological", Marion's reading is supported by the text. His evidence is that SZ §83 quotes Husserl's "Bewußtsein" and "Ding" in quotation marks, and that SZ §63 sets "Realität" against "Existenz". The claim of filiation rests on a terminological echo and a shared pair of terms. Marion calls it a presumption ("comment ... ne pas présumer", how not to presume), which is the right strength.

The break between Husserl and Heidegger was played out over the interpretation of this difference. GA 20, citing Ideen I §§49 and 76, observes that the "most radical distinction of being" is claimed while "no question is properly posed about the being of the beings that enter into the difference", nor about "the sense of being" that governs it. Husserl names the difference and stops "là même où s'ouvre la véritable difficulté" (just where the real difficulty begins), and by not questioning the being of the intentional he becomes "unphänomenologisch" (R IV.4 ¶3, nn. 40–41). Marion's paragraph reports GA 20 and adopts its conclusion. As a statement about Ideen I, the point that Husserl poses no question about "the sense of being" is accurate in Heidegger's sense: Ideen I has no *Sinn von Sein überhaupt*. Ideen I does say what it takes the being of each term to be, and Heidegger's "pas une seule fois" (not once) passes over it. The being of the real is "bloß phänomenales Sein" (merely phenomenal being), "Einheit des Sinnes" (unity of sense) for consciousness (§§44, 55). The being of consciousness is "absolutes Sein" in the sense of needing no "res" in order to exist (§49), given without shading (§§42, 44), "nicht selbst wieder durch Sinngebung" (not itself in turn by sense-giving, §55). Heidegger's objection is that these determinations are drawn from the order of constitution and of givenness, not from being. The objection is fair as a difference of programme. As a report that Husserl asks no question about the being of the terms, it is not. Marion's "jamais il ne pense ce qu'il nomme" (never does he think what he names) inherits the overstatement.

The confrontation was also direct. Heidegger's letter of 22 October 1927 (Marion gives 27 October; the Hua IX editor dates it to the 22nd) calls the central problem of SZ the mode of being of the being in which the "world" is constituted, Dasein's mode of being "total verschieden" (totally different) from all others, and asks what the "Seinsart" (mode of being) of "this absolute ego" is "im Unterschied" (in its difference) from the purely psychical (R IV.4 ¶4, nn. 42–43). The letter belongs to the *Britannica* drafts (Hua IX), not to Ideen I. Its questions correspond to two Ideen I loci. The difference of the absolute ego from the pure psychical is Ideen I's distinction of the pure I from the psychological I as a real object (§§33, 53–54, 57). The question of the absolute ego's mode of being is the question Ideen I explicitly holds back: "für viele Untersuchungen [können] die Fragen des reinen Ich in suspenso bleiben" (for many inquiries the questions of the pure I can remain in suspense; §57, Hua III/1 124), and the I is admitted only "soweit die unmittelbar evident feststellbare Wesenseigentümlichkeit ... reicht" (as far as its immediately evident essential peculiarity reaches). Heidegger asks of Husserl what Ideen I had deliberately suspended. Marion identifies the "absolute ego" of the letter with Dasein. That is Heidegger's own identification, put to Husserl in Husserl's word.

Three conclusions. (a) Husserl gave Heidegger the locution without the concept (LU, 1913) and the concept without its ontological determination (Ideen I). (b) Heidegger interpreted the difference of the modes of being of consciousness and thing "décidément comme ontologique" (decidedly as ontological), from the sense of being that Ideen I lacks: "Husserl ne comprend pas ce qu'il a dit" (Husserl does not understand what he said). (c) Husserl's unthought difference "pouvait seul susciter l'effort de pensée heideggérien" (alone could provoke Heidegger's effort of thought; R IV.4 ¶5). Conclusion (a) should read: Husserl gave the locution with his own regional-formal concept of the ontological, and the concept of a difference of modes of being grounded in modes of givenness. The second half of (a) is Marion's strongest reading of Ideen I and is correct: §42 grounds the cardinal difference in a "prinzipieller Unterschied der Gegebenheitsart" (Hua III/1 89). Conclusion (b) is radicalization in its plainest form. "Husserl does not understand what he said" means that Husserl's sentence had a sense he did not intend and Heidegger did. That is Heidegger's claim on Ideen I, and Marion makes it his own. Conclusion (c) is a historical thesis that GA 20 supports. Marion does not draw the consequence for his own project here: if the cardinal difference rests on modes of givenness, then Ideen I had already thought a difference of being from givenness, which is the direction of the third reduction. This is the second time the book passes over that point (see II.2 ¶2).

### 5 - The Irreducibility of the "Question of Being" to "Ontological Difference"

SZ distinguishes being from beings clearly ("being is not something like a being", "being is never explicable by beings"), yet being is always the being of a being, to be read off its surface. The difference is needed because ontically being and beings are not distinguished. It is ontological in the strict sense, the phenomenological method applied where the phenomenon is no being (R IV.5 ¶1, nn. 44–46). No P-locus. Ideen I's nearest formula is "Sein ist ... Titel für ..." (being is a title for): "Realität und Welt sind hier eben Titel für gewisse gültige Sinneseinheiten" (reality and world are titles for certain valid unities of sense, §55), and the "truly existing object" is the correlate of a rational positing (§142). For Husserl being is not a phenomenon but a title for correlations. That is why Ideen I has no use for a difference between being and beings.

Three figures of difference overlap in 1927: (α) the "strictly ontic" difference that Husserl already marks between "consciousness" and "reality"; (β) the difference between their modes of being (existence, *Vorhandenheit*), which SZ calls "ontological", worked by an analogy of proportionality; (γ) the difference between being and beings, later called ontological and never so called in SZ. Of these, (α) "n'a absolument rien d'ontologique" (has absolutely nothing ontological about it; R IV.5 ¶2). The classification contradicts IV.4. There, Husserl's difference was "une différence d'essence quasi ontologique" (an essential difference, quasi-ontological), "a difference of the *manners* of being, hence already ontological", the concept of (β) without its determination. Here it is (α), purely ontic. Ideen I supports IV.4 over IV.5. §42 speaks of "Seinsweisen" (modes of being), not of two beings, and §49 says the two are alike only under empty logical categories. Husserl's difference is a difference of modes of being in his own vocabulary, (β) in Marion's scheme. The reclassification lets Marion assign (β) to SZ alone ("elle ne se rencontre que dans *Sein und Zeit*", it is found only in *Being and Time*) and so make SZ's analogy the place where the ontic and the ontological are confused. Against Ideen I, (β) is already in §42, and the confusion Marion finds in SZ is inherited with it.

How is Dasein's intrusion into the difference justified? The question of being has three terms: the interrogated (*Befragtes*, the being), what is asked about (*Gefragtes*, its being) and what is to be found out (*Erfragtes*, the sense of being). The first two would yield canonical ontological difference. The third makes the construction ternary and irreconcilable with the binary difference (R IV.5 ¶3, nn. 47–48). Chapter II.6 treated the same structure. No new P-locus.

Neither gap of the ternary question can receive canonical ontological difference. The first (Dasein and its being) is too narrow ontically. The second (being of beings and sense of being) is not reached in SZ, and its horizon, time, is later abandoned (R IV.5 ¶4, nn. 49–51). No P-locus.

The difference is multiplied rather than lost, as Heidegger's reported plan for SZ I.3 confirms with three differences: transcendental, transcendence-wise, and transcendent or theological (Müller). The list "indique plus une hésitation qu'une élaboration rigoureuse" (indicates hesitation more than rigorous elaboration; R IV.5 ¶5, n. 52). The third, "theological" difference (of God from beings, beingness and being) has a counterpart in Ideen I that Marion does not mention: §58 names "die Transzendenz Gottes" (the transcendence of God) as a third transcendence beside the world's and the pure I's, and switches it off. Ideen I places God at the edge of the field of differences, as Heidegger's plan does.

### 6 - The Ontic Primacy of the Question of Being as Interrogation

The ternary question is an impasse for the difference but an advance for Dasein, which alone performs it (R IV.6 ¶1). No P-locus.

First, Dasein performs the difference as transcending. "Husserl offre, ici encore, le point de départ négatif, en ayant confié la transcendance aux seuls objets et choses « extérieurs » à la conscience, désormais confinée à l'immanence" (Husserl again offers the negative starting point, having assigned transcendence to objects and things "external" to consciousness alone, now confined to immanence). Heidegger inverts this: subjects transcend, and being is "the transcendens pure and simple" (R IV.6 ¶2, nn. 53–55). The description of Husserl leaves out the transcendence Ideen I gives the I. The pure I is "eine eigenartige — nicht konstituierte — Transzendenz, eine Transzendenz in der Immanenz" (a peculiar, not constituted transcendence, a transcendence in immanence; §57, Hua III/1 124), and §58 adds the transcendence of God. Ideen I also characterizes every act as a "Blick auf" (regard toward) its object, issuing from the I (§§37, 80). That is not Heidegger's self-surpassing, but it is a directedness beyond the act that Ideen I attributes to the subject. Marion's "seuls objets" is true of Ideen I's sense of "transcendent" as the shading thing. It is not true of the word, which Husserl applies to the I itself. The inversion Marion credits to Heidegger takes over a term Husserl had already applied to the subject, and gives it a new sense.

Second, Dasein *is* the difference, as the being that is ontological, for which being has a sense. The later note on "es geht um das Sein" reads "Das Seyn überhaupt zu bestehen" (to withstand Beyng in general; R IV.6 ¶3, nn. 56–60). No P-locus.

Yet in 1927 Heidegger identifies Dasein with the question of being, not with the difference, because the binary difference is absorbed into the ternary question, and the third term is Dasein itself (R IV.6 ¶4, n. 61). No P-locus. Here Marion completes the move by which Husserl's two-term difference (consciousness and reality) passes into a structure whose third term, Dasein, takes the place of Husserl's consciousness. The chapter's argument now has the shape of a substitution. In SZ §§63 and 83, Dasein occupies the position Ideen I gave to consciousness in the cardinal difference, and that occupation is what blocks the canonical difference.

### 7 - The Misconstrual of Ontological Difference by "Ontological Difference"

SZ knows an "ontological difference", and does not yet think ontological difference, because the ternary question introduces Dasein as mediating term. Did SZ tie its incompletion to the obstacle of Dasein (R IV.7 ¶1)? No P-locus. "Ontological difference" in quotation marks now names the inherited difference of Ideen I §42, reworked in SZ §63.

The last two published pages of SZ, §83, pose exactly this question and answer it (R IV.7 ¶2). No P-locus.

The question: the analytic is "preparatory" and "provisional", and the goal is the question of being in general. Twice the last page determines Dasein by "the 'difference' of 'consciousness' and 'thing'", but only as "a point of departure ... not anything where philosophy may rest", and it asks whether this suffices "for an originary unfolding of the ontological problematic" (R IV.7 ¶3, nn. 62–66). The final sentences of SZ name Husserl's pair in quotation marks and declare it an insufficient starting point. Marion's argument now applies to Ideen I directly. The difference SZ inherited from §42 is what §83 questions. SZ's self-critique is in part a critique of the Husserlian starting point it had absorbed, and Marion's reading supports that conclusion without stating it.

Can the second gap (being of beings to sense of being) be crossed on the same ontic foundation as the first? §83 asks whether ontology needs "an *ontic* foundation, and *which* being must take over the function of founding", which turns back on §2's "*Which* being is the sense of being to be read off?" and §4's threefold primacy of Dasein (R IV.7 ¶4, nn. 67–69). No P-locus. Ideen I raises the question of a founding being in its own terms and answers it: consciousness is the "Urregion" "in der alle anderen Seinsregionen wurzeln" (in which all other regions of being are rooted, §76, Hua III/1 159). SZ §4's "third primacy" of Dasein, as "the ontico-ontological condition of the possibility of all ontologies", has the structure of §76's primal region. When §83 asks whether ontology needs an ontic foundation, it asks a question that §76 had answered in the affirmative for consciousness.

The marginalia confirm a self-critique: "Two different questions put together; misleading, above all as to the role of Dasein"; Dasein is "exemplary" only as *Bei-spiel* (playing-alongside) of being; and against reading ontology off even the authentic ontic, "Verkehrt!" (wrong!). SZ owes its incompletion to the concealment of ontological difference by "ontological difference", that is, by Dasein (R IV.7 ¶5, nn. 70–75). No P-locus. The conclusion has a Husserlian corollary Marion leaves unstated: if Dasein, in the position of Ideen I's consciousness, is the obstacle, then the obstacle SZ failed to remove was the founding role of a privileged being that Ideen I §76 had established.

Chapter IV rests on Ideen I at one point, IV.4, and there its reading is the book's most careful so far. The quotations of §§42, 49 and 76 are exact and taken from the 1913 text. §49 is quoted to the end of its period. The claim that the "most cardinal" difference of modes of being reached SZ from Ideen I is supported by SZ §§63 and 83, where Husserl's "Realität", "Bewußtsein" and "Ding" stand in quotation marks. Marion qualifies the filiation as a presumption. Three faults remain. §43's unbridgeable difference, of perception from image and sign, is counted among differences of modes of being. "Ontological" in Husserl's 1913 usage, regional and formal eidetics, is treated as a word without a concept. And Husserl's difference is classified as quasi-ontological in IV.4 and as purely ontic in IV.5, so that the confusion of ontic and ontological can be credited to SZ alone. The verdict taken from GA 20, that Husserl names the cardinal difference without asking about the being of its terms, holds only for a question about the sense of being in general. Ideen I does say what the being of each term is: phenomenal being as unity of sense, absolute being as not needing a "res" and not given by shading. It grounds the whole difference in modes of givenness (§42, Hua III/1 89). Marion's chapter, built to show how Dasein blocked ontological difference, thereby finds in Ideen I a difference of being thought from givenness. The next chapter, on being and region, has to decide whether that is Husserl's failure or Marion's resource.

## V. Being and the Region

No commentary on chapters V and VI exists yet, so Marion's move in each unit is stated from the source, slightly more fully than in the preceding chapters.

### 1 - The Possibility of Ontology

Marion asks whether the phenomenologist should take up the question of being, as one object among others or as a privileged one, and so whether phenomenology should take the old title "ontology" (R V.1 ¶1). The question presupposes Ideen I's own use of the title. "Ontology" in 1913 is the name of the eidetic sciences of objects, formal and regional (§§9–10), restored "der geänderten Zeitlage entsprechend" (in keeping with the changed times; §10 n., Hua III/1 28). For Husserl, phenomenology does not take that title. Marion's chapter will test whether it should.

Between Husserl and the question stands "un tiers écran" (a third party acting as a screen), Heidegger, whose account of Husserl's greatness and failures predetermines every reading. Marion proposes to follow that subjection for a time in order to make it visible (R V.1 ¶2). This is a methodological admission that applies to the four preceding chapters, which read Ideen I largely through GA 20. Its value for this commentary is that Marion names the mediation that guards 4, 11 and 12 have been tracking. From here on the chapter undertakes, by Marion's account, to read Husserl without Heidegger, and the comparison can test that.

Heidegger's two theses: (a) phenomenology "taken in its content" is the science of the being of beings, since only it can make visible what does not show itself, being; (b) Husserl misses this essence twice. He applies phenomenology indifferently to every field of objects in an "ivresse des constitutions" (intoxication with constitutions), and he leaves undetermined the modes of being of the two original regions, consciousness and objectness in general, so that his phenomenology is "unphänomenologisch" (R V.1 ¶3, nn. 1–5). The "intoxication with constitutions" has a precise locus in Ideen I: the programme of §§149–153, in which "jede gegenständliche Region konstituiert sich bewußtseinsmäßig" (every objective region is constituted in consciousness, §149, Hua III/1 343), every region furnishes "den Leitfaden für eine eigene geschlossene Untersuchungsgruppe" (the guiding thread for a closed group of investigations of its own), and the "volle Extension des transzendentalen Problems" (full extension of the transcendental problem, §153) covers all regions (Commentary P on §§149–153). The second failure repeats GA 20, assessed at II.2 ¶2 and IV.4 ¶3. Ideen I does determine the being of its two regions, as absolute and as relative "Sein für ein Ich" (being for an I; §44, Hua III/1 92), but from givenness and constitution, which Heidegger counts as no determination of being.

### 2 - Phenomenology as the "Universal and Authentic Ontology"

Late texts refute Heidegger's charge. The *Cartesian Meditations* call fully developed transcendental phenomenology "die wahre und echte universale Ontologie" (the true and genuine universal ontology). They agree with earlier positions: Ideen III (1912), where phenomenology "engulfs" all rational ontologies and the *mathesis* is "mother of all ontologies"; *Erste Philosophie* (1923–24), where the "Grundbegriffe und Grundsätze der Ontologien" (basic concepts and principles of the ontologies) are, "in the language of my *Ideas*", the necessary "guiding threads" of constitutive phenomenology; and the texts on "complete ontology" (Hua VIII). "At least from 1912 and without later repentance", Husserl made phenomenology end in a universal ontology, so Heidegger's charge cannot stand: "cette thèse, Husserl l'avait soutenue avant Heidegger lui-même" (Husserl had held this thesis before Heidegger himself; R V.2 ¶1, nn. 6–9). Only one of these texts is Ideen I, and Marion cites it through *Erste Philosophie*: ontological concepts as "Leitfäden" (guiding threads) is the doctrine of §§149–153. Marion now reports it correctly. The ontologies supply guiding threads to constitutive phenomenology. That agrees with the 1913 sentence of §153, which says that phenomenology is not grounded by them, and it corrects the inverted rendering of the same section in II.1 ¶2 (guard 9) without remarking on it. The rest of the dossier (Ideen III, Hua VIII, CM) is not Ideen I. In Ideen I phenomenology is not called an ontology. It is the eidetics of transcendental consciousness that switches off the ontologies (§§59–60) and gives them their "final determination of sense" (§62). Marion's "at least from 1912" rests on the Ideen III draft, which Husserl did not publish.

Husserl's priority does not mean he thought ontology in SZ's sense. The question moves: not whether Husserl missed the phenomenological approach to ontology, but in what sense each worked out ontology phenomenologically, since "Husserl l'occupait déjà, et solidement" (Husserl already occupied it, and firmly; R V.2 ¶2). No P-locus. The shift of question is the turning point of the book's relation to Husserl. Chapters II–IV followed Heidegger's charge of a missed question. From here Husserl is credited with an ontology, to be examined for what it misses.

### 3 - The Anonymous Concept of an Ontology

Husserl traces the alliance of phenomenology and ontology back to the LU, Heidegger's privileged text: FTL §27 claims that the LU first introduced formal ontology, though "not yet under the *name*" (R V.3 ¶1, nn. 10–11). No P-locus. The point to retain is the one Marion makes against himself: the self-interpretation dates from 1929, two years after SZ.

Ideen I §10 says the same in 1913: "Den aus historischen Gründen anstößigen Ausdruck Ontologie habe ich damals noch nicht aufzunehmen gewagt ... Demgegenüber halte ich es jetzt, der geänderten Zeitlage entsprechend, für richtiger, den alten Ausdruck Ontologie wieder zur Geltung zu bringen" (I did not yet dare to take up the expression ontology, offensive for historical reasons ... I now think it more correct, in keeping with the changed times, to restore the old expression ontology; R V.3 ¶2, n. 12 / §10 n., Hua III/1 28). The concept was the formal-objective categories of Prolegomena §67 and LU III, and the 1913 draft preface says the same (n. 13). The quotation is exact, and Marion reads its context correctly: Ideen I's own notes to §10 refer to LU III §11 and Prolegomena §67 (Hua III/1 28, nn.). What §10 restores is the *name* for the eidetic sciences of objects. It does not say that phenomenology is an ontology. In the same book those sciences are switched off for the sake of phenomenology's purity (§§59–60).

Two conclusions. (a) "C'est dès 1913, au moins, que Husserl confie la charge de l'ontologie à la phénoménologie, d'abord explicitement dans *Ideen I* et *III*" (it is from 1913 at least that Husserl entrusts phenomenology with the charge of ontology, explicitly first in *Ideen I* and *III*). (b) Unlike Heidegger, Husserl locates the LU's ontological centre of gravity in the Prolegomena and Investigations II–IV, never in the Sixth (R V.3 ¶3, n. 14). Conclusion (b) is borne out by Ideen I §10's notes, which refer to Prolegomena §67 and LU III, though Ideen I §3's note on categorial intuition shows that the Sixth was not absent from Husserl's 1913 self-reading. Conclusion (a) overstates what Ideen I says. Ideen I restores "ontology" as the name of eidetic sciences that phenomenology switches off (§§59–60) and clarifies (§§149–153). It does not "entrust phenomenology with the charge of ontology". The words "eo ipso die wahre und echte universale Ontologie" (eo ipso the true and genuine universal ontology) that close the paragraph are CM §64 (1929). The 1913 relation is the one §76 states: phenomenology's "Herrschaftsbereich" (sphere of dominion) extends over the sciences "die sie doch ausschaltet" (which it nonetheless switches off; Hua III/1 159). Ideen I gives phenomenology a dominion over ontology without identifying the two. Marion's thesis that Husserl "occupied" ontology before Heidegger holds for 1913 in that form, as dominion and clarification, and not as identity.

### 4 - The Formality of Objectness

Husserl neither ignores nor regionalizes ontology, since he sets a universal ontology as one of phenomenology's accomplishments. What universality is it, and does it answer the question of being (R V.4 ¶1)? No P-locus. The premise "il ne la régionalise pas" (he does not regionalize it) will be withdrawn at V.5 ¶4 and V.6 ¶5.

First strategic text: Ideen I §§8–10. Every science of experience is bound "an die Gesetze ..., die zum Wesen der Gegenständlichkeit überhaupt gehören" (to the laws belonging to the essence of objectness in general), hence to the "Komplex formal-ontologischer Disziplinen" (complex of formal-ontological disciplines): formal logic "im engeren Sinne" (in the narrower sense) and the formal *mathesis universalis* (§8, Hua III/1 23). §9: regional ontology is regional eidetic science, and its truths belong "teils zur leeren Form von Gegenständlichkeit überhaupt, teils zum Eidos der Region, welches gleichsam eine notwendige materiale Form aller regionalen Gegenstände darstellt" (partly to the empty form of objectness in general, partly to the eidos of the region, which is as it were a necessary material form of all objects of the region; Hua III/1 24). The four terms reduce to two, and "formal ontology" looks like a contradiction in terms (R V.4 ¶2, nn. 15–16). The quotations are exact and Marion's reconstruction is accurate. "Objectité" (objectness) renders *Gegenständlichkeit*, kept apart from objectivity, as Marion's note 33 explains. That is the distinction guard 5 asked for, and Marion keeps it here. His sense of a contradiction in "formal ontology" is his own; Husserl treats the formal as ontological because it is the eidetics of "Gegenstand überhaupt", which §10 goes on to state.

§10: in a regional ontology, the categories "thing", "property", "relation", "state of affairs" point back to an objectness with "den Vorzug der Urgegenständlichkeit" (the privilege of primal objectness). Regions are determined by the "Form der Region überhaupt" (form of region in general), the "leere Form von Region überhaupt" (empty form of region in general), so that "die formale Ontologie zugleich die Formen aller möglichen Ontologien überhaupt ... in sich birgt" (formal ontology at the same time contains within itself the forms of all possible ontologies in general). Formal ontology, "immer als der reinen Logik der vollen Extension bis zur mathesis universalis" (always taken as pure logic in its full extension up to the *mathesis universalis*), is the eidetic science of the object in general. Husserl must "extend" logic to the *mathesis* and understand ontology as pure objectness (R V.4 ¶3, n. 17 / Hua III/1 25–27). The quotations are exact. One context is cut. In §10, "Urgegenständlichkeit" (primal objectness) is illustrated by the thing within the region nature: "In unserem Beispiel hat diesen Vorzug natürlich das Ding selbst, gegenüber der dinglichen Eigenschaft, Relation usw." (in our example this privilege naturally belongs to the thing itself, as against the thing's property, relation, etc.; Hua III/1 25). Primal objectness is the substrate as against its syntactic derivatives (§11, "letzte Substrate", ultimate substrates), not objectness in general as against other modes of being. Marion moves from the first to the second without marking the step. His later thesis that formal ontology knows only "the subsistence of the substrate" (V.5 ¶3) draws on this passage, and the passage concerns the order of substrates and derivatives within objects.

How can formal logic reach objectness in general? By doubling the categories. (a) The categories of signification belong to apophantics, and "remarquablement Husserl ne les nomme pourtant pas des catégories logiques" (remarkably, Husserl does not call them logical categories). (b) The logical categories, "Kategorien der logischen Region Gegenstand-überhaupt" (categories of the logical region object-in-general), express the necessary determinations "eines Gegenstandes als solchen, eines irgend Etwas — sofern es überhaupt soll Etwas sein können" (of an object as such, of any Something whatever, insofar as it is to be able to be a Something at all). Husserl thinks the objectness of the object as the sole original form, so the question is whether objectness can be raised to the rank of first form (R V.4 ¶4, n. 17 / Hua III/1 27–28). Two departures, both bearing on the argument. (1) Remark (a) is contradicted by §10. After listing the logical categories, Husserl writes: "Aber auch die »Bedeutungskategorien« ... gehören hierher" (but the "categories of signification" also belong here), "gemäß unserer Definition" (according to our definition), because the essential truths connecting "object-in-general" and "signification-in-general" let "reine Bedeutungswahrheiten sich in reine Gegenstandswahrheiten umwenden" (pure truths of signification be converted into pure truths of objects). "Eben darum ist die »apophantische Logik« ... doch mitgehörig zur formalen Ontologie im vollumfassenden Sinne" (precisely therefore "apophantic logic" belongs to formal ontology in the fully comprehensive sense; Hua III/1 28). The categories of signification are logical categories, set apart only as a group within them. (2) Marion's French for the decisive clause reads "pour autant qu'un [tel] quelque chose doit en général pouvoir être" (insofar as such a something must in general be able to be). Husserl's clause is "sofern es überhaupt soll Etwas sein können" (insofar as it is to be able to be a *Something* at all). The rendering drops the predicate: Husserl's category determines what it takes to be *something*, and Marion's what it takes to *be*. That shift lets the chapter treat formal ontology as Husserl's answer to the question of being, which Marion will then judge insufficient.

FTL completes the unification. Formal apophantics and non-apophantic mathematics meet in "the most general concept of object", "the extension of the concept supreme *object in general*", so that the whole of mathematics is "a *formal* ontology". Ontology becomes possible only on condition of absolute abstraction. "Dès lors, doit-on encore parler d'une ontologie ?" (should one then still speak of an ontology?; R V.4 ¶5, nn. 18–19). FTL is not P. Its doctrine is Ideen I §10's (formal ontology as "eidetische Wissenschaft vom Gegenstande überhaupt. Gegenstand ist in ihrem Sinne alles und jedes", eidetic science of the object in general; object in its sense is anything whatever; Hua III/1 27), and Marion's question applies to 1913 as much as to 1929.

Husserl acknowledges the difficulty "après coup" (after the fact) when he has to distinguish the apophantic and ontological attitudes. The empty concept "something" occurs only in judgment, and their separation "is at the same time an equivalence", so that they must count "as one and the same science" (R V.4 ¶6, nn. 20–22). The equivalence is not an afterthought. It is Ideen I's thesis in 1913: "Jedes formal-logische Gesetz ist äquivalent umzuwenden in ein formal-ontologisches. Statt über Urteile wird jetzt über Sachverhalte ... geurteilt usw. Die Rede ist auch nicht mehr von der Wahrheit ... der Urteilssätze, sondern vom Bestande der Sachverhalte, vom Sein der Gegenstände" (every formal-logical law can be equivalently converted into a formal-ontological one; instead of judgments one now judges about states of affairs; the talk is no longer of the truth of judgments but of the obtaining of states of affairs, of the being of objects; §148, Hua III/1 342; Commentary P on §148). The same equivalence underlies §10's inclusion of apophantics in formal ontology. Marion presents FTL's "Scheidung, die zugleich doch Äquivalenz ist" (separation that is at the same time an equivalence) as an admission wrung from Husserl. In Ideen I it is the founding doctrine of formal ontology. The difference matters for the next paragraph, which finds in it an "irresolvable limit".

Husserl's four arguments for distinguishing ontology from apophantics (judging is directed at objects; syntactic variations refer to the substrate; syntactic forms derive from the "object" as first category; categorial forms are "ontological forms") reduce to intentionality. Intentionality introduces no new concept and shows the object without showing itself, so the separation of logic and ontology is "une inconceptualisable transgression, sans *discrimen* objectif dans un champ commun" (an unconceptualizable transgression, with no objective criterion within a common field; R V.4 ¶7, nn. 23–27). FTL is not P. The thesis that intentionality "never shows itself" is tested by Ideen I's doctrine of the noema, which makes the directedness of an act describable as the noematic sense and its relation to "seinen" Gegenstand (its object): "Jedes Noema hat einen »Inhalt«, nämlich seinen »Sinn« und bezieht sich durch ihn auf »seinen« Gegenstand" (every noema has a "content", namely its "sense", and relates through it to "its" object; §129, Hua III/1 297; Commentary P on §§129–131). In Ideen I the passage from judgment to object is the passage from noematic sense to the determinable X (§131). It has a conceptual articulation: the X as "Gegenstand schlechthin" (the object simpliciter) as against the object "im Wie seiner Bestimmtheiten" (in the how of its determinations). Marion's "inconceptualisable" overlooks the 1913 analysis designed for this step.

"One and the same science, a strict separation", said Husserl; better, "une seule et même objectité et ... deux sciences" (one and the same objectness and two sciences). Analytics has an "ontic", and by its *a priori* generality an "ontological", direction (R V.4 ¶8, n. 28). No new P-locus. Marion's reformulation is a fair statement of Ideen I §§10 and 148 as well: one formal domain, convertible in two directions.

### 5 - The First Insufficiency — by Universality

Husserl built a formal ontology, "authentic" and universal. Does it withstand Heidegger's objection? Husserl misses the question of being not because he failed to build an ontology but "parce qu'il n'a que trop parfaitement réussi à la construire" (because he succeeded all too perfectly in building it; R V.5 ¶1). No P-locus. This is the chapter's reversal of chapters II–IV: the failure is now an excess of success.

(a) The logical status of the being: formal ontology is equivalent to analytics, so the being remains an object of predication, a being "pour celui qui juge" (for the one who judges) as long as he does not cancel its validity (FTL). Husserl never cites the results of the Sixth Investigation in this context and holds to the equivalence of logic and ontology. A 1923 text makes formal ontology "the theory of objects as substrates of possible predicative senses" (R V.5 ¶2, nn. 29–31). The paragraph's texts are FTL and Hua VIII. Its Ideen I counterpart, cited in n. 31, is §148's equivalence of formal-logical and formal-ontological laws, and §142 (Commentary P), where "truly existing object" is equivalent to what can be posited in a perfect reason-thesis. Both support Marion's verdict that the being of formal ontology is the correlate of possible judging. Ideen I marks the limit of that thesis itself: formal ontology "geht ... sehr weit hinaus über die Sphäre solcher bloßen Umwendungen" (goes very far beyond the sphere of such mere conversions), through nominalizations (sets, numbers), and "es [ist] nicht die Aufgabe der Phänomenologie, diese Disziplinen zu entwickeln" (it is not the task of phenomenology to develop these disciplines; §148, Hua III/1 343). The equivalence of logic and ontology is the formal ontologist's, not the phenomenologist's. For Ideen I phenomenology's interest lies in the axioms only "als Titel für phänomenologische Analysen" (as titles for phenomenological analyses). Marion's verdict holds for formal ontology. As a verdict on what Husserl's phenomenology does with ontology, it skips the step by which Ideen I turns ontological concepts into indices for constitution.

(b) The primacy of objectness: formal ontology needs nothing but objectness, a point of reference for predication and intention. Husserl "enlarges" formal ontology to values, but by subordinating them to the "object in general" (§148, n. 34), and "il ne semble pas envisager d'autre mode d'être que la subsistance du substrat" (he does not seem to envisage any other mode of being than the subsistence of the substrate), whereas Heidegger reaches toward *Zuhandenheit* and existence (R V.5 ¶3, nn. 32–34). The §148 report is accurate as far as it goes: "Die Werte, die praktischen Gegenständlichkeiten, ordnen sich dem formalen Titel »Gegenstand«, »Etwas überhaupt« unter" (values and practical objectnesses fall under the formal title "object", "something in general"; Hua III/1 343). The next sentences qualify it in a way Marion leaves out. From the standpoint of universal analytic ontology, value-ontologies are "material" disciplines. The analogies between doxic and axiological syntheses have their own force, and Husserl warns against Kant's having "Analogie mit Identität verwechselt" (confused analogy with identity), since "das eigentlich Analytische ... darf nicht vermengt werden mit seinem formalen Analogon, das bezogen ist auf die Synthesen der Gemüts- und Willensthesen" (the properly analytic must not be confused with its formal analogue, referred to the syntheses of the theses of feeling and will; Hua III/1 343). Ideen I also gives axiological and practical positings their own "truth" and "evidence" (§139; Commentary P). Husserl subordinates values to the formal title "object", and refuses to reduce their syntheses to doxic ones. Ideen I §27's world of "Gebrauchsobjekte" (objects of use) is the descriptive counterpart. Marion's "no other mode of being than the substrate" is true of formal ontology's title and not of Ideen I's phenomenology of reason, which distinguishes the thetic genera. Whether those genera are modes of being in Heidegger's sense is the real question. Marion answers it by omission.

(c) The originality of possibility: formal ontology concerns the possible ("any object whatever"), intuition "founds" possibility rather than actuality (FTL), and ontology is the *logos* of possible worlds (Hua VIII). CM's "new ontology ... unlike the 18th century's" is therefore Leibniz-Wolff made more decisive. Formal ontology is universal in domain and regional in horizon: "elle laisse ininterrogée la manière d'être de l'objectité" (it leaves the mode of being of objectness unquestioned; R V.5 ¶4, nn. 35–38). The paragraph's texts are not P. Its Ideen I ground is the doctrine of eidetic science, which posits no existence and treats "ideale Möglichkeiten" (ideal possibilities; §7, Hua III/1 21): for the geometer, "statt der Erfahrung die Wesensschauung der letztbegründende Akt" (instead of experience the seeing of essences is the ultimately grounding act). Marion is right that this is the ground of formal ontology's priority of the possible. Ideen I does not leave the mode of being of objectness wholly unquestioned. It assigns objects of every region to the correlation with possible consciousness (§§142, 149), and that is the answer Marion called "constitution" in chapter II and now counts as no answer. His conclusion, that formal ontology "se régionalise face à la question de l'être" (becomes regional in the face of the question of being), withdraws the premise of V.4 ¶1 ("il ne la régionalise pas"), and the reversal is argued, not slipped.

### 6 - The Second Insufficiency — by Reduction

The unquestioned privilege of objectness requires its subordination to the transcendental I: "subjectivity comes *before* objectivity in dignity of being" (Hua VIII). "Toute région d'objectité se constitue à la mesure de la conscience" (every region of objectness is constituted in consciousness, §148); the world has being only "for the I" (§§44–45), a "thoroughly relative being", while pure consciousness has "absolute priority" (Ideen III, *Nachwort*), is the "region of 'absolute being'" (Beilage X), "Sein als Bewußtsein" (§§42, 49), "eine prinzipiell einartige Seinsregion" (a region of being unique in principle, §33). Immanent and transcendent being are "both called 'being', 'object'" only by "empty logical categories", across "an abyss of sense" (§49). If "being" fits both only emptily, why keep it for consciousness? And if consciousness is the "Urkategorie des Seins" (§76) and, in the transcendental attitude, a region "unweltlich, unreal" (unworldly, unreal; Beilage IX), does the original category still belong to being (R V.6 ¶1, nn. 39–45)? The paragraph mixes 1913 and 1929, and the questions it draws depend on the later texts. From 1913: "Jede gegenständliche Region konstituiert sich bewußtseinsmäßig" (§149, not §148; Hua III/1 343); being "für ein Ich" (§44, Hua III/1 92) and "den bloßen sekundären, relativen Sinn eines Seins für ein Bewußtsein" (the mere secondary, relative sense of a being for a consciousness; §49, Hua III/1 106); "Sein als Bewußtsein" (§42); "eine prinzipiell eigenartige Seinsregion" (a region of being peculiar in principle; §33, Hua III/1 68, R's "einartige"); §49's sentence, quoted to its end (Hua III/1 105); "Urkategorie des Seins überhaupt (oder in unserer Rede die Urregion)" (§76, Hua III/1 159). These are exact, and they support Marion's first question, which is a good one. §49 does say that "being" is common to the two only formally. That makes the attribution of "being" to consciousness a question that Ideen I raises and does not answer. From later years: "region of 'absolute being'" and "unweltlich, unreal" in "Beilage X" and "Beilage IX" (Biemel, Hua III 394) are Hua III/2 Beilagen 37–38, which Schuhmann dates "Herbst 1929" (Hua III/2 589–594), written in copy D. They are late additions in Husserl's hand, two years after SZ. The 1913 text speaks of consciousness as "des in einem bestimmten Sinn »absoluten« Seins" (of being "absolute" in a certain sense, with quotation marks; §76, Hua III/1 159), and the quotation marks signal that the 1913 Husserl already hesitated over the word. The "durchaus relatives Sein" (thoroughly relative being) and the "absolute priority" are Ideen III and the 1930 *Nachwort* (n. 41). Marion's second question (does the primal category still belong to being?) gains most of its force from the 1929 phrase "unworldly, unreal". Within Ideen I, consciousness has "ein Eigensein ... in seinem absoluten Eigenwesen" (a being of its own, in its absolute proper essence; §33, Hua III/1 68). It is "unreal" only in the sense that it is not *Realität*, the being of the world.

If the I precedes objectness and ontology concerns objects, the I is excepted from being. Husserl drew the consequence: "a first philosophy ... prior even to ontology" (Cairns, 1932). Phenomenology "makes the *a priori* an *a posteriori*" (Ideen III); the ontology of the real world is "einseitig und nicht im Endsinne philosophisch" (one-sided and not philosophical in the final sense, CM §59); "phenomenology ... is no ontology at all" (1906–07; Ideen III Beilage) (R V.6 ¶2, nn. 46–48). None of these texts is Ideen I. The 1913 counterpart is §§59–60: phenomenology may take no premise from the ontologies it switches off. That supports Marion's thesis of phenomenology "before" ontology, in 1913 as a methodological rule and not as a doctrine of an I outside being.

Two remarks. (a) In Ideen I the regional and formal ontologies are worked out (§§8–10) "avant et sans le critère de la donation phénoménologique (« le principe de tous les principes », au § 24) ni l'opération qui le met en œuvre, la réduction phénoménologique (au § 31 sq.)" (before and without the criterion of phenomenological givenness, the principle of all principles at §24, and the operation that puts it to work, the reduction at §31ff.). They must later appear before the tribunal: "we can include formal logic and the whole of *mathesis* in the expressly switching-off epoché" and "we carry out *explicitly an extension of the original reduction* to all the transcendental eidetic domains and to the *ontologies* belonging to them" (§§59–60). (b) The reduction brackets the ontologies and the question of being: "universal epoché with regard to the being or non-being of the world" (CM §15), "Ausser-Vollzug-Setzen des Seinsglaubens" (putting the belief in being out of action, *Nachwort*), "the science of *onta* dissolves into the phenomenological" (Ideen III). What does phenomenology seek in an object it does not recognize as a being (R V.6 ¶3, nn. 49–51)? Remark (a) reads Ideen I's architecture exactly. The ontologies of Part I precede the principle (Part I, §24) and the reduction (Part II), and §§59–60 then subject them to an extended epoché (Hua III/1 126–129; guard 3 holds). The second quotation is mistranslated. §60 reads "eine Erweiterung der ursprünglichen Reduktion auf alle transzendent-eidetischen Gebiete und die ihnen zugehörigen Ontologien" (an extension of the original reduction to all *transcendent*-eidetic domains and the ontologies belonging to them; Hua III/1 129). Marion's "transcendentaux" (transcendental) reverses the point. What the extended reduction switches off are the eidetic sciences of the *transcendent*, the regions outside pure consciousness, so that the transcendental eidetics, phenomenology itself, remains. Rendered "transcendental", the sentence has phenomenology bracketing its own domain. Remark (b) rests on CM, the *Nachwort* and Ideen III. Ideen I's epoché "verschließt jedes Urteil über räumlich-zeitliches Dasein" (closes off every judgment about spatio-temporal existence, §32) but "negiere ... nicht" (negates nothing), subtracts nothing from the being of the world (§55), and takes back the bracketed "mit einem Index versehen" (provided with an index, §76). In 1913 "the being or non-being of the world" is suspended, and the being of the world returns as sense. "Dissolves into the phenomenological" is Ideen III's metaphor. Ideen I's word is "Vorzeichenänderung" (change of sign).

The paradox deepens: phenomenology brackets ontology and claims to contain all ontologies as their "Mutterboden" (mother soil). It works on "the same" propositions as the ontologist, but "a 'thing' as correlate is not a thing", and phenomenology is directed not at objects "schlechthin" but "exclusively at experiences and correlates of experiences". A "cardinal difference" separates the two attitudes. Marion's note adds that the quotation marks around "object" are the hinge, and that their thematic use "remonte ... à *Ideen I*, § 10" (goes back to *Ideen I* §10; R V.6 ¶4, nn. 52–55). The texts quoted are Ideen III §§14–16. The 1913 locus for the quotation marks is not §10, where "Gegenstand" in quotation marks names a formal title, but §§88–90, the doctrine of the noema: "Der Baum schlechthin, das Ding in der Natur, ist nichts weniger als dieses Baumwahrgenommene als solches ... Der Baum schlechthin kann abbrennen ... Der Sinn aber ... kann nicht abbrennen" (the tree simpliciter, the thing in nature, is anything but this perceived-tree as such; the tree simpliciter can burn up; the sense cannot burn up; §89, Hua III/1 205). Ideen III's "a 'thing' as correlate is not a thing" is §89 restated. Marion's note looks for the source of the quotation marks in Ideen I and points to the wrong section. The right one, §89, has already been passed over twice (I.5 ¶5, II.3 ¶2).

Conclusions. (a) Under the reduction ontology is regionalized: the "cardinal difference" separates what belongs to beings from what frees itself from them, and is therefore non-ontological. "La différence ontologique n'est pas cardinale, la différence cardinale n'est pas ontologique" (ontological difference is not cardinal, the cardinal difference is not ontological). Heidegger is right that the question of the mode of being yields to the question of access. (b) Phenomenologically, privileging the "thing" in quotation marks over the thing itself could sacrifice the return to the things "à une régression vers le psychologisme" (to a regression toward psychologism), so Heidegger is right again. (c) If the I is excepted from every ontology, where *is* it? Either the cardinal difference is weakened and the I *is* in an undetermined mode, or the I, and the reduction with it, "n'*est* pas" (*is* not; R V.6 ¶5, nn. 56–57). Conclusion (a) cites "Ideen I, § 42, Hua, V, p. 96" for "die kardinalste Unterschiedenheit". The volume number is a misprint, and the 1913 text is Hua III/1 88. §43's "unbridgeable essential difference" is cited again for the cardinal difference (guard 12). Conclusion (a) also reverses chapter IV, where the cardinal difference was "quasi-ontological" and the origin of SZ's "ontological difference" (guard 13). Here it is "not ontological". In chapter IV, being shared by consciousness and thing only formally made the difference one of modes of being. Here, the same formality makes it a difference between what is and what frees itself from being. Both readings use §49. Conclusion (b) is contested by Ideen I. §89 introduces the noema as the sense "of" the tree, and §§128–131 show how "jedes Noema ... bezieht sich durch ihn [den Sinn] auf »seinen« Gegenstand" (every noema relates through its sense to "its" object; §129, Hua III/1 297), the object "schlechthin" being the determinable X in the noematic sense (§131; Commentary P). Ideen I does not exchange the thing for the "thing". It analyses the reference of the one to the other, and calls the thing "das Ding in der Natur" (the thing in nature), which "can burn". Marion's charge of psychologism meets in 1913 Husserl's explicit refusal of it (§§89–90: the noema is not a *reell* psychic content). Conclusion (c) is a genuine alternative. Ideen I sides with the first branch, weakening the cardinal difference: consciousness has "ein Eigensein" (a being of its own, §33), and §49's formal "being" is a word Husserl keeps. Marion takes the second branch in §7.

### 7 - "I" outside Being

Two bold assumptions: (a) that Husserl first envisaged that the I, which performs the reduction of all ontology, transcends ontology, "que le Je se dise hors l'être" (that the I is said outside being); (b) that Heidegger's charge can be turned around. If being is not the ultimate *Sache*, Husserl's failure to return to it would be "un saut presque sans précédent" (an almost unprecedented leap) toward a phenomenological horizon not determined by being, and the possibility of phenomenology could be turned against SZ (R V.7 ¶1). No P-locus. Assumption (a) is Marion's reading of the pure I. Its textual base is set out in ¶6.

Husserl did not thematize this horizon, for two reasons: his "ivresse des constitutions" (intoxication with constitutions), and his inability to see ontological difference. Why should the *Seinsfrage* be exempt from the reduction when "all ontologies fall under" it? Husserl already reduced an "authentic and universal ontology", and Heidegger never says why fundamental ontology should resist the reduction; "Heidegger présuppose que la question de l'être puisse réduire la réduction : il ne le démontre jamais" (Heidegger presupposes that the question of being can reduce the reduction; he never demonstrates it; R V.7 ¶2, nn. 58–59). The argument "all ontologies fall under the reduction" is Ideen III's sentence. In Ideen I it is the extended reduction of §§59–60, which brackets the eidetic sciences of the *transcendent* (Hua III/1 129, above). Ideen I does not reduce a "universal ontology". It reduces regional and formal eidetics, in order to keep phenomenology free of their premises. Marion's question about the Seinsfrage's exemption is sharpened by Ideen I's §32 rather than answered by it. The 1913 epoché brackets "jedes Urteil über räumlich-zeitliches Dasein" (every judgment about spatio-temporal existence), and its own limits are methodological ("mit gutem Grunde"). Whether a question of being as such can be bracketed is a question Ideen I could not pose. Marion's argument is a radicalization of the 1913 reduction, and he says so ("parfois continuer sans, voire au-delà de Husserl", at times to go on without and even beyond Husserl).

The crisis comes to a head in two sentences. Heidegger (1943/49): man alone, called by being, experiences "the wonder of all wonders: *that* beings are". Husserl (Ideen III, 1912): "the wonder of all wonders is the pure I and pure consciousness", a wonder that "verschwindet" (vanishes) as soon as the light of phenomenology falls on it and it becomes a science with problems (R V.7 ¶3, nn. 60–61). Not P. The Ideen I counterpart to the "wonder" is §84's "wunderbare Eigenheit" (wonderful peculiarity) of intentionality, "auf die alle vernunfttheoretischen und metaphysischen Rätsel zurückführen" (to which all the riddles of the theory of reason and of metaphysics lead back; Hua III/1 189). In 1913 the wonder is intentionality, not the pure I, and it is likewise turned into a programme of research.

The two sentences oppose Husserl and Heidegger on the phenomenon par excellence: transcendence of the I or transcendence of being. Read to its end, Husserl's sentence refuses any wonder that would escape reduction and constitution. "Toutes les ontologies tombent ... sous le coup de la réduction", "pas même l'être de l'étant, pas même non plus ... le Je", and the gaze is "sans idole" (without idol; R V.7 ¶4, n. 62). Marion's reading of Ideen III is careful, and it supplies the book's own principle: the reduction admits no exception. Ideen I has a counter-text that Marion does not cite. The pure I is exempted from switching off, "Bei der unmittelbar wesentlichen Rolle, die diese Transzendenz bei jeder *cogitatio* spielt, werden wir sie einer Ausschaltung nicht unterziehen dürfen" (given the immediately essential role this transcendence plays in every *cogitatio*, we may not subject it to a switching off; §57, Hua III/1 124). In 1913 the pure I is precisely the exception to the reduction. Marion's "pas même le Je" goes against Ideen I's stated limit, and it is the move by which the third reduction will exceed Husserl.

The objection is that disqualifying the wonder only frees Husserl's programme of constituting the objects of the regional ontologies. Granted, but a phenomenology without exception can be thought. (a) The reduction that exceeds all ontologies cannot be conceived in ontological terms. Husserl never determined such a realm and often fell back to objectness. Yet the question stands: "D'où réduit la réduction ?" (from where does the reduction reduce?). Metaphysical reductions always proceed from an identified instance (the place of ideas, ontology, a privileged being: ego, transcendental I, Dasein), and this one keeps no ontological ground. It "demeure « hors d'être »" (remains "outside being"; R V.7 ¶5). No P-locus. In the list of privileged beings from which reductions proceed, "Je transcendantal" is Husserl's. Ideen I's reduction does proceed from an identified instance: it is performed by the phenomenologist "wie es meine volle Freiheit ist" (as is my full freedom, §32, Hua III/1 65) and yields "consciousness" as residue (§33). Marion's "from nowhere" is the demand of his own third reduction, stated as a requirement Husserl's phenomenology "designates" without thinking it.

(b) The reduction's conceptual grasp of the I does not dissolve it into objectness. Husserl asks to think it correctly as the "cardinal difference" that tears the I from the mode of being of things. Is that difference irreducible to ontological difference by defect (Heidegger) or by excess? "Indescriptible en soi et par soi : pur Je et rien d'autre" (indescribable in and of itself: pure I and nothing further; §80). The ontological indeterminacy in which Husserl "indisputably" leaves the I could indicate that the I "n'a pas d'abord ni surtout à se déterminer selon l'être" (does not have first or chiefly to be determined according to being). "Mis hors d'être" (put outside being), as a building is "hors d'eau" (secured against water) while still exposed, the I may offer itself to other transcendences (R V.7 ¶6, nn. 63–64). The quotation of §80 is exact and taken from the 1913 text (Hua III/1 178, R's 195). It is cut at its opening clause: "Von seinen »Beziehungsweisen« oder »Verhaltensweisen« abgesehen, ist es völlig leer an Wesenskomponenten ... an und für sich unbeschreiblich" (apart from its "modes of relation" or "modes of comportment", it is wholly empty of essential components; in and for itself indescribable). Husserl's emptiness is relative to the I's relations. It is empty of components because it is nothing but the pole of its relations to the experiences that are "its" own (§80). Marion reads the indescribability as indeterminacy with respect to being. Husserl's sentence makes it an indeterminacy with respect to content. These are different, and Marion's argument needs the first while Husserl wrote the second. The reading is defensible as an extrapolation, since Ideen I never says the pure I *is* in any determinate mode beyond its relations. It is productive: it gives Marion the I "hors d'être" that chapter VI will develop. It also departs from the text's own stated measure, the I as "phänomenologisches Datum" (phenomenological datum) admitted only within its "evident co-givenness" (§57). For Ideen I the pure I is a *datum*, inside givenness. Marion's "outside being" keeps that, and adds "outside being" where Husserl said "outside content". Marion's note lists Merleau-Ponty, Levinas, Henry and Derrida as developing "une doctrine sans l'être du Je" (a doctrine of the I without being). Henry's starting point was Ideen I's "Das Erlebnis stellt sich nicht dar" (the experience does not present itself, §44), the being given without appearing that II.4 ¶4 found unremarked.

The greatness of phenomenology is that possibility surpasses actuality. Its present possibility is neither the scientific restoration of objectness nor the Seinsfrage, but to determine how the I that reduces even the ontologies "n'a pas lui-même d'abord à être" (does not itself first have to be). Husserl glimpsed this without developing it: "ce que Husserl ouvre de possibilité, il ne le voit pas" (the possibility Husserl opens, he does not see; R V.7 ¶7). No P-locus. The sentence names exactly the relation between R and P that this chapter has enacted: Ideen I's pure I is the "possibility" Marion takes up, and its "actuality" (a pole of relations, admitted as a datum, exempted from switching off) is what he declines.

Chapter V is the book's direct reading of Husserl's ontology, and in it Ideen I is read more closely and more fairly than before. The analysis of §§8–10 is exact, the distinction between objectness and objectivity is kept, and the architecture of Part I (ontologies before the principle and the reduction, then switched off in §§59–60) is described as it is. The final thesis, an I "outside being", rests on 1913 texts that bear it as an extrapolation: §49's formal community of "being", §80's indescribable I, and §57's I exempted from switching off and not constituted. Five departures carry weight. (1) Ideen I is made to "entrust phenomenology with ontology", where it restores the *name* for eidetic sciences that phenomenology switches off and clarifies (§§10, 59–60, 76). (2) §10's categories of signification are said not to be logical categories, though §10 counts them among them and puts apophantics within formal ontology. Formal ontology's "être" replaces Husserl's "Etwas sein", and §148's 1913 equivalence of logic and ontology is presented as FTL's late admission. (3) §60's "transzendent-eidetisch" becomes "transcendental", reversing which domain the extended reduction brackets. (4) The "region of absolute being", "unworldly, unreal" are autumn-1929 Beilagen printed by Biemel inside Ideen I. (5) The charge that the "thing" in quotation marks replaces the thing passes over §§89 and 128–131, where Ideen I analyses the relation of noematic sense to "its" object, the tree that "can burn". Chapter IV called the cardinal difference quasi-ontological. Chapter V calls it not ontological at all, and the change of verdict enables the chapter's conclusion: an I outside being can only be reached through a difference that is not ontological. Ideen I supports neither verdict exclusively, since its difference is drawn from modes of givenness (§42). Marion's next chapter, which makes givenness itself the last word, is closer to that ground than either.

## VI. The Nothing and the Claim

### 1 - Access to the "Phenomenon of Being"

SZ's aporia is that it cannot reach the "phenomenon of being" directly, since its question of being is always the question of Dasein's being. Two reasons keep the access open. (a) SZ intends to construct the question of being as such, with Dasein as its worker, not its goal. (b) "En stricte phénoménologie, l'ultime instance de décision reste « le principe de tous les principes », à savoir la donation justifiée inconditionnellement par la présence intuitionnée" (in strict phenomenology the last court of decision remains "the principle of all principles", namely givenness justified unconditionally by intuited presence; R VI.1 ¶1, nn. 1–2). Ideen I §24 is now the court before which Heidegger's phenomenon of being must appear. Marion uses Husserl's principle to test Heidegger, a reversal of chapters II–IV, where Heidegger judged Husserl. His gloss on the principle departs from §24 in one word. "Inconditionnellement" (unconditionally) contradicts the clause of limits, "aber auch nur in den Schranken, in denen es sich da gibt" (but also only within the limits in which it gives itself there; Hua III/1 51). Marion quoted that clause himself at I.3 ¶2 and II.3 ¶1. In §24 the right of intuition is conditioned by the measure of what is given. That condition is what will tell against the "phenomenon of being", which by Heidegger's account is never given in person as a being is.

The question doubles: does being give itself as a phenomenon satisfying the principle, and is Dasein finally defined by its destination to being? Difficulties come from chapters II, V and IV: the reduction may founder in objectivity, may free an I "outside being", and Dasein's difference of mode of being does not coincide with ontological difference (R VI.1 ¶2, nn. 3–5). The three cross-references summarize the book's reading of Husserl: objectivity (chapter II, the "flat" phenomenon), the I outside being (chapter V, §80), and Husserl's cardinal difference inherited in SZ (chapter IV). The verdicts that the earlier chapters left in tension (II.3 on presence, IV.4 against V.6 on the cardinal difference) are carried forward here without being resolved.

*Was ist Metaphysik?* (1929) repeats the attempt without the analytic of Dasein, keeping only anxiety. The 1943 *Nachwort* says that being "west" (comes to presence) "wohl ... ohne das Seiende" (indeed without beings), a reversal of SZ's "being is always the being of a being", withdrawn in 1949 (R VI.1 ¶3, nn. 6–9). No P-locus. The phrase "sans aucun préalable ontique" (without any ontic prerequisite) states for being what Ideen I §49 states for consciousness, that it "nulla 're' indiget ad existendum" (needs no thing in order to exist). The parallel is structural only: both are absolutes defined by independence from beings. Marion does not draw it.

### 2 - The Tonalities of Dasein

The 1929 lecture starts from the sciences, which relate to "beings themselves — and nothing besides" (*und sonst nichts*). The "nothing" they disclaim returns by the very disclaimer, as a "Rien majeur" (major Nothing) of "double essence" (R VI.2 ¶1, nn. 10–12). No P-locus. The sciences' "beings and nothing besides" is what Ideen I calls the natural attitude of science, in which beings are posited "schlechthin" (simply; §§27–30). Heidegger's starting point is Husserl's starting point for the epoché, and his Nothing occupies the place where Ideen I puts the switching off of the general thesis.

Common sense and Carnap (1932) reduce the Nothing to a logical particle expressing a negative existential statement. Modern metaphysics in general seeks refuge in this reduction (R VI.2 ¶2, nn. 13–14). No P-locus. Ideen I treats negation as a doxic modification whose correlate is "ein Nichtsein, das selbst wieder Sein ist" (a non-being that is itself in turn being; §109, Hua III/1 248, recalling §106). For Husserl the "not" belongs to the noematic being-characters, as Carnap's particle belongs to logic. Neither gives the Nothing as a phenomenon.

Bergson derives the "idea of nothingness" from negation, a substitution misread as suppression, and so concedes the fact he denies. Either negation presupposes the power of the Nothing as its horizon, or it attests it by its weakness (R VI.2 ¶3, nn. 15–19). No P-locus.

Does logical impossibility disqualify effective givenness? Heidegger: "the Nothing is more original than the not and negation", and it must be "zuvor gegeben" (given beforehand), "es selbst" (itself). For Marion that means given "en personne, originairement et intuitivement, donc — selon le « principe de tous les principes » — en se légitimant de droit" (in person, originarily and intuitively, hence, according to the principle of all principles, legitimating itself by right). The Nothing is "the negation of the totality of beings", which must itself first be given. We find ourselves amid beings as a whole in moods: boredom, and joy in the presence of a loved one (R VI.2 ¶4, nn. 20–25). Marion reads Heidegger's "gegeben", "es selbst" with Ideen I's vocabulary: "Selbstgegebenheit" (self-givenness) and §24's "Rechtsquelle" (source of right) in "se légitimant de droit". That is a mediation: Ideen I's principle supplies the standard against which Heidegger's Nothing is now measured. Heidegger's lecture does not invoke §24, and the standard is Marion's. It also has an implication Marion leaves aside. By §24 the Nothing would be legitimated only "within the limits in which it gives itself". The question is not only whether the Nothing is given but how far, and that question runs through VI.3–4.

Deep boredom, analysed in *Grundbegriffe der Metaphysik* (1929/30), renders beings as a whole indifferent, and the "whole" so opened is "the world" (R VI.2 ¶5, nn. 26–28). No P-locus. Ideen I's world is also "beständig für mich vorhanden" (constantly present-at-hand for me) as a horizon of indeterminacy (§27). It is given as the horizon of attention, not in a mood. Boredom gives in one tonality what Ideen I describes as a horizon that is always co-given and never attended to as a whole.

Two remarks for later. (a) Heidegger does not justify boredom's ontological reach: an exceptional being might dissipate it, or boredom might exceed the ontico-ontological field. (b) Why are joy and love mentioned and never analysed (R VI.2 ¶6, nn. 29–30)? No P-locus. Joy and love are "Gemütsakte" (acts of feeling) in Ideen I, intentional and founded on doxic acts, with their own noematic value-characters (§§116–117, 121; Commentary P). Marion's question "par défaut (comme de simples intentionnalités de la conscience)" (by defect, as mere intentionalities of consciousness) names Ideen I's treatment as the defective option.

Anxiety, the fundamental mood, inverts boredom's indifference: beings as a whole slip away and press upon me, and I am anxious "about the Nothing" (R VI.2 ¶7, n. 31). No P-locus.

"Anxiety reveals the Nothing"; "the Nothing itself, as such, was there"; the question about the Nothing "remains constructed". What does the Nothing that is *there* show to being-there (R VI.2 ¶8, nn. 32–33)? No P-locus.

### 3 - The Hermeneutic of the Nothing as Being

Anxiety reaches the Nothing as phenomenon, but the Nothing is "bifid", enigmatically plurivocal, so its entry into phenomenality does not yet manifest the phenomenon of being (R VI.3 ¶1, n. 34). No P-locus.

The Nothing appears "in one with" beings as a whole, as an "abweisende Verweisung" (repelling referral). Referral defines, in SZ §7, appearance (*Erscheinung*) as distinct from phenomenon proper (*Phänomen*). The Nothing, appearing by referral, is only an *Erscheinung* and remains undetermined until it refers to a phenomenon proper (R VI.3 ¶2, nn. 35–37). The appeal to SZ §7's distinction of *Erscheinung* and *Phänomen* is Heidegger's. Ideen I uses "Erscheinung" for the shading presentation of the transcendent (§§41–44), not for a referral-structure. Marion's argument rests on SZ alone here.

To what does the Nothing refer? In 1929 to Dasein's transcendence. In SZ, anxiety's "nothing of the ready-to-hand" refers to being-in-the-world, "the Nothing, that is, the world". The 1929 formulas linking Nothing and being ("Im Sein des Seienden geschieht das Nichten des Nichts", in the being of beings the nihilating of the Nothing happens; they "belong together") stop short of identity and pass through Dasein (R VI.3 ¶3, nn. 38–44). No P-locus.

Only the 1943 *Nachwort* completes the referral: "dieses Nichts west als das Sein" (this nothing comes to presence as being). Several referrals compete for the one Nothing (Dasein's transcendence, the world, being). By what right does it refer to being (R VI.3 ¶4, n. 45)? No P-locus.

How did Heidegger come to recognize the referral to being that neither 1927 nor 1929 achieved? Through "accentuations, substitutions and an addition" (R VI.3 ¶5). No P-locus.

The 1949 marginalia: "Nothing as 'being'", "Nothing and being the same"; "ab-weisen: das Seiende für sich; ver-weisen: in das Sein des Seienden"; "Wesen: verbal"; and the 1931/1949 additions of "ontologische Differenz" and "der Unterschied". Heidegger's twenty years of reworking show that the phenomenon of being was not reached with the Nothing (R VI.3 ¶6, nn. 46–51). This is Marion's most precise use of an author's later notes in the book, and it is the method this commentary has applied to Marion's Ideen I. Marion holds Heidegger's 1949 marginalia apart from the 1929 text, and reads the gap as evidence. In chapters II and V he did not hold Husserl's 1929 additions apart from the 1913 text (guards 1, 14). The asymmetry is the book's.

How is the hermeneutic to be guided, since it must itself make the phenomenon of being appear (R VI.3 ¶7)? No P-locus.

### 4 - Last Call — the Claim

The step from the Nothing to being requires an interpretation: why it is needed, by what guiding thread, and whether it can succeed (R VI.4 ¶1, nn. 52–53). No P-locus.

Its necessity: in anxiety "das Seiende spricht nicht mehr an" (beings no longer address us). The Nothing, silent, says nothing and can refer to nothing but itself (R VI.4 ¶2, n. 54). Marion's "ne <nous> revendique plus" (no longer lays claim to us) for "spricht nicht mehr an" is the first rendering of *Anspruch* in the chapter's sense, "revendication". Its first appearance in the book was the §59 norm (II.3 ¶1), where "in Anspruch nehmen" meant "to rely on" a premise. Marion's French uses one word for both. The continuity is the translator's, and the argument of this chapter does not depend on the Ideen I occurrence.

The *Nachwort* says "das Nichts ... ist der Schleier des Seins" (the Nothing is the veil of being), which keeps the gap, and "man alone, called by the voice of being, experiences the wonder of wonders: *that* beings are", although anxiety has cut off all speech. How could a call sound there, and how would we know that it comes from being (R VI.4 ¶3, nn. 55–57)? No P-locus.

Heidegger's appeal to the mutual belonging of being and beings presupposes what is to be shown, since in "essential anxiety" that belonging is hidden: "nous autres phénoménologues (pour parler comme Husserl) demeurons encore, ici, dans « l'angoisse essentielle » et son Rien, sans portes ni fenêtres" (we phenomenologists, to speak like Husserl, still remain here in "essential anxiety" and its Nothing, without doors or windows). The 1949 correction ("never without beings") strengthens the link to cover an insufficiency (R VI.4 ¶4, nn. 58–59). "Wir Phänomenologen" (we phenomenologists) is Ideen I's idiom: "der Norm ..., der wir als Phänomenologen folgen wollen" (the norm we as phenomenologists mean to follow; §59, Hua III/1 127). Marion borrows Husserl's methodological voice to name the position of one who refuses to cross a gap that has not been shown. The posture is Husserl's §59 norm, to claim nothing that cannot be made evident, turned against Heidegger's step from the Nothing to being. That use of the norm is faithful to its 1913 sense, a rule about premises. Here, unlike in II.3 ¶1, Marion's use of Husserl matches Husserl's.

The guiding thread is the *Anspruch des Seins* (claim of being), unknown in 1929: being calls man, demands an answer and an offering, through the *Ereignis*. "Seul l'être peut appeler à l'être" (only being can call to being), and the phenomenality of being is decided by hearing its claim (R VI.4 ¶5, nn. 60–61). No P-locus.

### 5 - Boredom in Depth

Does the interpretation, backed by the *Ereignis*, finally stage the phenomenon of being (R VI.5 ¶1)? No P-locus.

Doubts: being speaks with a "soundless voice", so how is its silence told from the silence of beings? SZ's call of conscience says "nothing" and calls Dasein only to itself. Gratitude may fail, since the 1941 course admits that we can stay deaf to a claim, and the 1949 corrections subordinate gratitude (*Danken*) to thinking (*Denken*) (R VI.5 ¶2, nn. 62–66). No P-locus.

If being is accessible only through a claim that can be refused, the ontological hermeneutic of the Nothing *can* fail, which calls for a "contre-existential" (counter-existential) that suspends Dasein's destination to being. Might the blank voice reveal an abyss prior to being (R VI.5 ¶3)? No P-locus. The counter-existential that "suspends" is a bracketing applied to being's claim. The reduction is generalized beyond Ideen I's specific epoché, as II.6 announced.

The counter-existential is boredom, which Heidegger confined to an ontological role. Could boredom free us from being's call, so that "der Anspruch des Seins spricht nicht mehr an" (R VI.5 ¶4, n. 67)? No P-locus.

Pascal's boredom belongs to the human condition. Man keeps his distance from all that would determine him as a being, and boredom can be a disgust even with "spiritual things" (R VI.5 ¶5, nn. 68–69). No P-locus.

Boredom is not nihilism, which evaluates and affirms; not negation, which predicates and desires; not anxiety, in which beings fail. In boredom beings remain in full splendour, and the one who is bored withdraws (R VI.5 ¶6). No P-locus.

Boredom leaves beings intact and abandons them. *Ennui* comes from *in odio*: it "hates" by radical lack of interest. "La mise entre parenthèses des choses mondaines ne réduit pas à la région de la conscience, mais découvre que toute conscience s'absente" (the bracketing of worldly things does not reduce to the region of consciousness but discovers that all consciousness absents itself; R VI.5 ¶7, n. 70). This sentence sets boredom against Ideen I's reduction, term for term. In 1913 the bracketing of the world reduces to consciousness as the "phänomenologisches Residuum" (§33). Of the annihilation of the world Husserl writes: "Wir haben eigentlich nichts verloren, aber das gesamte absolute Sein gewonnen" (we have really lost nothing, but have gained the whole of absolute being; §50, Hua III/1 107). Boredom inverts §§49–50: Husserl's experiment destroys the world and keeps consciousness, and Marion's boredom keeps the world and loses consciousness. The inversion is exact and Marion intends it. Ideen I has a name for the absence Marion describes, which he does not take up: inactual consciousness, the "Hintergrund" (background) of experiences to which no glance is turned (§§35, 45), and acts merely "stirring" rather than performed, "Aktregungen" (§115; Commentary P on §115). On Ideen I's terms, the bored consciousness has not absented itself. It has passed from actuality to inactuality, still "conscious" and still reflectively graspable (§45). Marion's claim that consciousness is absent thus needs more than the description of boredom provides. Ideen I's question is whether inactuality is absence, and Marion does not ask it.

A double defeat. (a) The bored I abandons itself, becomes impersonal: "je ne suis plus, *Je* est" (I no longer am; the I is). It loses "its character as the being in whose being being is at issue". (b) Beings are left "comme si de rien n'était" (as if nothing were the case), present but struck with vanity (R VI.5 ¶8, nn. 71–74). The impersonal I who no longer attends has a counterpart in Ideen I's pure I, whose "Blickstrahl" (ray of regard) goes "durch jedes aktuelle *cogito* auf das Gegenständliche" (through every actual cogito toward the objective) and is "mit dem neuen neu hervorschießend und mit ihm verschwindend" (shooting forth anew with each new one and vanishing with it), while "das Ich aber ist ein Identisches" (the I, however, is an identical; §57, Hua III/1 123). Boredom suspends the ray. Whether it suspends the identical I is the question Ideen I would put. Marion's "je ne suis plus, *Je* est" can be read as an answer in Husserl's terms: the ray is gone, and an identical "I" without a ray remains, which is §80's I without its modes of relation, "reines Ich und nichts weiter". The phrase that served chapter V as the I outside being here describes the bored I.

If boredom hates all that is and disqualifies every call, why should being's claim be exempt, and does boredom not obscure the phenomenon of being that anxiety claimed to disclose (R VI.5 ¶9)? No P-locus.

### 6 - The Third Reduction

Boredom suspends the claim of being: the I can withdraw from the destiny of Dasein and suspend every claim. Two theses must be established: that being is exposed to boredom, and that Dasein can be bored ontologically (R VI.6 ¶1, n. 75). No P-locus.

Being is exposed to boredom because it demands wonder: Plato's *thaumazein*, Descartes' admiration ("the first of all the passions"), Heidegger's "wonder of wonders". Boredom can be deaf to the call and blind to the wonder (R VI.6 ¶2, nn. 76–78). The wonder of Ideen III (chapter V.7) is not recalled here, though Marion's note to it (V n. 61) announced this section. Ideen I's "wunderbare Eigenheit" (wonderful peculiarity) of intentionality (§84, Hua III/1 189) belongs to the series: Husserl too places a wonder at the origin of phenomenology, and makes it a programme of research. Boredom, on Marion's account, could be indifferent to that wonder as well. The third reduction would then suspend intentionality's wonder along with being's.

Dasein can be bored ontologically through two features of its being. Inauthenticity can mean refusing to stake oneself on being. Dasein's "having to be" its being implies that it can fail to take the being of beings as its ownmost. Boredom frees the *there* for "an other instance than the being of beings" (R VI.6 ¶3, nn. 79–81). No P-locus.

The *there* freed from being's call is exposed to other calls: the Father's claim (Heidegger, *Letter on Humanism*), Levinas's face, and the *Shema*, "Écoute, Israël". Before any message, the call performs the call as such: "Avant que l'être n'ait revendiqué, l'appel comme pur appel revendique" (before being has made its claim, the call as pure call lays claim; R VI.6 ¶4, nn. 82–83). No P-locus.

Is this still phenomenology? Yes, by the same right by which the analytic of Dasein replaced the constitution of the transcendental I: both proceed by reduction. After the transcendental reduction (objects to the consciousness of an I) and the existential (beings to Dasein, beings to being), comes the reduction "to and of the call". "La réduction transcendantale ne pouvait donner aucun objet au *Je*, si ce dernier n'admettait la donation à lui faite comme une parfaite effectivité — selon le « principe de tous les principes »" (the transcendental reduction could give no object to the I unless the latter admitted the givenness made to it as a perfect actuality, according to the principle of all principles). The call is "le schème originaire des deux réductions antérieures" (the original schema of the two earlier reductions; R VI.6 ¶5, n. 84). Here Ideen I is defined as the first of three reductions, and the definition departs from Ideen I on three points. (1) Ideen I's reduction is a switching off and bracketing of the general thesis (§§31–32). "Reconduction à…" (leading back to) is Marion's general concept, which Ideen I's word "Residuum" fits only at its result. (2) The principle of all principles is not the reduction's condition in Ideen I. It precedes the reduction (Part I) and governs every intuition, natural and eidetic. Its clause of limits forbids "perfect actuality" except where givenness is adequate. Adequate givenness of the thing is in principle impossible and only an "Idea in the Kantian sense" (§§138, 143). (3) "Objects to the consciousness of an I": Ideen I's residue is consciousness as a "Seinsregion" (§33), the pure I is admitted only as a co-given datum (§57), and constitution is the work of consciousness's noetic-noematic structures (§§86, 149–153). The "constituting I" of the first reduction is Husserl's later doctrine (CM). The schema "the call as original schema of the earlier reductions" holds for Ideen I in one respect Marion could have cited. The pure I's "Blickstrahl" (§57) is a turning *toward*, a "Zuwendung" (§§35, 37), and a structure of address runs through Ideen I's attention. Its direction is the reverse of the call's: from the I to the object.

### 7 - "There" outside Being

Boredom makes the call appear as such: nothing gives itself as a phenomenon unless a response gives itself over to it as to an originary claim, and "l'*Anspruch* précède et rend seul possible le *Sein*" (the claim precedes being and alone makes it possible). Neither constituting I nor Dasein, who receives the call (R VI.7 ¶1)? The first denial, "ni *Je* constituant" (neither constituting I), uses the term that VI.6 ¶5 gave Ideen I. Ideen I's subject is not a "constituting I" (see above). Marion's denial targets the Husserl of the *Cartesian Meditations*.

The claim calls *me*: before I can say *I*, I answer "Me voici !" (here I am!). This is not the classical critique of the empirical I in favour of a transcendental I, "à la manière soit de Kant, soit du premier Husserl et de Sartre" (in the manner of Kant, or of the first Husserl and Sartre). Such critiques restore a transcendental I "non objectivé parce qu'objectivant, non constitué parce que constituant, sauf de la réduction parce que réduisant" (non-objectified because objectifying, non-constituted because constituting, exempt from the reduction because reducing). The *me* refers to no transcendental I, only to the claim, in the accusative (R VI.7 ¶2). Three of Marion's five predicates of the transcendental I are Ideen I's own. It is "nicht konstituierte" (not constituted, §57), exempt from switching off ("einer Ausschaltung nicht unterziehen dürfen", we may not subject it to switching off, §57, Hua III/1 124), and "Transzendenz in der Immanenz" (§57). "Autarcique et inconditionné" (autarkic and unconditioned) are not Ideen I's: its pure I is "völlig leer an Wesenskomponenten" (wholly empty of essential components) apart from its modes of relation (§80), a ray toward the object that "vanishes" with each cogito (§57). It is relational through and through, which is the property Marion claims for the interloqué against it (¶5 (a)). "Le premier Husserl" (the first Husserl) is the LU, which denied a pure I. Marion distinguishes him from a later Husserl he does not name, and the later Husserl in question is Ideen I. Marion's criticism of the transcendental I therefore applies to Ideen I's I only in its exemption from the reduction. In its relationality, Ideen I's I is nearer to the interloqué than Marion allows, though turned the other way.

Dasein, likewise, exposes itself to being only because a claim calls it to be the *there*. "Il faudra apprendre à lire *Dasein* plus comme *Da (sein)* que comme *(da) Sein*" (one must learn to read *Dasein* more as *Da(sein)* than as *(da)Sein*); being lodges there "comme un errant reçu chez un hôte" (as a wanderer received by a host). The locative *there* and the accusative *me* agree (R VI.7 ¶3). No P-locus.

Does the alteration dissolve every interlocutor of the call? The interlocutor is the one the claim interloques: "l'interloqué (*der Angesprochene*)" (the one addressed; R VI.7 ¶4). No P-locus. The German gloss, "der Angesprochene", takes up "das Seiende spricht nicht mehr an" (VI.4 ¶2) and makes the addressee the passive participle of the claim.

(a) The interloqué receives a convocation and renounces autarky; the call precedes "toute forme d'intentionnalité ou d'être-au-monde" (every form of intentionality or being-in-the-world), and relation subverts substance: "un sujet sans subjecti(vi)té" (a subject without subjectity or subjectivity). (b) Surprise, the antagonist of every ecstasy, of intentionality as of resolute anticipation, seizes the interloqué from an absolutely foreign event and forbids it to comprehend the convocation it receives (R VI.7 ¶5). Two points concern P. (a) "Precedes every form of intentionality": Ideen I makes intentionality the principal theme of phenomenology (§84) and calls it a "universelles Medium" (universal medium) bearing all experiences (§85, Hua III/1 192). A call that precedes intentionality would be, for Ideen I, an experience without intentional structure: a hyletic datum or a pure affection, which Ideen I treats as matter for intentional forming (§85). Marion's claim that the call precedes intentionality removes the interloqué from the field of Ideen I's description entirely. It is the point at which the book's relation to P is a break, not a reading. (b) "Surprise as antagonist of ecstasy": Ideen I's intentionality is not Heidegger's ecstasy, and Husserl's account of attention includes "Affektion" (affection) as the pull of the object on the I, which Ideen I names and does not analyse (§§35, 37; an addition in copy F to §45, Hua III/1 95, recorded in Hua III/2, names "»Affektion« und Wesensbedingungen der Affektion" (affection and essential conditions of affection) as "eine besondere, hier nicht zu erörternde Frage", a special question not to be discussed here). In Husserl's later hand the I is solicited before it attends, which is the near Husserlian ground of "surprise". Marion does not cite it, and it belongs to Husserl after 1913.

(c) The interloqué does not dissolve: as *me* and as *there* it identifies itself by responding, and mineness is accomplished without resolute anticipation, "pourvu qu'une revendication m'impose un choix" (provided a claim imposes a choice on me). (d) The interloqué is subject to an "interlocutory judgment", a question of fact (which claim?) before any question of right, a facticity "absolument autre et antécédente" (absolutely other and antecedent) to Dasein's own (R VI.7 ¶6, n. 85). No P-locus. The distinction of fact and right applied to the subject inverts Ideen I's order. In 1913 the eidetic (right) precedes the factual. The "Ich bin" is "das allgemeine natürliche Faktum" (the general natural fact) whose essential content is to be studied (§34), and to begin a phenomenological science of facts before the eidetic doctrine would be "ein nonsens" (nonsense; §62, Anmerkung, Hua III/1 134). Marion's interloqué makes a fact, the claim, precede every eidetic determination of the subject: "un *a priori* essentiellement après coup" (an essentially after-the-fact *a priori*).

Convocation, surprise, identification and facticity institute the interloqué "sans aucun autre présupposé que la pure forme de l'appel" (without any presupposition but the pure form of the call), which originates in "the first passion, admiration" (R VI.7 ¶7). No P-locus.

Objection: who or what claims the interloqué? Naming God, being, the other or auto-affection would make it a derivative, regional subject. In strict description I recognize myself interloqued before knowing by what. The indeterminacy of the claiming instance alone makes the claim possible: "Sans savoir ni par qui, ni par quoi, je *me* sais dès l'origine déjà interloqué" (without knowing by whom or by what, I know myself from the origin already interloqued; R VI.7 ¶8). The appeal to "stricte description phénoménologique" (strict phenomenological description) restores Ideen I's criterion of the beginning of this chapter. The indeterminacy of the claiming instance plays the role §80 assigned to the pure I's emptiness: something given whose content cannot be described. The structure is Husserl's indescribable pole moved from the subject to the source of the call.

Chapter VI uses Ideen I at its beginning and its end. At the beginning the principle of all principles is the court before which Heidegger's phenomenon of being must appear, with "unconditionally" where §24 says "only within the limits". At the end the first reduction is defined by an I that constitutes objects under the principle of all principles. Between the two, the boredom that "does not reduce to the region of consciousness but discovers that all consciousness absents itself" is an exact inversion of Ideen I §§49–50, which Marion intends and does not cite. Three findings concern P. First, the definition of the "first reduction" (an autarkic, constituting I receiving objects as perfect actuality) is Ideen I combined with the *Cartesian Meditations*, without §24's limits, §143's Idea, or §80's relational emptiness. Second, the interloqué's relationality, a subject derived from a relation, is closer to Ideen I's pure I than Marion's opposition to "the transcendental I" allows. The difference lies in direction: Husserl's ray goes from the I to the object, the call comes to *me*. Third, the claim that the call precedes intentionality is where the book stops reading Ideen I and leaves it. That is a decision, not a misreading. Marion states it as the consequence of a reduction pushed past every limit the 1913 reduction set itself. Marion holds Heidegger's 1949 marginalia apart from the 1929 lecture with exemplary care. He did not do the same for Husserl's 1929 additions to the 1913 book.

## The Figures of Givenness

"As much appearance, so much being": a thesis "common to Husserl and Heidegger". It presupposes that appearing suffices for being only insofar as appearing gives itself perfectly, and it does so only insofar as it is reduced to its givenness to consciousness. Hence "autant de réduction, autant de donation" (so much reduction, so much givenness): the more the reduction narrows its point of reference, the wider the givenness (R Figures ¶1). The thesis is Husserl's for the transcendent only. In Ideen I the real has "bloß phänomenales Sein" (merely phenomenal being, §44), while consciousness is "prinzipiell nicht durch Abschattung und Erscheinung zu geben" (in principle not to be given through shading and appearance, §49, Hua III/1 105). As much appearance, so much being holds for one side of the cardinal difference. On the other side, being is given without appearing. The formula "so much reduction, so much givenness" has an exact 1913 precedent, which Marion does not cite: of the world switched off, "Wir haben eigentlich nichts verloren, aber das gesamte absolute Sein gewonnen" (we have really lost nothing, but have gained the whole of absolute being; §50, Hua III/1 107). Husserl's sentence states the inverse proportion as Marion states it: the most radical narrowing yields the whole. The book's concluding law is thus Ideen I §50's, generalized from the reduction to consciousness to every reduction.

The law requires, for each reduction, a constellation: (a) to whom things are led back, (b) what is given, (c) how, within what horizon, (d) how far, what is excluded (R Figures ¶2). No P-locus. The four questions fit Ideen I's reduction well. Marion's answers for the first reduction come in the next paragraph.

The first reduction, transcendental: (a) to the intentional, constituting I; (b) gives constituted objects; (c) in regional ontologies, through formal ontology, within the horizon of objectness; (d) excludes whatever cannot be led back to objectness, "à savoir les différences principielles de manières d'être (de la conscience, de l'ustensilité, du monde)" (namely the differences in principle of modes of being: of consciousness, of equipmentality, of the world; R Figures ¶3). Answers (b) and (c) describe Ideen I's architecture accurately (§§9–10, 148–153). Answer (a) gives the 1913 reduction a constituting I that Ideen I does not posit (see VI.6 ¶5, VI.7 ¶2). Answer (d) contradicts Ideen I on its central point. The reduction does not exclude the difference in principle of the mode of being of consciousness. It is the method by which that difference is first won: "dieser radikalsten aller Seinsunterscheidungen — Sein als Bewußtsein und Sein als sich im Bewußtsein »bekundendes«, »transzendentes« Sein —, die, wie man einsieht, nur durch die Methode der phänomenologischen Reduktion in ihrer Reinheit gewonnen und gewürdigt werden kann" (this most radical of all distinctions of being, being as consciousness and being as transcendent being "announcing itself" in consciousness, which, as one sees, can be won and appreciated in its purity only through the method of phenomenological reduction; §76, Hua III/1 159). The world as horizon (§27) and objects of use (§27) are described in the natural attitude and return under the reduction as noematic correlates (§76, "Vorzeichenänderung", change of sign). Of the three exclusions Marion lists, the first is the contrary of what Ideen I says. The second and third concern modes of being that Ideen I describes but does not treat as modes of being in Heidegger's sense. The book's last word on Ideen I thus repeats Heidegger's GA 20 verdict, that Husserl did not ask about the being of consciousness, in a form that removes the difference Husserl asserted. Chapter IV had established that difference as the origin of SZ's "ontological difference".

The second reduction, existential or ontological: (a) to Dasein, its intentionality enlarged to being-in-the-world; (b) gives the modes of being and the phenomenon of being; (c) within the horizon of time; (d) excludes what does not have to be, the conditions of the phenomenon of being (boredom, claim) (R Figures ¶4). No P-locus. "Intentionality enlarged to being-in-the-world" names Heidegger's relation to Ideen I §84 in one phrase: Husserl's principal theme, widened.

The third reduction "à proprement parler n'*est* pas" (strictly speaking *is* not): (a) to the interloqué; (b) it gives "le don lui-même : le don de se rendre *ou* de se soustraire à la revendication de l'appel" (the gift itself: the gift of yielding *or* withdrawing from the claim of the call); (c) within no horizon but the unconditioned call and the unconstrained response; (d) excludes nothing, and calls what does not have to be and what must be. Rigorous paradoxes remain to be worked out to think givenness "as originally unconditioned" (R Figures ¶5–¶6). No P-locus. Answer (c), "the call absolutely without condition", is the book's final restatement of §24 without its clause of limits. In I.3 Marion read that clause as the measure of total presence. In VI.1 he dropped it from his gloss. Here a givenness without condition becomes the third reduction's horizon. Ideen I's principle conditions the right of every givenness by its limits. Marion's "givenness as originally unconditioned" takes the principle's first half, "whatever presents itself is to be accepted simply as what it gives itself as", and drops the second.

Over the whole book, Marion's use of Ideen I falls into three phases. In chapters I–II the text is a witness, then a defendant. Its principle of all principles and its §62 longing frame phenomenology as the fulfilment of metaphysics. Its §§24, 43–49 and 153 are read through Biemel's 1950 text, which prints Husserl's later additions ("νοεῖν", "als strömende Gegenwart", "trotz dieser leibhaften Gegebenheit"), and in places through renderings that reverse the German (§153's direction of grounding). In chapters III–IV it is Heidegger's Husserl, the Cartesian of GA 20, and also the source, through §§42, 49 and 76, of the cardinal difference SZ inherited. Marion reads that source with the most care and the fewest errors in the book. In chapters V–VI it becomes the ground of Marion's own thesis. §80's indescribable pure I and §57's exemption from switching off yield an I "outside being", obtained by cutting §80's opening clause on the I's "modes of relation". The principle of all principles, without its limits, becomes the "unconditioned givenness" of the third reduction. Throughout, two resources of Ideen I go unused, and both bear on the thesis of givenness. One is the noema, the sense "in the how of its mode of givenness" (§94), which answers the questions of I.5, II.2 and V.6. The other is §42's grounding of the cardinal difference of being in a difference of modes of givenness (Hua III/1 89), which would have given Marion a Husserlian precedent for a thinking of being from givenness. The book's thesis, that phenomenology is defined by givenness and not by objectness or being, has its best support in Ideen I, in the texts Marion does not read.

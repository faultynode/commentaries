# Husserl, *Ideen III*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md) works through the whole book at equal depth. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries instead: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds that meaning, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the book and in his other works.

The text is the German of Husserliana V, *Ideen zu einer reinen Phänomenologie und phänomenologischen Philosophie, Drittes Buch: Die Phänomenologie und die Fundamente der Wissenschaften*, edited by Marly Biemel, quoted from the converted file in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-5-ideen-iii-die-phanomenologie-und-die-fundamente-der-wissenschaften.md`). Passages are cited by the commentary's divisions (main text §§ 1–20, Supplement I §§ 1–6, Supplements II–IV, the Afterword) and by printed page, "Hua V n", taken from the running heads the file keeps. Many pages have no running head, so a page given here may be off by one. Unit references such as "§3.14" name the fourteenth paragraph of the main commentary under § 3. All English renderings are my working translations, kept close enough to the German to show the structure under discussion. They are not a published translation.

The book rests on the second part of Husserl's pencil manuscript of 1912. Supplement I prints the first part of the same manuscript, the first draft of *Ideen II* (apparatus, "Zu Beilage I"). Biemel collated the manuscript with two transcriptions, among them Ludwig Landgrebe's typescript, into which Husserl later entered additions and marginal notes. Her text-critical notes, keyed "page,line", report these as made "von H. in L." [by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript], and several readings below turn on them.

The file is not reliable everywhere, and three of its faults bear on more than one reading. First, text is lost at five mid-sentence seams: in the opening paragraph (Hua V 1), in § 8 at pp. 40–41 and at p. 45, in § 12 at p. 74, and in the Afterword at pp. 152–153. The readings near them say what is missing. Second, Husserl's spaced-type emphasis has survived only in part, as italics, as bold or as spaced letters, and on many pages not at all. Emphasis is argued from only where the file shows it, and its absence is never taken as evidence. Third, some paragraphs are hard-wrapped with line-end hyphens, and in § 8 the print's margin line numbers have been carried into the text. In quotations, line-end hyphens are joined, margin numbers and footnote markers are dropped, and emphasis broken across a line is given whole. Everything else is quoted as the file has it, and any oddity that matters is discussed in a note.

## Chapter I, opening paragraph, Hua V 1: the division of experience prescribes the division of the sciences

Reads [Ch. I.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the first paragraph under the First Chapter's title heading.

The book opens on the thesis that governs all of it. The sciences are not divided by convention, by the history of their growth or by the habits of specialists. They are divided by the fundamental distinction among the realities that experience gives, material thing, body and soul, because a science of experience has its right only from experience, and it has that right only by binding itself to the sense of what it experiences. The method that secured the distinction Husserl calls "phenomenological-kinetic": it studied the regions at their sources, in the acts that constitute them.

> In our phenomenological-kinetic method we have assured ourselves of the fundamental distinction, governing all apprehension of the world, between mere material thing, body, soul, or psychic I, and have at the same time studied it with regard to its phenomenological primal sources. As an intuitively given articulation of the experienced realities it precedes all thinking, and especially all scientifically theorizing thinking; and insofar as experiential thinking can draw an ultimate ground of right from experience only by "orienting itself" by it, and this involves above all binding itself to the proper sense of what is experienced, it is certain from the outset that such fundamentally essential distinctions of objectivities, which from fundamentally essential separation of scientific domains and for the sense of their problematic must be decisive. This is what we now mean to pursue.

German:

> In unserer phänomenologisch-kinetischen Methode haben wir uns der alle Weltauffassung beherrschenden Fundamentalunterscheidung zwischen bloß materiellem Ding, Leib, Seele, bzw. seelischem Ich versichert und sie zugleich hinsichtlich ihrer phänomenologischen Urquellen studiert. Als eine anschaulich gegebene Gliederung der erfahrenen Realitäten geht sie allem Denken, und speziell allem wissenschaftlich theoretisierenden Denken vorher, und sofern Erfahrungsdenken überhaupt letzten Rechtsgrund aus der Erfahrung nur zu schöpfen vermag, indem es sich nach ihr „richtet“, und darin liegt vor allem, indem es sich an den eigenen Sinn des Erfahrenen bindet, so ist es von vornherein sicher, daß solche grundwesentlichen Unterscheidungen von Gegenständlichkeiten, die aus grundwesentlichen Sonderung wissenschaftlicher Gebiete und für den Sinn ihrer Problematik entscheidend sein müssen. Dem wollen wir jetzt nachgehen.

*Note on the text.* The last sentence but one is broken. Between "aus grundwesentlichen" and "Sonderung" the end of the relative clause and the start of the main predicate are missing, so the sentence as the file has it does not construe. The English above reproduces the break. The main commentary supplies "arising as they do from fundamental features of constitutive apprehension". That is a plausible sense, since the chapter title pairs the regions with "the correlative fundamental kinds of apperception", but the file has no such words and they cannot be checked. This reading uses only what survives on both sides of the gap: the subject, "fundamentally essential distinctions of objectivities", and the predicate, that they "must be decisive" for the separation of scientific domains and for the sense of their problems.

The sentence is built as a conditional that delivers a certainty. "Insofar as" (*sofern*) states a premise any empiricist would grant, that thinking about experience has its right from experience. "It is certain from the outset" (*von vornherein sicher*) draws the conclusion before any science has been examined. The certainty is a priori, and the verb of the first sentence prepares it: Husserl has "assured" himself (*sich versichert*) of the distinction, he has not found it. What turns the commonplace premise into Husserl's thesis is the verb in quotation marks. Thinking "orients itself" (*„richtet“ sich*) by experience. The quotation marks hold the ordinary word at a distance, and the next clause says what it is to mean: "and this involves above all" binding oneself "to the proper sense of what is experienced" (*an den eigenen Sinn des Erfahrenen*). Experience gives right to thinking not as a store of data but as sense, and the sense of an experienced thing includes the kind of reality it is. Here the inference is weaker than the wording. That science must follow experience is granted by everyone. That it must follow experience's division into regions holds only if the regional type belongs to the experienced sense itself, and that is what the constitutive analyses of the Second Book had to show. The opening sentence takes it over as already secured.

The rest of the vocabulary fixes where the distinction lives. It "governs all apprehension of the world" (*alle Weltauffassung*), so it belongs to apprehension before it belongs to theory, and it "precedes all thinking" as an "intuitively given articulation". The doubled "fundamentally essential" (*grundwesentlich*) says that the distinctions are essential, not classificatory. The chapter title gives the scheme the paragraph presupposes: region, "correlative fundamental kind of apperception", science. The first section titles carry it out term by term: "Material Thing, Material Perception, Material Natural Science (Physics)" for § 1, "Body, Bodily Apprehension, and Somatology" for § 2. Each region has its originary act and its science, and the act decides the science. The first paragraph of § 1 accordingly calls material perception "a fundamental kind of perception, in its essence wholly closed off against every other kind of perception" (Hua V 2).

The word "kinetic" points outside the main text. The note to the chapter title sends the reader to Supplement I, which prints the first part of the same 1912 manuscript, so "our phenomenological-kinetic method" names the method of the analyses that manuscript had just carried out. Supplement I § 6 later supplies the gloss (Hua V 129). Ontology is "so to speak katastematic": it takes unities "as something fixed". Phenomenological-constitutive consideration takes "the unity in flux" and follows the processes in which it is the correlate of identity, and so it is "so to speak kinetic or 'genesis'" (*gewissermaßen kinetisch oder „Genesis“*). The opening adjective therefore already says what the chapter's thesis needs. The distinctions among regions are not read off finished objects. They are traced back to the flowing manifolds in which each kind of object is constituted, and only there does it become visible that each region has a manner of givenness that no other shares.

The promise "This is what we now mean to pursue" is redeemed at the start of Chapter II, where Husserl looks back on the analyses of the first chapter (§ 5, Hua V 22). They served "to draw the essence of the categories of reality founded in one another, matter, body, soul and psychic I, from the primal sources and thereby to grasp the original sense of the corresponding fields of knowledge" (*aus den Urquellen zu schöpfen und damit den dadurch bestimmten originären Sinn der entsprechenden Wissensgebiete zu erfassen*). The "primal sources" of the opening return, and the thesis returns in polemical form. Not what calls itself "modern science", nor those who call themselves "specialists", make the method. The essence of the objects and of their possible experience, "that is the a priori of phenomenological constitution", "prescribes everything principial in method". Husserl adds that the general theory of method, a "noetics", is not yet available, and that even without it this much is clear: the method of every science "must be determined" by "the fundamental kind of originary apprehension" that belongs to its category of objects. The opening's certainty "from the outset" thus stands in for a noetics that does not yet exist.

The pair static and kinetic outlived the 1912 draft, though its coinage did not. In the lectures on passive synthesis, a "so to speak static consideration" of two related data shows their unity from homogeneity, and a "so to speak kinetic consideration", which passes comparingly "from kin to kin", shows what that unity is (*in sozusagen kinetischer Betrachtung, im vergleichenden Übergang von Verwandtem zu Verwandtem*, Hua XI § 28, 130). There both considerations are phenomenological, and "kinetic" names one way of describing within consciousness. In *Ideen III* the word draws the line between phenomenology and ontology, and the book's first sentence uses it to claim, for the analysis of constitution, the right to tell the sciences where their borders lie.

## §1, Hua V 3–4: reality as something principally relative

Reads [§1.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the fourth paragraph under § 1.

A real thing, for Husserl, has no essence of its own apart from its causal relations. It is what it is only in causality, as one term in a nexus whose other terms it demands. The old word "substance" can be kept for it, but only in that sense: each real is the bearer of real properties only in its link to others, and a substance that stood alone would make no sense at all. The definitions of Descartes and Spinoza name something else.

> Only one thing should be pointed out, which must constantly be borne in mind in all knowledge of reality, be it material or of whatever kind. According to our analyses, and in view of the essence of the experiences in which reality constitutes itself, knowledge of reality and knowledge of causality are inseparably one. All *science of reals* is *causally explanatory*, if it really and in the sense of objective validity means to *determine* what the *real* is. Knowledge of causal relations is not something secondary to knowledge of the real, as if the real first of all were in and for itself and then only occasionally, as something extraneous to its being, entered into relation with other realities, acting on them and suffering from them (experiencing effects), as if knowledge could accordingly determine an essence proper to it that would be independent of knowledge of its causalities. It is rather precisely the *principle of reality* as such not to have an essence of its own of this kind at all; it is rather what it is only in causality. It is something *principally relative*, which demands its counter-terms, and only in this connection of term and counter-term is each a "*substance*" of real properties. A *substance* (in the sense in which every objective real is substance) that would be alone is a *nonsense*. A substance in the sense of the well-known definitions of Descartes and Spinoza is therefore something principally other than an objective reality in the sense of our delimitations.

German:

> Nur auf eins sei hingewiesen, das bei aller Erkenntnis von Realität, sei es materielle oder welche immer, beständig zu beachten ist. Nach unseren Analysen und in Rücksicht auf das Wesen der Erfahrungen, in denen sich Realität konstituiert, ist Erkenntnis von Realität und Erkenntnis von Kausalität untrennbar eins. Alle *Wissenschaft von Realen* ist *kausal erklärende*, wenn sie wirklich und im Sinne objektiver Gültigkeit *bestimmen* will, was das *Reale* ist. Die Erkenntnis von kausalen Beziehungen ist nicht etwas der Erkenntnis des Realen Sekundäres, als ob das Reale zunächst einmal an und für sich wäre und dann nur gelegentlich, als etwas seinem Sein Außerwesentliches, zu anderen Realitäten in Beziehung träte, auf sie wirkend und von ihnen leidend (Wirkungen erfahrend), als ob demgemäß die Erkenntnis ein ihm eigenes Wesen herausbestimmen könnte, das unabhängig wäre von der Erkenntnis seiner Kausalitäten. Vielmehr ist es eben das *Prinzipielle der Realität* als solcher, ein derart eigenes Wesen gar nicht zu haben, sie ist vielmehr, was sie ist, nur in der Kausalität. Sie ist ein *prinzipiell Relatives*, das seine Gegenglieder fordert, und nur in dieser Verknüpfung von Glied und Gegenglied ist jedes ,,*Substanz*“ von realen Eigenschaften. Eine *Substanz* (in dem Sinne wie jedes objektive Reale Substanz ist), die allein wäre, ist ein *Nonsens*. Eine Substanz im Sinne der bekannten Definitionen Descartes’ und Spinozas ist also etwas prinzipiell anderes als eine objektive Realität im Sinne unserer Abgrenzungen.

The word "substance" appears three times, and each time it means something different. The first time it stands in quotation marks and italics: each real is a "substance" of real properties "only in this connection of term and counter-term". The quotation marks lend the word and restrict the loan in the same clause. The second time it is italic without quotation marks, and a parenthesis fixes its sense at once: substance "in the sense in which every objective real is substance". Husserl has now taken the word over, and he uses it to state the absurdity. Such a substance "that would be alone", in the subjunctive, is "a nonsense" (*Nonsens*). The third time it is unmarked and belongs to others, "the well-known definitions of Descartes and Spinoza". Husserl does not quote them. He has already described the view he rejects in his own words: the real "first of all" (*zunächst einmal*) "in and for itself" (*an und für sich*), entering into relations only "occasionally" and "as something extraneous to its being". The "therefore" (*also*) of the last sentence draws no new conclusion. It records that the rejected view and Husserl's sense of substance have parted company.

The negation is narrower than it sounds. It is "the principle of reality" not to have "an essence of its own *of this kind*" (*ein derart eigenes Wesen*), that is, an essence knowledge could determine "independently of the knowledge of its causalities". Reals do have an essence. The regional ontologies of § 7 describe it. But relation belongs to that essence, so the essence cannot be read off a real taken alone. The second sentence states the ground: "according to our analyses", knowledge of reality and knowledge of causality are "inseparably one". The proof lies in the constitutive analyses of the Second Book. This paragraph only draws the methodological consequence: every science of reals that means to determine what the real is must explain causally. The italics carry the steps: *science of reals*, *causally explanatory*, *determine*, *real*, then *principle of reality* and *principally relative*. The apparatus adds a later layer. The parenthesis "(experiencing effects)" (*Wirkungen erfahrend*) is Husserl's addition in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus 4,9). It glosses the scholastic pair of acting and suffering in plain terms, and so takes the old vocabulary of substance and accident out of the description of real relation.

The paragraph corrects a misreading that the one before it invites. There, material nature appears to the theoretical gaze as "a being in itself, not founded" (*ein in sich Seiendes, nicht Fundiertes*), "something wholly closed" that keeps its "closed unity" (Hua V 3). That could be read as the self-sufficiency of substance. Husserl blocks the reading at once. Material nature is closed as a whole: it presupposes no other kind of being beneath it. No single real in it is closed, since each demands its counter-terms. The two kinds of independence must be kept apart because the book divides the sciences by the first. Physics is independent because material nature presupposes nothing beneath it, and somatology and psychology are dependent because body and soul are founded on matter. The relativity of each real within its region is a different matter, and it decides only that the science of the region must explain. The sentences after the passage add the epistemic side. Causality is "not straightforwardly given". It is conscious along with every real, but "wholly unclearly", and it must first be "prepared out" by theoretical analysis (Hua V 4). The real itself is given only one-sidedly. So explanation is not a method chosen by physics. It is what the givenness of reals demands.

The understanding returns twice in the book, and the second time it is turned around. In § 7 the imaginative variation that breaks the regular order of sensuous schemata destroys the thing, because a "thing is not being in general but something identical in the nexus of causal dependencies. It is something that can live only in the atmosphere of causal lawfulness" (*nur in der Atmosphäre kausaler Gesetzlichkeit leben kann*, Hua V 31). That is the principle of § 1 restated as a limit on variation. In the 1930 Afterword, transcendental-phenomenological idealism holds that "only transcendental subjectivity has the being-sense of absolute being, that only it is 'irrelative' (i.e. relative only to itself), whereas the real world indeed is, but has an essential relativity to transcendental subjectivity" (Hua V 153). The relativity of § 1 holds among reals. The relativity of the Afterword holds between the whole real world and subjectivity. The Afterword defines the absolute by negating the very predicate that § 1 made the principle of reality, and it puts that negation in quotation marks, as § 1 put "substance".

The Second Book had kept the word without the quotation marks. In § 17 of *Ideen II* Husserl writes that "that universal which would surely best be called reality is called *substance*", and he adds in parentheses: "Unfortunately all these philosophical terms are burdened with equivocations and lack any deeper clarification" (*Leider sind alle diese philosophischen Termini mit Aequivokationen behaftet*, Hua IV 54). He settles there on "substantial reality" as his term. In *Ideen III* the quotation marks and the parenthesis that fixes the sense do the work of that complaint. They keep the word and mark that it no longer means what the tradition defined. On this sense of the real the next sections build. The body and the soul are reals too, so each must be what it is in its own kind of dependence. § 3 accordingly separates the soul's dependence on the body from the body's dependence on matter.

## §3, Hua V 11: the two senses of "sensing"

Reads [§3.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the first paragraph under § 3.

The verb "to sense" (*empfinden*) has two subjects, and with them two meanings. When the body senses, a sensation is localized in it as a state of the body: the touch is felt on the skin. When we "sense" things through the body, the same sensation serves our perceiving of a spatial thing, and the perceiving is an act of the I that directs its gaze at the thing. A separate science of the body is possible only if the first sense is isolated from the second, and this paragraph isolates it by marking the second with quotation marks.

> The separation that we have carried out here under the title of somatology is a thoroughly natural one; it delimits a class of investigations as radically as is at all conceivable for a science, namely by a fundamental form of experience and of experiential objectivity. Nevertheless it is understandable that an independent somatology of its own has never been developed, as it is also understandable that the idea of such a science (important as it is already on grounds of the theory of science) has never been conceived. Its conception presupposes the pure singling out of sensation from the web of apprehension into which it is woven, hence unaccustomed phenomenological analyses, and a turning of the gaze away from what is given in the full apprehensions and determines our natural directions of regard. The body we perceive, but at one with it also the things that are perceived "by means of" the body in their respective modes of appearance, and at one with that we are also conscious of ourselves as human beings and as I who perceive such things by means of the body. The body, apprehended as body, has its localization stratum of tactile sensations, but we touch this thing there, we "sense" the touch of the clothes, and so on. Hence the ambiguity of "sensing". The body senses: that concerns the localized; through it we "sense" things: there "sensing" is the perceiving of spatial things, and it is we who have directed the mental gaze in perceiving at the thing, and this body is our body.

German:

> Die Absonderung, die wir hier unter dem Titel Somatologie vollzogen haben, ist eine durchaus natürliche, sie umgrenzt eine Klasse von Forschungen so radikal, als es bei einer Wissenschaft nur denkbar ist, nämlich durch eine Grundform der Erfahrung und Erfahrungsgegenständlichkeit. Gleichwohl ist es begreiflich, daß eine selbständige eigene Somatologie nie ausgebildet worden ist, wie es auch begreiflich ist, daß die Idee einer solchen Wissenschaft (so wichtig sie schon aus wissenschaftstheoretischen Gründen ist), nie konzipiert worden ist. Ihre Konzeption setzt die reine Aussonderung der Empfindung aus dem Auffassungsgeflecht voraus, in das sie verwoben ist, also ungewohnte phänomenologische Analysen, und eine Abwendung des Blickes von dem in den vollen Auffassungen Gegebenen und unsere natürlichen Blickrichtungen Bestimmenden. Den Leib nehmen wir wahr, aber in eins damit auch die Dinge, die „mittels” des Leibes wahrgenommen sind in ihren jeweiligen Erscheinungsweisen, und in eins damit sind wir unserer selbst auch bewußt als Menschen und als Ich, die da mittels des Leibes solche Dinge wahrnehmen. Der Leib, als Leib aufgefaßt, hat seine Lokalisationsschicht von Tastempfindungen, aber wir tasten dieses Ding da, wir „empfinden” die Berührung der Kleider usw. Daher die Zweideutigkeit von ,,Empfinden". Der Leib empfindet, das betrifft das Lokalisierte; durch ihn ,,empfinden" wir Dinge — da ist ,,Empfinden" das Wahrnehmen von Raumdingen, und wir sind es, die den geistigen Blick im Wahrnehmen auf das Ding gerichtet haben, und dieser Leib ist unser Leib.

The distinction is carried by the grammatical subject and by the quotation marks together. "The body senses" (*Der Leib empfindet*) stands unmarked, with the body as subject. "We 'sense' things" (*„empfinden“ wir Dinge*) stands marked, with "we" as subject and the body demoted to a means, "through it" (*durch ihn*). The marks fall on the use in which the verb means perceiving. Husserl does not call that use improper. He says only that "there" (*da*) sensing is the perceiving of spatial things. The marks hold the second sense apart from the first without ranking them. The cleft sentence that follows assigns the act: "it is we who have directed the mental gaze" (*wir sind es, die den geistigen Blick … gerichtet haben*). The body is the place of sensation. The I is the one who perceives. The paragraph's last clause joins them as possession: "this body is our body".

The "hence" (*Daher*) makes the ambiguity a result, not a looseness of speech. The sentences before it describe three apprehensions given "at one with" (*in eins damit*) one another: the body, the things perceived "by means of" it (*„mittels“*, the instrumental relation also set off), and ourselves as human beings and as I. Because these are woven together, one word serves both the body's state and the I's act. The example sits on the border between them. "We 'sense' the touch of the clothes": the touch is localized on the skin, and yet what is meant is the clothes. The verb falls under the quotation marks because the sentence is about a thing. The ambiguity of the word thus reports a structure of experience, and that structure is the reason no independent somatology has been "conceived". Natural regard goes to what the full apprehensions give, the thing and the person. The localized layer has to be singled out against it by "unaccustomed phenomenological analyses".

The noun that fixes the first sense is missing from the paragraph, though § 2 has just used it. There the physiology of the sense organs and the nervous system is said to be "better a doctrine of the sensings of the body" (*Lehre von dem Empfindnissen des Leibes*, Hua V 8), and the body's "layer of real properties of sensitivity" shows itself "in the original sensings" (*ursprünglichen Empfindnissen*, Hua V 9). *Empfindnis* belongs to the body alone. The verb belongs to both subjects, so the quotation marks have to do for the verb what the coinage does for the noun.

The rest of § 3 turns the ambiguity of the word into a doctrine of the sensation. The phenomenological analysis shows the somatological layer of apprehension "intimately fused" with the layers constitutive of the soul, so intimately "that the apprehension of the soul must necessarily take the sensation-states of the body into itself" (Hua V 11). The next sentences restate the point in terms of function, and correct the *Logical Investigations* (row 4). When § 3 compares the two apprehensions, the noun returns (Hua V 15). On one side the sensation gives "a sensitivity of the body, or a sensing (*Empfindnis*) as a behaviour of it". On the other side, in the experience of a psychic state, "the sensing as what manifests something bodily has no place". Both apprehensions are interwoven "through the double function of sensation, which is double not only in fact but in its basic nature", "interwoven, but neither entering into the other" (*verflochten, aber keine in die andere eintretend*). The two subjects of the verb in § 3.1 become two functions of one sensation, and the rule that neither enters the other is what allows somatology and psychology to be separate sciences. The footnote at "this body is our body" points to Supplement II, the text that once continued here in brackets and that Husserl marked for deletion (row 25).

The 1912 draft printed as Supplement I introduces the noun explicitly. Husserl rejects the current phrase "body-sensations" (*Leibesempfindungen*) as unfitting, "because all sensations whatever are in a certain way experienced as related to the body". He will "speak of *sensings*" (*von Empfindnissen sprechen*) to single out the groups of sensations that have *localization* on or in the body (Supp. I § 4a, Hua V 119). The worked-out Second Book keeps the term in the title of § 36, "Constitution of the Body as Bearer of Localized Sensations (Sensings)". There the same pressure sensation of a hand resting on the table is apprehended once as perception of the table's surface, and, "with 'another direction of attention'" (*bei „anderer Richtung der Aufmerksamkeit“*), as pressure on the finger (Hua IV 147). *Ideen II* puts the direction of attention in quotation marks. *Ideen III* puts them on the verb, because its subject is the division of sciences, and a science is divided by what it means by its words. Somatology is to mean by "sensing" only what the body does.

## §3, Hua V 11–12: one sensation in three apprehensions

Reads [§3.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the third paragraph under § 3.

One and the same sensation enters three apprehensions, and each of them makes something real out of it. In the perception of a material thing it presents a feature of the thing. In the experience of the body it is localized and becomes a state of the body. As a component of the perceiving, which is a state of the soul, it belongs to the soul and to the life of the I. Psychology therefore has to do with every sensation, and it does so from essential grounds, not by accident. The thesis carries a correction of the *Logical Investigations*, whose name for these sensations Husserl now calls misleading.

> The layer of apprehension in which the sensitivities of the body, and so the body itself, constitute themselves proved on the other hand to be intimately fused with those that are constitutive for the soul and the psychic I, and so intimately that the apprehension of the soul must necessarily take the sensation-states of the body into itself. Sensations are, after all, from the standpoint of pure consciousness the indispensable material substrates for all fundamental kinds of noeses; and if the consciousness that we call thing-experience, or again body-experience, contains in its concrete unity essentially sensations as materials of apprehension (in the *Logical Investigations* I said, with a misleading expression, "representing contents"), as every consciousness enters into the apperception of the soul and becomes a real state of the soul and of the psychic I, and with relation to real circumstances, then it is evident that the same sensations that function in the realizing apprehension of material perception as presenting contents for material features receive, in the new realizing apprehension that we call body-experience, localization as sensation-states and now let specific bodiliness appear, and finally, thirdly, under the title of perceptual states of the I (material perceiving and likewise body-experiencing) are components of something psychic and so belong in the soul (namely in the soul's states) and correspondingly in the life of the I. All this one can *see*, can bring it to clear givenness by pure essential analyses, and whoever has followed our demonstrations *has* seen it with us. So it is not accidental but to be understood from essential grounds that psychology, understood as the science of the soul, also has to do with all sensations.

German:

> Die Auffassungsschicht, in der sich die Empfindsamkeiten des Leibes und somit dieser selbst konstituieren, zeigte sich uns andererseits innig verschmolzen mit denjenigen, die für die Seele und das seelische Ich konstitutiv sind, und zwar so innig, daß die Seelenauffassung notwendig die Empfindungszustände des Leibes in sich hineinnehmen muß. The correction is made by substitution, not by argument. The parenthesis withdraws "representing contents" (*repräsentierende Inhalte*) and gives no reason. One clause later the replacement stands in the same place: the sensations function "as presenting contents for material features" (*als darstellende Inhalte*). The noun stays and the participle changes. The corpus text of the *Logical Investigations* shows what the old participle covered. In the Sixth Investigation a mixed intuition has "a representing content" that functions for one part of the object "as an imaging or self-presenting representative" and for the rest "as mere indication" (*als abbildender oder selbstdarstellender Repräsentant … als bloße Hindeutung*, Hua XIX/2 620). The word covered image, self-presentation and sign alike. "Presenting" (*darstellend*) keeps only the function in which a sensation lets a feature of the thing appear. The editor of the revision manuscripts reports a related change of mind from about the same years: the first edition had defined the relation between representing contents and represented features as "a representation of the like by the like", and Husserl now denies that a colour sensation resembles the colour of the thing at all (editor's introduction, Hua XX/1 XXXIX). *Ideen III* states only the result, in a parenthesis. The general term of 1912 is a third word, "materials of apprehension" (*Stoffe der Auffassung*), and with "material substrates for all fundamental kinds of noeses" the sentence speaks in the vocabulary of *Ideen I*.

The thesis rests on the word "the same" (*dieselben*). The sensations that present features of the thing are the very sensations that are localized in the body and that make up, as components, a perceptual state of the soul. Each time the verb is one of making real: a "realizing apprehension" (*realisierende Auffassung*), and then "the new realizing apprehension". The count is explicit, "and finally, thirdly" (*und endlich drittens*), and the third function differs from the others in kind. The first two are functions of the sensation, presenting and being localized. The third is a function of the whole act: the perceiving, with the sensations in it, is a state of the I and so of the soul. The sentence is built as a conditional again: "if" sensations are the stuff of every thing- and body-experience, and "as" every consciousness becomes a real psychic state, "then it is evident". The two premises are doctrines of *Ideen I* and of the analyses of the Second Book. What is "evident" is their conjunction.

The spaced *hat* is one of only two places where the file keeps the print's spaced type, so here the emphasis can be argued from. It puts the stress on the perfect tense. The seeing has already taken place, in the analyses of the Second Book, and the reader who has not followed them is not in a position to object. The next paragraph names that reader. He thinks one must "go to the psychological institutes and ask the specialists" about the essence of psychology, and "I cannot argue with him", since he has not understood what philosophy wants (Hua V 12). Then the thesis of the book's opening returns: the idea of each science and of its method precedes the science as its "sense" and must be grounded "a priori" from the essence of its objects (Hua V 13). The essence of the soul is "not the business of the psychological technician but of the philosopher". The triple function is the first instance. Psychology has to do with all sensations, and the reason lies in the essence of the soul's apprehension, not in the practice of the laboratory.

The count changes when § 3 returns to the point. In the comparison of somatic and psychological experience (Hua V 15), the sensation has a "double function", "not merely factually double but double in its basic nature". The two functions are now the somatic one, a sensing as "a behaviour" of the body, and the psychological one, in which the sensation belongs to a psychic state, "more precisely the state of thing-perception, in which the sensation functions as presenting". The presenting function of § 3.3 has not been dropped. It has moved inside the psychic state, because a thing-perception is itself a state of the soul. Three apprehensions become two sciences: somatology for the localized sensation, psychology for the perceiving in which the sensation presents. The rule that the two are "interwoven, but neither entering into the other" lets the same sensation be counted in both without making either science a part of the other. So the parenthesis of § 3.3 has a systematic point. The *Logical Investigations* named sensations by their function in the act, as contents that represent. *Ideen III* names them first as stuff, and only then counts the apprehensions that make that stuff present a thing, belong to a body and belong to a soul.

## §3, Hua V 17: the soul cannot return to the same state

Reads [§3.14](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the fourteenth paragraph under § 3.

The soul depends on the body, but not in the way the body's sensations depend on its matter. A material thing, and with it the body's layer of sensation, can come back to the same state when it comes back into the same circumstances. The soul cannot. It cannot persist unchanged, and so it can never return to a state it was once in. This one-way character is what Husserl calls its "necessary character of development", and it is the first ground on which § 3 sets psychology apart from somatology and rejects psychophysical parallelism.

> On closer inspection, however, we found in psychic reality, with regard to its dependence on body and matter, something fundamentally different from every other dependence, including the one that belongs to the body: namely the principial impossibility of the unchanged persistence of the soul, and at one with it the principial impossibility of a return to the same state. Already in this an **absurdity** of **psychophysical parallelism** shows itself. If the soul were dependent on the physical body in the same way as sensibility is, it would have to be possible in principle that the psyche of the old man should develop back into the psyche of the child: of the same child with identical states that has become the old man. But that is excluded in principle by the peculiar nature of the soul, its necessary character of development.

German:

> Genauer besehen fanden wir aber in der psychischen Realität hinsichtlich ihrer Abhängigkeit von Leib und Materie grundwesentlich Verschiedenes gegenüber jeder anderen Abhängigkeit, auch gegenüber der, die dem Leib eignet: nämlich die prinzipielle Unmöglichkeit des unveränderten Verharrens der Seele und damit in eins die prinzipielle Unmöglichkeit der Rückkehr in denselben Zustand. Schon darin zeigt sich ein **Widersinn** des **psychophysischen Parallelismus**. Wäre die Seele in derselben Weise wie die Sinnlichkeit abhängig von dem physischen Leib, so müßte es prinzipiell möglich sein, daß die Psyche des Greises sich zurückentwickelte zur Psyche des Kindes: desselben Kindes mit identischen Zuständen, das zum Greise geworden ist. Das ist aber durch die Eigenart der Seele, ihren notwendigen Entwicklungscharakter, prinzipiell ausgeschlossen.

*Note on the text.* The file sets "Widersinn" and "psychophysischen Parallelismus" in bold, with the second broken by stray asterisks. Bold is one of the ways the file renders the print's spaced type, so the emphasis is Husserl's and is kept here.

The sentence states two impossibilities and makes them one: the impossibility of persisting unchanged, "and at one with it" (*damit in eins*) the impossibility of returning to the same state. Unchanged persistence is the limiting case of return, a return at every moment, so one excludes the other. Both are "principial" (*prinzipiell*), and the word recurs a third and fourth time in the example: it "would have to be possible in principle", it is "excluded in principle". The argument lives in that word. Husserl does not claim that a body ever in fact returns to an earlier state. He claims that it could, and that a soul could not.

The subjunctive defines the opponent. Parallelism is the view that the soul depends on the body "in the same way as sensibility" does. The paragraph before has described that way: when the physical body takes on a definite material state, a definite change occurs in the layer of sensation, which "exercises no counter-effect" on nature, so that sensations "seem to follow certain material states of the body like a kind of shadow (as epiphenomena)" (Hua V 17). The soul would then be "a univocal functional consequence of bodily states", and psychology a somatology of a higher level. Against this version the argument holds. If psychic states were a function of present bodily states, then a return of the body would bring a return of the soul, and the old man's psyche could become the child's again, "of the same child with identical states". The weak point lies outside the sentence. A parallelism that tied the soul to the whole history of the body, not to its present state, would not be touched. "Already in this" (*schon darin*) says that the absurdity is shown by this alone. The argument is shorter than the wording makes it look, because it refutes only the parallelism its own subjunctive has described.

The example also says something about the soul's identity. The child "has become the old man", and it is the same child. The soul's identity runs through change that does not repeat itself, whereas a thing's identity is proved by repetition. The last sentence names the essence that grounds this, "its necessary character of development" (*notwendiger Entwicklungscharakter*), but it does not say what develops. The apparatus shows that the ground was there and was cut. Supplement III, it reports, "gives the original text for a passage of the manuscript taken into the worked-out version only in abridged form, namely for the *sentence*: namely the principial impossibility … into the same state, 17,27–30" (src L2415). The original reads (Hua V 130–131):

> The material thing may change as it will; it belongs to its essence (in the sense of the apprehension of matter) that the circle of the thing's states can return into itself. The material real must remain identical, and indeed in such a way that on return into the same circumstances it has the same states, even if factually the course of the world may be such that this is infinitely improbable. The same holds for the bodily real, insofar as sensitivity gives itself in such a way that we can say: if the body is the same with regard to its materiality and its material states, it would also have to be the same as body, and on return into the identical bodily state the layer of sensation too would have to be the same. Quite otherwise the soul: to its essence belongs, under the title of the formation of dispositions, one-sided changeability. It cannot in principle return into the same state.

German:

> Das materielle Ding mag sich beliebig verändern, zu seinem Wesen gehört (im Sinn der Auffassung von Materie), daß der Kreis der dinglichen Zustände in sich zurückkehren kann. Das materielle Reale muß identisch bleiben, und zwar so, daß es bei Rückkehr in die gleichen Umstände dieselben Zustände hat; mag auch faktisch der Weltengang so sein, daß das unendlich unwahrscheinlich ist. Für das Leibesreale gilt dasselbe, sofern sich die Empfindsamkeit so gibt, daß wir sagen können, wenn der Leib derselbe ist hinsichtlich seiner Materialität und seiner materiellen Zustände, so müßte er auch als Leib derselbe sein, und bei Rückkehr in die identische Leibeszuständlichkeit müßte auch die Empfindungsschicht dieselbe sein. Ganz anders die Seele: zu ihrem Wesen gehört unter dem Titel der Ausbildung von Dispositionen einseitige Veränderlichkeit. Sie kann prinzipiell nicht in denselben Zustand zurückkehren.

The manuscript sentence supplies the two things the worked-out text leaves implicit. First, the contrast of three essences: the thing's states can come round in a "circle", the body's sensitivity follows that circle, and the soul's change is "one-sided". The concession "even if factually … infinitely improbable" confirms that the whole argument concerns possibility in principle. Second, the ground: "the formation of dispositions" (*Ausbildung von Dispositionen*). Every state leaves a disposition behind, so no later state can be the earlier one again. The worked-out text shortened this to "the principial impossibility" and added what the manuscript lacks, the polemic against parallelism and the image of the old man. The abridgement kept the conclusion and the polemic and dropped the reason.

This understanding carries the rest of the chapter. § 3.15 restates the one-sidedness of the somatic dependence: the founded layer "brings no circumstances of its own", so the body is a dependent annex of matter (Hua V 17–18). The soul is not such an annex, so it has a causality of its own. § 3.17 draws the conclusion that the soul is a real unity manifesting itself in experiences, not a "bundle" of them (row 6). At the start of § 4 Husserl adds that the ego's development is only one case of the soul's: "how the I as empirical I develops in this, transforms itself, gains ever new dispositions, is only a particular case of the question how soul in general develops" (Hua V 19). Supplement I shows what the formation of dispositions looks like within the life of the I. A proof goes "more easily" on "repetition", and a newly won insight crosses out the earlier errors, so that they tend to be lived through "in the character of being crossed out" when they recur (Hua V 113–114). The quotation marks around "repetition" say what § 3.14 says in principle: the repeated act is not the earlier state again.

The worked-out Second Book treats parallelism as an open question. § 63 of *Ideen II* asks whether the essence of consciousness "raises objection" to a universal correspondence of brain states and experiences, and weighs "the opposed view": that only "the sensory data" have their correlates in the brain, and not "what is noetic in consciousness in a wider sense" (Hua IV 291–292). That is the division of § 3, the somatic layer of sensation on one side and the soul on the other, posed as a hypothesis to be weighed. In *Ideen III* the same division is stated as an essential finding, and parallelism is called absurd outright. The difference fits the two books' tasks. The Second Book investigates how the soul is constituted. The Third assigns sciences their domains, and for that it needs the asymmetry as a thesis.

## §3, Hua V 19: the soul is not a "bundle"

Reads [§3.17](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the seventeenth paragraph under § 3.

The soul is the real unity that manifests itself in conscious experiences. It is not their sum. It stands to its experiences as a real thing stands to its states: it is one and identical, and the experiences are the manifold in which it shows itself. Those who deny it still apprehend by it, so the soul governs psychological research whether or not psychology names it. The passage closes Chapter I's account of the sciences of reality with this correction, and makes it a matter of speaking correctly.

> As far as our investigation has so far got, the *idea of a psychology* results as that of a science directed at psychic reality, which is to be distinguished from somatology, both from physical somatology (which takes its place in the general science of material nature) and from aesthesiological somatology, and on the other hand is interwoven with it, exactly in correspondence with the founding of the realities. If the psyche is not a reality of its own but only a layer of reality above the soma, it cannot ground an independent science. An independent science is physical natural science, and relatively independent within it are disciplines such as physical somatology; an independent science is somatology, but dependent is somatological aesthesiology, and again independent is anthropology (or zoology fully understood). That does not prevent a pre-eminent interest of research from being turned precisely to the soul and to the questions of reality, and so also of causality, that belong to it. Here, however, as in all sciences of reality, the peculiar object is precisely the kind of reality in question, that is, the soul, or the human being with regard to his soul, and the soul is not a "bundle" of conscious experiences but the real unity that manifests itself in them. One can hush the soul up, one can contemptuously call it a *façon de parler*; it is nonetheless what governs in the apprehension and, with the correlatively belonging ideas, what determines in the investigation. But it is better to speak correctly and not to explain away what, so far as there is to be correct thinking, must always remain alive.

German:

> So weit unsere Untersuchung bis jetzt gediehen ist, ergibt sich die *Idee einer Psychologie* als einer auf die psychische Realität gerichteten Wissenschaft, die von der Somatologie, und zwar sowohl von der physischen Somatologie (die sich in die allgemeine Wissenschaft von der materiellen Natur einordnet) als auch von der aesthesiologischen zu unterscheiden ist und andererseits mit ihr verflochten ist, genau der Fundierung der Realitäten entsprechend. Ist die Psyche keine eigene Realität sondern nur eine Realitätsschicht über dem Soma, so kann sie keine selbständige Wissenschaft begründen. Eine selbständige Wissenschaft ist die physische Naturwissenschaft und relativ selbständig in ihr Disziplinen wie die physische Somatologie; eine selbständige Wissenschaft ist die Somatologie, unselbständig aber die somatologische Aesthesiologie, und wieder selbständig die Anthropologie (bzw. die voll verstandene Zoologie). Das hindert aber nicht, daß ein vorzügliches Forschungsinteresse eben der Seele und den zu ihr gehörigen Realitätsfragen, also auch Kausalitätsfragen, zugewendet sein kann. Dabei ist aber wie bei allen Realitätswissenschaften das eigentümliche Objekt eben die betreffende Art Realität, also die Seele oder der Mensch hinsichtlich seiner Seele, und die Seele ist nicht ein „Bündel“ von Bewußtseinserlebnissen sondern die reale Einheit, die sich in ihnen bekundet. Man kann die Seele totschweigen, man kann sie verächtlich als eine *façon de parler* bezeichnen, sie ist darum doch das Herrschende in der Auffassung und mit den korrelativ zugehörigen Ideen das Bestimmende in der Untersuchung. Es ist aber besser, wenn man korrekt redet und nicht wegdeutet, was, soweit korrekt gedacht werden soll, immer lebendig bleiben muß.

*Note on the text.* The file runs "*Ideeeiner Psychologie*" together. The words are separated here.

The verb defines the soul's relation to its experiences. The soul is the real unity "that manifests itself in them" (*die sich in ihnen bekundet*). *Bekunden* is the verb the book uses for the way a real property shows itself in changing states. In § 2 the body's layer of sensitivity is "manifesting itself in the original sensings" (Hua V 9), and in Supplement I § 6 every real unity has its "history" in "ways of manifestation and attestation" (*Bekundungs- und Beurkundungsweisen*, Hua V 129). Applied to the soul, the verb places the experiences where a thing's states stand. They are what is given, and the soul is the identical something that holds across them and is never given apart from them. A "bundle" (*„Bündel“*) is the opposite structure: a whole with nothing in it but its members. The quotation marks show that the word is someone else's, and the text names no one. The two disposals of the soul are also impersonal, "one can hush it up" (*totschweigen*), "one can contemptuously call it a *façon de parler*". The French stays French in Husserl's German, the dismissal quoted in the idiom in which it circulates.

The answer to both disposals is a claim about function, not existence. The soul "is nonetheless what governs in the apprehension" (*das Herrschende in der Auffassung*) and "what determines in the investigation". The psychologist who calls it a manner of speaking still apprehends experiences as states of someone, and his questions about dispositions and causes are about that someone. The argument therefore turns the opponent's research against his theory. It does not show that the unity exists apart from the apprehension of it, and it does not need to. For Husserl a reality is the unity that a fundamental kind of apprehension constitutes, and the apprehension "governs" whatever one says about it. That is why the last sentence is about speech: "it is better to speak correctly and not to explain away" (*nicht wegdeutet*). *Wegdeuten* is a reinterpretation that removes what it interprets. The paragraph asks that the words of psychology say what its thinking already presupposes.

The first half of the paragraph limits what the correction gives the soul. "If the psyche is not a reality of its own but only a layer of reality above the soma, it cannot ground an independent science." The list that follows answers the conditional without saying so. Physics, somatology and anthropology are named independent, and psychology is not named at all. The psyche is a founded layer, and the independent science of which it is a part is the science of the whole animal being, anthropology or "zoology fully understood". So the soul is a reality, but not "a reality of its own" (*keine eigene Realität*). Psychology is a "pre-eminent interest of research" within anthropology. Its object is "the soul, or the human being with regard to his soul". The rejection of the bundle secures the object of psychology. It does not make psychology a science on the level of physics.

The correction ends the chapter's argument and points beyond it. § 3.14 showed that the soul, unlike the body, cannot return to the same state (row 5). That presupposes an identical soul to which the impossibility applies, and § 3.17 names it. The next paragraph opens § 4 by admitting that the account "appears incomplete" because it has not taken "the pure and the psychic I" into account, and it treats the I's development and its "ever new dispositions" as a special case of the soul's (Hua V 19). Supplement I § 2 describes the I that "apprehends itself" as "subject of such real properties", dispositions and traits of character (Hua V 114; row 22). In § 6 Husserl names the reason why a psychology of experiences could pass for phenomenology: "the old mistrust of the reality of soul and I (which also still governed the author of the *Logical Investigations*)" had to be given up (Hua V 24; row 7). The verb returns. In § 3.17 the soul "governs" the apprehension, and in § 6 the mistrust "governed" Husserl's earlier book.

The corpus shows what that admission covers. In the Fifth Logical Investigation the phenomenologically reduced I is "simply identical with its own connection-unity" of contents (*einfach mit ihrer eigenen Verknüpfungseinheit identisch*), which forms itself by the contents' own laws, "without there being any further need of a proper I-principle bearing all contents and unifying them all once more" (LU V § 4, Hua XIX/1 363–364). That is a thesis about the phenomenological I, not about the soul, so it is not the "bundle" of *Ideen III*. But it has the same form: a unity that is nothing beyond its members. Three sections later the same Investigation rejects that form for bodies. "The Berkeley-Humean doctrine, which reduces the appearing bodies to bundles of 'ideas'" fails because the bundles, "the intended complexions of the elements", are never really present in any consciousness (LU V § 7, Hua XIX/1 370). In the *Investigations* the bundle is refused for the physical thing and accepted, in all but name, for the I. *Ideen III* extends the refusal to the soul, on the same ground: the unity is intended in the apprehension and cannot be put together from what is really present in it.

## §6, Hua V 24: phenomenology and rational psychology, once identified

Reads [§6.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the second paragraph under § 6.

A rational psychology is the ontology of the soul: the essential doctrine of a real that constitutes itself in the nexus of experiences. Phenomenology is the essential doctrine of that nexus itself. The two are therefore different sciences, though each can in a sense be ordered into the other. The *Logical Investigations* treated them as one. Husserl now names that identification as his own error and traces it to a mistrust of the reality of soul and I that the first chapter of this book has removed.

> That there must be such a discipline, and indeed one of enormous extent, one not constructed from above out of empty "concepts" (vague word-meanings) like the old metaphysical psychology, but an essential doctrine drawn from pure intuition, came to consciousness only in the investigations on the phenomenology of knowledge (in the *Logical Investigations*). To all earlier researchers of knowledge and researchers of consciousness in general this seems to have escaped entirely, despite all the age-old talk of an a priori of thinking and willing under the titles of logic and ethics. For what was given, and meant to be given, under these titles was anything but psychological essential doctrine in the sense in question here. In the work named, phenomenology presented itself as a purely immanent description of what is given in inner intuition (in a loose way it says there at times "inner experience"), a description, however, that does not establish empirical facts but, in the attitude of "ideation", throughout connections of essence. On just this rested the definitive refutation of psychologism in the theory of knowledge attempted in the Sixth Investigation. Accordingly, in the *Logical Investigations* phenomenological essential doctrine and rational psychology coincided. That this is incorrect in various respects, that a rational psychology must be conceived as the ontology of a real constituting itself in the nexus of experiences and then cannot coincide with the essence of a nexus of experiences itself, we shall easily be able to see once we have clarified, as the idea of reality in general, so that of the reality of the soul, and have given up the old mistrust of the reality of soul and I (which also still governed the author of the *Logical Investigations*). The remarkable relation between phenomenology and psychological ontology, which allows the former to be ordered into the latter, and in a certain way also again the latter, like all ontological disciplines, into the former, will occupy us at length, and we shall come to see parallel relations for the ontology of spirit.

German:

> Daß es eine solche Disziplin, und zwar von ungeheurem Umfang geben muß, eine nicht von oben aus leeren ,,Begriffen" (vagen Wortbedeutungen) heraus konstruierte wie die alte metaphysische Psychologie, sondern eine aus reiner Intuition geschöpfte Wesenslehre, das kam erst in den Untersuchungen zur Phänomenologie der Erkenntnis (in den ,,Logischen Untersuchungen") zum Bewußtsein. The paragraph credits and corrects in one movement, and the grammar keeps the two apart. The credit is stated as a fact of history: the idea of a psychological essential doctrine came to consciousness "only" in the *Logical Investigations* (*kam erst … zum Bewußtsein*), and "all earlier" researchers missed it. The coincidence with rational psychology follows from that self-presentation by "accordingly" (*danach*). It is not a thesis the *Investigations* argued, but what their description of themselves implied. The judgment is moderate: the identification is "incorrect in various respects" (*in verschiedener Hinsicht unrichtig*), not wrong as such. The coming chapters will show that phenomenology can still be ordered into psychological ontology, and the other way round.

Two pieces of the earlier book's vocabulary are quoted and held at a distance. "Inner experience", in quotation marks, is said to have been used "in a loose way" (*in lässiger Weise*). The correction is not pedantry. *Ideen III* uses "experience" strictly, as "a title for acts that exhibit existence, grasping it originally as perception" (§ 8, Hua V 49). A description resting on inner experience would posit the existence of the experiences it describes, and so be an empirical science. The *Investigations* meant "inner intuition", and intuition can serve the grasp of essence without positing anything. The second distanced term, "ideation", is kept as the name of the attitude in which that description established "connections of essence". The quotation marks report the earlier name without endorsing it. The parenthesis on the "empty 'concepts' (vague word-meanings)" of the old metaphysical psychology aims at a different target. Rational psychology in Husserl's sense is "drawn from pure intuition", not constructed "from above" out of concepts.

The ground of the correction is in a subordinate clause, and it carries the weight of the chapter. Rational psychology "must be conceived as the ontology of a real constituting itself in the nexus of experiences". A real is a unity that manifests itself in a manifold. Its ontology is the essential doctrine of that unity, of its properties, dependencies and causality. The phenomenology of experiences describes the manifold. The two "cannot coincide" for the same reason that the ontology of the thing cannot coincide with the phenomenology of thing-perception. But this holds only if the soul is a real. The *Investigations* could identify the two because they did not count the soul as one, and so the decisive clause is temporal: we will see it "once we have clarified … the idea of reality in general" and that of the soul's reality, and "have given up the old mistrust". That is the order of Chapter I. § 1 defined the real as what is what it is only in causality (row 2). § 3 showed that the soul is a real with its own kind of dependence and a real unity, not a "bundle" (rows 5 and 6). The participle "governing" (*beherrschende*) puts the mistrust on the same footing as the soul in § 3.17, which "governs" the apprehension (Hua V 19). The mistrust governed a theory, the soul governs experience, and *Ideen III* sides with experience.

The paragraph promises a "remarkable relation" in two directions, and the book keeps the promise unevenly. § 8 separates rational psychology from phenomenology and both from experimental psychology. Its "there is" passage grounds the phenomenologist's statements in "essential intuition", not experience (row 11). § 11 states the asymmetry of description in physics and psychology (row 13). § 12 returns to "the relation of rational psychology and phenomenology", and there the second direction appears: pure consciousness "has the absolute priority", and every apprehension of an experience as a psychic state "makes an a posteriori out of the a priori" (row 14). The stretch of § 12 that the main commentary reads as the approach to this conclusion is lost in the file (Hua V 74). § 14 puts the second direction into its title, "The Inclusion of the Ontologies in Phenomenology". The "parallel relations" for "the ontology of spirit" are not carried out in *Ideen III*. § 4 has just said that "now there are, for us, simply no spirits" (*jetzt gibt es für uns eben keine Geister*, Hua V 21), and the book never comes back to the point.

The second edition of the *Logical Investigations*, prepared just after this manuscript, made the same correction in the book itself. The first edition had answered the objection "Phenomenology is descriptive psychology" by granting "a psychological grounding of pure logic, namely a strictly descriptive one", and recommending the name "phenomenology" only because some researchers used "descriptive psychology" for an inquiry marked by "the methodical preference for inner experience" (LU, Introduction, § 6, first-edition text, Hua XIX/1 24 n.). The second edition replaced that answer: "If the word psychology keeps its old sense, phenomenology is precisely not descriptive psychology". Its "pure" description is "the seeing of essences performed on the basis of exemplary single intuitions of experiences (even if fictive in free fantasy)" (Hua XIX/1 23–24). The revision says what *Ideen III* says: phenomenology is essential description, not description of facts. It does not take the second step, that rational psychology is the ontology of the soul as a real. That step needs the account of reality that only the Second and Third Books supply, and it is the step this paragraph adds.

## §7, Hua V 25: a "transcendental clue" without a "transcendental deduction"

Reads [§7.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the first paragraph under § 7.

The regional concepts, material thing, body, soul, are derived a priori, but not deduced. Husserl means by their derivation a path that the regions themselves supply: one follows them, finds each concept and grasps it seeing, step by step. Kant's deduction of the categories went the other way, from a system of judgment-forms that is not itself given in intuition. Because the regions are found and not postulated, they can decide how many ontologies there must be, and so how many fundamentally distinct empirical sciences.

> First of all it is of the greatest importance for the philosopher and phenomenologist to have brought to clarity for himself, intuitively and perfectly, what distinguishes the objective regional concepts, as I call them, and what the method is according to which they can be derived *a priori*. This derivation is meant, however, not in the sense of a "transcendental deduction" from some postulate, from some system of thought not itself given through intuition (as the system of judgment-forms is in the Kantian deduction of the categories, as he calls them), and yet according to an apodictically evident "transcendental clue", following which we do not *deduce* them but find them ourselves and can grasp them seeing, step by step. What must be made clear is what gives these concepts their unique significance and predestines them to be the domain-concepts of ontologies, in such a way that there must be *a priori* as many ontologies as regional concepts, whether these ontologies are then rich or poor in content, divide into great sciences or are exhausted in small groups of propositions. What must then further be seen is that all radical classification of the sciences, and in the first place of the empirical sciences, must depend on this concept-formation "region", and in particular that there must be as many principially separate empirical sciences (or groups of disciplines) as ontologies.

German:

> Zunächst ist es für den Philosophen und Phänomenologen von größter Wichtigkeit, es sich intuitiv und vollkommen zur Klarheit gebracht zu haben, was die von mir so genannten gegenständlichen regionalen Begriffe auszeichnet, welches die Methode ist, der gemäß sie *a priori* abgeleitet werden können. Gemeint ist diese Ableitung aber nicht in dem Sinne einer „transzendentalen Deduktion“ aus irgend einem Postulat, aus irgend einem nicht selbst durch Intuition gegebenen Denksystem (wie es das System der Urteilsformen in der kantischen Deduktion der von ihm so genannten Kategorien ist) und doch nach einem apodiktisch einleuchtenden „transzendentalen Leitfaden“, dem folgend wir sie nicht *deduzieren*, sondern selbst finden und Schritt für Schritt schauend erfassen können. Es gilt sich klar zu machen, was diesen Begriffen ihre einzigartige Bedeutung gibt und sie zu Gebietsbegriffen von Ontologien prädestiniert derart, daß es *a priori* soviel Ontologien geben muß als regionale Begriffe: mögen diese Ontologien dann inhaltsreich oder inhaltsarm sein, in große Wissenschaften auseinandergehen oder in kleinen Satzgruppen sich erschöpfen. Es gilt dann weiter einzusehen, daß alle radikale Klassifikation der Wissenschaften und zunächst der Erfahrungswissenschaften von dieser Begriffsbildung „Region“ abhängig sein muß, daß es insbesondere soviel prinzipiell geschiedene empirische Wissenschaften geben muß (bzw. Disziplingruppen) als Ontologien.

The sentence sets two pairs of quoted terms against each other and keeps the adjective in both. A "transcendental deduction" (*„transzendentale Deduktion“*) is refused, a "transcendental clue" (*„transzendentaler Leitfaden“*) is accepted. Both are Kant's words. The second is the title of the chapter of the *Critique of Pure Reason* that derives the categories from the forms of judgment: "Of the Transcendental Clue to the Discovery of All Pure Concepts of the Understanding" (*Des transzendentalen Leitfadens der Entdeckung aller reinen Verstandesbegriffe*, Transcendental Analytic, Book I, ch. 1, in the corpus text of the second edition). So Husserl does not oppose his clue to Kant's deduction from outside Kant's vocabulary. He takes the name of Kant's own clue and gives it another bearer. In Kant the clue is the table of judgment-forms, the very "system of thought not itself given through intuition" that Husserl's parenthesis rejects. In Husserl it is something "apodictically evident", and the following sections show that it is the region, the essence of the object-kind given in intuition. The phrase is the same, and its referent has moved from the forms of thought to the objects.

A pair of courtesies marks the parallel. Husserl speaks of "the objective regional concepts, as I call them" (*die von mir so genannten*), and of Kant's "categories, as he calls them" (*der von ihm so genannten Kategorien*). Each author's term is given back to him, and the sentence does not grant that the categories and the regions are two names for one thing. The verbs settle the method. The concepts "can be derived" (*abgeleitet werden können*), and the following clause explains the derivation by negating its usual sense: "we do not *deduce* them" (*nicht deduzieren*, the italics Husserl's) "but find them ourselves" (*selbst finden*) and "grasp them seeing, step by step" (*Schritt für Schritt schauend erfassen*). The word "derivation" (*Ableitung*) is kept and emptied of inference. What is left is a sequence of seeings, and "step by step" keeps the claim to an order without the claim to a proof.

The inference to the number of sciences is stated as an a priori count. There "must be *a priori* as many ontologies as regional concepts", and "as many principially separate empirical sciences … as ontologies". Two "must"s connect three sets one to one: regions, ontologies, empirical sciences. The concession between them, that an ontology may be rich or poor, "a great science" or "small groups of propositions", shows how strong the claim is. The count does not depend on how much there is to say. A region with little content still has its ontology and still divides the sciences. This is the book's opening thesis (row 1) given its formal shape. There the division of experience prescribed the division of the sciences. Here a concept-formation, "region" (*„Region“*, in quotation marks as a technical term), mediates between them.

The paragraph is a preface to an objection. Its last sentence promises to say only "as much as is needed to exclude misleading empiricist objections" (Hua V 25), and the next paragraph gives the empiricist's question: why should "material thing" play "a fundamentally different role" from "heavenly body", since all concepts grow out of experience by generalization? The answer, in § 7.15–7.18, is that a region is not a highest genus but a frame of all possible change in its objects (row 10). That answer is why the clue must be "apodictically evident". A concept reached by generalization can always be surpassed by a further one. A region found in the essence of the object cannot be, and so it can fix a number. § 7.12 then gives Kant credit of a different kind. Free variation that breaks causal order leaves only "phantoms", and Kant's "unique genius" foresaw the corresponding split between the transcendental aesthetic and the analytic (row 9). Kant is praised for what he saw and corrected for how he derived it.

The 1912 draft printed as Supplement I says the same of Kant in plainer terms. The problems of constitution "move Kant's critique of reason, though not fully matured, but they plainly presuppose a phenomenology and an insight into the constitution of the lower levels of experience, a task that Kant did not grasp and, where he touched on it, did not divine in its magnitude. Hence he already lacks the right formulation of the problem" (Supp. I § 6, Hua V 128). The "transcendental clue" without deduction is that missing formulation in brief: the problem of the categories put as a problem of constitution, to be worked out at the level of the object-kinds. In *Ideen I* the formula becomes a method. In § 149 of the published First Book, read here in the English of the *Collected Works*, "each region here furnishes the clues to an intrinsically self-contained group of investigations", and "let us take, for example, the region, Material Thing, as clue" (the file's OCR slips, "self-co l ained" and "ex mple", are corrected). The 1930 Afterword returns to the word once more, but the file has lost the page before it (Hua V 158). The surviving half-sentence says that the questioning "goes inevitably from the objective sense as 'clue'" (*vom gegenständlichen Sinn als „Leitfaden“*) to its modes of givenness, and upward to "the correlative specifically egoic noeses" (Hua V 159). The quotation marks remain on the word. Husserl never stopped marking it as borrowed, and never gave the clue back to the forms of judgment.

## §7, Hua V 31: the thing lives only in the atmosphere of causal law

Reads [§7.12](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the twelfth paragraph under § 7.

Free variation shows where the region "thing" ends. Imagination may change everything about a thing, its shape, its qualities, the laws of its properties, and the thing survives as long as its real properties stay related to real circumstances. Break that relation and the thing falls apart into phantoms, sensuous schemata that behave as no real thing could. A world of phantoms is no longer a nature, yet it still keeps the laws of time and space. A thing is therefore not being in general but something identical in a nexus of causal dependencies, and the lower layer it is built on has its own a priori, which Husserl finds foreshadowed in Kant's division between the transcendental aesthetic and the analytic.

> It is clear that the totality of arbitrary productions that we obtain from one thing are to be obtained identically from every other; indeed, everything can be transferred continuously into everything, the totality of formations is the same and a fixed one. And yet we see that even in this variation in phantasy, released from all binding by natural law, the system of the offspring of our phantasy still keeps its rules, which entitle us to speak of a closed system: they are the offspring of a phantasy that shapes and reshapes things, constitutes things and again destroys the constitution of things, carries real properties of things through and again gives them up as sham properties. The thing that served us as our starting point changes, remains for a little while something appearing as a thing; and if we deal too freely, if we do not respect the essential relation of real properties to real circumstances, if we do not take care that our phantasy orders the formations so that it allows this relation to be held through, then the thing falls apart into manifolds of phantoms (sensuous schemata), running in a way in which manifolds that constitute real things precisely cannot and may not run. A thing is precisely not being in general, but something identical in the nexus of causal dependencies. It is something that can live only in the atmosphere of causal lawfulness. That, however, demands definitely regulated orders for the constituting sensuous schemata. If freely ruling phantasy breaks through these orders in unbridled fashion, then not only is a single schema transformed into a "mere phantom", but the whole world becomes a course of mere phantoms; it is thus no longer a nature. But it is not on that account wholly lawless. In his unique genius Kant foresaw this, and it is expressed in him in the separation between transcendental aesthetic and analytic. For the mere phantom-world the pure doctrine of time and pure geometry still hold; but it is a world without any physics. There are regulations also with regard to the sensuous fullness of the phantom-extension, but the sensuous fullness attests no material properties.

German:

> Es ist klar, daß die Gesamtheit willkürlicher Erzeugungen, die wir von einem Ding aus gewinnen, identisch auch von jedem anderen zu gewinnen sind; in der Tat, alles ist in alles kontinuierlich überzuführen, die Gesamtheit der Gestaltungen ist die selbe und eine feste. Und dabei sehen wir doch, daß selbst in dieser Phantasie und aller naturgesetzlichen Bindung enthobenen Variation das System der Ausgeburten unserer Phantasie noch seine Regeln behält, die es berechtigen, von einem geschlossenen System zu sprechen: es sind Ausgeburten einer dinggestaltenden und umgestaltenden, ding konstituierenden und wieder Dingkonstitution zerstörenden, reelle Dingeigenschaften durchführenden und wieder als Scheineigenschaften preisgebenden Phantasie. Das Ding, das uns als Ausgang diente, wandelt sich, verbleibt ein Weilchen Dingerscheinendes; und gehen wir gar zu frei um, respektieren wir nicht die wesentliche Beziehung realer Eigenschaften auf reale Umstände, sorgen wir nicht dafür, daß unsere Phantasie die Gestaltungen so ordnet, daß sie diese Beziehung durchzuhalten erlaubt, so zerfällt das Ding in Mannigfaltigkeiten von Phantomen (sinnlichen Schemata), so verlaufend, wie Mannigfaltigkeiten, die reale Dinge konstituieren, eben nicht verlaufen können und dürfen. Ding ist eben nicht Seiendes überhaupt, sondern Identisches im Verband kausaler Abhängigkeiten. Es ist etwas, das nur in der Atmosphäre kausaler Gesetzlichkeit leben kann. Das aber fordert für die konstituierenden sinnlichen Schemata bestimmt geregelte Ordnungen. Durchbricht frei schaltende Phantasie diese Ordnungen in zügelloser Weise, so wird nicht nur ein einzelnes Schema in ein ,,bloßes Phantom" verwandelt, sondern die ganze Welt wird zum Ablauf bloßer Phantome, sie ist also keine Natur mehr. Aber völlig gesetzlos ist sie darum noch nicht. In seiner einzigartigen Genialität hat das Kant vorausgesehen, und es drückt sich bei ihm in der Scheidung zwischen transzendentaler Aesthetik und Analytik aus. Für die bloße Phantomwelt gilt noch immer die reine Zeitlehre und die reine Geometrie; sie ist aber eine Welt ohne jede Physik. Auch hinsichtlich der sinnlichen Fülle der Phantomextension bestehen Regelungen, aber die sinnliche Fülle beurkundet keine materiellen Eigenschaften.

The paragraph describes two limits, and its vocabulary marks each. The first is the limit of the thing. Variation that does "not respect the essential relation of real properties to real circumstances" makes the thing "fall apart" (*zerfällt*) into "phantoms (sensuous schemata)". The manifolds that result run as manifolds constituting real things "cannot and may not run" (*nicht verlaufen können und dürfen*). The doubled modal joins impossibility and norm. Constitution has rules, and to break them is both to fail to produce a thing and to offend against what a thing requires. The definition follows with "precisely" (*eben*): a thing is "not being in general, but something identical in the nexus of causal dependencies". This is § 1's principle of reality (row 2) found again at the end of a variation. There it was stated from the analyses of the Second Book. Here it is reached as the point at which free phantasy stops producing things.

The image says more than the definition. The thing "can live only in the atmosphere of causal lawfulness" (*nur in der Atmosphäre kausaler Gesetzlichkeit leben kann*). Causality is not one of the thing's properties. It is the medium in which any property of a thing can exist at all, and without it the thing does not change its nature but suffocates. The next sentence turns the image back into constitutive terms: this "demands definitely regulated orders for the constituting sensuous schemata". The verbs describing phantasy show the same doubleness across the whole passage. It "constitutes things and again destroys the constitution of things" and "carries real properties of things through and again gives them up as sham properties". So the variation is a test of constitution, not an idle play of images. Real properties are held "through" (*durchführen*) or "given up" (*preisgeben*) according to whether phantasy keeps the order that constitutes them.

The second limit is that of law. The phantom world "is no longer a nature", but "it is not on that account wholly lawless". Pure time-doctrine and pure geometry still hold, and "there are regulations" for the sensuous fullness of the phantom too. What is lost is physics, and the fullness no longer "attests" (*beurkundet*) material properties. Husserl ties this to Kant in a sentence that praises and limits in the same words. Kant "foresaw" it (*vorausgesehen*), and the insight "is expressed in him" (*drückt sich bei ihm aus*) in the division of the aesthetic from the analytic. "Foresaw" says that Kant had the result without the analysis that grounds it, the analysis of constituting schemata that Husserl has just run. The mapping is also looser than the praise suggests. The phantom world keeps more than Kant's aesthetic, since its sensuous fullness has regularities of its own. The line Husserl draws runs between schema and real causality, not between sensibility and understanding. Kant is credited with a division that has the same place in the structure, and his reasons for it are left aside.

The paragraph before sets up the experiment: a piece of gold, the eidetic attitude, freedom from "all the fetters that physics and chemistry could lay on us", and a phantasy that produces "the maddest ghostly things, mocking all physics and chemistry" (Hua V 30). The paragraph after reins phantasy in. "Let us now leave this phantom world. Let us now hold our phantasy in check" (Hua V 31), and the variation starts again from the perception of a tree. Held to the condition that the first perception stay valid as perception of an extended real, phantasy "can no longer behave as destroyer of worlds but only as builder of worlds" (*nicht mehr als Weltvernichterin … sondern nur als Welterbauerin*, Hua V 33). The worlds it builds may have quite different physical laws, but every one of them is a nature. From this the region is defined in § 7.15 (row 10). The idea of the thing is "a categorial (or, as we better say, regional) frame" for every possible sense of such experience. Even when reality falls apart into a chaos of phantoms, "in the idea of the phantom too there lay a rule that spans the circle of possibilities" (*lag auch in der Idee des Phantoms eine Regel, die den Kreis der Möglichkeiten umspannt*, Hua V 33). The two limits of § 7.12 thus become two levels of a priori: the frame of the thing, and below it the frame of the schema.

The understanding returns at the end of the book as a lesson in clarification. "A thing is not given, a concept of thing has not been brought to real clarity, when a thing is simply seen. A phantom is seen too; a simple seeing yields no more than what corresponds to the phantom, namely the sensuous schema" (§ 20, Hua V 103–104). To clarify what "thing" means, intuition "must follow the manifolds of sensuous schemata in relation to the manifolds of schematized circumstances". The variation of § 7.12 has become a rule of method: the clarifying intuition must reach the causal layer, or it has only clarified a phantom. *Ideen II* had made the same point from the side of the single thing, without variation. If we hold to the thing "for itself, disregarding the nexus of things", we "have no means at all of distinguishing the essence of the thing from the essence of an empty *phantom*". The stereoscope is the example, which shows a spatial body with shape and colour and nothing material about it (Hua IV § 15 b, 36). *Ideen III* turns that observation into a limit of eidetic variation, and uses it to separate the a priori of nature from the a priori of space and time.

## §7, Hua V 33–35: the region as the frame of all change

Reads [§7.15](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md) and §7.17–7.18, the fifteenth, seventeenth and eighteenth paragraphs under § 7.

A region is not a highest genus. A genus is reached by lifting out what the material contents of many objects have in common: this tone, tone in general, sensory quality in general. A region is the form that every such content fills and that no change of content can alter. A heavenly body may cease to be a heavenly body, but it cannot cease to be a thing, because the thing-form is the frame in which all its changes take place. This form is prescribed by the essence of a fundamental kind of experience, not by thought, and so there is a difference of a priori and a posteriori within the realm of essences itself, which Husserl calls "transcendental".

> Accordingly an a priori is really prescribed in the course of all possible experience, and evidently prescribed by the essence of thing-perception as a fundamental kind of perception, or experience. Precisely thereby the idea of the thing has a unique distinction: it designates a categorial (or, as we better say, regional) frame for every sense belonging to and possible for an experience of such a fundamental kind, a frame to which, as to a necessary form, all closer determination of an object indeterminately posited in any experience whatever is bound a priori. If anything at all is experienced (within this system of experience), then eo ipso there is posited not merely an object in general but a *res extensa*, a material thing, and this expression determines not a content but a form for all possible objects of possible experience of this kind in general.

> […] The thing is not a genus-concept of the kind of mineral, ranking equally with it and similar genus-concepts, only at most more general. As long as we ascend in the formation of species and genera and form genuine genera, we ascend from the full material essence of the object to general essential traits that could be common to several, to unboundedly many objects; from the material specific essences lifted out, a "common" material content can again be lifted out, and so on. In this way we obtain from the essence of a definite tone, setting it in a series with the essence of other tones, the essence tone in general, acoustic in general, sensory quality in general, and the like. All their material content, however, is in our sphere of realities something "contingent", bound to something "necessary", a necessary form, precisely the one the concept thing expresses. All factual content can change and does change in the thing's *alteration*; only one thing cannot change: the general form of the thing. […] The *what* of the thing, its factual content, may change however it will, foreseeably or not; but the universal that the word thing there means (and it means a great deal) cannot change; it is the frame in which all change takes place. […] So in the world of essences itself and of essential concepts a distinction between *priority* and *posteriority* is prefigured, which surely justifies speaking in a definite sense of *a priori* and *a posteriori* concepts. This sense of a priori belongs to the concepts of realities and is a "transcendental" distinction, insofar as it and its difference from the a posteriori have their source in the fundamental property of realities, to "constitute" themselves as unities of manifolds.

German:

> Demnach ist wirklich im Gange aller möglichen Erfahrung ein Apriori vorgezeichnet und offenbar vorgezeichnet durch das Wesen der dinglichen Wahrnehmung als einer Grundart der Wahrnehmung, bzw. Erfahrung. Eben damit hat die Idee des Dinges eine einzigartige Auszeichnung, sie bezeichnet einen kategorialen (oder, wie wir besser sagen, regionalen) Rahmen für jeden zu einer Erfahrung solcher Grundart gehörigen und möglichen Sinn, einen Rahmen, an den als an eine notwendige Form alle Näherbestimmung eines in irgend welcher Erfahrung unbestimmt gesetzten Gegenstands a priori gebunden ist. Ist überhaupt etwas erfahren (innerhalb dieses Erfahrungsystems), so ist damit eo ipso nicht nur überhaupt ein Gegenstand, sondern eine *res extensa*, ein materielles Ding gesetzt, und dieser Ausdruck bestimmt nicht einen Inhalt, sondern eine Form für alle möglichen Gegenstände möglicher Erfahrung dieser Art überhaupt.

> […] Ding ist nicht ein Gattungsbegriff der Art wie Mineral, mit ihm und ähnlichen Gattungsbegriffen gleich rangierend, nur allenfalls allgemeiner. Solange wir in der Art- und Gattungsbildung emporsteigen und echte Gattungen bilden, steigen wir vom vollen sachhaltigen Wesen des Gegenstands zu allgemeinen Wesenszügen empor, die mehreren, unbegrenzt vielen Gegenständen gemein sein könnten; von den herausgehobenen sachhaltigen Artwesen läßt sich dann wieder ein „gemeinsames” Sachhaltiges herausheben usw. In dieser Art gewinnen wir aus dem Wesen eines bestimmten Tons, es in eine Reihe stellend mit dem Wesen anderer Töne, das Wesen Ton überhaupt, Akustisches überhaupt, sinnliche Qualität überhaupt u.dgl. All ihr sachhaltiger Inhalt ist aber in unserer Sphäre der Realitäten ein „Zufälliges”, das gebunden ist an ein „Notwendiges”, eine notwendige Form, eben die der Begriff Ding ausdrückt. Aller sachliche Inhalt kann wechseln und wechselt in der dinglichen *Veränderung*, nur eins kann nicht wechseln: die allgemeine Dingform. […] Das *Was* des Dinges, sein sachlicher Inhalt, mag sich wie immer, voraussehbar oder nicht voraussehbar, ändern, das Allgemeine aber, das das Wort Ding da besagt (und es besagt sehr viel), kann sich nicht ändern, es ist der Rahmen, in dem alle Veränderung statthat. […] So ist in der Welt der Wesen selbst und der Wesensbegriffe ein Unterschied zwischen *Priorität* und *Posteriorität* vorgebildet, der es wohl berechtigt, in einem bestimmten Sinne von *apriorischen* und *aposteriorischen* Begriffen zu sprechen. Dieser Sinn von Apriori gehört zu den Begriffen von Realitäten und ist ein „transzendentaler“ Unterschied, sofern er und sein Unterschied vom Aposteriori seine Quelle in der Grundeigenschaft der Realitäten hat, sich als Einheiten von Mannigfaltigkeiten zu „konstituieren“.

Husserl corrects his own adjective in mid-sentence: "a categorial (or, as we better say, regional) frame" (*einen kategorialen (oder, wie wir besser sagen, regionalen) Rahmen*). "Categorial" was the obvious word after the dispute with Kant in § 7.1 (row 8), and it is withdrawn as soon as it is written. The correction keeps the region apart from the formal categories, object, property, relation, which hold for every object whatever. The region is the frame for "every sense belonging to and possible for an experience of such a fundamental kind" (*Grundart*). Its scope is the scope of one kind of experience, not of objecthood as such. The next sentence makes the point with a scholastic formula: anything experienced in this system of experience is eo ipso posited "not merely as an object in general but as a *res extensa*", and the expression "determines not a content but a form". *Res extensa* is Descartes' name for body. Here it names a form of experience.

The verb that carries the argument is "means" (*besagt*). The thing's factual content may change however it will, "but the universal that the word thing there means (and it means a great deal) cannot change". The parenthesis forestalls a misreading. A form is not an empty abstraction. "Thing" means a great deal: extension, real properties, dependence on circumstances, causality, everything §§ 1 and 7.12 have found to belong to the thing as such. What it does not mean is any particular content. The quotation marks around "contingent" and "necessary" (*„Zufälliges“*, *„Notwendiges“*) mark that the two words are used in a sense restricted to "our sphere of realities". Material content is contingent relative to the form, not lawless. The marks around "common" in the account of genus-formation mark something else. A genus rests on what objects happen to share. Its "common" is a relation between contents, and the thing-form is not a relation of that kind.

The heavenly body, which the empiricist set beside "material thing" in § 7.2, now serves Husserl's argument. It "can cease to correspond to the idea heavenly body", and "other genus-concepts then take its place". It may "dissolve into gas and disperse in space: thing remains thing", and dispersal or fragmentation "changes nothing in this, for their possibility is itself prescribed in the general form 'thing'" (Hua V 35). The test separates genus from region exactly. A genus can be lost by a change of content. A region cannot, because every change is a change within it. Here the inference is weaker than the wording. That a dispersed gas "remains" a thing is not shown by the example. It is what the definition of the region requires, and the example applies it.

The two italic pairs at the end turn a contrast of concepts into a contrast in the order of essences: *priority* and *posteriority*, *a priori* and *a posteriori* concepts. Both members are essences, so the a posteriori here is not the empirical. "Tone in general" is an essence and is still a posteriori relative to the region, because it is contingent content within the thing's form. Husserl calls the distinction "transcendental", in quotation marks, and gives his reason: its source is "the fundamental property of realities, to 'constitute' themselves as unities of manifolds". Both technical words are held in quotation marks, "transcendental" and "constitute", as if the terms were still being introduced. The distinction is transcendental because it is read off constitution. The region is the rule by which manifolds of experience form a unity of a given kind, and genera are what such unities turn out to have in common.

The pair returns in § 12, where it is turned on consciousness. Of § 12's argument the file keeps only the conclusion, after a lost page (Hua V 74). "Pure consciousness has the absolute priority, in relation to which all being is the a posteriori" (*Das reine Bewußtsein hat die absolute Priorität, in Beziehung auf welche alles Sein das Aposteriori ist*, Hua V 75; row 14). In § 7 the a priori belonged to the concepts of realities, prefigured "in the world of essences". In § 12 the whole of being, regions included, is a posteriori relative to the consciousness in which it is constituted. The word "priority" climbs a level, and the "transcendental" source named in § 7.18 becomes the a priori itself.

*Ideen I* had defined the region in other terms. "A region is nothing other than the total highest generic unity belonging to a concretum, i.e., the essentially unitary nexus of the summa genera pertaining to the infimae species within the concretum". With that definition the concept of region is said to be fixed "in a strictly 'analytical' way" (Ideas I § 16, CW2 English, Hua III/1 31). There the region is a unity of highest genera, defined formally, through the concepts of individuum and concretum. *Ideen III* does not deny this, but it does not take that route. It denies that the thing is "a genus-concept of the kind of mineral", and it defines the region from the side of experience, as the frame that a fundamental kind of perception prescribes. The title of § 7 already marks the change: "Regional Concepts and 'Genus'-Concepts" (*Regionale Begriffe und „Gattungs“-begriffe*). In 1913 the region was the highest genus. In the 1912 manuscript of the Third Book, "genus" is the word set in quotation marks.

## §8, Hua V 47: the phenomenologist's "there is"

Reads [§8.17](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the seventeenth paragraph under § 8.

When the phenomenologist says that there are perceptions and memories, he says no more than the mathematician says when he says that there are relative primes. Neither asserts that anything exists in the world. Both state what belongs to an essence, and both ground the statement on the seeing of essences, not on experience. Eidetic phenomenology therefore contains no description in the sense of the descriptive natural sciences, which record what occurs. Its "there is" is an existential of essence, and the passage holds this one phrase in quotation marks while it moves the phrase from numbers to experiences.

> As against this, in eidetic phenomenology there is no description in this sense at all. In its domain all real existence, the whole spatio-temporal actuality, is in principle excluded. Its findings concern not realities but essences; its truths state what holds *as such*, in unconditional necessity and universality, for such essences, or for everything falling under such essences. They therefore speak of experiences exactly as the geometrical truths speak of bodies, the arithmetical of numbers, the phoronomic of motions: as little as these speak of the earth or even of the factual universe and the bodies, motions, numbers occurring in it, so little does phenomenology speak of psychic beings and psychic states in this universe, in any sphere of actual existence. When the phenomenologist says, then, there are experiences, there are psychic states such as perceptions, memories and the like, his "there is" says exactly as much as the mathematical "there is", e.g. there is a series of cardinal numbers, there are relative primes, there is no regular decahedron. This "there is" is grounded on both sides not by experience but by the seeing of essences. Experience is a title for acts that exhibit existence and grasp it originally as perception. But what the seeing of essences brings to original grasp are not singulars of existence but essences of lowest generality, or, as species and genera, of higher generality; there need be no singular corresponding to them, and if there is to be such a thing, only actual experience can exhibit it. Truths of essence are absolutely binding, not to be overstepped, to be confirmed or refuted by no experience. Truths of experience, statements that by their sense posit existence and so demand experience of existence in their *grounding*, are contingent truths, which, as they are grounded by experience, so are modified and cancelled by experience: they hold only presumptively, subject to further confirmation by experience.

German:

> Demgegenüber gibt es in der eidetischen Phänomenologie eine Beschreibung in diesem Sinne überhaupt nicht. In ihrer Domäne ist prinzipiell jedes reale Dasein, die ganze räumlich-zeitliche Wirklichkeit ausgeschaltet. Ihre Feststellungen betreffen nicht Realitäten sondern Wesen, ihre Wahrheiten sagen aus, was für solche Wesen, bzw. für alles unter solche Wesen Fallende *als solches* in unbedingter Notwendigkeit und Allgemeinheit gültig ist. Sie sprechen also von Erlebnissen genau so wie die geometrischen Wahrheiten von Körpern, die arithmetischen von Zahlen, die phoronomischen von Bewegungen: so wenig diese von der Erde oder selbst vom faktischen Weltall und den in ihm vorkommenden Körpern, Bewegungen, Zahlen sprechen, so wenig die Phänomenologie von psychischen Wesen und psychischen Zuständen in diesem Weltall, in irgend einer Sphäre wirklichen Daseins. Wenn der Phänomenologe also sagt, es gibt Erlebnisse, es gibt seelische Zustände wie Wahrnehmungen, Erinnerungen u.dgl., so sagt sein „es gibt“ genau so viel wie das mathematische „es gibt“, z. B. eine Reihe von Anzahlen, es gibt relative Primzahlen, es gibt keinen regelmäßigen Zehnflächner. Begründet ist dieses „es gibt“ beiderseits nicht durch Erfahrung sondern durch Wesensschauung. Erfahrung ist ein Titel für Dasein aufweisende, als Wahrnehmung originär erfassende Akte. Was aber die Wesensschauung zur originären Erfassung bringt, das sind nicht Einzelheiten des Daseins, sondern Wesen von niederster Allgemeinheit oder als Arten und Gattungen von höherer Allgemeinheit, ihnen entsprechendes Einzelnes braucht es nicht zu geben, und soll es so etwas geben, so kann nur aktuelle Erfahrung es aufweisen. Wesenswahrheiten sind absolut bindende, sind unüberschreitbare, durch keine Erfahrung zu bestätigen oder zu widerlegen. Erfahrungswahrheiten, Aussagen, die ihrem Sinn nach Dasein setzen, also in der *Begründung* Daseinserfahrung fordern, sind zufällige Wahrheiten, die wie durch Erfahrung begründet, so durch Erfahrung modifiziert und aufgehoben werden: sie gelten nur präsumptiv, vorbehalten weiterer Erfahrungsbestätigung.

*Note on the text.* The pages before this passage are damaged. The file has no text of p. 45 (src L879, where the sentence "die mittelbare Ableitung spielt im wesentlichen nur auf dem Umweg über andere" runs straight into "Vermutung verknüpft ist"), and the commentary's § 8.15–8.16 cannot be checked. The running heads for pp. 48 and 49 stand together before this paragraph, but the apparatus places Husserl's later insertion "eidetischen" (eidetic) at 47,9 and the insertion "durch sie im echten Sinne … Wissenschaft gestaltet" at 49,10f., both inside the same file line (src L889). The heads are misplaced, the paragraph opens on p. 47, and the passage quoted lies on Hua V 47–48. So the word "eidetic" in the first sentence is a later addition in Landgrebe's typescript.

The phrase is quoted three times, and the paragraph also uses it, unquoted, in more than one sense. Unquoted, the phenomenologist says "there are experiences", and the mathematician says "there is a series of cardinal numbers", "there are relative primes", "there is no regular decahedron". Quoted, "his 'there is' says exactly as much as the mathematical 'there is'", and "this 'there is'" is grounded "on both sides" by essential seeing. The quotation marks turn the phrase into an object of discussion, and the claim is about its force: "says exactly as much" (*sagt genau so viel*). The negative example carries the most. "There is no regular decahedron" is not a report that none has been found. It says that the essence excludes it. The phenomenological "there is" has the same modality. It states what an essence admits or excludes, and it is indifferent to whether anything falls under it.

The contrast is with another existential verb, which the preceding paragraphs keep using. The descriptive sciences describe what "occurs" (*vorkommt*). "'Experience teaches': that means, such a thing has been established by perception as existing in natural actuality". The lion is yellow, and blue-green lions occur "in no natural history". Psychology too describes types of character "not fictive but experienced. That means: they occur" (*sie kommen vor*, Hua V 46). In the passage, geometry does not speak of "the bodies, motions, numbers occurring" (*vorkommenden*) in the universe. Occurring belongs to experience, "there is" to essence. The sentence that defines experience states the boundary: "Experience is a title for acts that exhibit existence" (*Dasein aufweisende*).

The test of the analogy comes two clauses later, and the text fails it in its own wording. Essential seeing grasps essences, and "there need be no singular corresponding to them" (*ihnen entsprechendes Einzelnes braucht es nicht zu geben*), "and if there is to be such a thing" (*soll es so etwas geben*), only experience can exhibit it. Here "there is" (*es gibt*) means existence in the world, the very sense the quotation marks excluded a few lines before. German has one phrase for both senses, and the paragraph uses both. The analogy with mathematics holds for the modality, the necessity and universality of what holds "*as such*". It does not show that the phenomenologist's "there are perceptions" has content in the way "there are relative primes" has. The second is a theorem in a deductive science. The first states that an essence is given, and what follows from it has to be seen case by case. The inference is weaker than the "exactly as much" makes it look.

Husserl had said so himself just before the damaged page, and it limits what the passage can carry. "Rational psychology is no mathematics, and in particular the phenomenology of experiences is no mathematics of experiences". Both are "essential doctrines", but "not every essential doctrine has the type of a mathematics". The stream of experience offers "nothing like a space", and so the field of immediate insights is "an endless one", whereas mathematics grows "in pure deduction from the few axiomatic foundations" (Hua V 44–46). The "there is" passage keeps the common modality and leaves the difference of type aside. The rest of the paragraph draws the consequence for psychology. Even where the psychologist could make do with factual description, it would be "an enormous extension of knowledge" to see that the fact holds "not only factually but by essential necessity", and that all factual laws stand out against "a mighty background" of essential laws, as physical laws select among the motions that phoronomy fixes (Hua V 48–49). Then come the Abbé Galiani, who refused to look through a telescope, and the phenomenological "instrument".

The same contrast between deductive and intuitive science returns in the 1930 Afterword, with the analogy restated in its limits. Phenomenology uses facts "only as examples", as the abacus serves the mathematician (Hua V 142). But mathematics is "'deductive'", with "an infinity of deductions" resting "on a few axioms", while transcendental phenomenology has "an infinity of cognitions that lies before all deduction", whose mediations are "mediations of intentional implication" and "have nothing to do with deduction" (Hua V 143). In 1912 the mathematical "there is" lent phenomenology its modality. In 1930 the Afterword grants the modality again and marks the difference of type more sharply than § 8 did.

## §10, Hua V 58–59: the idea of intentionality and intentional analysis

Reads [§10.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the third paragraph under § 10.

Husserl separates two things that the name "intentionality" can cover. One is the idea that consciousness is consciousness of something, drawn from immanent description of consciousness. Brentano gave the modern age that idea. The other is intentional analysis: the method that follows how objects are meant and constituted in the structures of consciousness. Brentano, Husserl says, did not see what that is. Phenomenology therefore did not continue Brentano's psychology. It grew out of intuitions Brentano's concepts trained, after a turn to essential intuition that Brentano refused. The sharpest form of the verdict is a later insertion in the typescript.

> Finally, some take phenomenology as a kind of continuation of Brentano's psychology. However highly I value this work of genius, and however strongly it (and the other writings of Brentano too) worked on me in younger years, it must yet be said here too that Brentano has stayed far from a phenomenology in our sense, and that to this day. So much so, indeed, that he flatly refuses to recognize the new movement as a progress. Nonetheless he has earned epoch-making merits precisely for making phenomenology possible. He offered the modern age the idea of *intentionality*, drawn from consciousness itself in immanent description; although he often oversteps the lines of pure description (as I would think), yet at least many of his concept-formations have their source in real intuition. So they could and had to work educatively for intuition in general, and so also after the turn, once accomplished, into essential intuition; but the essence of intentional analysis he did not see.

German:

> Endlich fassen manche die Phänomenologie als eine Art Fortsetzung der Psychologie Brentanos. Wie hoch ich dieses geniale Werk schätze, und wie stark es (und auch die anderen Schriften Brentanos) in jüngeren Jahren auf mich gewirkt hat, es muß doch auch hier gesagt werden, daß Brentano von einer Phänomenologie in unserem Sinne ferngeblieben ist, und das bis zum heutigen Tage. Dies aber so sehr, daß er es durchaus ablehnt, die neue Bewegung als einen Fortschritt anzuerkennen. Gleichwohl hat er sich epochemachende Verdienste gerade um die Ermöglichung der Phänomenologie erworben. Er bot der Neuzeit die aus dem Bewußtsein selbst in immanenter Deskription geschöpfte Idee der *Intentionalität* dar; obschon er die Linien reiner Deskription vielfach überschreitet (wie ich meinen möchte), so haben doch mindestens viele seiner Griffsbildungen ihre Quelle in wirklicher Intuition. So konnten und mußten sie für Intuition überhaupt erziehend wirken und somit auch nach der vollzogenen Wendung in Wesensintuition; aber das Wesen intentionaler Analyse hat er nicht gesehen.

*Note on the text.* The file has "Griffsbildungen", an obvious slip for *Begriffsbildungen* (concept-formations), rendered so above. The italics on *Intentionalität* are the file's. The apparatus records the last clause, "aber das Wesen .... nicht gesehen" (but the essence … did not see), as an "Einfügung von H. in L." [insertion by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript] at 59,18. The 1912 manuscript ended the paragraph with "after the turn, once accomplished, into essential intuition".

The paragraph is built as a balance of praise and limit, and the connectives carry it. "However highly I value … it must yet be said". "Nonetheless he has earned". "Although he often oversteps … yet at least many". Every concession is answered by a restriction, and every restriction by a concession. The noun phrases are precise about what is credited. Brentano "offered" (*bot … dar*) the idea of intentionality, "drawn from consciousness itself in immanent description" (*in immanenter Deskription geschöpft*). The participle "drawn" is the book's word for a source in intuition. In § 5 the categories of reality were "drawn from the primal sources" (row 1), and the regional concepts of § 7 come from intuition, not from a system of thought. So Brentano's idea is credited with the right kind of origin. What is withheld is the step beyond it, "the turn … into essential intuition" (*Wendung in Wesensintuition*). His concepts could "educate" (*erziehend wirken*) for that turn because they came from intuition, but he did not take it. The present tense marks a living opponent. Brentano "flatly refuses" (*durchaus ablehnt*) to count the new movement as progress, and the distance holds "to this day", the present of the 1912 manuscript.

The inserted clause changes the verdict from one about direction to one about method. Without it, the paragraph says that Brentano did not turn to essences. With it, the paragraph says that he did not see "the essence of intentional analysis". The second claim is narrower and harder. It concedes that Brentano had intentionality as an idea and denies that he had it as a method. "But" (*aber*) sets the insertion against everything before it: his concepts could educate, "but" what intentional analysis is, he did not see. The later hand has turned a balanced appreciation into a judgment about what separates Brentano from phenomenology.

The corpus shows what Brentano's idea did in his own book. In the *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* every psychic phenomenon is "characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (or also mental) inexistence of an object, and what we would call, though with not wholly unambiguous expressions, the relation to a content, the direction upon an object". "This intentional inexistence is exclusively peculiar to psychic phenomena", and "so we can define the psychic phenomena" by it (Book II, ch. 1, pp. 124–125 of the edition in the corpus file, a scan-grade conversion). Intentionality is there a mark that defines a class: it sorts phenomena into psychic and physical. Intentional analysis, in the sense of *Ideen III*, asks how the object is meant in the act, through what manifolds of appearance and in what layers of apprehension. It is what § 3 did with sensation, and what § 7 did with the thing. The "idea" Husserl credits is the mark. The "essence" he says Brentano missed is the analysis. Brentano also apologizes for his terms, "not wholly unambiguous expressions". Husserl's "(as I would think)" (*wie ich meinen möchte*) answers in the same tone, a scruple about where Brentano oversteps pure description.

The neighbouring paragraphs make the same distinction against other claimed founders. Bolzano "had not the faintest inkling of phenomenology" in Husserl's sense, though his doctrine of propositions and truths in themselves was "of inestimable value" to Husserl (Hua V 57). Lotze's "phenomenology", in quotation marks, comes down to some a priori relations among sensory contents (Hua V 58). The footnote attached in the file at the end of the Brentano paragraph says where the real problems lie. They lie "not in the sphere of sensory stuffs, the a priori of the orders of colours and tones noticed by Lotze, but in the full and whole stream of experience and above all in consciousness with its intentionality and its intentional meanings". Bolzano is credited with logic and Lotze with a sensory a priori, and each is denied what lies beyond his merit. Brentano is credited with intentionality and denied the analysis of it.

The 1930 Afterword returns to Brentano twice, in terms that agree with the insertion. A descriptive psychology founded on transcendental phenomenology has "a quite different sense" from that of "the Lockean tradition, but also from that of the Brentanian school" (Hua V 159). Brentano's "psychognosy" is "of course 'phenomenological' psychology" only in the loose sense in which any description of inner experience is so called (Hua V 155–157; row 29). The 1912 text says that Brentano did not turn to essences. The insertion and the Afterword say what he lacked: not intentionality, but the analysis of it.

## §11, Hua V 67–68: the soul has no "appearances"

Reads [§11.9–11.10](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the ninth and tenth paragraphs under § 11.

"Appearance" (*Erscheinung*) is, in § 11, a relational term with a fixed range. It names a constitutive unity of a lower level through which a unity of a higher level shows itself: the adumbration, the visual thing, the sensory schema, and finally the intuitive thing as a whole, which is "mere appearance" of the thing that physics determines. The soul has nothing of this kind beneath it. It constitutes itself directly in its states, and those states belong to its own being. From this one difference Husserl draws the difference of method between the two sciences. For physics, description of what is experienced is a means to a theme that is never experienced. For psychology, description of what is experienced determines the theme itself.

> The situation is quite different in the sphere of psychological and aesthesiological description. The soul is no substance of appearances; the parallel of appearance in the sense indicated above is missing, as indeed the whole extraordinarily complicated system of constitutive unities, each of which is "appearance" in relation to the unity of the higher level and is in turn a unity that appears in appearances of a lower level (sensory schemata, visual things of different level and stratum, adumbrations), is missing here. The psychic unity constitutes itself directly in its states, and the soul-states, these are the "consciousness-experiences" adequately given in the plane of immanent temporality (in which all our analyses keep), themselves and without mediations by way of appearance. (Falling away of the "relativity" to the apprehending subjects. There is the possibility of "deception", but not of "consideration from different standpoints".)
>
> These experiences, as belonging to the true being of the soul itself, are therefore *theme* of psychology itself and not merely media through which the theme is first to be won. There is here, then, a cardinal difference in the manner of the constitution of the soul as against the constitution of the thing, and it determines the method. The *intuitive* thing, the thing of my direct experience, attests itself in the sensory schema much as the soul does in its states of consciousness. Were the intuitive thing the objectively actual one, that of physics, then the physicist too would have to describe the sensory schema in each case as the momentary and changing state of the thingly property, and the thingly properties themselves would contain the secondary qualities unified, which would then find their expression in the descriptive concepts. Because the intuitive thing is mere "appearance" of the thing of physics, which is not to be had intuitively at all, and so is *properly* never experienced, the experienced and its experiential properties are also not the physical theme but only a means of coming to the theme. In psychology, however, there are no "appearances" of the soul, but experience of it itself; experience, as there can be experience of something real at all, through the states being experienced, which now, as states of the real itself, belong to the scientific theme and require scientific description.

German:

> Ganz anders ist die Sachlage in der Sphäre der psychologischen und aesthesiologischen Deskription. Die Seele ist keine Substanz von Erscheinungen, die Parallele der Erscheinung in dem oben bezeichneten Sinne fehlt, wie ja überhaupt das ganze überaus komplizierte System konstitutiver Einheiten, deren jede „Erscheinung“ ist in Beziehung auf die Einheit der höheren Stufe und ihrerseits Einheit ist, die in Erscheinungen niederer Stufe erscheint (sinnliche Schemata, Sehdinge verschiedener Stufe und Schicht, Abschattungen), hier fehlt. Die seelische Einheit konstituiert sich direkt in ihren Zuständen, und die Seelenzustände, das sind die in der Ebene der immanenten Zeitlichkeit (in der sich alle unsere Analysen halten) selbst und ohne erscheinungsmäßige Mittelbarkeiten adäquat gegebenen „Bewußtseinserlebnisse“. (Fortfall der „Relativität“ auf die auffassenden Subjekte. Es besteht die Möglichkeit der „Täuschung“, aber nicht der „Betrachtung von verschiedenen Standpunkten“.)
>
> Diese Erlebnisse als zum wahren Sein der Seele selbst gehörig sind also *Thema* der Psychologie selbst und nicht etwa bloß Medien, durch die erst das Thema zu gewinnen ist. Es ist hier also ein kardinaler Unterschied in der Weise der Seelenkonstitution gegenüber der Dingkonstitution, der die Methode bestimmt. Das *anschauliche* Ding, das Ding meiner direkten Erfahrung, beurkundet sich im sinnlichen Schema ähnlich wie die Seele in ihren Bewußtseinszuständen. Wäre das anschauliche Ding das objektiv wirkliche, das der Physik, dann müßte auch der Physiker das jeweilige sinnliche Schema als den momentanen und wechselnden Zustand der dinglichen Eigenschaft beschreiben, und die dinglichen Eigenschaften selbst enthielten die sekundären Qualitäten vereinheitlicht, die nun in den beschreibenden Begriffen ihren Ausdruck fänden. Dadurch, daß das anschauliche Ding bloße „Erscheinung“ des Dinges der Physik ist, das selbst anschaulich gar nicht zu haben, also *eigentlich* nie erfahren ist, ist auch das Erfahrene und seine Erfahrungseigenschaften nicht das physikalische Thema sondern nur ein Mittel, zum Thema zu kommen. In der Psychologie aber gibt es keine „Erscheinungen“ der Seele, sondern Erfahrung von ihr selbst; Erfahrung, wie es von einem Realen überhaupt Erfahrung geben kann dadurch, daß die Zustände erfahren werden, die nun als Zustände des Realen selbst zum wissenschaftlichen Thema gehören und wissenschaftlicher Beschreibung bedürfen.

*Note on the text.* The second paragraph is one of the pages (Hua V 68) on which the print's margin line numbers ("5", "10" … "35") were carried into the file; they are dropped here, with the line-end hyphen in "Erfah-/rungseigenschaften". The italics on *Thema*, *anschauliche* and *eigentlich* are the file's. The sentence that follows the passage in the file, "Die *naturwissenschaftliche Beschreibung* bestimmt nur eine Erscheinung, nicht aber das selbst, das bestimmt werden soll" (natural-scientific description determines only an appearance, but not that itself which is to be determined), looks as though it has lost a noun after "das"; the apparatus has no note at 68, and nothing below depends on the missing word.

The quotation marks do the defining. Unmarked, *Erscheinung* is the ordinary word, and Husserl uses it so in "appears in appearances of a lower level". Marked, "appearance" is the technical relation: a unity that is "appearance" *in relation to* a higher unity. The marks recur at each point where the relation is asserted or denied. The intuitive thing is mere "appearance" of the physical thing, and there are no "appearances" of the soul. The parenthesis extends the marks to the vocabulary of perspective: "relativity", "deception", "consideration from different standpoints". It has no main verb, "Falling away of …", and reads like a manuscript jotting. It makes the case precise. What falls away with appearance is relativity to standpoint, which belongs to adumbration. What remains is the possibility of being deceived. Adequate givenness is not infallibility.

"The soul is no substance of appearances" answers the opening of the book. In § 1 every real was "substance" only as the term of causal relations, and the physical thing was the identical substrate of properties attested in appearances (row 2). The soul is real, but not in that way. Husserl's word for how it shows itself is the one he uses for the thing: the intuitive thing "attests itself" (*beurkundet sich*) in the sensory schema "much as" (*ähnlich wie*) the soul in its states. At the level of intuition, then, the two are parallel. The "cardinal difference" arises one level up. The counterfactual carries it. "Were the intuitive thing the objectively actual one", physics would have to describe schemata as states of the thing, as psychology describes experiences as states of the soul. It is not, because the thing of physics is "*properly* never experienced". The italic *eigentlich* restricts the claim: the physical thing is experienced only improperly, through appearances, and never in its own determinations. The asymmetry therefore rests less on the soul than on the thing. It depends on § 11's prior thesis that the physical thing is a thought-unity, a bearer "for abstract-physical thought-symbols" (*Denksymbole*), determined only by "physical-exact concepts" (Hua V 66). Without that thesis the soul and the intuitive thing would differ in degree.

The words for the role of description follow the same line. The experiences are "*theme*" (*Thema*), not "media" (*Medien*); for physics, what is experienced is "only a means" (*nur ein Mittel*). The reflexive *selbst* runs through the whole passage: "the true being of the soul itself", "psychology itself", "experience of it itself", "states of the real itself". It marks the point at which the description reaches its object. On the next lines it becomes the definition: if description is the conceptual expression "of the perceived itself, of what is experienced in the proper sense", then psychological description "determines the psychic itself", and natural-scientific description "determines only an appearance".

The passage opens with "psychological *and aesthesiological* description". The body's sensitive layer, on the preceding page, has properties that are "not thought-unities, related to unities of 'appearances', but *themselves* given", which "by their essence exclude anything like attestation through appearance" (Hua V 66). So the line Husserl draws does not separate soul from body. It separates the thing of physics from everything given in its own being, the aesthesiological body included. The commentary's "the psychic sphere" (§11.9) narrows it.

The passage turns physics' indifference to description, set out on the page before, into psychology's dependence on it. Physics names appearances only "sufficiently for the purposes of objective-causal analysis" (Hua V 67). Psychology has "an infinite field of determining description", and its "strict shaping" demands phenomenological analysis (Hua V 68). That is why phenomenology, not a descriptive natural science, is what psychology lacks, and why "the true method" is to do the work of essential distinction in rational phenomenology and then apply it (Hua V 69). The argument needs the asymmetry here: if the soul had appearances, psychology could treat its descriptions as physics treats its own, as a means to be left behind.

Supplement I qualifies the thesis from the side of the other subject. For me, the foreign human being is mere "appearance", "an expression, admittedly, easily misunderstood". "The soul does not appear in the sense that it adumbrates itself", but it becomes presentive to every pure I "in a mere (noematic) 'representation'-appearance" (*„Vorstellungs“-erscheinung*) (Supplement I § 1, Hua V 112; row 21). The quotation marks there hold the word away from the sense of § 11. The soul has no appearances in the sense of the lower levels of thing-constitution. Its givenness to another still needs a word for "appearance", and Husserl marks it as a borrowing.

*Ideas I* had drawn the asymmetry for the experience. "A mental process of feeling is not adumbrated. If I look at it, I have something absolute; it has no sides". Yet "I can think something true or something false about a feeling" (§ 44, Hua III/1 81–82, in Kersten's translation). That is the "possibility of deception" without "different standpoints". The title of *Ideas I* § 40 gives the other side in the same marks: the physical thing given "in person" is a "mere appearance" of the "true physical thing" determined in physics. *Ideen III* moves the experience's privilege to the soul. The real unity that *Ideas I* counted as transcendent is here experienced "itself", because its states belong to its being. So psychology's descriptions reach what they describe, and physics' descriptions never do.

## §12, Hua V 75: pure consciousness as the a priori of all being

Reads [§12.7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the seventh paragraph under § 12.

In § 7 the pair *a priori* and *a posteriori* sorted concepts: the regional concept of thing is prior to every concept of a kind of thing, because realities constitute themselves as unities of manifolds. § 12 moves the pair from concepts to being. Pure consciousness is the a priori, and "all being" is the a posteriori in relation to it. The same pair then decides the book's question about psychology. When an experience is apprehended as the state of a soul, the apprehension leaves it unchanged but makes it an a posteriori, and it presupposes the a priori, because the apprehension is itself a pure experience. The eidetics of consciousness-states is therefore a piece of the ontology of the soul. Pure phenomenology, the eidetics of transcendentally purified consciousness, is no more rational psychology than it is rational natural science.

> Pure consciousness has the absolute priority, in relation to which all being is the a posteriori, and this relation between a priori and a posteriori already belongs into the essential sphere. Accordingly it is a fundamental necessity, of cardinal importance for philosophy, to raise oneself to the knowledge that between the eidetics of consciousness-*states*, which is a piece of the rational ontology of the soul, and the eidetics of *transcendentally purified* consciousness (or experience-being) a distinction must be made, that the latter, genuine and pure phenomenology, is just as little rational psychology as rational doctrine of nature. One thing alone justifies calling the eidetics of the psychic consciousness-*states* phenomenology, as we too did above: namely the circumstance already touched on, that the pure experience enters with its whole essence into the psychic state and undergoes only an apperception that does not alter it but grasps it in a peculiar way. It makes an a posteriori out of the a priori and itself again presupposes the a priori. For it itself belongs to a pure I as its pure experience, to which, as to every one, belongs the essential possibility of being empirically apperceived, and so in infinitum. These are connections that, once one has understood them, have nothing wonderful about them. The wonder of all wonders is pure I and pure consciousness: and just this wonder vanishes as soon as the light of phenomenology falls on it and subjects it to essential analysis. The wonder vanishes in transforming itself into a whole science with a fullness of difficult scientific problems. The wonder is something incomprehensible; the problematic in the shape of scientific problems is something comprehensible, it is the incomprehensible that turns out, for reason, in the solving of the problem, to be comprehensible and comprehended.

> Das reine Bewußtsein hat die absolute Priorität, in Beziehung auf welche alles Sein das Aposteriori ist, und diese Beziehung zwischen Apriori und Aposteriori gehört schon in die Wesenssphäre hinein. Demnach ist es eine Grundnotwendigkeit, für die Philosophie von kardinaler Wichtigkeit, sich zur Erkenntnis zu erheben, daß zwischen Eidetik der Bewußtseins*zustände*, die ein Stück rationaler Ontologie der Seele ist, und Eidetik des *transzendental gereinigten* Bewußtseins (oder Erlebnis-Seins) geschieden werden muß, daß die letztere, die echte und reine Phänomenologie, ebensowenig rationale Psychologie ist wie rationale Naturlehre. *Note on the text.* The passage follows a seam. The paragraph in the file opens with the question of "psychomonism" and breaks off in mid-sentence: "Löst nicht die Phänomenologie in Sein durchaus relatives Sein ist, darstellt." (Does not phenomenology dissolve in being is throughout relative being, represents.) Everything between "in" and "Sein" is lost. The commentary gives the missing text as §12.6 and the opening of §12.7: the "psychomonism", the prejudice that experience is eo ipso psychic, the reduction that alone wins "the absolute relatum for all realities, whose being is throughout relative being". The surviving fragment agrees with the end of that clause, and nothing more can be checked. The reading therefore starts at the first whole sentence. "apperz[i]piert" is the file's, with the editor's bracket. The italics are the file's; the apparatus has no note on the page.

Two words carry the first sentence: "priority" (*Priorität*) and "the a posteriori" (*das Aposteriori*), a noun with the article. In § 7, priority and posteriority were "prefigured in the world of essences themselves and of essential concepts", which justified speaking "in a certain sense of *a priori* and *a posteriori* concepts" (Hua V 35; row 10). That "certain sense" was a "'transcendental'" difference, because it had its source in the property of realities to "'constitute'" themselves as unities of manifolds. § 12 draws the conclusion that § 7 held back. What the regional concepts owed to constitution, being as a whole owes to the consciousness in which it is constituted. The adjective has become a noun, and the noun has a referent: "all being". The second clause keeps the step within eidetics. The relation "already belongs into the essential sphere". The priority is one of essence, not a causal or temporal order.

"Accordingly" (*Demnach*) draws the consequence for the two eidetics, and the file's italics mark the opposition: consciousness-*states* against *transcendentally purified* consciousness. The parenthesis "(or experience-being)" (*Erlebnis-Seins*) names the second with the hyphenated compound for being of the kind experience has, set against the being of the soul. The comparison "just as little rational psychology as rational doctrine of nature" puts psychology and physics on the same side. Both are ontologies of transcendencies. In the commentary's version of the lost text, material thing, body and soul are "quite determinate and specific transcendencies", each constituted in pure consciousness; the file cannot confirm the wording. Phenomenology stands to rational psychology as it stands to geometry.

The concession that follows is exact. "One thing alone" justifies the name phenomenology for the eidetics of states, and it is a fact about apperception. The pure experience "enters with its whole essence" into the psychic state, and the apperception "does not alter it but grasps it in a peculiar way". That is why everything found in the eidetics of states holds of pure experience too, and why the two are so easily taken for one. Then the decisive sentence: the apperception "makes an a posteriori out of the a priori and itself again presupposes the a priori". "Makes … out of" (*macht aus*) is a verb of transformation, but the transformation is one of sense, not of content. The same experience, now a state of a soul, counts as founded in nature.

The justification, "For" (*Denn*), is an iteration. The apperception is itself a pure experience of a pure I, and it can in turn be empirically apperceived, "and so in infinitum". This shows that no apperception exhausts the pure: behind every psychic state there stands a pure experience that apperceives it. It does not by itself show that the pure is prior in being. That rests on the lost sentences, on the claim that all transcendencies are constituted in pure consciousness. The "for" claims more than the iteration gives.

The close turns on two word families. *Wunder* (wonder) answers the opening of § 12. Following the a priori, the experiential stream was found to harbour "all sources for possible world-constitutions and soul-constitutions", and Husserl exclaimed "But what a wonder!" (*Aber welches Wunder!*, Hua V 72). Now the connections between pure experience and psychic state "have nothing wonderful about them" once understood, and the wonder is located in "pure I and pure consciousness". The wonder does not vanish by being explained away. It "transforms itself" into a science. The second family is *begreifen* (to comprehend), and with it *Begriff* (concept): the incomprehensible (*Unbegreifliches*) becomes the comprehensible (*Begreifliches*) and then the "comprehended" (*begriffen*). The last clause keeps the identity. It is "the incomprehensible" itself that turns out comprehensible. Phenomenology does not replace the wonder with something else. It is the wonder in the form of problems.

The passage ends § 12 and the second chapter. Before it, Husserl has let the paradox grow: the part swallows the whole, rational psychology swallows every rational discipline, empirical psychology every empirical one, and the inclination to "suppose psychology" wherever the word "consciousness" appears makes it seem "self-evident enough" (Hua V 73–74). The passage stops the growth by refusing its premise: experience is not eo ipso psychic. What follows, the third chapter, can then ask how the ontologies are "included" in phenomenology (§ 14) without becoming psychology. The Afterword of 1930 states the same priority in other words. Only transcendental subjectivity has "the being-sense of absolute being", only it is "'irrelative' (i.e. relative only to itself)", while the real world has "an essential relativity" to it (Hua V 153–154; row 28). The 1912 text speaks of an a priori where 1930 speaks of absolute being. Both rest on one relation: of being to the consciousness that constitutes it.

*Ideas I* had the same structure without the vocabulary of a priori and a posteriori. In § 53, the phenomenological attitude "finds, as an absolute mental process, the apperception of a sequence of states", and the question was "how that which in itself is absolute can relinquish its immanence and take on the characteristic of transcendence" (Hua III/1 103–105, in Kersten's translation). The title of § 54 sets "the transcendent psychological mental process accidental and relative" against "the transcendental mental process necessary and absolute". *Ideen III* adds two things to this. One is the name a priori for the absolute side, which ties the distinction of consciousness and psyche to the book's doctrine of regions. The other is the iteration *in infinitum*, which makes every apperception of the pure again a pure experience.

## §15, Hua V 81: the science of the "origins", of the "mothers" of all knowledge

Reads [§15.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the second paragraph under § 15.

Phenomenology's priority over the ontologies is not one of rank within the sciences. It is the priority of a source over what flows from it. Ontology judges, "dogmatically", what belongs to objectivities "as such". Phenomenology goes back to the origins of every such judgment, and every philosophical method leads back to it. Husserl says this in figures he marks as figures: "origins" and "mothers" in quotation marks, "mother-soil" without them. In the same paragraph he limits the figure. A clarification that goes back to the origins is phenomenological. A clarification that carries the results back into a dogmatic science is only application.

> In the eidetics of space, of material nature, of spirit and so on, we pursue *dogmatic* science under the title "ontology". We judge about spatial formations as such, souls and psychic properties as such, about human beings as such. We judge about what belongs to such objectivities "as such" in truth, and that means here, in unconditional necessity and universality. Phenomenology in our sense is the science of the "origins", of the "mothers" of all knowledge, and it is the mother-soil of all philosophical method: to it and to work in it all leads back. If philosophers have said again and again, or were guided without saying it by the conviction, that the natural knowledge of practical life and of the dogmatic sciences does not suffice, that it is burdened with unclarities, that the fundamental concepts of all sciences (sc. the dogmatic ones) need a "clarification", a leading-back to their origins, then everywhere what is felt here as a lack finds its final fulfilment in phenomenology, and the conceptual clarification in question is only a step towards the phenomenological and phenomenologically continuing investigation of essence within the frame of the problems of phenomenological constitution. The expression "phenomenological" thereby takes on of itself a very extended meaning, which is harmless once one has seen through the whole situation, but which must be expressly stressed. Phenomenological can mean every investigation and every stock of knowledge, every result of knowledge, that belongs to phenomenology in our specific sense. In this sense, then, a clarification of the geometrical fundamental concepts would be phenomenological only if it occurred within the frame of the phenomenological problematic.

> In der Eidetik des Raumes, der materiellen Natur, des Geistes usw. treiben wir unter dem Titel „Ontologie“ *dogmatische* Wissenschaft. Wir urteilen über Raumgebilde als solche, Seelen und seelische Eigenschaften als solche, über Menschen als solche.
>
> Wir urteilen darüber, was dergleichen Gegenständlichkeiten „als solchen“ in Wahrheit ist, und das besagt hier, in unbedingter Notwendigkeit und Allgemeinheit zukommt. Die Phänomenologie in unserem Sinne ist die Wissenschaft der „Ursprünge“, der „Mütter“ aller Erkenntnis, und sie ist der Mutterboden aller philosophischen Methode: zu ihm und zur Arbeit in ihm führt alle zurück. Wenn die Philosophen immer wieder sagten oder unausgesprochen davon geleitet waren, daß die natürliche Erkenntnis des praktischen Lebens und der dogmatischen Wissenschaften nicht ausreiche, daß sie mit Unklarheiten behaftet sei, daß die Grundbegriffe aller Wissenschaften (sc. der dogmatischen) einer „Klärung“ bedürften, einer Zurückführung auf ihre Ursprünge, so findet überall, was hier als Mangel empfunden wird, seine letzte Erfüllung in der Phänomenologie, und die fragliche Begriffsklärung ist nur ein Schritt zur phänomenologisch und phänomenologisch fortführenden Wesensuntersuchung im Rahmen der Probleme phänomenologischer Konstitution. Three readings are doubtful and left as the file has them. "was dergleichen Gegenständlichkeiten „als solchen“ in Wahrheit ist, und das besagt hier, in unbedingter Notwendigkeit und Allgemeinheit zukommt" mixes two constructions ("is" and "belongs to"); the English gives the sense of the second. "zu ihm … führt alle zurück" is probably *alles* (everything). "zur phänomenologisch und phänomenologisch fortführenden Wesensuntersuchung" repeats a word, probably for *phänomenologischen und phänomenologisch fortführenden*. The apparatus has no note at 81. The italics on *dogmatische* are the file's.

The paragraph opens with a contrast of judging. Ontology is "*dogmatic*" science, the file's italics, and its formula is "as such" (*als solche*), said three times of its objects and a fourth time in quotation marks. Dogmatic here is not a reproach. It names the attitude that judges straight out about what belongs to objects "in unconditional necessity and universality". Phenomenology is introduced against this formula. It does not judge about the objects as such. It is the science of where such judgments come from.

The figures are graded by their marks. "Origins" (*„Ursprünge“*) is the philosophers' word, and the quotation marks hold it at a distance: the question of "origin" was the catchword of the "thoroughly mistaken problem-formulations", such as the "much-discussed questions about the 'origin' of the idea of space, time and thing" (§ 9, Hua V 55). Phenomenology takes the word back, but in quotation marks, not in the genetic-psychological sense. "Mothers" (*„Mütter“*), plural and marked, is a figure on a figure. The genitive makes the mothers the same as the origins: "the science of the 'origins', of the 'mothers' of all knowledge". Then, unmarked, comes "mother-soil" (*Mutterboden*), a compound in common use, and the image changes from parentage to ground. Phenomenology is the soil "to which and to work in which all leads back". The sentence moves from the marked figure to the ordinary one, and from a genealogy to a ground on which one works.

The plural is the point. Three pages earlier, formal mathesis stood "so to speak as the mother of all ontologies in their series" (*sozusagen als die Mutter aller Ontologien*, § 12, Hua V 72). That is one mother at the head of a series: the formal ontology that every material ontology presupposes. Phenomenology is not a further mother at the head of the same series. It is the "mothers" of all knowledge, ontology included, many sources and not a first member. The commentary's "the 'mother' of all cognition" loses the contrast with § 12 that the plural makes (see Corrections).

The rest of the paragraph does two things with the figure. First, it gives it a history. Philosophers "again and again" said that natural and dogmatic knowledge does not suffice and that its fundamental concepts need "clarification" (*„Klärung“*), "a leading-back to their origins" (*Zurückführung auf ihre Ursprünge*). The second phrase repeats the first figure without quotation marks: the philosophers' demand is put in their words, and phenomenology is its "final fulfilment". Second, it bounds the figure at once. "The expression 'phenomenological' thereby takes on of itself a very extended meaning". The extension is harmless only if one sees through it. A clarification of the geometrical concepts is phenomenological "only if it occurred within the frame of the phenomenological problematic". The next paragraph draws the line sharply. To interpret geometry "'phenomenologically'", to "'clarify'" it and to "'interpret back'" (*„zurückdeutet“*) its theorems into the constituting nexus of consciousness, all in quotation marks, "is application of phenomenology, not phenomenology itself" (Hua V 81–82). The return to the origins is phenomenology. The way back from them into a dogmatic science is not.

This sets up the chapter. § 15 has asked whether "the same" (*„dasselbe“*) concepts and propositions function in the same way in ontology and in phenomenology (Hua V 80). The figure of origins answers: they occur in both, but in phenomenology as what is to be traced to its source, not as what is judged about. § 15.5 states the consequence in typography: a "thing" as correlate is not a thing, "hence the quotation marks" (Hua V 83; row 16). The fourth chapter carries the figure into method. Clarification must "lead back the sciences to their origin" (*auf ihren … Ursprung zurückzuführen*) (§ 18, Hua V 97), and the concept is to be "fed from the primal source (*Urquelle*) of conceptual validity, intuition" (§ 20, Hua V 103). The commentary's § 20.5 says that phenomenology "contains the mother-soil from which all ontological insights originate", and that it is "nothing less than a kind of continuation of the ontologies". The file cannot confirm this. It breaks off in that paragraph at "Alle solche Forschung hat nun aber, im Sinne der schon Ausgeführten, nahe" (all such research has, however, in the sense of what has been set out, close…) and goes straight to the Supplements (src L1440). The end of the main text, to 105,30 by the apparatus, is lost.

The figure is older than *Ideen III* in Husserl's own work. In the lectures of 1906/07, to escape epistemological scepticism "we must study knowledge in its sources, we must descend to the mothers of knowledge" (*wir müssen zu den Müttern der Erkenntnis hinabsteigen*; Hua XXIV 233, § 38). An appendix to the same course has the whole constellation of § 15. Phenomenology is "the all-embracing doctrine of the 'origins'", it "teaches us to know the mother-soil of all principles", and "it does not lead us to mystical mothers, but its realm is the realm of pure evidence or clarity" (*Nicht zu mystischen Müttern führt sie uns hin, sondern ihr Reich ist das Reich reiner Evidenz*; Hua XXIV 376–377, Beilage X). There the "mothers" are a literary figure of a mythic realm one descends to, invoked and disowned in the same breath. The corpus has no text that names the source of the figure, so the row's suggestion of Goethe stays unconfirmed here. What the 1906/07 text shows is how Husserl handles it. In 1906/07 he denies the mystical sense in words. In 1912 the quotation marks do the denying, and the plural keeps the figure's point: many sources, below every science, to which all method leads back.

## §15, Hua V 85: a "thing" as correlate is not a thing

Reads [§15.5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the fifth paragraph under § 15.

The ontological concepts occur in phenomenology, but they do not name its domain. "Thing", "spatial shape" and the rest return there as names for correlates of consciousness: the thing as perceived, as named, as meant. Husserl marks the change of sense with quotation marks and then says that he has done so. The marks are the difference between the two attitudes in its shortest form. The ontologist judges about things as such. The phenomenologist judges about "things", the thing-meant as such, and that "is not to investigate things".

> Let us now consider phenomenology on the other side and see what it has to do with "the same" essences and essential relations (sc. the axiomatic ones of the ontologies). Its domain is not spatial shapes, things, souls and so on as such in eidetic universality, but transcendental consciousness with all its transcendental occurrences, to be investigated in immediate intuition and in eidetic universality. So into its domain falls, among other things, the intuition of spatial shapes, the thinking and thinking cognition of spatial shapes, the experiencing of things, experiential-scientific consciousness and so on. So there occur in it "spatial shape", "thing", and whatever other ontological concepts and essences of the kind there are. But we see already that it occurs there in a quite different way, as is already in a certain way expressed in the fact that it does not designate the domain. In the phenomenology of thing-consciousness the question is not how things in general *are*, what belongs to them as such in truth; but how consciousness of things is constituted, what kinds of thing-consciousness are to be distinguished, in what manner and with what correlates a thing as such presents and attests itself in the manner of consciousness. But also how consciousness can of itself be knowledge of the existence and non-existence, possibility and impossibility of a thing, what that is according to its essence: a "showing-itself" of a thingly existence, grounding of a natural-scientific proposition and so on. Constantly, as we know, both come into consideration in the phenomenological investigation of acts: consciousness itself and correlate of consciousness, noesis and noema. To describe and determine essentially the phenomenon thing-intuition, vague thing-presentation, thing-memory and the like, that is, at the same time also to heed that the act in itself is a "meaning" of something and that the meant as such is "thing". But to establish this, indeed to make the thing-meant as such, namely as correlate (the perceived as such with respect to the perception, the named as such with respect to the naming), the object of investigation, to make statements about what belongs eidetically to such thing-meanings as such, that does not mean investigating things, things as such. A "thing" as correlate is no thing, hence the quotation marks. The theme is therefore a totally different one, however much essential relations may run across and back.

*Note on the text.* The passage is on Hua V 85 (running head at src L1246), not 82–83 as the list had it. "Dingern" (for *Dingen*) and "der gleichen" (for *dergleichen*) are slips, rendered without comment. The italic *sind* is the file's. The apparatus has no note on the page.

The paragraph is built on two formulas taken over from the ontological side and turned. The first is "as such" (*als solche*). In § 15.2, ontology judged about spatial formations, souls and human beings "as such" (row 15). Here the formula first denies the ontological domain to phenomenology ("not spatial shapes, things, souls … as such"), then recurs in "a thing as such presents and attests itself", where it is the thing as it appears, and finally attaches to the correlate: "the meant as such", "the perceived as such with respect to the perception, the named as such with respect to the naming", "such thing-meanings as such". The phrase stays; what it qualifies changes. The phrase "with respect to" (*hinsichtlich*) makes the dependence explicit. The perceived as such is the perceived only relative to the perceiving. The sentence ends by giving "as such" back to ontology: this "does not mean investigating things, things as such".

The second formula is "the same" (*„denselben“*), set in quotation marks in the opening sentence. § 15 had asked whether "the same" (*„dasselbe“*) concepts function alike in both sciences (Hua V 80). The marks answer in advance. The essences are only "the same", in quotation marks, as they recur on the other side.

Between these two formulas the passage builds the phenomenological vocabulary by nominalization. "Meaning" (*„Vermeinen“*), in quotation marks, is the act. "The meant" (*das Vermeinte*) is its correlate. "The thing-meant" (*das Dingvermeinte*) gives the correlate a kind. "Thing-meanings" (*Dingvermeintheiten*) makes the correlates countable, a plural of the abstract. Each step moves the noun further from "thing" while keeping "thing" in the compound. That is the work the quotation marks do in a single stroke. The verb "designate" (*bezeichnet*) states it negatively: the ontological concept "occurs" (*kommt … vor*) but "does not designate the domain". The singular "it occurs" (*es kommt*) after the plural list of concepts treats them as one case.

Then Husserl names his own typography: "A 'thing' as correlate is no thing, hence the quotation marks." The sentence is the conclusion of the paragraph, not a gloss on it. It shows that in this book the marks are a terminological device, and that a reader who drops them changes the thesis. The marks the earlier readings followed fall under the same rule: the "appearance" of § 11 (row 13), the "'transcendental clue'" of § 7 (row 8). So do the "identical" and the "'exists'" of § 16 (row 17). The negation is exact, "no thing" (*kein Ding*), not "not a real thing". A correlate is not a defective thing. It belongs to another category.

The last clause qualifies the separation. The theme is "totally different", "however much essential relations may run across and back" (*mögen auch Wesensbeziehungen herüber- und hinüberlaufen*). The concessive subjunctive admits what § 16 will work out. The noema of a clear thing-intuition and the essence thing are distinct, yet "a change of attitude" lets the grasp of the one pass into the grasp of the other (Hua V 85–86; row 17). The separation of themes is not a separation of contents. It is a difference of direction in one field, which a turn of the gaze crosses.

The paragraph closes the comparison that § 15 set up. Ontology makes "*eidetic existential judgments*" about essences and their individual instances; "all science goes to what is" (Hua V 84). Phenomenology's domain is transcendental consciousness, and into it falls "the experiencing of things", together with the correlates. The phrase "showing-itself" (*„Ausweisen“*), again in quotation marks, marks the case where the difference matters most. Phenomenology asks what it is for consciousness to be knowledge of existence and non-existence. It does so without itself positing the existence in question. The domain is not narrowed by this. Existence itself returns in it, as a correlate in quotation marks.

*Ideas I* had stated the rule in the same terms. In the reduced perception we find "the perceived as perceived, to be expressed as 'material thing,' 'plant,' 'tree'", and "the inverted commas are significant in that they express that change in sign, the correspondingly radical signification-modification of the words". "The tree simpliciter … can burn up", the sense of the perception "cannot burn up" (§ 89, Hua III/1 184, in Kersten's translation). *Ideen III* adds the ontological side to that rule. In *Ideas I* the quotation marks separated the noema from the actual thing in nature. Here they separate it from the essence thing, the object of the regional ontology. That is the harder case, because the essence is no more real than the noema, and § 16 has to separate them again.

## §16, Hua V 85–86: noema and essence

Reads [§16.1–16.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the first and second paragraphs under § 16.

The noema of a thing-intuition is not the essence of the thing, and it does not contain it. The two are given in different kinds of intuition and are posited in different judgments. The test case is the absurd. The meaning "round square" is a noema with its own being, while the essence round square does not exist. In intuition the two run strictly parallel: wherever the noema can be grasped, the essence can be too, and with the same inadequacy. So the difference between noema and essence is not a difference in what is given. It is a difference in the direction of grasping and in what is posited as being. Husserl draws it so sharply that he withholds a word, and leaves the intuition of the noema without a name of its own.

> One must not confuse noema (correlate) and essence. Even the noema of a clear thing-intuition, or of a continuous concordant nexus of intuition directed at one and the same thing, is not and does not contain the essence of the thing. The grasping of the one is not that of the other, although here, by essence, a change of attitude and of the direction of grasping is possible through which the grasping of the noema can in each case pass over into that of the corresponding ontic essence. In the latter case, however, we have an intuition of another kind than in the former. If we speak of *Erschauung* only for the intuitive grasping of essences, then we need precisely another term for the intuition of the noema.
>
> It is good to make clear to oneself here first that to posit a noema as being does not mean to posit the objectivity "corresponding" to the noema, although it is the objectivity meant in the noema. That is easy to make clear with thought-meanings, the specifically logical ones. Here it is to be noted that this objectivity occurs in the noema as a moment of unity that *different* noemata can have as "identical", but admittedly in such a way that the unity or objectivity is in quotation marks, just as the identical was with reason put in quotation marks by us a moment ago. With logical meanings we now see that the thought as such (logical meaning in the noematic sense) can be "absurd", it which, within the being-category "logical meaning" and, more generally, "noema", does "exist", has its actual being, as for example the thought-meaning "round square". It is also self-evident that, as the meant simpliciter (which only is if the meaning is a valid one) is something other than the meaning, so too the essence of the meant is something other than the meaning. The essence "round square" there is not; but to be able to judge that, it is presupposed that "round square" is a meaning that is in this unity. It is now clear in further consequence that in the sphere of intuition things cannot stand otherwise at all than in the sphere of thought, only that here an important essential relation holds between noema of intuition and essence of the intuited: that where the former can be grasped, the latter can too, and conversely. That holds generally, even when the intuition is an imperfect one, namely merely one-sided, burdened with undetermined determinabilities, as necessarily in the case of thing-intuition. Then the intuition of the essence too is a precisely correspondingly inadequate one; one must never overlook that intuition of essence by no means eo ipso means adequate intuition. (How full-valid knowledge of essence is nonetheless possible in axioms, and in a certain way, though not on the ground of isolated thing-intuition, a fully sufficient grasping of essence, we shall set out more closely in the "doctrine of ideas".)

The italics on *verschiedene* are the file's. *Erschauung* is left untranslated in the English because the passage is about the word: it is Husserl's term for "seeing" an essence. The apparatus has no note on these pages.

The first paragraph separates by double negation and then concedes a passage between the two. The noema "is not and does not contain" (*ist nicht und enthält auch nicht*) the essence: neither identity nor part. "Even" (*Selbst*) puts the strongest case for confusion first. A *clear* intuition, a *concordant* nexus aimed at *one and the same* thing, comes as near to the essence as a noema can, and still is not it. The concession, "although" (*obschon*), allows "a change of attitude and of the direction of grasping" through which the grasp of the noema "can pass over" into the grasp of the essence. The verb "pass over" (*übergehen*) admits continuity, and the next sentence denies it at the level of intuition: we have "an intuition of another kind". The separation is placed in the act, not in a difference between two contents found side by side.

The terminological sentence is a conditional, and it stops short. "If we speak of *Erschauung* only for the intuitive grasping of essences, then we need precisely another term for the intuition of the noema". Husserl gives the condition and the need and does not meet the need. "Precisely" (*eben*) makes the need follow from the decision, but the decision is not taken either: "if we speak". The file shows that Husserl did not hold to it. Three pages earlier, the work of phenomenology itself stood "in pure *Erschauung*" (*in der puren Erschauung*) and "in immediate *Erschauung*" (Hua V 82). Two pages later, the intuition that brings dark acts into coincidence with clear ones, in which "the meant is conscious as exactly the same, but 'clear'", is again called "the *Erschauung* that accomplishes this", and is set against the "insight" that posits the essence as being (Hua V 88). The second term never comes. The book marks the difference of intuitions by its objects and its postures, not by a name.

The second paragraph moves the argument from intuition to positing, and its marks carry it. "Corresponding" (*„entsprechende“*) is put in quotation marks: the noema has an objectivity that it "means" (*bedeutete*), but positing the noema as being does not posit that objectivity. The objectivity recurs in the noema as "identical", a moment of unity different noemata share. Husserl then notes his own quotation marks: the unity "is in quotation marks, just as the identical was with reason put in quotation marks by us a moment ago". As in § 15.5 (row 16), the marks are part of the argument. Here they mark an objectivity *within* the noema, not the object outside it.

The absurd meaning makes the difference visible. The thought can be "absurd" (*„widersinnig“*) and still "exist", in quotation marks, within the category "logical meaning" and "noema". The next words drop the marks: it "has its actual being" (*sein wirkliches Sein hat*). The marked "exists" is relative to a category. The unmarked "actual being" says that within that category the being is not diminished. Then, with "there is" in its eidetic use (*gibt es*; compare row 11): "The essence 'round square' there is not". The proof of the difference is a presupposition. To judge that the essence does not exist, one must already have "round square" as a meaning that is. The noema is presupposed by the very judgment that denies the essence.

The intuitive case adds the parallel that keeps the two from being separable in content: "where the former can be grasped, the latter can too, and conversely". Husserl turns the parallel against a familiar assumption. If the thing-intuition is one-sided, the intuition of the essence is "precisely correspondingly inadequate". "Intuition of essence by no means eo ipso means adequate intuition". The essence does not escape the inadequacy of the noema it is read from. The parenthesis defers the question of how axioms can still be fully valid to a "doctrine of ideas" (*„Ideenlehre“*), marked as a title. No such part follows in *Ideen III*. The commentary's § 16.2 stops before these sentences.

The passage turns § 15's difference of attitude into a difference of positing. § 15.5 separated the "thing" as correlate from the thing. § 16 separates it from the essence thing, which is harder, because the essence is as ideal as the noema. What follows draws the consequence. Judging ontologically about essences instead of phenomenologically about noemata is "an essential change of judgment-posture" (*Urteilsstellung*), and with it "we have overstepped the domain of phenomenology and entered that of ontology" (Hua V 86–87). The method of clarification in §§ 19–20 depends on this. Clarification brings the meaning of a word to coincidence with the intuitive noema (rows 19 and 20). It shows whether a concept "fits" what is meant, and does not need the "insight" that the essence is or is not. Husserl's example is the squaring of the circle: clarifying the concept "does not require that we carry out the geometrical proof of the impossibility" (Hua V 88). Where the essence is impossible, the noematic intuition grasps the incompatibility, "so to speak the non-essence" (*das Unwesen*), a coinage the file sets in bold.

The *Logical Investigations* had stated the logical half of the case. "The combination *a round square* yields truly a unitary meaning, which has its manner of 'existence', of being in the 'world' of ideal meanings; but it is an apodictic evidence that no existing object can correspond to the existing meaning" (LU IV § 12, Hua XIX/1 334). The quotation marks around "existence" are already there. *Ideen III* changes two things. The meaning that exists is now a noema, and the denied correlate is not an object but an essence. And the intuitive sphere is brought under the same rule, so that the separation of noema and essence holds for perception too, the case where the two are hardest to tell apart.

## §18, Hua V 96–97: the right of intuitionism

Reads [§18.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the third paragraph under § 18.

Knowledge, for Husserl, "is insight" (*Einsicht*): truth drawn from intuition and so fully understood. The sciences, as they grow, lose it. Concepts that were once oriented by intuition become counters in a symbolic method, and the sciences become factories of useful propositions that their workers use without understanding. The reaction of his decade, which he calls "intuitionism", is therefore right against them. Husserl grants the right and takes it over in a sober form. Intuitionism must not turn into mysticism. It must take on the task of clarification, which leads the concepts of the sciences back to the essences and the givens they express. The fourth chapter is that task.

> Taken by the first creators of the sciences partly from the very imperfect naive experience of everyday man, partly clarified only one-sidedly and incompletely in the new formation, they [the concepts] enter into the work of science with a stock of confusion, without in general gaining in depth of clarity as this work goes on. Indeed, here the reverse process takes place, which the occasional going back of original thinkers to the fundamental concepts, in order to remove confusions and conflations that have become noticeable, and with it occasional deepenings of clarity, do not change. The higher science grows, the richer its "methodology" becomes, the more the main work moves into the symbolic sphere of thought; the concepts originally oriented by intuition are used merely symbolically, as counters, with which one operates, without going back again to intuition, in the style of the developed, essentially symbolic method. The art of inventing ever new symbolic procedures, whose rationality is essentially one that merely hangs on the symbolic and already presupposes the cognitive value of the symbols but does not have it with insight, is practised ever more perfectly; what was relatively insightful at a lower level is symbolized again at a higher level and robbed of its insightfulness (as a superfluous burden on thought), and so the sciences become what we know them as: factories of very valuable and practically useful propositions, in which one can work as worker and inventing technician, from which one can draw as practitioner without inner understanding, at best grasping the technical rationality. The "specialists", that is, the engineers of the art of science, may be highly satisfied with this state of affairs, in the consciousness of their greatness and in the organized collaboration of the large-scale scientific enterprise with its infinitely fruitful achievements. And so may the technicians in the ordinary sense be satisfied, whose goal is the practical mastery of reality. Knowledge counts for them from the outset as nothing other than the artful invention of thought for the purposes of artful achievements in the practice of the mastery of nature and of man. However much this conception of knowledge seemed to prevail in the "century of technology", there was no lack of reactions, which in the last decade have grown mightily in force and have inaugurated a great reversal. The advances of science have not enriched us in treasures of insight. The world has not become in the least more understandable through them, it has only become more useful for us. Treasures of knowledge may lie in the sciences, indeed they must lie in them, since we cannot doubt that the claim to validity of their propositions is a good one, though still to be limited. But these propositions of knowledge we do not have; we must first win them. For knowledge is insight, is truth drawn from intuition and thereby fully understood. Only through a work of elucidation and of making-insightful newly carried out on the given sciences do we raise the intrinsic values that lie hidden in them. True statements are not eo ipso intrinsic values, and just as little, then, are methods for producing such statements. Only mediately, first as means of insight, do they have value, for "insights" are intrinsic values; and then further as means for founded values, e.g. for activities of valuing and practical reason. Against the one-sided devotion of humanity to the development of the sciences as thought-techniques, intuitionism therefore reacts with full right. It is a matter of putting an end to the plight of reason, which has become unbearable, which in the midst of all the riches of theoretical possession sees its proper goal, understanding of the world, insight into truth, recede ever further. But admittedly intuitionism must not break out into mysticism, instead of approaching the sober tasks that arise from the situation described. It is a matter of leading the sciences back to their origin, which demands insight and strict validity, and of transforming them into systems of insightful knowledge through clarifying, elucidating, ultimately grounding work, of leading the concepts and propositions back to the conceptual essences themselves graspable in intuition and to the factual givens themselves, to which they give adequate expression in so far as they are really true.

The sciences become "factories" (*Fabriken*) with "workers" and "inventing technicians", and their masters are the "specialists" (*„Fachmänner“*), glossed at once as "the engineers of the art of science". The quotation marks hold the honorific at a distance, as they did twice before. In § 3 the "specialists" (*„Fachmännern“*), mathematicians among them, were said to hold that only the specialist may judge his science (Hua V 12–13). In § 5 it was not those "who call themselves 'specialists'" who make the method, but the essence of the objects (Hua V 22). "Knowledge counts for them from the outset as nothing other than the artful invention of thought". The adjective *kunstmäßig* (artful, done by craft) is said twice in one sentence, and *Kunst* returns in *Wissenschaftskunst*. Technique takes over the word for the science itself.

The dated phrases place the passage. The "century of technology" (*„Jahrhundert der Technik“*) is a slogan in quotation marks, and the reactions against it "have grown mightily in force in the last decade". In the manuscript of 1912 that is the first decade of the century. Husserl names the doctrine, "intuitionism", and none of its holders. The diagnosis he gives it is his own: the world "has not become in the least more understandable", "it has only become more useful for us".

The argument turns on "For" (*Denn*): "For knowledge is insight". This is a definition, and everything before it depends on it. If knowledge were valid propositions, the sciences would have it. Because knowledge is insight, the sciences only harbour "treasures of knowledge" that "we must first win". The modal verbs are exact: the treasures "may lie" in the sciences, "indeed they must", since their validity claims are good, "though still to be limited". Husserl does not doubt the sciences' truth. He denies that their truth is already knowledge.

The typescript change sharpens this. The manuscript said true statements "are no intrinsic values" (*keine Selbstwerte*). Husserl later wrote "are not eo ipso intrinsic values" (*nicht ohne weiteres Selbstwerte*). The first version makes truth without insight worthless in itself, the second only conditional. The footnote at "'insights' are intrinsic values" builds the condition into the concept of insight. "Insight" "means more than the fleeting act of seeing-into". Only truths "acquired from insightful primal institution" (*aus einsichtiger Urstiftung*) and "at any time to be restored to the original state of actual insight" have "wisdom-value, that is, genuine personal value". Insight is thus a habitual possession that can be reactivated, not a single act. The footnote also admits judgments that are "not explicitly seen" but recommended "in the manner of a presentiment, of an evidence anticipating in its way". This is the "mixture of insight and instinct" of the preceding paragraph (§ 18.2), from which valuable products arise without insight.

"Therefore" (*also*) then draws the conclusion: intuitionism reacts "with full right". The right is limited at once by "But admittedly" (*Aber freilich*): it must not "break out into mysticism" (*in Mystizismus ausschlagen*). Mysticism would keep the intuition and give up the sciences. Husserl keeps the sciences and requires that their concepts be led back to intuition. The verb "lead back" (*zurückführen*) appears twice in the last sentence: the sciences to "their origin", the concepts and propositions to "the conceptual essences themselves" and "the factual givens themselves". Three adjectives name the work: "clarifying, elucidating, ultimately grounding" (*klärende, verdeutlichende, letzt-begründende*). The first two are the paired terms of § 20 (row 20), elucidation and clarification. So the passage sets the programme of the fourth chapter. § 19 calls clarification "in the main the Socratic procedure" (row 19), and § 15 had called phenomenology the science of the "origins" (row 15). The sober task of intuitionism is phenomenology.

The 1930 Afterword shows the alliance turned round. There the opponents are "life-philosophy struggling for dominance", the "new anthropology" and the philosophy of "existence", and their charge against phenomenology is "'intellectualism' or 'rationalism'" (Hua V 138; row 27). In 1912 Husserl grants the reaction against technique "full right" and warns it off mysticism. In 1930 the heirs of that reaction accuse him of intellectualism. Husserl's own work was charged with mysticism too. A late text printed in the Hua XXVII volume under the title "Über Ursprung" says that his accounts of essential universals "had to suffer the often repeated reproach of mysticism, of Platonizing realism" (*den oft wiederholten Vorwurf der Mystik, des platonisierenden Realismus*; Hua XXVII 131). Husserl places phenomenology between the two charges. It is intuitionism without mysticism, and it is a science of essences without being the "factory" that symbolic method made of the sciences.

## §19, Hua V 100: what the thing is "called" for

Reads [§19.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the fourth paragraph under § 19.

Clarification asks what a word "properly means". It does not answer by collecting what the instances have in common, and it does not fix how the word is used. It brings the word-meaning to coincidence (*Deckung*) with an intuitively given example, and sees which moments of the example the word covers: the moments "on account of which the thing is so 'called'". Those moments are an essence. When it is found, the concept proves valid, because an essence now corresponds to it. Husserl calls this "in the main the Socratic procedure". The qualification matters. What survives of Socrates is the question what a thing is called for. What is new is that the answer is read off intuition, in coincidence, and that it is an essence.

> In order now to bring to clarity the essence of clarification itself, which serves the noetic perfecting of all sciences, let us consider the sense of the achievements to be demanded in an example. Suppose it is a matter, for example, of bringing the concept of the material body to clarity: what that "properly means", "thing", what it looks like. We then start from examples that represent indubitable applications of the word thing, e.g. stones, houses and the like, but we are not content merely to scoop these up by names, so to speak, that is, to "think" them through mere word-meanings; we pass over rather to intuition, to the perception of such individual things or to lively phantasy, which plays the role here of a "phantasying oneself into the perceptual givenness of such things". Were we to make comparisons between the different objects presenting themselves as examples in givenness or quasi-givenness, we would find differences and things in common. But it is not a matter of finding, proceeding inductively, what is everywhere common; we look rather to see what in the intuitively given is, so to speak, lifted out, covered, conceptually meant by the word-concept and what is not, what in it is properly the "so-called", or which essential moments of the intuitively given are those on account of which the thing is "called" just so. All the same, in the main the Socratic procedure. Naturally it is not a matter of fixing a usage of language, but of bringing a noetic essence to relief in the intuitively given in such coincidence, and of fixing it as that which is what is meant by the mere word-meaning. Precisely thereby the word-meaning, the concept, proves valid; for now an essence corresponds to the concept.

German:

> Um uns nun das Wesen der noetischen Vervollkommnung aller Wissenschaften dienenden Klärung selbst zur Klarheit zu bringen, überlegen wir den Sinn der zu fordernden Leistungen an einem Beispiel: Es handle sich z.B. darum, den Begriff des materiellen Körpers zur Klarheit zu bringen, was das „eigentlich bedeute“: „Ding“, wie das aussehe. Wir gehen dann von Beispielen aus, die unzweifelhafte Anwendungen des Wortes Ding darstellen, z.B. Steine, Häuser u. dgl., begnügen uns aber nicht damit, diese bloß durch Namen sozusagen aufzuraffen, also durch bloße Wortbedeutungen zu „denken“, wir gehen vielmehr zur Anschauung über, zur Wahrnehmung solcher Einzelheiten oder zur lebendigen Phantasie, die hier die Rolle spielt eines „sich in die wahrnehmende Gegebenheit solcher Sachen hineinphantasieren“. Würden wir Vergleichungen anstellen zwischen den verschiedenen sich in der Gegebenheit oder Quasi-Gegebenheit exemplarisch darbietenden Gegenständen, so fänden wir Differenzen und Gemeinsames. Es handelt sich aber nicht darum, induktiv vorgehend das überall Gemeinsame zu finden, wir sehen vielmehr zu, was an dem anschaulich Gegebenen von dem Wortbegriff sozusagen herausgehoben, gedeckt, begrifflich gemeint wird und was nicht, was daran das „so heißende“ eigentlich ist, bzw. welche Wesensmomente des anschaulich Gegebenen es sind, um derentwillen die Sache gerade so „heiße“. Immerhin, der Hauptsache nach das Sokratische Verfahren. Natürlich handelt es sich nicht darum, einen Sprachgebrauch zu fixieren, sondern in solcher Deckung im anschaulich Gegebenen ein noetisches Wesen zur Abhebung zu bringen und es das als zu fixieren, welches das durch die bloße Wortbedeutung Gemeinte ist. Eben damit erweist sich die Wortbedeutung, der Begriff, als gültig; denn dem Begriff entspricht nun ein Wesen.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph is hard-wrapped in the file (src L1389–L1418, Hua V 100); line-end hyphens are joined. Two places are doubtful. "es das als zu fixieren" is out of order, probably for *es als das zu fixieren* (to fix it as that which …), and is rendered so. "ein noetisches Wesen" (a noetic essence) is the file's reading where the commentary has "noematic essence". The paragraph's first sentence also has "noetic", in "the noetic perfecting of all sciences" (*noetischen Vervollkommnung*), where the word means pertaining to knowledge, so the second "noetic" may be the same word in the same older sense, or a slip for *noematisches*. The file cannot settle it and the apparatus has no note at 100; the reading below does not rest on either word (see Corrections, §19.4). The apparatus has no other note on the page.

The question is put in the subjunctive of reported speech and in quotation marks: what the concept "'properly means'" (*„eigentlich bedeute“*), then the word itself, "'thing'", then "what it looks like" (*wie das aussehe*). The three phrases set the order of the procedure. The meaning is asked for, the word is held up, and the answer is to come from how the thing looks. The rejected alternative is to "scoop up" (*aufzuraffen*) examples "by names" and to "'think'" them "through mere word-meanings". The quotation marks on "think" make the empty use of words a kind of thinking only so called. Phantasy is admitted as an equal source in a phrase that also takes quotation marks: "'phantasying oneself into the perceptual givenness'". It gives "quasi-givenness" (*Quasi-Gegebenheit*), and that is enough for an essence.

Then comes the refusal of induction. Comparison would yield "differences and things in common", but the procedure is not "to find, proceeding inductively, what is everywhere common". What replaces it is a single look at the example, with the word-concept held against it. Three participles describe what the concept does to the given: it is "lifted out, covered, conceptually meant" (*herausgehoben, gedeckt, begrifflich gemeint*). The middle one is the technical word. "Covered" (*gedeckt*) becomes "coincidence" (*Deckung*) in the next sentence: the concept covers part of the intuited as a fulfilling intuition covers an intention. The procedure is therefore a test of fulfilment, not of extension.

The key phrases are built from *heißen* (to be called). What the thing is "properly" is "the 'so-called'" (*das „so heißende“*), and the essential moments are those "on account of which the thing is 'called' just so" (*um derentwillen die Sache gerade so „heiße“*). The verb is the ordinary one for naming, and the quotation marks hold it to that sense. The question "why is this called a thing?" is asked in the words of everyday speech. "On account of which" (*um derentwillen*) gives the answer the form of a ground. The name is not arbitrary. It is answerable to moments in the thing.

"All the same" (*Immerhin*) is a concession, and a verbless one: "All the same, in the main the Socratic procedure." The comparison comes after the refusal of induction, and it survives it only "in the main". The Socratic dialogue asks what courage or justice is, and tests answers against examples. Husserl keeps the question and the test. He drops what the dialogue relies on, the collection of instances and the search for what they share, and he replaces it with coincidence in one example. "Naturally" (*Natürlich*) then rules out the reading that would make the method linguistic: "not a matter of fixing a usage of language". The essence is brought "to relief" (*zur Abhebung*) in the given and is fixed as what the word-meaning means. Validity follows: "for now an essence corresponds to the concept". The connective "for" (*denn*) makes the validity of a concept consist in the correspondence of an essence. A concept without one, such as "squaring of the circle" in § 16, can be clarified (Hua V 88), but it does not prove valid.

The paragraph is the first example of the method that § 18 demanded. The sciences' concepts had become "counters" used "without going back again to intuition" (Hua V 96–97; row 18). § 19 sorts concepts into the logico-formal, the regional and their material particularizations, and gives the order in which they should be clarified (Hua V 98). It then begins, as the example shows, with a regional concept, "thing". The next section separates this procedure from another that also works on concepts. Elucidation (*Verdeutlichung*) analyses "what lies in the meaning", as in "decahedron", "within the mere sphere of thought", and needs no intuition (§ 20, Hua V 101; row 20). Clarification is the procedure of § 19. § 20 also adds the later steps the example leaves out. For a concept from the sphere of reality, a single look does not suffice: "a thing is not given, a thing-concept is not brought to real clarity, if a thing is simply seen", because "a phantom is seen too". Clarification "must follow exactly the levels of constitution" (Hua V 104). The "Socratic" look at a stone becomes, for "thing", the whole analysis of the earlier chapters.

The late Husserl keeps the comparison with Socrates and supplies the method it lacked here. In a supplementary essay of the *Crisis* volume, the "Socratic return to evidence" is "the making-clear of the fields of pure possibilities in the example, the free variation, which keeps the identity of sense … and lets this identity be seen" (*der sokratische Rückgang zur Evidenz … das sich am Exempel Klarmachen der Felder reiner Möglichkeiten, die freie Abwandlung*; Hua VI 280). There the example is varied freely, and what holds through the variation is the essence. In 1912 the example is held against the word, and what the word covers is the essence. Both reject induction, and both credit Socrates with the example. The later text gives Socrates variation, which § 19 does not mention, and so moves him closer to phenomenology than the "in the main" of 1912 allows.

## §20, Hua V 101–102: elucidation and clarification

Reads [§20.1 and §20.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the first and third paragraphs under § 20.

Two procedures work on a complex concept, and they work in different spheres. Elucidation (*Verdeutlichung*) makes the concept distinct. It takes apart what lies in the meaning, and stays "within the mere sphere of thought", without intuition. Clarification (*Klärung*) makes it clear. It brings the meaning to coincidence with the noema of an intuition, so that each partial concept the elucidation found has a moment of the intuited to correspond to it. The word adds a third relation. The word "means" something, but its sound carries a "word-tendency" that demands a meaning, and may demand several. So confusion enters science through the word as well as through the concept, and clarification has to fix the word too: it underlines one tendency and crosses out the rest.

> Here the following is still to be distinguished: if the concept is a complex one, *elucidation* and *clarification* are to be kept apart. The elucidation of the concept, of what is meant with the word as such, is a procedure that plays itself out within the mere sphere of thought. Before the least step towards clarification is taken, while no intuition or a quite unfitting and indirect one is one with the word, it can be considered what lies in the meaning, e.g. in "decahedron": a body, a polyhedron, regular, with ten congruent faces. Likewise it can be successively drawn out and joined in the forms belonging to the sphere of thought: "a geometrical body which is completely bounded by ten congruent plane surfaces". The logical form, the "synthesis" in which the path of thought is formed, can here be different; there are many "equivalent" expressions for the same state of affairs. We have therefore to distinguish the unelucidated, unanalysed concept and the analytically elucidated one, and the "analytic judgments", in the sense Kant probably had in view at first, which, identifying, set up the noematic object of the one and of the other as the same. […]
>
> It is further to be noted that attention is to be paid not merely to the relation between meaning and the intuited as such (thought-noema and intuition-noema), but also to the relation of the word itself, with its word-sound and the word-tendency adhering to it, and the noemata. The word "means" something; that can say: its meaning demands a fitting essence that fulfils it. But it can also be said: the word with the tendency adhering to its word-sound demands a meaning and is one with it as word. The word can, however, be equivocal and as word demand different meanings, and it can demand now unconnected, now closely related, now wider, now narrower, now more general, now less general meanings.

> Dabei ist noch Folgendes zu unterscheiden: ist der Begriff ein komplexer, so sind *Verdeutlichung* und *Klärung* auseinanderzuhalten. Die Verdeutlichung des Begriffs, des mit dem Wort Gemeinten als solchen, ist eine Prozedur, die sich innerhalb der bloßen Denksphäre abspielt. Ehe der mindeste Schritt zur Klärung vollzogen wird, während keine oder eine ganz unpassende und indirekte Anschauung mit dem Worte eins ist, kann überlegt werden, was in der Meinung liegt, z. Desgleichen kann sukzessiv herausgeholt und in den zur Denksphäre gehörigen Formen verknüpft werden: „ein geometrischer Körper, welcher von zehn kongruenten ebenen Flächen vollständig begrenzt ist“. Das Wort „meint” etwas, das kann sagen, seine Bedeutung fordert ein passendes, sie erfüllendes Wesen. The second block is the middle of src L1428 (Hua V 102); the file runs the paragraph on from the end of the conflict sentence across the running head for p. 102 (L1426), and the commentary counts it as § 20.3. "das Wort mit seinem Wortlaut anhaftenden Tendenz" has lost a word (probably *mit seiner seinem Wortlaut anhaftenden Tendenz*, with the tendency adhering to its sound), and "und Wort verschiedene Bedeutungen" probably lacks *als* (as word); both are rendered in the sense the first sentence fixes. The italics on *Verdeutlichung* and *Klärung* are the file's. The apparatus has no note on these pages.

The terms are the adjectives *deutlich* (distinct) and *klar* (clear) turned into procedures, and Husserl's example places them. The decahedron is the case of a concept that can be fully elucidated and never clarified in the sense of § 19. It is the "regular decahedron" that "there is not", by § 8's eidetic "there is" (Hua V 47–48; row 11). Elucidation can analyse it into "a body, a polyhedron, regular, with ten congruent faces", and join the marks in a definition, while "no intuition or a quite unfitting and indirect one is one with the word". The example is chosen so that elucidation succeeds where clarification must fail. That makes the independence of the two procedures visible.

Elucidation is described by its products. Its forms are the "synthesis" of a path of thought, in quotation marks, and they vary; there are "many 'equivalent' expressions for the same state of affairs". The marks on "equivalent" mark a logician's word: the expressions differ as meanings, and agree only in their object. The next sentence states this. The elucidated concept "is as concept, as meaning, another" (Hua V 101). Elucidation therefore does not lay bare what was already there. It sets a second concept beside the first. Then Husserl names its judgments: the "analytic judgments" "in the sense Kant probably had in view at first". "Probably at first" (*wohl zunächst*) is a double hedge. Husserl claims Kant for elucidation only in a first, provisional sense of "analytic". Kant had called these judgments "explicative" (*Erläuterungs-*) judgments: they break the concept of the subject up "into its partial concepts, which were already thought in it (though confusedly)" (*die in selbigen schon (obgleich verworren) gedacht waren*; KrV, Introduction, section IV). Husserl's "partial concept" (*Partialbegriff*) takes up Kant's *Teilbegriffe*, and "confusedly" is the opposite of "distinctly". Husserl adds what Kant's account did not have, the noema. The analytic judgment "identifying, sets up the noematic object of the one and the other as the same".

Clarification "transcends the sphere of mere word-meanings". Its relation is "coincidence" (*Deckung*), and its standard is completeness: to "every partial concept" taken out by elucidation "an explicated moment of the intuitive noema" must correspond (Hua V 101). Elucidation thus sets the agenda of clarification. It lists the marks that intuition must then fill. Where intuition cannot fill them together, the result is "conflict" (*Widerstreit*), and the meaning-explicates demand correlates that "'do not get on'" (*„nicht vertragen“*) in one intuition. That is what happens with the decahedron.

The second block adds the word. Husserl separates two senses of "the word 'means' something", with "means" in quotation marks. In the first, "its meaning demands a fitting essence that fulfils it", the relation clarification serves. In the second, "the word with the tendency adhering to its word-sound demands a meaning". The verb "demands" (*fordert*) governs both clauses. The word demands a meaning, as the meaning demands an essence. The "word-tendency" (*Worttendenz*) is the habit by which a sound calls up a meaning. A word can be "equivocal" (*äquivok*) and demand many meanings, "now unconnected, now closely related, now wider, now narrower". The six "now … now" phrases (*bald … bald*) list the kinds of equivocation from homonymy to differences of generality.

The function this gives clarification is stated in typography. "Certain of the word-tendencies adhering to it are, so to speak, crossed out, and a single tendency clarified on the intuited is underlined once for all as the valid one and impressed on memory" (*durchgestrichen … unterstrichen*; Hua V 102). Clarification gives "the old words a newly constituted sense". It does not invent terms. It fixes old ones. That is the practice of the whole book: "body" (*Leib*), "appearance", "region" and "thing" are old words given a sense clarified on the intuited, and their quotation marks are the underlining. § 19 had said that clarification is not "fixing a usage" (row 19). § 20 shows how it still ends in one. The usage follows the essence and is fixed by it.

Husserl does not hold his own pair to one use. A few lines on, "word-elucidation (verbal sense-analysis) has a propaedeutic function for the intuitive elucidation properly to be carried out" (*die eigentlich zu vollziehende intuitive Verdeutlichung*; Hua V 102). Elucidation is now split into a verbal kind and an intuitive kind, and the intuitive kind is part of clarification. The distinction of § 20.1 is one of spheres, thought and intuition. Analysis goes on in both. The pair separates where analysis takes place, not whether it takes place.

The section then states the aim. Clarification would "create the pre-given concept anew, so to speak", "feed it from the primal source of conceptual validity, intuition". For the found essence "the 'expression' is created and the relevant word-tendency underlined" (Hua V 103). For concepts of reality, the clarifying intuition must "follow exactly the levels of constitution", because "a phantom is seen too" (Hua V 104). This gives the book's analyses of thing, body and soul a place in the programme of § 18. They are the intuitive side of the clarification that the sciences' concepts need, and their terminology is the underlined word-tendency. Where the file breaks off at the end of § 20, the method is still being joined to the idea of a complete system of clarified terms "lying at infinity".

## Supplement I §1, Hua V 112: the soul's "representation"-appearance

Reads [Supplement I §1.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the fourth paragraph under § 1 of Supplement I.

The soul does not appear as a thing appears, by adumbrating itself. It is nonetheless given to every other I in a way that the draft calls an "appearance", a "representation"-appearance in the noema of empathy. Each I has its own such representation of the other's soul. The objective soul is the unity attested across all of them, and it is constituted only when the subjects can come to an understanding about it. Husserl keeps the word "appearance" for this and warns against it in the same breath. In the main text (§ 11) the word is refused: there are no "appearances" of the soul. The draft and the main text describe the same givenness. They differ over whether "appearance" may name it.

> With this, however, we do not yet have the objectivity of the soul and the objectivity of the real connection of soul and body, and so the *full* human being; rather, this is the experiential unity, primarily intuitive for *my* experiencing pure I $I_o$, "foreign human being for me", mere "appearance" $S^o$ (an expression, admittedly, easily misunderstood); any other I $I^k$, as purely experiencing, has of "the same" being $S^k$. But what does that say other than that, by virtue of a possible mutual understanding, this identification is possible, that only with it is the *objective* soul constituted, objectively one with the objective body, and with that the objective human being (so no longer $L_m^k$ and $L_m^k S_m^k$, nor $L^k S^k$, but $L^k S^k$ and $M^k$ without lower index). We thereby understand the phenomenological construction of the apperception of soul and of human being according to noesis and noema, so far as constitution in general is in question. The soul does not appear in the sense that it adumbrates itself, but the soul becomes presentive in every pure consciousness (or becomes so for every pure I) in a mere (noematic) "representation"-appearance.
>
> Apprehended as objective being, it is the unity attesting itself in all these representations of the soul (and every I that has experience of the same soul possesses such a representation: the $S_k$) and certifying itself in mutual understanding. In the *sense* of this apperception lies this kind of certification (and so of experience that shows forth), and to want to demand that the soul be given itself and adequately, or given in the same way as an experience or a stream of experience, is absurd.

German:

> Damit haben wir aber noch nicht die Objektivität der Seele und die Objektivität der realen Verknüpfung Seele und Leib, also *voller* Mensch; vielmehr ist das die für *mein* erfahrendes reines Ich $I_o$ primär anschauliche Erfahrungseinheit „fremder Mensch für mich“, bloße „Erscheinung“ $S^o$ (ein freilich leicht mißverständlicher Ausdruck), ein beliebiges anderes Ich $I^k$ als rein erfahrendes hat von „demselben“ Sein $S^k$. Was besagt das aber anderes, als daß vermöge einer möglichen Wechselverständigung diese Identifizierung möglich ist, daß sich erst mit ihr die *objektive* Seele, objektiv einig mit dem objektiven Leib, und damit der objektive Mensch konstituiert (also nicht mehr $L_m^k$ und $L_m^k S_m^k$, auch nicht $L^k S^k$, sondern $L^k S^k$ und $M^k$ ohne unteren Index). Die Seele erscheint nicht in dem Sinne, daß sie sich abschattet, die Seele wird aber in jedem reinen Bewußtsein (bzw. wird jedem reinen Ich) vorstellig in einer bloßen (noematischen) „Vorstellungs“-erscheinung.
>
> Als objektives Sein aufgefaßt ist sie die sich in allen diesen Vorstellungen von der Seele (und jedes Ich, das von derselben Seele Erfahrung hat, besitzt eine solche Vorstellung: das $S_k$) bekundende und in der Wechselverständigung sich beurkundende Einheit. Im *Sinn* dieser Apperzeption liegt diese Art der Beurkundung (also der ausweisenden Erfahrung), und verlangen wollen, daß die Seele selbst und adäquat gegeben wäre, oder gegeben wäre in derselben Weise wie ein Erlebnis oder Erlebnisstrom, ist Widersinn.

*Note on the text.* The passage is on Hua V 112 (src L1459–L1460, between the running heads for pp. 111 and 113). The italics are the file's. Two places are damaged in ways that bear on the formulas, not on the argument. "hat von „demselben“ Sein $S^k$" lacks a word or more ("has of 'the same' being [its own] $S^k$" is the likely sense, as the commentary reads it). In the parenthesis, "auch nicht $L^k S^k$, sondern $L^k S^k$" sets the same formula against itself: the conversion has lost a distinction of indices, so the reading does not argue from the notation. The second block is the beginning of src L1460; the paragraph goes on to ask whether one has a more direct experience of one's own soul. The apparatus has no note on p. 112.

The sentence turns on the marks around "appearance". The experiential unity "foreign human being for me" is "mere 'appearance'" (*bloße „Erscheinung“*), and Husserl adds at once "(an expression, admittedly, easily misunderstood)". The parenthesis concedes that the word is the wrong one and keeps it. The danger is the sense that "appearance" has for the thing: a unity presented in adumbrations, with sides and standpoints. The last sentence rules that sense out, "The soul does not appear in the sense that it adumbrates itself", and gives the word a second sense, in a compound with its own marks: "a mere (noematic) 'representation'-appearance" (*„Vorstellungs“-erscheinung*). The marks fall on "representation" (*Vorstellung*), not on "appearance". What is given is a representing of the other's soul, which counts as an appearance only in the noema of empathy, and the adjective "noematic" in parentheses says so.

The other words in quotation marks are the ones that carry the step to objectivity. "Foreign human being for me" (*„fremder Mensch für mich“*) is a phrase in quotation marks, the name of a noema. The other I has its own appearance of "the same" (*„demselben“*) being. The marks hold the sameness open until the next sentence earns it. "What does that say other than" (*Was besagt das aber anderes*) turns the description into a definition: the sameness is the possibility of identification "by virtue of a possible mutual understanding" (*Wechselverständigung*). "Only" (*erst*) makes it a condition. The objective soul is constituted "only with" this identification. Before it there are as many soul-appearances as there are subjects.

The second block names the soul's way of being given. Objectively, the soul is "the unity attesting itself (*sich bekundende*) in all these representations" and "certifying itself (*sich beurkundende*) in mutual understanding". The two reflexive verbs divide the work. Each I has the soul attested in its representation; the soul is certified, as a document is certified, only between them. Then Husserl turns the description into a norm. To demand "that the soul be given itself and adequately, or given in the same way as an experience" is "absurd" (*Widersinn*). The word is the strongest in his vocabulary of error. A soul given adequately would no longer be a soul, but an experience.

The page belongs to the first part of the 1912 manuscript, the draft of the second book; the main text of *Ideen III* is its second part (apparatus, "Zu Beilage I"). § 11 of the main text says that the soul "is no substance of appearances", that "there are no 'appearances' of the soul", and that the soul-states are "adequately given … themselves and without mediations by way of appearance" (Hua V 67–68; row 13). Read against the draft, this is not a contradiction but a change of standpoint and of vocabulary. § 11 speaks of the soul in psychology's experience, in which its states are themselves the theme and are given without adumbration. The draft speaks of another's soul in empathy. There the soul is not given in its states, but in a representation of them. Both refuse adumbration. Only the draft calls what remains "appearance", and even the draft distrusts the word. The same paragraph says that one's own soul, as objective, is a unity of "'appearances', of 'representations'", which are already "'realities'" for the single I but "not 'substances'". "They are mere 'appearances', or whatever one wants to call it" (*oder wie man es nennen will*; Hua V 112–113). The marks and the shrug both show a word kept for want of a better one. § 11 then refuses it.

The draft also qualifies § 11's privilege of the first person. Every pure I has of its pure consciousness "immediate, and that means here: not empathy-based, experience". The objectivity of one's own soul "presupposes mutual understanding" all the same, and the grasp of one's own soul would remain "quite rudimentary" without the grasp of another's (Hua V 112–113). Adequate givenness holds of experiences. It does not extend to the soul as an objective real, one's own included.

*Ideen II* in its worked-out form keeps the draft's word and its hedge. Empathy, interwoven with outer perception, is appresenting, "in an apperception that *realizes* the whole soul-life and soul-being into a kind of appearance-unity (*einer Art Erscheinungseinheit*), namely an identical of manifold appearances and of states localized in them" (Hua IV § 46, 169). "A kind of" does the work of the 1912 parenthesis. In both books the other's soul has "appearances" in a sense that has to be marked, and the soul attests itself in them without adumbrating itself. The main text of *Ideen III* states the asymmetry with the thing without the word. The draft and *Ideen II* state it with the word and the warning.

## Supplement I §2, Hua V 114: the pure I realized as an "empirical" I

Reads [Supplement I §2.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the second paragraph under § 2 of Supplement I.

The pure I is the identical subject of its cogitations, adequately given in each of them. When the cogitations are apprehended as dependent on the body and on the world, their subject is apprehended with them. It becomes an "empirical" I: real, dependent on circumstances, and known only through experience, which never comes to an end with it. The draft of 1912 reaches this I by the route of empathy. The pure I first gets a relation to experience when it is interpreted into another human being, and each I can "accordingly" apprehend itself in the same way. Husserl's later marginal note in the typescript reverses the route. What is "first in itself" is the experiential apperception "empirical I", and it is the foundation of empathy, not its result.

> That, e.g., the carrying out of a proof goes "more easily" on "repetition", that a newly won insight crosses out the corresponding earlier errors of one's own, or tends to experience those that recur in the character of crossing-out, that accordingly such acts stand there as dependent on earlier ones, are familiar facts. But if we now look to the pure subject of the cogitations, and consider that it is something identically one in their many-shaped change, we see that it must, precisely as their identical subject, necessarily undergo with them a corresponding realizing apprehension. The I that thinks there is now no longer just the one thinking thus at one time and otherwise at another, but a real I that, belonging to this body, animating it and dependent on it, comports itself in a corresponding manner in relation to a change of real circumstances. The pure I has now entered into an apprehension, has undergone an apprehension, through which an "empirical" I constitutes itself, an I that is now no longer to be taken, as what it is, from every cogito and adequately given in the taking, but that one must first get to know, and with the knowledge of which one yet never comes to an end, because it is experience that has to give us this knowledge (experience of the type of apprehension that is constitutive precisely for this I), and, like all experience (as related to something real), leads us into the endless possibilities of ever new circumstances, under which the real shows precisely what it is, in the manner of its dependences.

German:

> Daß z.B. der Vollzug eines Beweises bei „Wiederholung“ „leichter“ vonstatten geht, daß eine neu gewonnene Einsicht die entsprechenden eigenen früheren Irrtümer durchstreicht, bzw. dahin tendiert, die wiederautretenden im Charakter der Durchstreichung zu erleben, daß demnach solche Akte als von früheren abhängig dastehen, sind bekannte Tatsachen. Blicken wir nun aber auf das reine Subjekt der Cogitationen hin und bedenken wir; daß es ein Identisch-Einheitliches in ihrem vielgestaltigen Wechsel ist, so sehen wir, daß es mit ihnen eben als ihr identisches Subjekt notwendig eine entsprechende realisierende Auffassung erfahren muß. Das Ich, das da denkt, ist nun nicht mehr überhaupt das einmal so, das andere Mal anders denkend, sondern reales Ich, das — zu diesem Leib gehörend, ihn belebend und von ihm abhängig — sich mit Beziehung auf einen Wechsel realer Umstände in entsprechender Weise verhält. Das reine Ich ist jetzt in eine Auffassung eingegangen, hat eine Auffassung erfahren, durch die sich ein „empirisches“ Ich konstituiert, ein Ich, das nun nicht mehr als das, was es ist, aus jedem cogito zu entnehmen und in der Entnehmung adäquat gegeben ist, sondern das man erst kennen lernen muß, und mit dessen Kenntnis man doch nie zu Ende kommt, weil es Erfahrung ist, die uns diese Kenntnis zu geben hat (Erfahrung des Auffassungstypes, der eben für dieses Ich konstitutiv ist) und wie alle Erfahrung (als auf Reales bezogen) uns in die endlosen Möglichkeiten von immer neuen Umständen hineinführt, unter denen das Reale eben zeigt, was es ist, in der Weise seiner Abhängigkeiten.

*Note on the text.* The first sentence begins on Hua V 113 and runs across the page marker into p. 114 (src L1470/L1473); the rest is on p. 114. "wiederautretenden" is a slip for *wiederauftretenden* (recurring), and the semicolon in "bedenken wir;" stands for a comma. The apparatus prints two marginal remarks by Husserl in the Landgrebe typescript for these pages. At 113,35–45: "Das ist wohl kein klares Beispiel für die Konstitution des realen Ich. Die ganze Darstellung des weiteren ist unzureichend." (That is probably no clear example for the constitution of the real I. The whole presentation of what follows is insufficient.) At 114,6–16, which falls in the passage above: "Systematischer Mangel dieser Darstellung: Es müßte erst gezeigt werden, wie das solipsistische Ich sich selbst nie bloß als reines, sondern als empirisches Ich auffaßt. Das an sich Erste ist die Erfahrungapperzeption ,,empirisches Ich", und die ist Fundament der Eindeutung." (Systematic deficiency of this presentation: it would first have to be shown how the solipsistic I never apprehends itself merely as pure but as empirical I. What is first in itself is the experiential apperception "empirical I", and it is the foundation of interpretation [empathy].) Which example the first remark means the apparatus does not say; by the line numbers it falls on the end of p. 113, where the draft's example is the cup of coffee drunk "in order to have good theoretical ideas".

The inference that carries the passage rests on identity. The cogitations are apprehended as dependent on the body; the pure I "is something identically one in their many-shaped change"; so "it must, precisely as their identical subject, necessarily undergo with them a corresponding realizing apprehension". "Precisely as" (*eben als*) and "necessarily" (*notwendig*) make the step look like a consequence of identity. It is weaker than that. The paragraph before has just said that the pure I "is something totally other than all the unities we have come to know", "not a unity that presents or manifests itself in any sense", and that its intersubjective objectification "is self-evidently no realization" (Hua V 113). So the realization of the I cannot follow from what the I is. It follows from the apprehension of the stream it belongs to. The draft says as much a page earlier, in a hedged form. It is not ruled out that pure I's "can take on new apprehensions and thereby, in the sense of these apprehensions, transform themselves *as it were* (*gleichsam*) into real I's". The adverb "as it were" is the limit of the claim. The pure I does not become real. It is apprehended as real.

The verbs state this exactly. The pure I "has entered into an apprehension, has undergone an apprehension" (*ist … in eine Auffassung eingegangen, hat eine Auffassung erfahren*). The two phrases repeat the movement of § 12 of the main text, where the pure experience "enters with its whole essence into the psychic state and undergoes only an apperception that does not alter it" (Hua V 75; row 14). The result is marked: an "empirical" (*„empirisches“*) I. The quotation marks hold "empirical" to its sense here, the I given in experience as a real, against its sense as a mere fact.

The contrast is one of givenness. The pure I can be "taken" (*entnehmen*) "from every cogito" and is "adequately given in the taking". The empirical I is the one "one must first get to know" (*erst kennen lernen muß*), and "with the knowledge of which one yet never comes to an end". "Never" is grounded twice: by "experience" of the apprehension-type constitutive for this I, and by experience of the real "as related to something real", which "leads us into the endless possibilities of ever new circumstances". The empirical I is known as a thing is known, by its dependences, "under which the real shows precisely what it is". The draft makes the self a real in the sense of § 1 of the main text: something "that is what it is only in causality" (row 2). The examples are the draft's own: a proof that goes "more easily" on "repetition" (both words in quotation marks), and a new insight that "crosses out" the earlier errors. These are dependences of acts on earlier acts, "idiopsychic" dependences in the soul's own history.

The paragraph that follows shows where the draft starts. "The pure I gets a relation to experience already when it is apprehended as that of an experienced human being", as when I interpret into another a soul and with it a pure I. The realizing apprehension of one's own I comes second, by analogy with the realization of another's and with its objectification in "intersubjective exchange of experience". "Accordingly each will then also be able to apprehend himself" (*Demgemäß wird dann auch jeder sich selbst auffassen können*; Hua V 114). The self-apprehension is derived. The parenthesis that comes next, "of the known character that I have (I think at least that I know it)", turns the endless task back on the self with some irony.

The marginal note reverses this order of foundation, not only of exposition. "It would first have to be shown how the solipsistic I never apprehends itself merely as pure but as empirical I". What is "first in itself" (*an sich Erste*) is the experiential apperception "empirical I", and it "is the foundation of interpretation". Empathy presupposes my apprehension of myself as an empirical I. It does not produce it. The note on the preceding lines, "the whole presentation of what follows is insufficient", passes the same verdict on the draft. Husserl's later hand has kept the doctrine and rejected the route. The pure I is still apprehended as empirical. But the solipsistic I does so first, "never merely as pure", and the other's I is understood from there.

The worked-out *Ideen II* puts the empirical I where the marginal note asks. In § 27 the I as human being is "a component of the real surroundings of the pure I". In the constitution of the whole spatio-temporal world, "the empirical I in the form of the I-man" (*das empirische Ich in Form des Ich-Mensch*) functions "as phenomenal-real central member for the appearance-like constitution of the whole spatio-temporal world". "For all experience of spatio-temporal objectivity the co-appearance of the experiencing human being is presupposed", and also the apperceptive relation to fellow human beings (Hua IV § 27, 109–110; the converted file breaks off in mid-word at the foot of p. 109, so the second sentence is read only from the paragraph after the break, where the file's OCR "im pirische" stands for *empirische*). In this text the self-apperception as human being is a presupposition of experience, not an outcome of empathy. That is what the marginal note asks for. In *Ideen II* the pure I still apprehends itself as the empirical I. What has changed is where this self-apprehension stands in the order of constitution.

## Supplement I §4f, Hua V 122–123: the merely seeing subject

Reads [Supplement I §4f.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the second paragraph under § 4 f of Supplement I.

The body is constituted as a body by touch alone. Sight constitutes things, but it cannot constitute the body, because visual contents cannot be localized on the seen body: the eye does not feel itself seen. Husserl argues this by a thought experiment whose legitimacy he asserts in a parenthesis, "a merely eye-endowed subject (an idea that can be made evident)". Such a subject would have thing-appearances and would see real things, but it would have no appearing body. The experiment isolates the one property, localization, that makes a thing a body. It also exposes the doctrine's weakest joint. A subject without a body still sees "real things", and only a parenthesis says what is missing from them.

> If we now compare sensations of touch and sensations of sight with regard to their significance for the constitution of the body, we notice that they belong to quite different constitutive orders and play anything but the same role. This shows itself in the fact that a merely eye-endowed subject (an idea that can be made evident) could have no appearing body, but a merely tactually endowed subject could, understood only under conditions that are fulfilled in us human beings. The former would have its thing-appearances in the play of kinaesthetic motivations (which it could thus not apprehend bodily), it would see real things (admittedly only sense-things, which would be inaccessible, for lack of bodily experiences, to an objectification to be carried out intersubjectively). One will not say that the merely seeing one sees his body, for this seen body would lack the specific distinction from an arbitrary thing, and even the free movement of this "body", going hand in hand with the freedom of the kinaesthetic courses, would not make it a body and would bring about no localization. For it would then only be as if the I, in one with this freedom in the kinaesthetic, could move any other thing immediately and freely. What is missing is the possibility of localizing the visual contents on the visual body, the possibility of seeing the eye as the thing seen with the eye, as it were feeling it over, and correlatively the possibility of being able to see the seen thing as going over the resting eye, continuously "touching" it (touching visually!), in the familiar manner in which we can pass with a really touching organ, e.g. the palm of the hand, over the object and with the object over the palm of the hand.

German:

> Vergleichen wir nun Tastempfindungen und Gesichtsempfindungen hinsichtlich ihrer Bedeutung für die Leibeskonstitution, so bemerken wir, daß sie ganz anderen konstitutiven Ordnungen angehören und nichts weniger als dieselbe Rolle spielen. Es zeigt sich das darin, daß ein bloß augenhaftes Subjekt (eine evident zu machende Idee) keinen erscheinenden Leib haben könnte, wohl aber ein bloß taktuell begabtes Subjekt, nur unter Bedingungen verstanden, die bei uns Menschen erfüllt sind. Das erstere hätte im Spiel kinästhetischer Motivationen (die es also nicht leiblich auffassen könnte) seine Dingerscheinungen, es sähe reale Dinge (freilich nur Sinnendinge, die wegen des Mangels an Leibeserfahrungen einer intersubjektiv zu vollziehenden Objektivierung unzugänglich wären). Man wird nicht sagen, der nur Sehende sieht seinen Leib, denn es fehlte diesem gesehenen Leib die spezifische Auszeichnung gegenüber einem beliebigen Ding, und selbst die mit der Freiheit der kinästhetischen Abläufe Hand in Hand gehende freie Bewegung dieses „Leibes” machte ihn nicht zum Leib und brächte keine Lokalisation zustande. Denn es wäre dann nur so, wie wenn das Ich in eins mit dieser Freiheit im Kinästhetischen ein beliebiges anderes Ding unmittelbar frei bewegen könnte. Was fehlt, ist die Möglichkeit, die visuellen Inhalte auf dem visuellen Leib zu lokalisieren, die Möglichkeit, das Auge zu sehen als das mit dem Auge gesehene Ding sozusagen abtastend, und korrelativ die Möglichkeit, das gesehene Ding sehen zu können als über das ruhende Auge, es kontinuierlich „berührend“ (visuell berührend!) in der bekannten Weise hingehend, wie wir mit einem wirklich tastenden Organ, z.B. der Handfläche über den Gegenstand und mit dem Gegenstand über die Handfläche fahren können.

*Note on the text.* The passage is hard-wrapped (src L1650–L1673) and runs across the running head for p. 123 (L1664); line-end hyphens are joined and footnote markers dropped. The editor's footnotes say that "the following sentences are used almost verbatim in the second section of the second book" (Hua IV 150, lines 25–36), and that the sentences ending with the palm of the hand are "taken up almost verbatim" at Hua IV 148, lines 1–8. The comparison below rests on the corpus file of Hua IV. The apparatus has no note on p. 122 or 123.

The thought experiment is licensed in a parenthesis: "an idea that can be made evident" (*eine evident zu machende Idee*). The gerundive claims that the merely seeing subject is an eidetic possibility, not a fiction of convenience. It is what free variation allows when touch is subtracted from a subject. Its counterpart is hedged differently. A merely tactual subject could have an appearing body, but "understood only under conditions that are fulfilled in us human beings". The asymmetry is in the qualification. The seeing subject is thought in pure possibility. The touching subject is thought by subtraction from us, and so borrows our conditions: kinaesthesis, a surface that is felt and touched at once.

Two counterfactual subjunctives give the seeing subject what it has. It "would have its thing-appearances in the play of kinaesthetic motivations", and "it would see real things". The first is restricted by a parenthesis: the kinaesthetic courses are there, but it "could thus not apprehend [them] bodily". Kinaesthesis motivates appearances without being felt as the movement of a body. The second is restricted by another parenthesis, and here the inference is weakest. The things are "real", but "admittedly only sense-things" (*freilich nur Sinnendinge*), inaccessible "for lack of bodily experiences" to intersubjective objectification. "Admittedly" concedes almost everything that "real" claimed. The main text had made the thing real only "in the atmosphere of causal lawfulness" (§ 7; row 9). Here a subject without a body is credited with real things before the body has entered. The parenthesis keeps the result consistent with the paragraph that closes the section: nature, body and soul constitute themselves "in *mutual relatedness to one another*, in one with one another" (*in Wechselbezogenheit aufeinander, in eins miteinander*; Hua V 124). The merely seeing subject would have nature only at the level of sense-things, and that is the level the closing thesis does not reach.

The refusal "One will not say that the merely seeing one sees his body" turns on a word in quotation marks. The seen and freely moved thing is a "body" (*„Leibes“*) only in quotation marks, because what makes a thing a body is missing: "the specific distinction from an arbitrary thing". Free movement does not supply it. "For it would then only be as if the I … could move any other thing immediately and freely." Willed movement alone is telekinesis, not embodiment. The criterion is "localization" (*Lokalisation*). The body is the thing on which sensations are felt to lie.

The last sentence says what sight lacks by describing what touch has, and it does so in visual terms that fail. To localize visual contents, the eye would have to see itself as the seen thing, "as it were feeling it over" (*sozusagen abtastend*), and the seen thing would have to pass over the resting eye, "continuously 'touching' it (touching visually!)". The quotation marks and the exclamation mark carry the argument. "Touching visually" is a contradiction in terms, and the exclamation marks it as one. The comparison then names the structure of touch that sight lacks: "we can pass with a really touching organ, e.g. the palm of the hand, over the object and with the object over the palm of the hand". The double movement is the double function of § 3: the same touch-sensations are apprehended as the object's roughness and as felt on the hand (Hua V 11–12; row 4). The next lines give the formula: "So touching always offers something double" (Hua V 123).

The worked-out *Ideen II* takes the passage over and cuts it. In Hua IV § 37 the sentence reads: "A *merely eye-endowed* subject could have no appearing body at all; it would have its thing-appearances in the play of kinaesthetic motivations (which it could not apprehend bodily), it would see real things" (Hua IV 150). The parenthesis "(an idea that can be made evident)" is gone. So is the merely tactual subject, and so is the concession that the "real things" are "only sense-things" closed to intersubjective objectification. Where the draft had "any other thing", the later text has "the material thing body" (*das materielle Ding Leib*). It adds the thesis the draft only implied: "The body can constitute itself as such originally only in tactuality" (*Der Leib kann sich als solcher ursprünglich nur konstituieren in der Taktualität*; Hua IV 150). The revision removes exactly the hedges that showed the argument's dependence on its conditions. *Ideen II* states the conclusion more firmly, and it no longer says that the seeing subject's "real things" are real only as sense-things.

## Supplement I §6, Hua V 129–130: katastematic and kinetic

Reads [Supplement I §6.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the second paragraph under § 6 of Supplement I.

Ontology and constitutive phenomenology take the same unities in two opposite ways. Ontology takes a unity "as something fixed", for the sake of its identity. Phenomenology takes it "in flux", as the identical correlate of the courses of consciousness in which it is constituted, and follows those courses. Husserl names the two ways with a pair of Greek-formed adjectives, "katastematic" (standing, at rest) and "kinetic" (moving). The kinetic way is a "genesis", but one that belongs to a "transcendental" world, not to nature. That is why it can be called the "history" of a unity, in quotation marks: an immanent teleology in which the thing first shows what it is. Locke had the word and lost the sense, by taking this history for a psychological one.

> The ontological mode of consideration is, so to speak, katastematic. It takes the unities in their identity and for the sake of their identity as something fixed. The phenomenological-constitutive consideration takes the unity in flux, namely as unity of a constituting flux; it follows the movements, the courses, in which such a unity and every component, side, real property of such a unity is the correlate of identity. This consideration is, in a certain way, kinetic or "genesis", which belongs to a totally different, "transcendental" world than natural and natural-scientific genesis. Locke once speaks of a "history" of consciousness, and evidently his intention went to such a genesis, which he, leader of empiricism, at once confused with psychological genesis and thereby falsified through and through. Every unity of knowledge, in particular every real one, has its "history", or, correlatively speaking, the consciousness of this real has its "history", its immanent teleology in the form of a regulated system of modes of attestation and certification belonging to it *essentially*, which can be drawn out of it, asked of it. And what the matter itself is first comes forward in evidence on all sides of its essence in its history, which brings the unities and their moments to relief by setting the constituting manifolds in motion. In the method of phenomenological kinesis both are separated precisely in one: the essential directions of intentionality and their intentional correlates, and the essential determinations of the identical being that is conscious as identical in the intentional experiences and presents itself through the orders of its correlates as identical.

German:

> Die ontologische Betrachtungsweise ist sozusagen katastematisch. Sie nimmt die Einheiten in ihrer Identität und um ihrer Identität willen als wie ein Festes. Die phänomenologisch-konstitutive Betrachtung nimmt die Einheit im Fluß, nämlich als Einheit eines konstituierenden Flusses, sie verfolgt die Bewegungen, die Abläufe, in denen solche Einheit und jede Komponente, Seite, reale Eigenschaft solcher Einheit das Identitätskorrelat ist. Diese Betrachtung ist gewissermaßen kinetisch oder ,,Genesis“, die einer total verschiedenen, ,,transzendentalen“ Welt angehört als die natürliche und naturwissenschaftliche Genesis. Locke spricht einmal einer ,,Geschichte“ des Bewußtseins, und offenbar ging seine Intention auf solche Genesis, die er, Führer des Empirismus, alsbald mit der psychologischen Genesis verwechselte und damit durch und durch verfälschte. Jede Erkenntniseinheit, insbesondere jede reale, hat ihre ,,Geschichte“ oder auch, korrelativ gesprochen, das Bewußtsein von diesem Realen hat seine ,,Geschichte“, seine immanente Teleologie in Form eines geregelten Systems *wesenhaft* zugehöriger Bekundungs- und Beurkundungsweisen, die sich aus ihm herausholen, ihm abfragen lassen. Und was die Sache selbst ist, das tritt in Evidenz nach allen Seiten seines Wesens erst hervor in seiner Geschichte, die die Einheiten und ihre Momente zur Abhebung bringt, indem sie die konstituierenden Mannigfaltigkeiten in Bewegung setzt. In der Methode phänomenologischer Kinesis scheidet sich eben in eins beides: die wesentlichen Richtungen der Intentionalität und ihre intentionalen Korrelate und die wesentlichen Bestimmungen des identischen Seins, das in den intentionalen Erlebnissen als Identisches bewußt ist und sich durch die Ordnungen seiner Korrelate vorstellig macht als Identisches.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph is hard-wrapped (src L1814–L1838, Hua V 129) and its last clause runs past the running head for p. 130 (L1841–L1843); line-end hyphens are joined. "Locke spricht einmal einer ,,Geschichte“" has lost a word, presumably *von* (speaks of a "history"). The italic *wesenhaft* is the file's. The apparatus has no note on pp. 129–130.

The pair is set off by hedges. Ontology is "*so to speak*" (*sozusagen*) katastematic; phenomenology is "*in a certain way*" (*gewissermaßen*) kinetic. The Greek-formed words are introduced as figures, not as terms of art, and the hedges keep them from claiming more than a contrast of attitudes. *Katastematisch* is the rarer word. Its sense here, standing, settled, comes from the sentence that glosses it: ontology takes unities "as something fixed" (*als wie ein Festes*). "As" (*als wie*) says that the fixity belongs to the way of taking, not to the unity. Ontology does not deny that the unity is constituted in a flux. It takes it "for the sake of its identity" and leaves the flux aside.

The kinetic side is spelled out by its objects: "the movements, the courses" in which the unity "and every component, side, real property" of it "is the correlate of identity". The compound *Identitätskorrelat* turns identity itself into a correlate. In ontology identity is the presupposition. In phenomenology it is what is constituted. The list "component, side, real property" makes the point at every level. Even a side of the thing is the identity-correlate of a course of appearances.

Three words in quotation marks follow, and each borrows a word from the natural world for the transcendental one. "Genesis" (*„Genesis“*) is the first. The consideration "is … kinetic or 'genesis'", and the marks are needed because the genesis "belongs to a totally different, 'transcendental' world than natural and natural-scientific genesis". The second marked word, "transcendental", names that other world. The adjective "totally" (*total*) blocks any reading of the kinetic method as a psychology of development. The third word is "history" (*„Geschichte“*), taken from Locke. Locke's "intention went evidently to such a genesis". He then "at once confused" it with psychological genesis, "and thereby falsified [it] through and through" (*durch und durch verfälschte*). Husserl credits Locke with the intention and charges him with the confusion. That is his usual way with predecessors, as with Brentano's intentionality in § 10 (row 12). The epithet "leader of empiricism" explains the confusion. An empiricist could only read a history of consciousness as a natural history of the mind.

Husserl then gives "history" back its sense, in quotation marks, twice. "Every unity of knowledge … has its 'history', or, correlatively speaking, the consciousness of this real has its 'history'". The gloss is "its immanent teleology", "a regulated system of modes of attestation and certification belonging to it *essentially*". The italic *wesenhaft* separates this history from a factual one. It is the order of manifestations that the essence of the unity prescribes, and it "can be drawn out of it, asked of it" (*sich aus ihm herausholen, ihm abfragen lassen*). The history is the clue Husserl elsewhere calls "transcendental" (§ 7; row 8), interrogated from the side of consciousness. The teleology is in the system, not in a process in time. The thing has a history because its manifestations are ordered toward its full givenness.

The claim that follows is the strongest in the paragraph. "What the matter itself is first comes forward in evidence on all sides of its essence in its history". "First" (*erst*) makes the kinetic method a condition of the ontology's own evidence. The history "brings the unities and their moments to relief by setting the constituting manifolds in motion". The phrase "bring to relief" (*zur Abhebung bringen*) is the one § 19 used for clarification, which brings an essence to relief in the intuited (Hua V 100; row 19). So the kinetic method is clarification carried through the levels of constitution. The last sentence joins the two sides "in one" (*in eins*). Phenomenological "kinesis" separates the directions of intentionality and their correlates, and in the same act it separates the essential determinations of the identical being. Phenomenology does not replace ontology. It produces ontology's distinctions as a by-product of its own.

This is the result the supplement has worked toward. The paragraph before explains why ontologies of nature and spirit are so hard: these objectivities are constituted "not in one level … but in manifold levels", so that the objective attitude "overlooks much, pushes much aside as apparently irrelevant, that is entirely essential". "In itself, we shall still speak of it, ontology is not phenomenology" (Hua V 129). The next paragraph says why phenomenology must help. Mathematics succeeds without it, "but de facto … that does not succeed in the same way in the real ontologies. Here phenomenology first educates us to complete seeing" (Hua V 130). The pair katastematic/kinetic therefore restates § 15 of the main text (rows 15–16). There, phenomenology took the ontological concepts in quotation marks, as correlates. Here it takes the same unities as correlates of a flux. The first chapter's "phenomenological-kinetic method" (Hua V 1; row 1) is this second sense.

The word "history" comes back in Husserl's later work, and without quotation marks. In the *Cartesian Meditations*, "the ego constitutes itself for itself, so to speak, in the unity of a *history*" (*in der Einheit einer Geschichte*), under the "general laws of genesis" that make up "a unity of the universal genesis of the ego" (§ 37, Hua I 110). Of the 1912 hedges, only "so to speak" is left. The word that was Locke's and was given back under quotation marks has become the name of the ego's own self-constitution. The distinction from psychological genesis is still there, but it is no longer carried by typography. In the *Cartesian Meditations*, "genesis" no longer needs quotation marks to be heard as transcendental.

## Supplement II, Hua V 130: the I that governs our apprehensions

Reads [Supplement II.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the only paragraph of Supplement II.

The supplement claims that the I, once it comes into view, takes over the way everything else is apprehended. The I is not only the pure subject of our position-takings. It is also their favoured object, a person who preserves itself, has a character and is praised and blamed. From this personal I the whole natural picture of perception follows: the body "reacts" to things, the I gets perceptions and takes positions on them, and a soul, not told apart from the I, localizes its sensations on the body. Husserl wrote this passage as a continuation of § 3 and then rejected it: "the bracketed part probably won't do!" The reading has to find, in the passage itself, what he could not keep. The answer it gives is that the passage lets the I govern the very apprehension that § 3 had just separated from it.

> As soon as the I is touched upon, it governs our apprehensions. For it is not only alive as pure subject of the manifold experiencing, thinking, valuing, practical position-takings, but [is the] favoured object of our position-takings, and [is so] as the personal I that in its doing and suffering practises "self-preservation", that has personal properties, character-traits and manifold dispositions, with which it is exposed to praise and blame, and not least to its own; with which it must, as well or as badly as it can, protect itself against the dangers of the world of things and of human beings, or win them for itself, use them as tools, etc. So one can understand the apprehension that the body with its organs "reacts" to the external things, that as a result the I has certain perceptions of the things and, on the ground of these perceptions, takes a position toward them in this or that way. On the one side stand the things, among them the body, which is also only a thing, but a thing to which a soul-life (soul and I are not distinguished) is attached, first of all perceptions and sensations (which again remains undistinguished), which the soul, as it now once is in its causal relation to the body-thing, peculiarly localizes on the body in certain sense-domains, in others not; which needs a closer explanation.

German:

> Sowie an das Ich gerührt wird, beherrscht es unsere Auffassungen. Es ist ja nicht nur lebendig als reines Subjekt der mannigfachen erfahrenden, denkenden, wertenden, praktischen Stellungnahmen, sondern bevorzugtes Objekt unserer Stellungnahmen und als das personale Ich, das in seinem Tun und Leiden „Selbsterhaltung“ übt, das persönliche Eigenschaften, Charakteranlagen und vielfältige Dispositionen hat, mit denen es dem Lob und Tadel ausgesetzt ist und nicht zum mindesten dem eigenen; mit dem es sich recht und schlecht gegen die Gefahren der Dinge- und Menschenwelt schützen oder sie für sich gewinnen, als Werkzeug benutzen muß usw. So läßt sich die Auffassung verstehen, daß der Leib mit seinen Organen auf die äußeren Dinge „reagiert“, daß infolge davon das Ich von den Dingen gewisse Wahrnehmungen hat und auf Grund dieser Wahrnehmungen zu ihnen Stellung nimmt in dieser oder jener Weise. Auf der einen Seite stehen die Dinge, zwischen ihnen der Leib, der auch nur ein Ding ist, aber ein Ding, an das ein Seelenleben (Seele und Ich werden nicht geschieden) angeknüpft ist, zunächst Wahrnehmungen und Empfindungen (was wieder ungeschieden bleibt), die die Seele, wie sie nun einmal in ihrer kausalen Beziehung zum Leibesding ist, eigentümlicherweise in gewissen Sinnesgebieten dem Leib zu lokalisiert, in anderen nicht; was einer näheren Erklärung bedarf.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph is hard-wrapped (src L1859–L1883, Hua V 130); line-end hyphens are joined. Two places read awkwardly and are quoted as the file has them: "mit dem es sich … schützen … muß" has a singular relative where the plural "Dispositionen" would want *denen*, and "dem Leib zu lokalisiert" (localizes onto the body) is an unusual form. Neither changes the argument. The apparatus reports Husserl's verdict twice, and not quite in the same words: at 11,5 the bracketed text followed "… unser Leib." in § 3, carries a *deleatur* (delete) sign in Landgrebe's typescript, and Husserl noted in the manuscript "Das Eingeklammerte geht wohl nicht!" (the bracketed part probably won't do!); the note "Zu Beilage II" gives the same remark with a full stop and says the text is bracketed in the manuscript and in the transcriptions (src L2397, L2411). The file cannot settle which punctuation is the manuscript's. Husserl gave no reason.

The first sentence has a verb that § 3 also uses, and the two uses cannot both stand. In the supplement the I "governs" (*beherrscht*) our apprehensions. In the main text, at the place where the bracket was removed, the next paragraph says of the somatological layer of apprehension: "whether it is recognized rightly in reflection or not, it governs (*beherrscht*) theoretical experience" (Hua V 11; src L317–L320). The kept text gives the governing role to a layer of apprehension that does its work unnoticed. The struck text gives it to the I, the one thing that is always noticed. § 3 had just shown why a somatology was never conceived: it needs "the pure separation of sensation from the web of apprehension in which it is woven", and a turning of the gaze away from what "determines our natural directions of regard" (src L311). The supplement names what determines those directions, and so it explains the confusion. But it explains it by making the I its master, which leaves no room for the separation § 3 demands.

The second sentence shows how far the I's rule reaches. The I is the subject of position-takings and also their "favoured object", and as object it is "the personal I": it practises "self-preservation" in quotation marks, has character and dispositions, is open to praise and blame "and not least to its own", and must guard against the world of things and of other people or use them "as tools". The quotation marks around "self-preservation" (*„Selbsterhaltung“*) hold a biological word at arm's length while letting it describe the person. The list moves from ethics (praise and blame) to practice (danger, tools). Nothing in it belongs to the somatology § 3 is delimiting. The I enters as a person in a world of goods and threats, which is the subject of the human sciences, and the main text has just declared that "for us there are, just now, no spirits" (§ 4, Hua V 21; src L475).

The third sentence is where the passage becomes ambiguous, and the ambiguity bears on its rejection. "So one can understand the apprehension that (*So läßt sich die Auffassung verstehen, daß*) the body … 'reacts'". Grammatically, the body's reacting and the I's perceiving are the content of an apprehension that the I's dominance makes understandable. The quotation marks round "reacts" (*„reagiert“*) say the same: the word is the natural attitude's, not Husserl's. The commentary drops that frame and reports the causal chain as if Husserl asserted it. The last sentence leaves the frame unclear. "On the one side stand the things" is indicative, outside the *daß*-clause, and no "on the other side" ever follows. Inside it two parentheses mark what the picture fails to distinguish: "soul and I are not distinguished", and perceptions and sensations, "which again remains undistinguished". These parentheses read as Husserl's own diagnosis of the natural picture. Then the soul "localizes" its sensations on the body "in certain sense-domains, in others not", and the sentence ends on "which needs a closer explanation". The paragraph stops at the point where § 3 has already given the explanation: "the body senses (*empfindet*), that concerns the localized" (src L311). In § 3 localization belongs to the body as aesthesiological layer. Here the soul does it, by virtue of "its causal relation to the body-thing". Whether that is the natural picture reported or Husserl's own account slipping back into it, the text does not decide, and that indecision may be what would not do.

The rest of the book separates what the supplement fuses. At the end of § 3 Husserl concedes that the account "has not specially taken into account the pure and psychic I", and he places it at once: "the investigation of the psychic apperception-I is only one stage of the investigation of the soul in general … Not everything psychic is specifically egoic. Associations form whether the I takes part or not" (Hua V 19; src L461). The I no longer governs anything. It is one stratum of the soul. Supplement I, the draft of *Ideas II* that precedes the book, gives the distinction that the supplement's first parenthesis lacks: "we distinguish the *real soul* from the *real soul-subject*", so that a soul can lack an I and be "a *subjectless soul*" (Hua V 116; src L1560–L1569). It then states the fusion as a fact about ordinary thought: the soul-subject, "though abstractively distinguishable from the soul as its foundation, fuses with it into one in the ordinary apprehension of spirit" (Hua V 117; src L1579). Supplement II takes that ordinary apprehension and lets it govern. Husserl's verdict on it is consistent with § 3 and with Supplement I: the I must be distinguished from the soul, and the localizing body from both, before any of them can be given its own science.

## Supplement IV, Hua V 132 and 135–136: the type is no geometrical species

Reads [Supplement IV.2 and IV.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-5-ideas-iii-commentary.md), the second and fourth paragraphs of Supplement IV.

A type, for Husserl here, is an intuitive unity such as the egg-shaped or the jagged: a shape we see and can recognize again, but which no exact concept fixes. The supplement uses the type to limit the comparison that runs through the main text, between phenomenology and geometry. Both are essential doctrines. But geometry's species are not types, and geometry neither orders types nor gives laws for them. The field of phenomenology, the manifold of conscious experiences, "is no mathematical manifold": exact ideas can be thought into it, but nothing given there can be approached by exact concepts in a fixed method. Phenomenology does not therefore sink into flux. It reaches firm concepts at a higher generality, concepts that are firm without being exact.

> Phenomenology also cannot master the typical particularities down to the undifferentiable concretions in the way that geometry, say, masters all possible pure spatial shapes through its axiomatics and its fundamental concepts. Here an essential difference of phenomenological essential findings from geometrical ones comes forward. It becomes clear when we attend to the fact that the geometrical species-formations are not type-differentiations. The type is an intuitive unity, e.g. the type of the egg-shaped, of the jagged, of the serpentine line, etc. But although figure in general *can* be understood as a general type that comprehends all such types in itself, geometry still gives no systematics of these sensory types, turned toward the ideal, and no law for such types; it has instead its own kind of concept-formation and its own genera of ideas: geometrical purity excludes the typical of sensuously intuitive givens. Types, such as the type of the egg-form, are no geometrical formations. If we bring in geometry, as an essential doctrine, to illustrate the scientific character of phenomenology, that has its right in so far as phenomenology too is an essential doctrine; but we may not put phenomenological and geometrical formation of concepts and judgments on one level.

> If we reduce phenomenologically to pure consciousness, then of course exactly the same holds for the manifold of the cogitationes. It is no mathematical manifold. We can indeed think exact ideas into the flow of a perceptual appearance, for example, as we do when we speak of the temporal phases of perceptual appearances, when we think of the phenomenon as a duration with an ideally determined content, new from time-point to time-point; likewise when we say that a phenomenon fades from clarity into obscurity and think into it phases of this continuity of fading. But here there is in principle no possibility of determining what is given in each case by exact concepts, approximately, in a fixed method; and above all the gradual gradations are gradations of a rich qualitative content, and of a qualitative content of manifold genera and species, which one could not grasp mathematically in any way by relation to continua and exact concepts, or insert into a mathematical order.

German:

> Die Phänomenologie kann die typischen Besonderheiten bis herab zu den undifferenzierbaren Konkretionen auch nicht so beherrschen, wie etwa die Geometrie durch ihre Axiomatik und ihre Grundbegriffe alle möglichen reinen Raumgestalten beherrscht. Es tritt hier ein wesentlicher Unterschied der phänomenologischen Wesensfeststellungen gegenüber den geometrischen hervor. Er wird deutlich, wenn wir darauf achten, daß die geometrischen Artbildungen nicht Typendifferenzierungen sind. Der Typus ist eine anschauliche Einheit, z.B. der Typus des Eiförmigen, des Gezackten, der Schlangenlinie usw. Aber obschon Figur überhaupt als allgemeiner Typus verstanden werden *kann*, der alle solche Typen in sich faßt, so gibt doch die Geometrie nicht eine Systematik dieser sinnlichen und ins Ideale gewendeten Typen und kein Gesetz für solche Typen, sondern sie hat eine eigene Art der Begriffsbildung und eigene Gattungen von Ideen: die geometrische Reinheit schließt das Typische der sinnlich anschaulichen Gegebenheiten aus. Die Typen, wie z.B. der Typus der Eiform, sind keine geometrischen Gebilde. Wenn wir die Geometrie als eine Wesenslehre heranziehen zur Illustration der wissenschaftlichen Art der Phänomenologie, so hat das also zwar sein Recht, sofern auch die Phänomenologie Wesenslehre ist; aber wir dürfen nicht phänomenologische und geometrische Begriffs- und Urteilsbildung auf eine Stufe stellen.

> Reduzieren wir phänomenologisch auf das reine Bewußtsein, so gilt natürlich eben dasselbe für die Mannigfaltigkeit der cogitationes. Sie ist keine mathematische Mannigfaltigkeit. Zwar können wir z.B. im Abfluß einer Wahrnehmungserscheinung exakte Ideen hineindenken, wie wir es ja tun, wenn wir von den Zeitphasen der Wahrnehmungserscheinungen sprechen, wenn wir uns das Phänomen als eine Dauer mit einem ideal bestimmten, von Zeitpunkt zu Zeitpunkt neuen Inhalt denken; ebenso wenn wir davon sprechen, daß ein Phänomen von der Klarheit in die Dunkelheit abklinge und uns da Phasen dieser Kontinuität des Abklingens hineindenken. Aber es fehlt hier prinzipiell an Möglichkeiten, das jeweils Gegebene durch exakte Begriffe in fester Methode angenähert zu bestimmen, und vor allem die graduellen Abstufungen sind Abstufungen eines reichen qualitativen Gehaltes und eines qualitativen Gehaltes von vielfältigen Gattungen und Arten, die man nicht durch Beziehung auf Kontinua und exakte Begriffe irgendwie mathematisch fassen, einer mathematischen Ordnung eingliedern könnte.

A footnote marker after "auf eine Stufe stellen" (L1928) cannot be matched with certainty, since the file numbers several footnotes "11"; one of them reads "Vgl. S. 135 ff." (cf. p. 135 ff.), which would point forward to the second passage (L2086). Between the two passages the file has lost a page. The running head for p. 133 (L1952) is followed by that for p. 135 (L1962), and p. 135 opens mid-sentence with "sind, deren Interesse eben Wesensbetrachtung der Arten und Formen sinnlicher Gestaltungen erfordert" (are, whose interest requires precisely essential consideration of the kinds and forms of sensory configurations) (L1964). Page 134 is missing. The commentary passes over pp. 133–135 altogether. The apparatus dates the supplement: it rests on five shorthand sheets found with manuscripts "probably from the war years", preceded by three sheets "that deal with Brentano" which were not printed (src L2417). It is therefore later than the 1912 text it supplements.

The first passage turns on two compounds. Geometry has "species-formations" (*Artbildungen*); phenomenology has "type-differentiations" (*Typendifferenzierungen*). Both divide a genus, and the passage denies that they divide it the same way. A geometrical species is fixed by construction from axioms and fundamental concepts, so that geometry "masters" (*beherrscht*) every possible pure spatial shape. A type is fixed by seeing: "an intuitive unity". The examples are shapes we name from sight, the egg-shaped, the jagged, the serpentine line, and none of them has a definition. The concession that follows is marked by the file's only emphasis in the passage: figure in general *can* be understood as a general type. The italic *kann* grants that the types and geometry share a summit, and then denies that geometry gives "a systematics" or "a law" for anything beneath it. Even types "turned toward the ideal" (*ins Ideale gewendet*) stay outside geometry. Idealizing a type does not make it a geometrical formation, because "geometrical purity excludes the typical". Purity here is not a higher degree of clarity. It is a different kind of concept, made by excluding exactly what a type keeps.

The second passage carries the limit from shapes to experiences, and the verb "think into" (*hineindenken*), used twice, measures it. Exact ideas are thought into the flow of a perception when we speak of its temporal phases, and into a fading phenomenon when we divide its fading into phases. The verb concedes that we do this, "as we do", and denies that the ideas are found there. What is missing is "in principle" (*prinzipiell*) a fixed method of approximation, which is how physics reaches its exact nature through appearances. The final clause gives the reason. The gradations of experience are gradations "of a rich qualitative content … of manifold genera and species", so a continuum of degree cannot be laid under them. The sentence just before had said the same of mind in general: mental acts "are not measurable, not 'exactly' determinable as approximations to ideal mathematical formations" (src L1986–L1988). The quotation marks round "exactly" (*„exakt“*) keep the word to its mathematical sense.

The supplement is written "to §§ 7–9", and its target is a sentence of § 8. There Husserl had warned against overdoing the analogy with geometry: "rational psychology is no mathematics, and in particular the phenomenology of experiences is no mathematics of experiences", because "not every essential doctrine has the type of a mathematics"; the stream of experience offers "nothing like a space", "it is no field of a mathematics" (Hua V 44–45; src L879, before the seam at p. 45). The word "type" in § 8 meant the theoretical form of a science. In the supplement it means the intuitive unity that geometry excludes. The shift is the supplement's contribution. § 8 said negatively that phenomenology's theoretical type is not mathematical. The supplement says positively what phenomenology's concepts are made from: types and classes taken from the givens themselves. A few pages later it states the rule: "where we find the flowing, we must form the idea of the flow; where we find the vague, the idea of vagueness … we must form type-concepts. We do not therefore sink into a flow" (Hua V 136; src L2040–L2044). At a higher generality it finds "firm, though not mathematical, differences": perception and statement, willing and enjoying, are class-differences "as firm and absolutely separated as the imaginative differences of colour and tone" (src L2046–L2054). That last comparison sits oddly beside § 11, where a footnote warns that the great problems of phenomenology do not lie in "the a priori of colour- and tone-orders noticed by Lotze" (Hua V 59; src L982). The supplement uses colour and tone only for how sharp the class lines are, not for what lies inside the classes.

The doctrine is the one *Ideas I* had stated for the descriptive natural sciences. In the English *Ideas I* (Kersten), § 74 sets the geometer, who is not interested in "de facto sensuously intuitable shapes", against the descriptive scientist, whose "morphological concepts of vague configurational types" ("notches", "scalloped", "lens-shaped", "umbelliform") are "essentially, rather than accidentally, inexact and consequently also non-mathematical". The same section separates "the firmness and the pure distinguishability" of generic concepts that have their extension "in the realm of fluidity" from "the exactness of ideal concepts" (Hua III/1 138–139; CW2 :1927, :1931; the file's OCR is damaged, and these phrases were checked for legibility). *Ideas I* § 75 then calls phenomenology a "descriptive eidetic doctrine". Supplement IV applies § 74 to that claim. The egg-shaped and the jagged belong with the botanist's notched and lens-shaped, and the same distinction between firmness and exactness now lets phenomenology be rigorous without being a geometry of experience.

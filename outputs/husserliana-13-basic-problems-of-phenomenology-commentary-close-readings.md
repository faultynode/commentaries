# Husserl, *Grundprobleme der Phänomenologie* (1910/11): close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md) works through the lecture course at equal depth, section by section. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding goes in the rest of the course and in Husserl's other works.

The text is Text Nr. 6 of Husserliana XIII (*Zur Phänomenologie der Intersubjektivität*, first part, 1905–1920, edited by Iso Kern), "From the lectures *Basic Problems of Phenomenology*, winter semester 1910/11". It is quoted from the converted German file in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-13-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-ersterteil-1905-1920.md`). Passages are cited by § and by Hua XIII page. The main commentary cites § numbers only, and unit references such as "§10.7" name the seventh paragraph of the commentary under § 10. All English renderings of Husserl and of the editor's notes are my working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows.

The text has two layers. The body is the manuscript of 1910/11. The footnotes record what Husserl changed later, mostly "probably in 1921" and "in 1924 or later", when he reread the manuscript and Ludwig Landgrebe's transcription of it. Several readings turn on the difference between the layers, and each says which layer it quotes.

The file has limits that bear on more than one reading. Its page numbers come from page markers that sometimes trail mid-line or are missing, so a page can be one out. It has lost Husserl's emphasis: the italics it keeps are almost all Latin, and no reading argues from emphasis or its absence. Footnote numbers restart across the volume, so every note cited here is a note of Nr. 6. Four places are damaged and are not repaired: a later-struck paragraph of § 10 runs into footnote text, so that its opening cannot be quoted; note 70, on the 1924 revision of the end of § 22, is split across the heading of the Fourth Chapter; note 71, on the first appearance of an apodictic critique, breaks off mid-sentence, and its continuation stands inside the body of § 24; and the opening sentence of § 23 has a garbled word. The readings concerned say what the file has at that point.

## §1, Hua XIII 111: an experiencing phenomenology

Reads [§1.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-i-in-the-natural-attitude), third paragraph under § 1.

The course sets out to test whether phenomenology can be an experiencing science: one that posits individual being in the field the reduction opens, as natural science posits individual being in nature, and that is not, or not first, a doctrine of essence. The synopsis does not assert that such a phenomenology exists. It frames it as a question to be weighed. Everything later in the course that speaks of "phenomenological experience" works inside that question, and the last chapter answers it with a doubt.

> Starting from the natural concept of the world. The natural concept of the world as the starting point of a theory of knowledge. Possibility of a phenomenology. "Phenomenology" is here not taken from the outset as a phenomenological doctrine of essence; rather, the attempt is made to weigh whether an experiencing phenomenology, one that is not a doctrine of essence, is possible.
> The evidence of the *ego cogito* with regard to the evidence of the unity of the stream of consciousness, hence givenness of the phenomenological field. The transcendence in the immanence and the various concepts of transcendence. The right of transcendent positing in the immanence. The right of recollection and of expectation. Especially important is the right of the intentional relations, of the expectation-intentions, which yields the turning of an empirical transcendent positing into systematic connections of consciousness. Objectivity as index for transcendental subjectivity, and the right of "empirical" cognition in the sphere of consciousness. The phenomenological (not eidetic) reduction thus yields the possibility of going beyond the actually impressional within the subjective (transcendental-subjective). The same applied to empathy. First elaborations of it. Transcendental reduction (switching-off of physical nature) yields, besides the *ego*, also the other *ego* and its stream. Doctrine of monads. Connection of the monads. Recollection gives the self! Empathizing presentification, present-memory does not give the self.
> Especially important: the essence of the unified phenomenological I: how my stream of consciousness closes itself off phenomenologically against every other. Principle of unity.

German:

> <Inhaltsangabe:> Ausgang vom naturlichen Weltbegriff. Der natürliche Weltbegriff als Ausgangspunkt einer Erkenntnistheorie. Möglichkeit einer Phänomenologie. „Phänomenologie“ wird hier nicht von vornherein als phänomenologische Wesenslehre bezogen, sondern der Versuch gemacht, zu erwägen, ob eine erfahrende Phänomenologie, die nicht Wesenslehre ist, möglich ist.
> Die Evidenz des *ego cogito* in Hinsicht auf die Evidenz der Einheit des Bewusstseinsstromes, also Gegebenheit des phänomenologischen Feldes. Die Transzendenz in der Immanenz und die verschiedenen Begriffe von Transzendenz. Das Recht der transzendenten Setzung in der Immanenz. Das Recht der Wiedererinnerung und der Erwartung. Besonders wichtig ist das Recht der intentionalen Beziehungen, der Erwartungsintentionen, die die Umwendung einer empirischen transzendenten Setzung in systematische Bewusstseinszusammenhänge ergibt. Objektivität als Index für transzendentale Subjektivität und das Recht „empirischer“ Erkenntnis in der Bewusstseinssphäre. Die phänomenologische (nicht eidetische) Reduktion ergibt so die Möglichkeit, im Subjektiven (Transzendental-subjektiven) über das aktuell Impressionale hinauszugehen. Dasselbe angewendet auf Einfühlung. Erste Ausführungen darüber. Transzendentale Reduktion (Ausschaltung der physischen Natur) ergibt ausser dem *ego* auch das andere *ego* und seinen Strom. Monadenlehre. Verbindung der Monaden. Wiedererinnerung gibt das Selbst! Einfühlende Vergegenwärtigung, Gegenwartsereinnerung gibt nicht das Selbst.
> Besonders wichtig: das Wesen des einheitlichen phänomenologischen Ich: wie sich mein Bewusstseinsstrom gegenüber jedem anderen phänomenologisch abschliesst. Prinzip der Einheit.

*Note on the text.* The file has "naturlichen" without its umlaut in the first sentence and "Gegenwartsereinnerung" for *Gegenwartserinnerung* (present-memory, the memory of a present); both are quoted as the file has them and neither affects the reading. The angle brackets around "Inhaltsangabe" (summary of contents) mark an editorial supply, as they do elsewhere in Nr. 6.

The synopsis is not part of the lecture. It stands in Husserl's own note on the title, directly after the remark that only the sheets of the first weeks survive and that "later I lectured freely, without lecture notes". It is therefore written after the course, looking back on it, and its vocabulary shows it. "Eidetic" (*eidetisch*) occurs nowhere in the 1910/11 body of Nr. 6. It appears only here and in later additions: note 61 (undated, but in vocabulary the 1910/11 body never uses), note 70 (1924 or later) and the critical supplement to § 17. Where the body of the course speaks of the matter, it says "apriori" (*apriorisch*) and, in § 41, "pure essence-cognition" (*reine Wesenserkenntnis*). The synopsis translates the course into the terms of *Ideas* I, and in those terms it marks what the course did not do: its reduction was "not eidetic".

The decisive word is a participle. An "experiencing phenomenology" (*erfahrende Phänomenologie*) is not a phenomenology *of* experience, which any phenomenology would be. The phenomenology itself does the experiencing. Husserl uses the same participle for natural consciousness: the "experiencing consciousness" (*erfahrendes Bewusstsein*) of the critical supplement to § 17, probably of 1921 and the "experiencing eye" (*erfahrendes Auge*) before which nature stands in § 35. The phrase transfers to phenomenology the posture of natural experience, which posits what it sees as individual and existing. The relative clause then defines it by negation, "that is not a doctrine of essence", and the parenthesis repeats the negation at the reduction: "phenomenological (not eidetic)". The positive content of "experiencing" is left to the course.

The syntax keeps the matter open. "Not taken from the outset" (*nicht von vornherein*) restricts the denial to the start: the course does not presuppose that phenomenology is a doctrine of essence, and it leaves room for the possibility that it will end as one. The attempt is "to weigh whether" (*zu erwägen, ob*) such a phenomenology "is possible". It is an experiment, not a programme. The connective in the middle of the synopsis claims more. The reduction "thus" (*so*) yields the possibility of going beyond the actually impressional. "Thus" rests on the preceding clauses about the right of recollection, expectation and intentional relations. Those clauses name problems, and none of them is yet a result. The note's one exclamation mark, "Recollection gives the self!", marks where the later Husserl saw the decisive asymmetry: memory gives my own past itself, empathy never gives the other's present itself.

The lecture's first paragraph follows the note and sharpens the problem. "Phenomenology is by no means psychology, it lies in a new dimension", and it requires an attitude "essentially different" from that of "every science of spatio-temporal existence". If phenomenology were a science of essences, the distance from psychology would be easy to state, as the distance of geometry from physics. An experiencing phenomenology cannot use that distance. It posits individual being as psychology does, and so it must find its difference from psychology wholly in the attitude, in the reduction, and in nothing else. The course spends its first two chapters drawing that line, and §§ 15 and 29 return to it twice.

The understanding is carried through the course in three stages. Husserl's note to § 21 (note 61), added later, states it as the course's premise: "'Phenomenology' thought as empirical science on the ground of the phenomenological reduction, thus not as eidetic phenomenology, and without the question of 'apodictic' justification." § 32 (Hua XIII 174) states it in the body of the course: what remains after the reduction is "a world of individual being". § 41 (Hua XIII 193) takes it back. Husserl asks whether what the course has found "does not *au fond* concern pure essence-cognition", and whether "something like an experiential phenomenology" (*erfahrungsmässige Phänomenologie*) is "still entirely questionable, indeed perhaps impossible". The question of the synopsis returns in the last chapter, unchanged and now in doubt. When Husserl reread the manuscript in 1924 or later, he narrowed that doubt by inserting "as rational-empirical science" (note 122) after "perhaps". The later Husserl, it seems, no longer doubted that phenomenology could experience. He doubted only that it could be an empirical science of a rational kind.

*Ideas* I gives the answer the course withheld. Its introduction (Hua III/1 6, first edition 4) announces that "pure or transcendental phenomenology will be grounded not as a science of matters of fact, but as a science of essences (as an 'eidetic' science)" (*nicht als Tatsachenwissenschaft, sondern als Wesenswissenschaft (als „eidetische“ Wissenschaft)*; Kersten's translation, CW II, agrees). The synopsis's "not eidetic" names the alternative that *Ideas* I excludes. A disposition of the 1910/11 course that Husserl wrote "in one of the following years", printed as Beilage XXI of Hua XIII, confirms the direction: the right introduction, he says there, would first "lay free the way to the eidetic" and then distinguish rational psychology "as eidetic of the mind" from phenomenology, which "corresponds on the whole to the way of 1912 (summer)". The 1910/11 course is the one place where Husserl tried the other route to the end, and the synopsis, written from the far side of *Ideas* I, names that route by what it was not.

## §1, Hua XIII 113: the haver and the had

Reads [§1.7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-i-in-the-natural-attitude), seventh paragraph under § 1.

The I of the natural attitude finds itself as the one who has: who suffers a pain, performs a judgment, possesses fidelity as a trait. It does not find itself as anything it has. Husserl draws the difference between the I and its contents inside the natural description, before any reduction, and he draws it in grammar: the I is named by participles that make it the agent of what its contents are the products or objects of. The course then leaves open, at § 19, whether anything of this having I survives the reduction, and in § 37 it builds the unity of the phenomenological I without it.

> The having is here a different one according to what is had: a pain is suffered, a judgment is performed, fitness for life, fidelity, truthfulness are had as "personal" properties, and so on. If now the I finds itself as the one having, in different ways, all that is assertible, it finds itself on the other hand not as something of the same kind as this had. The I itself is no lived-experience but the one living it through, not an act but that which performs the act, not a character-trait but that which has the character-trait as a property, and so on. Further, the I finds itself and finds its I-experiences and dispositions in time, and knows itself here not only as now being and having this and that; it also has memories and finds itself, in the manner of memory, as the same who "just now" and in earlier time had these and those determinate lived-experiences, and so on. Everything had and had-had has its time-place, and the I itself is an identical one in time and has in time a determinate position.

German:

> Das Haben ist dabei je nach dem Gehabten ein verschiedenes, ein Schmerz wird erlitten, ein Urteil wird vollzogen, Lebenstüchtigkeit, Treue, Wahrhaftigkeit werden als „persönliche“ Eigenschaften gehabt usw. Findet sich nun das Ich vor als den all jenes Aussagbare in verschiedener Weise Habenden, so findet es sich andererseits nicht als etwas diesem Gehabten Gleichartiges. Das Ich selbst ist kein Erlebnis, sondern der Erlebende, nicht ein Akt, sondern das den Akt Vollziehende, nicht ein Charakterzug, sondern das den Charakterzug eigenschaftlich Habende usw. Ferner, das Ich findet sich und findet seine Icherlebnisse und Dispositionen in der Zeit vor und weiss sich dabei nicht nur jetzt als Seienden und dies und das Habenden; es hat auch Erinnerungen und findet sich erinnerungsmässig als denselben vor, der „soeben“ und in früherer Zeit die und jene bestimmten Erlebnisse usw. gehabt hat. Jedes Gehabte und Gehabt-gehabte hat seine Zeitstelle, und das Ich selbst ist ein identisches in der Zeit und hat in der Zeit eine bestimmte Stellung.

The passage turns one verb into a category. "Having" (*Haben*) begins as the ordinary verb of possession and is at once split by its objects: a pain is "suffered" (*erlitten*), a judgment "performed" (*vollzogen*), a trait "had" in the narrow sense. Because the having varies with the had, no single kind of had thing can fix what the haver is. The next sentence draws that conclusion. The I is "the one having" (*den … Habenden*) all of it "in different ways", and so it is "not something of the same kind as this had" (*nicht als etwas diesem Gehabten Gleichartiges*). The original earns its place here: *Gleichartiges* says "of the same kind", a claim about kinds, and the English "like" or "similar" would weaken it to a resemblance.

The three pairs that follow carry the claim entirely by word-formation. Each sets a noun for a content against a substantivized participle for its agent: lived-experience (*Erlebnis*) against "the one living it through" (*der Erlebende*), act against "that which performs the act" (*das den Akt Vollziehende*), character-trait against "that which has the character-trait as a property" (*das den Charakterzug eigenschaftlich Habende*). The grammatical gender shifts inside the series. The first agent is masculine, *der Erlebende*, and so is "the one having" in the sentence before; they name the person, the man with a proper name of the paragraph's opening. The second and third are neuter, agreeing with "the I" (*das Ich*). The sentence slides from the person who lives through his experiences to a neutral bearer of acts and traits. That slide is the whole distance between the natural self-understanding and the "pure I" the course will not yet decide on.

The inference is weaker than the wording makes it look. The pairs show that we speak of the bearer of an act differently from the act. They do not show that the bearer is anything over and above the connection of what it bears. Husserl does not claim more here. The verb throughout is "finds itself" (*findet sich vor*), and the clause "on the other hand" (*andererseits*) records a second finding beside the first, not a refutation of it. The difference belongs to what the natural I finds, and the description asserts it with the same confidence it gives to the finding of a surrounding world. The main commentary's §1.6 says that the natural attitude shows the I "as a thing among the things it possesses". The source says the opposite in the same paragraph, and the next section reserves the word "thing" for the body: "the lived-body is for its part no I but a spatio-temporal 'thing'" (§ 2, Hua XIII 113). The correction is logged.

Time then gives the having I its identity. The participle doubles into "had-had" (*Gehabt-gehabte*), what was had before, and every had thing, present or past, "has its time-place". The I "is an identical one in time". The identity is not argued. It is found through memory, which presents the I as "the same" who "just now" had these experiences. The quotation marks around "just now" (*soeben*) set off the word by which retention speaks in the first person, the word on which § 25 will make the reduction depend.

The passage sets up the next step of the description and the evidence claim of § 7. § 2 moves from the I to the lived-body and its surroundings, which the I also "has", in another sense again. § 7 (Hua XIII 121) claims absolute evidence that "I find myself as the one having this and that" (*als den das und jenes Habenden*), the same masculine participle, so that the having I becomes the first item of the evidence the course will test. When the course reaches the reduction, the having I is the problem. The objection of § 19 (Hua XIII 154–55) is that the reduction "wants to switch off one's own I", and Husserl answers that the empirical I is bracketed with nature, while whether "a pure I" remains as "something inseparable from the *cogitationes*" is a question on which "we have now no decision to make". In 1924 or later he inserted beside the conditional "if it finds something like a pure I" the words "which is indeed the case" (note 53). § 37 (Hua XIII 184) then returns to the verb: "the empirical I has a lived-body and has, on the other hand, evidently in a quite different sense, consciousness". The principle that § 37 finds for the unity of one phenomenological I is the unity of a stream in one time, not a haver. The "identical one in time" of § 1 is carried into the reduced sphere as the identity of the time in which the stream's contents stand, and the question of an I that has them is left where § 19 left it.

*Ideas* I (1913) decides that question in the terms of this passage. § 57 asks whether the pure Ego is excluded and answers that after the reduction we will meet it nowhere "as a lived-experience among other lived-experiences" (*als ein Erlebnis unter anderen Erlebnissen*), and that "the Ego is an identical one" (*Das Ich aber ist ein Identisches*, Hua III/1 123, first edition 109; Kersten's translation, CW II, agrees). The two findings of § 1, that the I is no lived-experience and that it is identical in time, become in 1913 the marks of the pure Ego that the reduction cannot switch off. In 1910/11 they are still findings of the natural attitude, and the course declines to say what of them the reduction keeps.

## §4, Hua XIII 115–16: empathy as positing without having

Reads [§4.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#empathy-and-the-foreign-i), first paragraph under § 4.

Empathy (*Einfühlung*) enters the course as a way of positing, not as a way of having. The I that § 1 described as the one who has its experiences finds, among the things around it, bodies to which another I belongs. It posits that I and its experiences, and in that sense they are "found". But they are not given to it as its own experiences are given to it: it does not live them, and it does not see the other as it sees itself. The course needs this asymmetry from the start, because the reduction will later have to decide whether what empathy posits survives it, and the answer of § 39 is built on the same asymmetry.

> Every I finds in its environment, and often also in its actual environment, things that it regards as lived-bodies, but sharply sets over against its "own" lived-body as foreign lived-bodies, such that to each such lived-body there belongs again an I, but another, foreign I (it regards the lived-bodies as "bearers" of I-subjects, but it does not "see" the foreign I's in the sense in which it sees itself, finds itself experiencingly. It posits them in the manner of "empathy"; thus foreign lived-experiencing, foreign character-dispositions too are "found"; but they are not given, had, in the sense in which one's own are): an I that likewise has its "soul", its actual consciousness, its dispositions, character-dispositions; that likewise finds its thingly environment, and among it finds its lived-body as its own, and so on; and in this, the environment that the foreign I, standing over against us *quasi* perceptually, finds would be by and large the same as our environment, and the lived-body that we apprehend in our environment as its lived-body would be the same one that it apprehends in its environment as its own lived-body.

German:

> Jedes Ich findet in seiner Umgebung und öfter auch in seiner aktuellen Umgebung Dinge, die es als Leiber ansieht, aber scharf dem „eigenen“ Leib gegenüberstellt als fremde Leiber derart, dass zu jedem solchen Leib wieder ein Ich, aber ein anderes, fremdes Ich gehört (es sieht die Leiber an als „Träger“ von Ichsubjekten, es „sieht“ aber nicht die fremden Ich in dem Sinn, wie es sich selbst sieht, erfahrend vorfindet. Es setzt sie in der Weise der „Einfühlung“, also auch fremdes Erleben, fremde Charakteranlagen werden „vorgefunden“; sie sind aber nicht in dem Sinn gegebene, gehabte wie eigene): ein Ich, das ebenfalls seine „Seele“, sein aktuelles Bewusstsein, seine Dispositionen, Charakteranlagen hat; das ebenfalls seine dingliche Umgebung vorfindet, darunter auch seinen Leib als den seinen vorfindet usw.; und dabei sei die Umgebung, die das fremde, aber uns *quasi* wahrnehmungsmässig gegenüberstehende Ich vorfindet, im grossen und ganzen dieselbe wie unsere Umgebung, und der Leib, den wir in unserer Umgebung als seinen Leib auffassen, sei derselbe, den es in seiner Umgebung als Eigenleib auffasst.

*Note on the text.* The file sets most of this paragraph in short lines with end-of-line hyphens ("an- deres", "Ur- teilen" in the paragraph before); the hyphens are joined here, and the footnote markers (notes 5–8) are left out of the quotation. The paragraph runs across the page break from Hua XIII 115 to 116, which falls inside the parenthesis.

The whole doctrine of empathy stands in a parenthesis. The main clause says only that every I finds bodies to which another I belongs, and the colon after the parenthesis goes straight on to describe that other I. Inside the brackets Husserl says how the other is there, and every key word in them is set in quotation marks: the bodies are "bearers" (*„Träger“*) of I-subjects, the I does not "see" (*„sieht“*) the other, it posits him by "empathy", and the other's experiences are "found" (*„vorgefunden“*). The last pair matters most. "Finding" (*Vorfinden*) is the course's word for everything the natural attitude has before it, and § 1 used it for the I's finding of itself. Here it is applied to the other only under quotation marks, and the next clause withdraws the sense in which it holds of one's own experiences: they are "not given, had, in the sense in which one's own are" (*nicht in dem Sinn gegebene, gehabte wie eigene*). "Had" (*gehabte*) takes up the verb of § 1. The I has its own experiences; the other's it finds only in a sense that the marks keep apart from having.

The positive verb is "posits" (*setzt*). The gloss before it equates seeing oneself with finding oneself experiencingly (*erfahrend vorfindet*), so the denial of "seeing" is also a denial that the other is experienced as I experience myself. What remains is positing, the act that takes something as existing. The weight of the passage rests on that distinction, and it is asserted, not argued. The quotation marks set off the words, but they do not say what positing without having is, and the description gives empathy no content of its own beyond the negation. Husserl later inserted after "empathy" the words "foreign-perception and foreign-experience" (*Fremdwahrnehmung und Fremderfahrung*; note 5, 1924 or later), which name the act by what it is the perception and experience of, and leave the manner open.

The grammar of what follows marks the same status. The other I "has" its soul and "finds" its environment in the indicative, but the two sameness claims are in the subjunctive: the environment the other finds "would be" (*sei*) by and large ours, and the body we take as his "would be" the one he takes as his own. The subjunctive is that of reported content: it gives what is posited in empathy, not what the describer asserts. In 1924 or later Husserl changed the first *sei* to "is in the sense of foreign-perception" (*ist im Sinne der Fremdwahrnehmung*, note 7) and the second to "is" (note 8). The later text asserts the sameness and moves the qualification into a phrase. The 1910 text lets the mood carry it. The main commentary renders both clauses in the indicative ("is by and large *the same*") and so reads the 1924 text into the 1910 one. The adverb "*quasi* perceptually" (*quasi wahrnehmungsmässig*) is the other mark of the same restraint: the other stands over against us as if perceived.

The recapitulation of § 6 (Hua XIII 119) tightens the contrast into a formula. The foreign I's are "found" (again in quotation marks) "in a quite different way" from one's own I, "not through 'self-perception' and 'self-recollection' but through empathy" (*nicht durch „Selbstwahrnehmung“ und „Selbsterinnerung“, sondern durch Einfühlung*). The pair of self-perception and self-recollection is the pair of § 1, where the I knows itself now and as "the same" in memory. The synopsis of the course, written after it, puts the asymmetry in the form the course reaches: "Recollection gives the self! Empathizing presentification, present-memory does not give the self."

In the reduced field the asymmetry first loses its edge and then becomes a law. § 28, whose heading pairs "Empathy and Self-Experience" (*Einfühlung und Selbsterfahrung*), counts empathy among the forms of phenomenological experience, beside retention, recollection and expectation, and says that "empathy too has no exceptional position here" (*Auch die Einfühlung hat hier keine Ausnahmestellung*, Hua XIII 172). § 38 (Hua XIII 187) restates § 4 with a sharper verb: the empathizing I "experiences" (*erfährt*) the other's consciousness, "but no one will say it lives it through" (*es erlebt es*). The pair *erfahren*/*erleben* is the course's final form of the 1910 "found but not had". § 38 then rejects image-consciousness as a model and says that "we see into the other his lived-experiencing, quite immediately" (*wir schauen in den Anderen sein Erleben ein, ganz unmittelbar*, Hua XIII 188). The seeing that § 4 denied in quotation marks returns without them. Husserl's own note to that sentence (note 112, undated) restores them: "Not always do we 'see in' (*„schauen“ … ein*), and necessarily, it seems to me, an empty appresentation stands in front." § 39 (Hua XIII 189) then turns the asymmetry into a principle of separation: an empathized datum and the empathizing experience "cannot in principle belong to the same stream of consciousness", and "from the empathized stream no channel leads into the stream to which the empathizing itself belongs". The positing of § 4 also returns there: "empathy posits the empathized as now" (*Die Einfühlung setzt das Eingefühlte als jetzt*). What the natural description only marked with quotation marks becomes, after the reduction, the boundary between monads.

The *Cartesian Meditations* argue the asymmetry that § 4 assumes. § 50 grants that in experience of another man "the other himself stands bodily before us", and yet that "not the other I itself, not his lived-experiences" come to original givenness. If they did, "it would be merely a moment of my own essence, and in the end he himself and I myself would be one and the same" (*er selbst und ich selbst einerlei*, Hua I 139). In 1929 the reason is given: givenness in the manner of having would abolish the other as other. In 1910/11 the course states the asymmetry as a finding, carries it into the reduced sphere through § 28, and in § 39 makes it the law on which the plurality of I's rests.

## §7, Hua XIII 121–22: the absolute evidence of finding

Reads [§7.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-natural-attitude-as-the-attitude-of-experience-the-problem-of-the-evidence-of-experiential-judgments), third paragraph under § 7.

The descriptions of §§ 1–6 claim absolute evidence, and the claim rests on a restriction: what is absolutely evident is that I *find* something, not that what I find exists. The paragraph then adds a second evidence, that "I am" and that a world is, and declines to say what kind it is. Husserl's later notes divide the paragraph along exactly that seam: the first evidence becomes "the evidence of the pure *cogito*", the second "apparently empirical evidence after all". The line the reduction will draw is already in this paragraph, and it runs between finding and being, not between the I and the world.

> On the other hand, the assertions in which we have descriptively carried out the givenness of the attitude of experience raise a claim to absolute evidence. It is indubitably true that we find such things; with indubitable truth, with absolute truth, I assert and see that I find myself as the one having this and that, as midpoint of an environment, etc., and that is indubitably true both when I assert that *hic et nunc* I experience this determinate thing and when I assert, indeterminately and generally, that I perceive and have perceived things at all in a thing-environment, etc. A further evidence is this: that I am certain not only of finding just this and that, but also that "I am" and that a world is, and that found-nesses of the described kind belong, according to their general type, in the connection of the I, although with regard to the determinate single thing doubt is possible, and error. Of what kind this evidence is we will not decide here.

German:

> Andererseits erheben die Aussagen, die wir die Gegebenheit der Erfahrungseinstellung beschreibend vollzogen haben, einen Anspruch auf absolute Evidenz. Es ist zweifellos wahr, dass wir dergleichen vorfinden; mit zweifelloser Wahrheit, mit absoluter, sage ich aus und sehe ich, dass ich mich als den das und jenes Habenden, als Mittelpunkt einer Umgebung etc. vorfinde, und das ist zweifellos wahr, sowohl wenn ich aussage, dass ich *hic et nunc* dieses bestimmte Ding erfahre, ebensowohl wie wenn ich unbestimmt und allgemein aussage, dass ich überhaupt Dinge in einer Dingumgebung etc. wahrnehme und wahrgenommen habe. Eine weitere Evidenz ist die, dass ich nicht nur sicher bin, gerade das und das vorzufinden, sondern auch, dass „ich bin“ und dass eine Welt ist und dass Vorgefundenheiten der beschriebenen Art ihrem allgemeinen Typus nach im Zusammenhang des Ich sind, trotzdem in Hinsicht auf das bestimmte einzelne Zweifel möglich ist und Irrtum. Welcher Art diese Evidenz ist, wollen wir hier nicht entscheiden.

Husserl's notes to the paragraph (notes 16–18; markers left out of the quotation above): after "have perceived", "Naturally: but that is the evidence of the pure *cogito* with the pure I" (*Natürlich: das ist aber die Evidenz des reinen* cogito *mit dem reinen Ich*); at "that a world is", the editor records that the words were "later marked as questionable" (*später als fragwürdig angestrichelt*); after the last sentence, "Apparently empirical evidence after all" (*Offenbar doch empirische Evidenz*). Notes 16 and 18 are undated and in Husserl's voice.

The first evidence attaches to a verb, not to an object. What is "indubitably true" is "that we find such things" (*dass wir dergleichen vorfinden*), and the next clause repeats the construction in the first person: I see "that I find myself" as the haver and the midpoint. The predicate of every evident sentence is *vorfinden*, the word § 7 has just identified with experiencing "in the pregnant sense". The evidence therefore covers the experiencing and leaves the experienced open. The paragraph before had made the same cut from the other side: a descriptive judgment is evident "as mere expression", as even the faithful description of a fiction is, while the "experience-thesis" (*Erfahrungsthesis*) that posits the described as existing has only an "imperfect" evidence. The present paragraph shows what survives that imperfection: the finding itself, and its general form.

The wording is stronger than the restriction needs. "With indubitable truth, with absolute" (*mit zweifelloser Wahrheit, mit absoluter*) corrects itself upward in an apposition, and "I assert and see" (*sage ich aus und sehe ich*) joins the assertion to an act of seeing that is supposed to warrant it. The seeing is not described. Nothing in the paragraph says how I see that I find, and the claim to absoluteness is made by the voice of the lecturer, who shifts from "we" (*wir*) to "I" (*ich*) at the moment of making it. The scope of the claim is wide too: it covers the determinate *hic et nunc* experience of this thing and the indeterminate general statement that I perceive things at all. The paragraph before had first included "indeterminate generality" among the descriptions whose experience-thesis is imperfect, and Husserl later struck those words there (note 15). Without the deletion the two paragraphs contradict each other: the general description would have both imperfect and absolute evidence. The deletion shows Husserl reading the two paragraphs together and choosing the restricted claim.

The second sentence turns on "further" (*weitere*). The certainty "that 'I am' and that a world is" is not the evidence of finding extended, but another evidence, and the clause that closes the sentence gives its structure: it holds of the general type "although with regard to the determinate single thing doubt is possible, and error". That is the structure § 2 (Hua XIII 114) gave to all the assertions of the natural description: each I knows that "it can err in the single case, while the general … is evident to it or can become so", and each I can turn its certainty "into an absolute evidence". At "absolute" there Husserl later struck the word (note 1 to § 2). Here he leaves the kind of the further evidence undecided, and his note decides it: empirical. The quotation marks around "I am" (*„ich bin“*) present the words as what each speaker says of himself, the self-statement of the person § 1 described. They do not set it apart from the unquoted "a world is". The 1910 sentence and the later notes group "I am" with the world, as a certainty about existence that is general in type and defeasible in detail. On the other side stands the finding, which note 16 assigns to the pure *cogito* "with the pure I". The main commentary reads the notes as separating "the evidence of 'I am'" from "the evidence of 'a world is'". The notes separate both from the evidence of finding (the correction is logged).

The paragraph sets up the course's first methodological problem without solving it. § 10 (Hua XIII 133) returns to it at the head of the critique of Avenarius. The description "posited the existence of the described", and what everyone says in it, "'I am and find myself in a spatio-temporal environment …'", states "facts" (*Fakta*), which may be doubtful. What remains evident is "that I can say: I have such and such perceptions", and that the judgments, as "pure expressions of the perceived as such", exclude error: "It may be that I am deceived about the thing being there, but that I perceive … that is indubitable." Husserl's note there (note 31) states the restriction as a condition: "The description is evident as long as it faithfully expresses the objective sense of the *cogitationes* in question." The "I am" of § 7 has moved to the side of the facts. § 14 then names the defect of the thing's being as "existence-pretension" (row 10), § 22 (Hua XIII 158) restates the result of § 7 as a principle, "the indubitable right of experiential cognition does not mean that it is absolute cognition", and § 24 (Hua XIII 161) finds the absolute in "the indubitable givenness of the meaning that is put in doubt". The evidence that § 7 claimed for finding becomes, after the reduction, the absolute givenness of the reduced perception.

*Ideas* I gives the seam of § 7 its final form. § 46 sets "the thesis of the world, which is a 'contingent' one" against "the thesis of my pure I and I-life, which is a 'necessary', absolutely indubitable one" (*Der Thesis der Welt, die eine „zufällige“ ist, steht also gegenüber die Thesis meines reinen Ich und Ichlebens, die eine „notwendige“, schlechthin zweifellose ist*, Hua III/1 98; Kersten's translation, CW II, agrees). The sentence before it states that the I which is absolute actuality is the I "with the exclusion of what 'of me' belongs to the thing-world". That exclusion is what the 1910 paragraph had not yet made. In § 7 "I am" still stands beside "a world is" under one undecided evidence; in 1913 the "I am" of the person falls to the world's side, and the necessary thesis is the pure I's. Husserl's notes to § 7 read the 1910 paragraph in the terms of 1913.

## §9, Hua XIII 128–29: formal ontology and "something whatever"

Reads [§9.7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#empirical-or-natural-attitude-and-apriori-attitude-ontology-of-nature-and-formal-ontology), seventh paragraph under § 9.

Formal ontology, in this course, is the apriori whose object is "something whatever" (*Etwas überhaupt*): anything at all that can be counted, judged or thought, whether a thing, an idea or a number. It differs from the ontology of nature not by being freer of existence, since geometry is already free of existence, but by being free of the idea of nature as well. Husserl states the difference in the vocabulary of existence-freedom that § 9 built for the apriori in general, and the vocabulary strains under the new load. The passage is part of the stretch of § 9 that Husserl later struck, and the course never builds on it directly. It survives as the second of two apriori routes that § 11 recalls and then leaves for the subjective.

> Of an essentially different character is another group of apriori disciplines, whose truths the natural sciences must often make use of: I mean the pure logic of asserting propositions, the pure theory of probability, pure arithmetic and finally the pure theory of manifolds. These sciences do not belong to the idea of nature; they do not lay out the apriori that constitutes the idea of nature. The existence-freedom of arithmetic concerns not only every actual positing of real existence, but also every positing of the idea of nature, every drawing-in of the idea of things, properties and so on. The one of arithmetic is something whatever, and under it falls not only the thingly, the spatio-temporal, but just something whatever, be it even an idea, be it perhaps itself a number. If formal logic treats of the truth of propositions, then the idea "proposition" in its unconditioned generality embraces not merely arbitrary propositions that have a natural-scientific thought-content, but propositions that have any thought-content, for example a purely arithmetical one. It can be shown that the group of disciplines named can also be interpreted as a universal apriori ontology, as an ontology that relates to thought being as such.

German:

> Von einem wesentlich anderen Charakter ist eine andere Gruppe apriorischer Disziplinen, von deren Wahrheiten die Naturwissenschaften öfters Gebrauch machen müssen: ich meine die reine Logik der behauptenden Sätze, die reine Wahrscheinlichkeitslehre, die reine Arithmetik und endlich die reine Mannigfaltigkeitslehre. Diese Wissenschaften gehören nicht zur Idee der Natur, sie legen nicht das Apriori auseinander, das die Idee der Natur konstituiert. Die Daseinsfreiheit der Arithmetik betrifft nicht nur jedwede aktuelle Setzung von realem Dasein, sondern auch jede Setzung der Idee der Natur, jede Heranziehung der Idee von Dingen, Eigenschaften usw. Die Eins der Arithmetik ist Etwas überhaupt, und darunter fällt nicht nur Dingliches, Räumlich-Zeitliches, sondern eben Etwas überhaupt, sei es auch eine Idee, sei es etwa selbst eine Zahl. Handelt die formale Logik von der Wahrheit von Sätzen, so umfasst die Idee Satz in ihrer unbedingten Allgemeinheit nicht bloss beliebige Sätze, die einen naturwissenschaftlichen Gedankengehalt haben, sondern solche, die einen beliebigen, z.B. einen rein arithmetischen Gedankengehalt haben. Es lässt sich zeigen, dass die bezeichnete Gruppe von Disziplinen auch interpretiert werden kann als eine universelle apriorische Ontologie, als eine Ontologie, die auf gedachtes Sein überhaupt sich bezieht.

*Note on the text.* The first part of the paragraph is set in the file in short lines with end-of-line hyphens, joined here; the page changes from Hua XIII 128 to 129 before "Handelt die formale Logik". The whole passage lies inside the stretch "from here to the end of § 9" that Husserl later struck (note 27, attached at "So spricht die reine Geometrie", Hua XIII 125). The file also gives the same characterization a second time, two paragraphs later and in different words (Hua XIII 130–31), after the paragraph on Kant's pure natural science. Neither the file nor the editor's notes say whether the two versions are a redraft in the manuscript; the reading quotes both as the file has them. The only emphasis in the section that the file keeps is the italic *formale Ontologie* in the heading.

The understanding turns on one compound. "Existence-freedom" (*Daseinsfreiheit*) is § 9's word for the purity of the apriori: the propositions of pure geometry and arithmetic hold "whether there is anything existent or not" (Hua XIII 126). Applied to arithmetic, it is stretched in two steps. It "concerns not only every actual positing of real existence", which it shares with geometry, "but also every positing of the idea of nature". The idea of nature is not an existent. Geometry posits no existing thing, yet it posits the idea of space, which belongs to the idea of nature. Freedom from that idea is therefore freedom from a content, not from an existence, and the compound with *Dasein* names it only by extension. The second version of the paragraph notices the strain and changes the word: there the "existence-purity" (*Daseinsreinheit*) of arithmetic means "not only" that every positing of real existence "remains switched off", but that "nothing of the special essence-content (*Wesensgehalt*) of the idea of a nature whatever is in question". "Essence-content" is the word the first version lacked. The difference between the two apriori groups is a difference of essence-content, and the course will call it formal against material.

The sentence on arithmetic's "one" carries the definition by a doubling. "The one of arithmetic is something whatever, and under it falls not only the thingly, the spatio-temporal, but just something whatever." The repetition of *Etwas überhaupt* is the argument: whatever list of kinds one gives, the one is not the most general of them but what is left when any kind is set aside. "Be it even an idea, be it perhaps itself a number" closes the circle, since numbers fall under the unit that defines number. The second version turns the copula into a statement of meaning, "the one of arithmetic *means as much as* (*besagt soviel wie*) some something whatever", and adds the corollary "everything and anything can be counted", numbers, space and time included. Formal logic follows the same pattern, at one remove: the idea "proposition" covers propositions of any content, arithmetic ones too, so that logic and arithmetic apply to each other.

The last sentence promises more than the course delivers. "It can be shown" (*Es lässt sich zeigen*) introduces the thesis that the group "can also be interpreted" as "a universal apriori ontology", and the showing never comes in Nr. 6. The ontology "relates to thought being as such" (*gedachtes Sein überhaupt*). The phrase defines the formal by the side of thought: being as it can be thought, judged and counted, not being of a region. The second version names the group without that phrase, "a formal, unconditionally general ontology", and sets against it "the much more restricted, because materially determined, idea of the ontology of nature". "Restricted" (*eingeschränktere*) makes the material ontology a narrowing of the formal one in scope. The two still "stand over against" (*gegenüber*) each other as two groups of disciplines, and the relation between them is left at that.

The section then stops at its own threshold. With these apriori disciplines "we do not yet have the higher and proper storey of the philosophical problematic", and the next question is whether they are "the only apriori disciplines". The strike removed the answer's premises with the section. § 10 turns to Avenarius, and § 11 (Hua XIII 139), which Husserl did not strike, recalls what § 9 had done as if it stood: "we spoke, after all, of formal ontology" (*wir sprachen ja auch von formaler Ontologie*). The recollection describes the formal differently. It finds "form" in "the proposition as meant state of affairs", in connections of propositions, in number, combination and manifold, and says that the apriori "concerned a form of objectivity as objectivity of scientific thinking in general". "In a certain sense," Husserl says, we were "objectively directed". The phrase "thought being" of § 9 has become "objectivity of scientific thinking", and the next sentence asks about "the whole sphere of knowledge in the subjective sense". The formal apriori is the last objective apriori before the turn to the subjective; § 11's rational psychology (row 8) is the first step past it. In § 11 Husserl also later changed "in real ontologies" to "in the real ontology" (note 40), so that nature has one material ontology, not several.

What Husserl struck is therefore not the distinction, which § 11 keeps, but the route by which § 9 reached philosophy. The struck text claimed that the passage from the impure apriori of an empirically bound mathematics to the strict apriori "is an indispensable step for the establishing of genuine philosophy", and that whoever has not taken it "can never scale the heights of true philosophy". The course reaches the reduction by another way, through the natural world-concept and the subjective sphere, and § 41 doubts at the end whether it has been anything but apriori. The withdrawn section is the place where the apriori was first offered as the way in.

*Ideas* I repairs the relation that the 1910/11 text left at "over against". § 10 warns that formal ontology "seems at first to stand in one row with the material ontologies", and that the so-called "formal region" is "not something coordinated with the material regions", but "properly no region, but the empty form of region as such", which has all regions "under itself (even if only *formaliter*)" (Hua III/1 26; the file is unreviewed and garbled here, and Kersten's translation, CW II, confirms the wording). In 1913 "something whatever" becomes the "formal essence object as such" (*das formale Wesen Gegenstand überhaupt*), an empty form, and the material regions fall under it. The 1910/11 passage has the scope relation in "restricted, because materially determined", but it still counts two groups of disciplines side by side, and it defines the formal by a freedom named after existence.

## §10, Hua XIII 136–37: the natural world-concept as a priori of sense

Reads [§10.6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-apriori-of-nature-the-natural-world-concept-and-natural-science-avenarius-critique-of-pure-experience), sixth paragraph under § 10.

The natural world-concept holds "absolutely and a priori", and the apriority is a condition of sense, not a necessity of the world. However the concept arose in human beings, any talk of a world with human beings in it, who perceive and experience it, presupposes the sense that the concept lays out, and nothing said within that talk can rationally overturn it. Husserl's restrictions on the claim show how little it asserts: it does not say that another kind of experience is impossible, and it rests on three facts that it does not justify. The passage is the premise of the attack on Avenarius that follows (row 7), and it already contains, in the form of an abstention, the attitude that the reduction will generalize.

> Naturally (so we judge in the natural attitude) every lived-experience and every group of lived-experiences that any human being of this world has itself belongs to this world, and arose in it according to some empirical laws, with empirical necessity, under the given circumstances. But however the actual lived-experiences in which human beings have the world-concept as unitary content may have arisen, as long as there is talk of a world in which there are human beings <who> have consciousness of the world, have lived-experiences and among them perceptions, experiences, etc. that posit empirical existence, as long as this talk keeps its sense, so long the natural world-concept holds absolutely and a priori. The apriority does not say that a thesis other than that of the natural world is in every sense impossible; it does not say that other perceptions of individual unities and other experiences at all than those we call thing-perceptions, perceptions of human beings and the like, are simply unthinkable: about this we rather abstain here from every judgment. Rather it says: if we start from the fact of the natural attitude, and from the fact of the thesis of the nature graspable in it and characterizable in general terms, and from the fact that this thesis has its indubitable right, then every natural-scientific statement, as a statement that scientifically determines the particular posited in this thesis, is senseless if it offends against the sense of this thesis according to its general sense-determining content.

German:

> Natürlich gehört, so urteilen wir in der natürlichen Einstellung, jedes Erlebnis und jede Gruppe von Erlebnissen, die irgendein Mensch dieser Welt hat, selbst dieser Welt an und ist darin entstanden nach irgendwelchen empirischen Gesetzen mit empirischer Notwendigkeit unter den gegebenen Umständen. Wie immer aber die aktuellen Erlebnisse, in denen die Menschen den Weltbegriff als einheitlichen Inhalt haben, entstanden sind, solange die Rede von einer Welt, in der Menschen sind, <welche> Bewusstsein von der Welt haben, Erlebnisse haben und darunter empirisches Dasein setzende Wahrnehmungen, Erfahrungen usw. haben, solange diese Rede ihren Sinn behält, solange gilt der natürliche Weltbegriff absolut und a priori. Die Apriorität besagt nicht, dass eine andere Thesis als die der natürlichen Welt in jedem Sinn unmöglich ist, sie besagt nicht, dass andere Wahrnehmungen von individuellen Einheiten und andere Erfahrungen überhaupt als solche, die wir Dingwahrnehmungen, Menschenwahrnehmungen u. dgl. nennen, schlechthin undenkbar sind: worüber wir uns hier vielmehr jedes Urteils enthalten. Sondern sie sagt: Gehen wir von dem Faktum der natürlichen Einstellung aus und von dem Faktum der Thesis der in ihr fassbaren und allgemein charakterisierbaren Natur und von dem Faktum, dass diese Thesis ihr zweifelloses Recht hat, dann ist jede naturwissenschaftliche Aussage als Aussage, die das in dieser Thesis gesetzte Besondere wissenschaftlich bestimmt, sinnlos, wenn sie gegen den Sinn dieser Thesis nach ihrem allgemeinen sinnbestimmenden Gehalte verstösst.

*Note on the text.* The file sets the paragraph in short lines with end-of-line hyphens, joined here; the page changes from Hua XIII 136 to 137 inside the last word, "verstösst". The angle brackets around *welche* (who) mark an editorial supply. Notes 36 and 37 are attached at "this thesis" and at the end of the paragraph. Note 37 is cut off in the file at "dass die Thesis, die wir jeweilig und" (that the thesis which we each time and); a stretch that reads as its continuation, "jeder für sich vollzogen, sich weiter durchhalten wird in der Einstimmigkeit der Erfahrungen. Diese Antizipation liegt aber im Einheitszuge der Welterfahrung selbst begründet" (… each for himself performed, will maintain itself further in the concordance of experiences. This anticipation is, however, grounded in the unitary character of world-experience itself), stands inside the opening sentence of the later-struck paragraph that follows. The reading quotes note 37 only in the two pieces the file gives and marks the join as its own.

The adverbs carry more than the syntax allows them. "Absolutely and a priori" (*absolut und a priori*) closes a correlative "as long as … as long as … so long" (*solange … solange … solange*) whose conditions are all about talk: as long as there is talk of a world with human beings in it, as long as that talk keeps its sense. The apriori is therefore relative to a sense, and its absoluteness is the absoluteness of the condition, not of the world. Husserl says this himself in the next sentence, which opens with the definitional verb "says" (*besagt*) twice in the negative and once in the positive. The apriority "does not say" that another thesis is "in every sense impossible", "does not say" that other kinds of experience are "simply unthinkable". "Rather it says" (*Sondern sie sagt*) a conditional: if we start from three facts, then any natural-scientific statement that offends against the sense of the thesis is "senseless" (*sinnlos*). The positive content is a rule of meaning for statements made inside the thesis. The weight of "absolutely" lies wholly in the antecedent, and the antecedent is a triple of facts: the fact of the natural attitude, the fact of its thesis, and "the fact that this thesis has its indubitable right". The last is the premise the apriori cannot supply. Husserl names it a fact and does not argue it.

The target is fixed in the paragraph before, and the concession at the start of this one answers it. The natural world-concept "does not and may not mean" a concept every man brought into the world "as an inheritance of animal development over millions of years, as the precipitate of the ever more perfect, ever more thought-economical adaptation" of animal and man to natural conditions. "Thought-economical" (*denkökonomisch*) is the word of Mach and Avenarius, and the genetic story is theirs. Husserl grants it in full, but marks the grant as a judgment made "in the natural attitude" (*so urteilen wir in der natürlichen Einstellung*): within the world, every experience arose in it "with empirical necessity". "However" (*Wie immer aber*) then cuts origin off from validity. The concept's genesis belongs to the world that the concept's sense makes speakable, and so the genesis cannot be a reason against the sense. Origin is separated from validity, and the separation is applied to the concept of the world. The next paragraph states its core: "In the world there cannot be anything that abolishes the sense of the talk of world, because it presupposes that sense (as essence)" (Hua XIII 137).

The clause between the negative and the positive is the most consequential. About whether other perceptions and other experiences are thinkable "we rather abstain here from every judgment" (*worüber wir uns hier vielmehr jedes Urteils enthalten*). The phrase is the formula of the epoché before the epoché. It brackets here only one question, the possibility of experiences of another kind, and only for the purposes of the excursus. The struck paragraph that ends § 10 takes the abstention further, granting that "another world" outside all connection with this one, "Euclideanly spaceless", would be "no nonsense" (row 7). The last chapter returns to the same verb. § 40 opens: everything holds "if we do not judge about the being of nature" (*wenn wir also nicht über das Sein der Natur urteilen*), and "mark well, we have not judged about the being of nature, in any way" (Hua XIII 192). The edition's heading for the section, supplied in angle brackets, names it "the abstention from every judgment about the being of nature". Between the two, the object of the abstention has changed. In § 10 Husserl refrains from judging whether a world of another kind is thinkable; in § 40 he refrains from judging whether this one exists.

The later notes move the weight of the passage from the third fact to the concordance of experience. In 1921, probably, Husserl inserted after "this thesis" the words "with its concrete content of actual and possible perception-series, which can stretch forth in infinite openness and confirm one another concordantly, maintaining the unbrokenness of the thesis" (note 36). Note 37 adds that the talk of a fact implies that "we think it as lasting *in infinitum*, thus presuppose by anticipation that the thesis which we each time and" — here the file breaks off, and the stretch displaced into the next paragraph supplies "each for himself performed, will maintain itself further in the concordance of experiences", an anticipation "grounded in the unitary character of world-experience itself". The note to the preceding statement that all natural science is "*a priori* bound to the real ontology" (note 33, Hua XIII 134) says what this costs: "the evidence of the thesis is always an experience-evidence that is subject to reservation and necessarily remains so", and "whether nature is really there … is always reserved". The "indubitable right" of 1910 has become a presumption that holds as long as experience goes on agreeing with itself. Note 38 sharpens the other side: the natural world-concept "is not the one that human beings formed for themselves before science", but the one that makes up "the sense of the natural attitude before and after science", and it must first be worked out "in the basic concepts of ontology". Avenarius's pre-scientific world-concept and Husserl's are not the same concept.

*Ideas* I (1913) states the possibility the 1910 lecture refused to judge. § 49 grants that nothing in the essence of perception guarantees that experience runs in concordant forms: "it is quite conceivable" (*es ist sehr wohl denkbar*) that experience swarms with irreconcilable conflicts and loses its fixed orders of appearance, "that there is no longer any world" (*daß es keine Welt mehr gibt*, Hua III/1 104; Kersten's translation, CW II, agrees). What § 10 left in abstention, *Ideas* I affirms as a possibility, and it uses the possibility to show that consciousness survives the annihilation of the world. The later notes to § 10 already point there: once the right of the natural thesis is a concordance that might fail, the apriori of the natural world-concept binds only for as long as there is a world to speak of, which is what the *solange* of 1910 had said.

## §10, Hua XIII 137–38: countersense and nonsense

Reads [§10.7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-apriori-of-nature-the-natural-world-concept-and-natural-science-avenarius-critique-of-pure-experience), seventh paragraph under § 10, with the later-struck paragraph that follows it (§10.8).

Countersense (*Widersinn*) is a claim that has a sense and contradicts it: what it asserts is excluded by the essence of what it is about. Husserl calls Avenarius's question countersense "in the sharpest meaning of the word" because a rationally motivated abandonment of the natural world-concept would have to draw its reason from the experience whose sense the concept lays out. The question is not empty talk; it is a meaningful question whose positive answer is impossible a priori. The paragraph that follows, later struck, draws the boundary of the claim in another word, "nonsense" (*Unsinn*), and finds on the far side of it a possibility that is "no nonsense": another world, unconnected with this one. The two words do not mean the same, and the passage turns on keeping them apart.

> As a further consequence, it is fundamentally wrong to grasp the problem as, if I remember rightly, Avenarius grasped it, and as at any rate his school meant it, namely: We can after all describe the world-concept that we all have before science, or that humanity too had before science, and we can then raise the further question: Does man, when he pursues natural science, have occasion to leave this world-concept? Such a posing of the question is wrong, because it sets up as possible that occasions could be brought up through experience to modify the natural world-concept, *nota bene* rationally. Our analysis has taught, however, that this supposed possibility is countersense, and indeed in the sharpest meaning of the word.

German:

> In weiterer Folge ist es grundverkehrt, wenn man das Problem so fasst, wie es, wenn ich mich recht entsinne, von Avenarius gefasst, jedenfalls in seiner Schule gemeint war, nämlich: Wir können doch den Weltbegriff beschreiben, den wir alle vor der Wissenschaft haben oder den auch die Menschheit vor der Wissenschaft gehabt hat, und wir können dann weiter die Frage aufwerfen: Hat der Mensch, wenn er Naturwissenschaft treibt, Anlass, diesen Weltbegriff zu verlassen? Eine solche Fragestellung ist verkehrt, weil sie es als möglich hinstellt, dass durch Erfahrung Anlässe herangebracht werden könnten, den natürlichen Weltbegriff, *nota bene* vernünftigerweise, zu modifizieren. Unsere Analyse hat aber gelehrt, dass diese vermeinte Möglichkeit Widersinn ist, und zwar in der schärfsten Bedeutung des Wortes.

*Note on the text.* The italic *nota bene* is in the file, which keeps Latin in italics. Note 38 is attached at the end of the paragraph. The struck paragraph that follows (note 39: "The following paragraph was later struck") is damaged. Its opening sentence breaks off at "dass eine" (that a), the file then gives text that belongs to note 37 (see the reading of § 10, Hua XIII 136–37), and the sentence resumes at "andere Welt als diese Welt wirkliche Welt sei" (another world than this world is the actual world) after the page marker for Hua XIII 138. Read without the displaced note text, the two pieces join without a gap. The join is this reading's, not the file's, and it is marked below with a vertical bar.

Husserl's vocabulary in the excursus climbs. The paragraph before called any natural-scientific statement that offends against the sense of the natural thesis "senseless" (*sinnlos*), and the next step said of the contingent world-concept "I say, that is senseless". The present paragraph replaces the word. The "supposed possibility" (*vermeinte Möglichkeit*) of a rational revision is not senseless but "countersense, and indeed in the sharpest meaning of the word". The qualifier marks the word as a term, and the term has a fixed meaning in Husserl's work. The Fourth Logical Investigation, § 12, "Nonsense and Countersense" (*Unsinn und Widersinn*), forbids confusing "the senseless (the nonsensical) with the absurd (the countersensical), which exaggerated speech also likes to call senseless, although it rather makes up a subregion of the meaningful". *A round square* "yields truly a unified meaning", but one to which "no existing object can correspond"; *a round or* yields none (Hua XIX/1 334, A 312; Findlay's translation, "Nonsense and absurdity", agrees). "In the sharpest meaning" withdraws the exaggerated "senseless" of the paragraph before. Avenarius's question is meaningful, and what it asks about is excluded a priori.

Two qualifiers carry the exclusion. "*Nota bene* rationally" (*nota bene vernünftigerweise*) restricts it to a revision with rational right. A change of world-concept by any other route, a forgetting, a mutation, a madness, is not in question. The countersense lies in the claim that experience could give a *reason* to leave the concept, because the right of experience is itself the right of the natural thesis, and the reason would have to presuppose what it abolishes. The paragraph before put the core in one sentence: "In the world there cannot be anything that abolishes the sense of the talk of world, because it presupposes it as sense (as essence)." The second qualifier is "our analysis has taught" (*Unsere Analyse hat aber gelehrt*). The analysis taught a conditional (row 6): *if* we start from the fact of the natural thesis and the fact that it has its indubitable right, then statements against its sense are senseless. The countersense inherits that condition. It is countersense only for one who stands within the thesis and grants it its right, which is exactly where Avenarius's question stands. Husserl's hedge on the attribution, "if I remember rightly", and the retreat to "at any rate his school", show that the target is a way of posing the problem, not a quoted text. "Fundamentally wrong" (*grundverkehrt*) and "wrong" (*verkehrt*) are the verdicts the *Prolegomena* passes on a theory that contradicts the conditions of its own possibility: such a theory is "not merely false but from the ground up wrong" (*nicht bloß falsch, sondern von Grund aus verkehrt*, Hua XVIII 118, § 32). The critique of skeptical relativism returns in 1910 as the critique of a positivism that would let experience refute the sense of experience.

The struck paragraph changes the word, and the change matters:

> If it is then nonsense to claim implicitly that in the world a human being could find with rational right that an|other world than this world is the actual world, then it is on the other hand no nonsense to claim that perhaps another world at all could be, indeed perhaps even that, out of connection with this world, the world of the natural attitude or of experience, there is yet a world, totally different in kind, Euclideanly spaceless and the like, *nota bene*: no nonsense! We then just do not claim what makes the nonsense, that human beings or beings of in principle the same kind, endowed with bodies etc., could find such a world and know it scientifically, or that natural science, the science on the ground of the natural world-concept, the science that with its first word, so to speak, posits things, space, time and so on, could be compelled by experience to leave the natural world-concept.

German:

> Ist es nun ein Unsinn, implizit zu behaupten, in der Welt könnte ein Mensch mit vernünftigem Recht finden, dass eine | andere Welt als diese Welt wirkliche Welt sei, so ist es andererseits kein Unsinn, zu behaupten, dass vielleicht eine andere Welt überhaupt sein könnte, ja vielleicht sogar, dass ausser Zusammenhang mit dieser Welt, der Welt der natürlichen Einstellung oder Erfahrung, noch eine Welt sei, total anders geartet, euklidisch raumlos u. dgl., *nota bene*: kein Unsinn! Wir behaupten dann eben nicht, was den Unsinn macht, dass Menschen oder prinzipiell gleichartige leibesbegabte etc. Wesen eine solche Welt vorfinden und wissenschaftlich erkennen könnten oder dass die Naturwissenschaft, die Wissenschaft auf dem Grunde des natürlichen Weltbegriffs, die Wissenschaft, die mit ihrem ersten Worte sozusagen Dinge, Raum, Zeit usw. setzt, durch Erfahrung genötigt sein könnte, den natürlichen Weltbegriff zu verlassen.

The opening is a conditional by inversion, "If it is then nonsense …, then it is on the other hand no nonsense", not a question. What the conditional calls "nonsense" is the claim § 10.7 called countersense, restated: a human being finding "with rational right" that another world is the actual one. In the vocabulary of the *Logical Investigations* that claim is not nonsense, since it has a unified meaning; it is countersense. The struck paragraph uses *Unsinn* loosely, as the "exaggerated speech" of the Fourth Investigation does, and then sets the meaningful against it: the closing sentence speaks of "the meaningful possibility (*sinnvolle Möglichkeit*) of other worlds". The main commentary renders *Unsinn* throughout as "countersense", and so loses the shift (the correction is logged). The second *nota bene*, "no nonsense!", is the paragraph's real claim. A world without Euclidean space, out of all connection with ours, is a meaningful thought, because thinking it does not claim that beings like us could find it. What the paragraph excludes is the finding, by bodily beings and by a science that "with its first word" posits things, space and time.

The struck paragraph goes further than the paragraph before it allowed. § 10.6 said that the apriority does not decide whether other kinds of experience are unthinkable, "about which we rather abstain here from every judgment". The struck paragraph judges: another world "could be", and saying so is "no nonsense". Husserl's strike removed a judgment that the course's own abstention had withheld, and the reason he gives in the paragraph's last sentence, that "great problems" hang on the meaningful possibility of other worlds and on "the facticity of this world", is a promise not kept in Nr. 6.

The word *Widersinn* keeps its sharp sense through the rest of the course, and the reduction depends on it. § 13 (Hua XIII 144) claims that "we can therefore without countersense" cut the empirical connection between the lived-experience and all thingly existence (row 9). The cut does not modify the natural world-concept; it suspends the positing of the world, and so it does not fall under § 10's verdict. § 14 (Hua XIII 146) defines the word in passing: to demand a finally closed series of perceptions that would give the thing completely "is a countersense, is something excluded by the essence of experience" (*ist ein Widersinn, ist etwas durch das Wesen der Erfahrung Ausgeschlossenes*). § 18 calls the epistemology of solipsism "countersensical" (*widersinnige*, Hua XIII 154), and § 29 calls the demand that the natural scientist's modes of givenness be absolute "foolish, indeed countersensical" (Hua XIII 169). *Unsinn* returns once, in § 38 (Hua XIII 188), for a genuinely empty construal: that my own anger could serve as the image of another's, "but that is a nonsense", since when I empathize anger I am not angry at all. The course uses countersense for what essence excludes, and nonsense for what cannot even be coherently described. The struck paragraph of § 10 is the one place where the two run together.

## §11, Hua XIII 139–40: rational psychology as the apriori of soul-positing

Reads [§11.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-sphere-of-knowledge-in-the-subjective-sense-and-empirical-and-rational-psychology), third paragraph under § 11.

Rational psychology, as Husserl revives it here, is the apriori of the psychic taken as nature: it lays out what belongs to the sense of positing a soul, a human being, an experience as the experience of a person, as the ontology of nature lays out what belongs to the sense of positing a thing. It is therefore not yet phenomenology. It answers the question of § 11, whether the subjective side of knowledge admits an apriori consideration, with an apriori that remains inside the natural attitude. That is why the next section has to ask for an attitude that switches off not only the empirical but "also its essence as essence of nature". The passage claims a discipline in order to mark what the reduction must also put out of play.

> Now, insofar as I spoke of knowledge in the subjective sense, I have seemingly just given the answer. Everything subjective itself belongs to the natural sphere, and more narrowly to the sphere of psychology. As a fact of the empirical subject in question, generally as a fact of cognizing lived-experiencing in the human world as such, it belongs of course in psychology as natural science. Does it not also admit of an apriori consideration? Certainly. As there is an apriori, and self-evidently is, with regard to the physical thing, an apriori that says nothing other than what belongs to the general sense of empirical thing-positing, so there is also a psychological apriori, namely the one that lays out what belongs to the essence or sense of empirical "soul"-positing, positing of human beings and the like. Given the crass empiricism that has ruled among psychologists for some decades, it may seem incredible to many that I dare here to reawaken the long-buried idea of a rational psychology. However, I cannot help it. From the standpoint of the matter, which, once one has seen it, is absolutely evident, it cannot be put otherwise. It is downright self-evident that a pure natural science must have a pure psychology running parallel to it. At any rate there must surely be some groups of propositions that lay out the sense lying in I-experience, in the experience of the psychic, and draw their evidence from transposing oneself into the perfect givenness of the modes of the psychic in question.

German:

> Nun habe ich soeben, sofern ich von Erkenntnis im subjektiven Sinn sprach, scheinbar die Antwort gegeben. Alles Subjektive gehört selbst zur natürlichen Sphäre, und näher zur Sphäre der Psychologie. Als Faktum des betreffenden empirischen Subjekts, allgemein als Faktum des erkennenden Erlebens in der Menschenwelt überhaupt, gehört es selbstverständlich in die Psychologie als Naturwissenschaft. Lässt es nicht auch eine apriorische Erwägung zu? Gewiss. Wie es ein Apriori gibt, und selbstverständlich gibt, hinsichtlich des physischen Dinges, ein Apriori, welches nichts anderes besagt als das zum allgemeinen Sinn empirischer Dingsetzung Gehörende, so gibt es auch ein psychologisches Apriori, nämlich dasjenige, das auseinanderlegt, was zum Wesen oder Sinn empirischer „Seelen“setzung, Setzung von Menschen u.dgl. gehört. Es mag bei dem krassen Empirismus, der unter den Psychologen seit einigen Jahrzehnten herrscht, so manchem unglaublich erscheinen, dass ich es hier wage, die längst begrabene Idee einer rationalen Psychologie wieder zu erwecken. Indessen, ich kann mir nicht helfen. Es ist vom Gesichtspunkt der Sache, die, wenn man sie einmal gesehen hat, absolut evident, ist, nicht anders zu sagen. Es ist geradezu selbstverständlich, dass einer reinen Naturwissenschaft auch eine reine Psychologie parallel laufen muss. Jedenfalls muss doch irgendwelche Satzgruppen geben, welche den in der Icherfahrung, in der Erfahrung von Seelischem liegenden Sinn auseinanderlegen und ihre Evidenz aus der Hineinversetzung in die vollkommene Gegebenheit der betreffenden Modi des Seelischen ziehen.

*Note on the text.* The page changes from Hua XIII 139 to 140 after "ein Apriori,". Two slips are quoted as the file has them: the comma in "absolut evident, ist" and the missing subject in "Jedenfalls muss doch irgendwelche Satzgruppen geben" (where *es* is wanted); neither touches the sense.

The apriori is defined twice in the same way, and the definition is what places rational psychology. The apriori of the physical thing "says nothing other than what belongs to the general sense of empirical thing-positing" (*welches nichts anderes besagt als das zum allgemeinen Sinn empirischer Dingsetzung Gehörende*). The psychological apriori lays out "what belongs to the essence or sense of empirical 'soul'-positing". Both are apriori of a positing, and of an empirical one. This is the conception § 10 worked out for the natural world-concept: an apriori that unfolds the sense of the natural thesis and binds every statement made within it (row 6). Rational psychology is the same kind of apriori for the other half of nature, the psychic. "Or sense" (*oder Sinn*) glosses "essence" (*Wesen*) as the sense of a positing, and so keeps the essence on the side of what natural experience means. The quotation marks around "soul" (*„Seelen“setzung*) hold the word at a distance without dropping it: the soul is what the natural attitude posits under that name, and Husserl does not commit himself to it.

The rhetoric is stronger than the claim. Husserl presents the revival as a personal risk, "that I dare here" (*dass ich es hier wage*), and a compulsion, "I cannot help it" (*ich kann mir nicht helfen*), against "the crass empiricism" of recent psychology. He then raises the stakes of evidence twice: the matter is "absolutely evident" once seen, and the parallel of a pure psychology with a pure natural science is "downright self-evident" (*geradezu selbstverständlich*). The parallel rests on analogy alone: as there is an apriori of the thing, so there "is also" one of the soul, and so a pure psychology "must" run parallel. The last sentence then lowers the claim to its minimum. "At any rate" (*Jedenfalls*) there "must surely be some groups of propositions" (*irgendwelche Satzgruppen*) that lay out the sense of I-experience. A rational psychology becomes, at the end of the sentence, some propositions whose existence follows from the existence of a sense to lay out. That weaker claim is the one the argument supports, and it is enough for what follows.

The method is named in the same breath, and it is the method of § 9's essence-attitude carried over to the psychic. The propositions draw their evidence "from transposing oneself into the perfect givenness" (*aus der Hineinversetzung in die vollkommene Gegebenheit*) of the modes of the psychic. The next paragraph spells it out: we make clear what a thing is by thinking ourselves into perception-connections in which the thing comes to ever more perfect givenness, and so the essence of an I-character must show itself in connections of experience "into which we intuitively transpose ourselves, perhaps in feigned but thoroughly clear connections of givenness". The ideal givenness that § 9 sought for numbers and figures is sought here for characters and persons, by the device of imagined experience. Nothing in this method leaves the natural attitude. The persons imagined are still human beings in the world, and the experiences are still "experiences of experiencing persons" with "their objective time-place" (Hua XIII 140).

That is the setting for § 12, and it is why the claim is made at this point. Husserl asks whether an attitude can be gained "such that the empirical … remains wholly switched off, and indeed in such a way that its essence as essence of nature also remains switched off" (*dass auch sein Wesen als Wesen von Natur ausgeschaltet bleibt*, Hua XIII 141). "Also" refers back to the passage. Rational psychology has just been shown to be an apriori of nature, because it is the apriori of soul-positing, and the reduction must put it out of play along with empirical psychology. The phenomenological attitude is defined against both: not the psychology of facts, and not the rational psychology of essences, since both concern the psychic as nature. The commentary's claim that the method of § 11 contains "*in nuce*, the method of eidetic variation" is right as far as it goes, but the course does not use that method for phenomenology. It uses it for the discipline the reduction excludes.

The rest of the course keeps rational psychology outside. It does not recur by name in Nr. 6. Its place is taken in § 29 by "transcendental psychology" (row 20), which is psychology only after the reduction, and in § 41 by the doubt whether the course's own results were apriori after all (row 32). The later Husserl kept the distinction and changed its emphasis. The disposition of the 1910/11 course, written "in one of the following years" and printed as Beilage XXI, says that what an introduction needs is to "lay free the way to eidetics" and then "to make clear the difference between rational psychology as eidetic of the mind and phenomenology" (*den Unterschied zwischen rationaler Psychologie als Eidetik des Geistes und Phänomenologie klarzulegen*, Hua XIII 196). In 1910/11 the difference ran between an apriori of nature and an experiencing science of the reduced field; in the disposition, it runs between two eidetic sciences.

*Ideas* I makes the exclusion explicit and gives it the same ground. § 60 switches off, with the empirical sciences of animal and personal beings, "the eidetic sciences corresponding to these objectivities", naming "rational psychology" and sociology, and it counts "soul", "psychic lived-experience (lived-experience in the psychological sense)", "person" and "character-trait" (*Charaktereigenschaft*) among the "transcendent essences" (*transzendente Wesen*), whose place is "in the doctrine of essence of the transcendent objectivities in question" (Hua III/1 128–29; Kersten's translation, CW II, agrees). The I-character of § 11 is there a transcendent essence. The same section allows that even a study of pure consciousness "in the way proper to a science of matters of fact" could not do without the apriori of consciousness, which is the question the 1910/11 course left open. In 1910 rational psychology was revived and set aside in two consecutive sections; in 1913 it is set aside by principle, because its essences are transcendent.

## §13, Hua XIII 143–44: the *distinctio phaenomenologica*

Reads [§13.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-resolvability-of-the-empirical-connection-of-res-cogitans-and-res-extensa-the-distinctio-phaenomenologica), fourth paragraph under § 13, with its foil in §13.2.

The *distinctio phaenomenologica* is a distinction in what is posited, not in what is. The lived-experience and the body remain connected; Husserl concedes it in so many words. What the distinction separates is a consideration of the experience that makes use of that connection from one that makes no use of any positing of nature. It is legitimate because the connection is only empirical: the essence of the *cogitatio* contains no relation to a thing, so cutting the relation offends against no essence. The Latin name sets it against the scholastic *distinctio realis*, which Husserl attaches to Hume and defines as the distinction of inseparable moments. The reduction is introduced here as a third kind of distinction, neither real nor abstractive, and its name says so.

> We can therefore without countersense as it were cut through the empirical connection between the lived-experience and all thingly existence. We carry out a certain *distinctio phaenomenologica*. What is it supposed to mean, what kind of cutting-through is that supposed to be? Is it then not true that the lived-experiences are lived-experiences of experiencing human beings, and so have relation to the lived-body, an ordering into nature? Can I change anything about that? It is just so, certainly. But we can after all consider the lived-experiences in and for themselves, without considering them in their empirical relation. We can switch off every natural positing (positing of natural existence) in the sense that we carry out scientific considerations in which we make simply no use of any positing of nature, and which thus keep their validity whether there is a nature, a spiritual-bodily world at all, or not.

German:

> Wir können daher ohne Widersinn die empirische Verbindung zwischen dem Erlebnis und allem dinglichen Dasein gleichsam durchschneiden. Wir vollziehen eine gewisse *distinctio phaenomenologica*. Was soll sie besagen, was soll das für ein Durchschneiden sein? Ist es denn nicht wahr, dass die Erlebnisse Erlebnisse von erlebenden Menschen sind, also Beziehung zum Leib haben, Einordnung in die Natur? Kann ich daran etwas ändern? Es ist nun einmal so, das gewiss. Aber wir können doch die Erlebnisse an und für sich betrachten, ohne sie in ihrer empirischen Beziehung zu betrachten. Wir können jede natürliche Setzung (Setzung von Naturdasein) in dem Sinn ausschalten, dass wir wissenschaftliche Betrachtungen anstellen, in denen wir schlechthin von keiner Setzung von Natur Gebrauch machen und die somit Geltung behalten, ob es eine Natur, eine geistig-leibliche Welt überhaupt gibt oder nicht.

*Note on the text.* The footnote markers 42–45 are left out of the quotation. The notes record later changes, none dated by the editor: the text from "But" onward was later marked with zeros in the margin (note 42); "and posit" (*und setzen*) was inserted after "consider in and for themselves" (note 43); "considering" in "without considering them in their empirical relation" was corrected to "positing" (note 44); and "thus" (*somit*) was struck (note 45). The italics on *distinctio phaenomenologica* are the file's, which keeps Latin in italics.

The foil stands two paragraphs earlier. Since no essential relation to a thing lies in the essence of a pain, a sensation or a judgment, "we can cut through the empirical relation between *cogitatio* and *res* without thereby performing an abstraction in the sense of the *distinctio realis* of Hume, in the sense of the distinction of essentially non-self-sufficient and inseparable moments of a *concretum*" (*ohne damit eine Abstraktion in dem Sinne der* distinctio realis *Humes, im Sinn der Unterscheidung wesentlich unselbständiger und unabtrennbarer Momente eines* concretum, *zu vollziehen*, Hua XIII 143). The Latin coinage of § 13.4 answers this Latin term. One distinction separates moments that cannot exist apart, such as colour and extension, and so can only abstract; the other separates what can be posited apart, because the essences have "nothing to do with one another". The attribution is odd. Hume's distinction of inseparable moments is the distinction of reason, and the Second Logical Investigation discusses it under that name: "Hume's doctrine of the *distinctio rationis*" (*Humes Lehre von der* distinctio rationis, Hua XIX/1 196, § 36), the question how we distinguish abstract moments that "cannot become ideas for themselves". "*Realis*" in the lecture is either a slip or a usage the file cannot explain, and it is quoted as the file has it. The main commentary drops both "*realis*" and Hume, and with them the contrast that gives the new term its name (the correction is logged).

The modal ground is laid in § 13.1, in a third vocabulary. A thing that were not spatial "would be a *nonsens*", but that no thing at all, "not even the well-known human lived-body", were a sensing thing "is no *nonsens*". The Latin *nonsens* here names what § 10 called countersense, the essentially excluded. The weight falls on "factually": "Thing, *res extensa*, is factually *res cogitans*", in so far as *cogitationes* are connected with it by experience. Descartes's two substances become two essences joined by a fact. The causal analogy of § 13.3 supplies the rule: "Not all necessity in the realm of experience is essence-necessity, otherwise all natural sciences would be apriori ones", and so the connection of lived-experience and the man who has it is "contingent" (*„zufällige“*, in quotation marks). "Therefore" (*daher*) at the head of § 13.4 draws the consequence. What is only factual can be cut "without countersense", since countersense in the course's strict sense is what essence excludes (row 7).

The wording of the cut is hedged at every step. The verb is qualified by "as it were" (*gleichsam durchschneiden*), the distinction is "a certain" one (*eine gewisse*), and Husserl at once asks what it is "supposed to mean" (*Was soll sie besagen*). The objection he raises against himself is the natural attitude's own: experiences are experiences of human beings, and "Can I change anything about that? It is just so, certainly." The concession is total, and the answer does not retract it. "But we can after all consider" (*Aber wir können doch … betrachten*): the cut falls in the consideration, not in the thing considered. The last sentence gives the cut its method. It is a "switching off" (*ausschalten*) of every natural positing, defined as making "simply no use" (*schlechthin … keinen Gebrauch*) of any positing of nature in scientific considerations. This is the first formulation of the reduction in the course, and its criterion is the one § 10's abstention prepared: the results "keep their validity whether there is a nature … or not".

The later revisions change both ends of the paragraph. "Consider" becomes "consider and posit", and "without considering them in their empirical relation" becomes "without positing them" there (notes 43–44). The later Husserl did not want the cut to read as a mere change of regard. The experiences "in and for themselves" are posited, as existing, in a new attitude. That is the premise of the course's experiencing phenomenology (row 1), which needs reduced experiences to be posited as individual being (§ 32, row 22). The strike of "thus" (note 45) withdraws an inference. In 1910 the independence of the results from the existence of nature followed "thus" from making no use of nature-positing. Without the particle, the independence is stated beside the method, not derived from it. § 17 (Hua XIII 151–53) is where the course argues that independence, and Husserl's critical note there, probably of 1921, restricts it: the judging is independent of natural judgment, but the truths are bound by essence-correlations (row 13).

The distinction carries the next three sections. § 14 grounds it in the "privilege of being" of the lived-experience, whose being is not, like the thing's, "existence-pretension" (row 10). § 15 gives the switching-off its lasting name, the bracket: "we set it as it were in brackets" (row 11), with the same "as it were". § 16 names Descartes as the first to carry out a phenomenological reduction (row 12). The Cartesian vocabulary of § 13, *res extensa* and *res cogitans*, *cogitatio* and *extensio*, is what makes that credit possible: Descartes is credited with the first phenomenological reduction, whose correlate is "the *cogitatio* in the Cartesian sense", and faulted for giving it up again at once and for seeking the indubitability that "for us is not the main thing" (Hua XIII 150). The Latin name does not return in Nr. 6. Its content does: every later section speaks of the "phenomenological reduction", and the distinction's two marks, that the empirical tie is acknowledged and that no use is made of it, stay the marks of that reduction to the end of the course (§ 40, Hua XIII 192: "we have not judged about the being of nature, in any way").

## §14, Hua XIII 145–46: existence-pretension

Reads [§14.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-privilege-of-being-of-the-lived-experience-over-against-the-natural-object-empirical-transcendent-perception-and-the-perception-of-the-pure-lived-experience), third paragraph under § 14.

The thing's existence is, for knowledge, a pretension: a claim to be that experience makes on the thing's behalf and that no experience can finally honour. However well confirmed, the experienced thing remains "mere existence-pretension" (*blosse Daseinsprätention*), because its being is posited as independent of being experienced, and so every right that experience gives it can be outweighed by a better right later. The coinage names the disadvantage against which the lived-experience's "tremendous privilege" of being becomes visible. It also names, in advance, what the reduction will refuse: the pretension of experience to posit more than itself.

> That is correct, speaking in the manner of natural science. On the other hand, the knowledge of thinghood has an unremovable disadvantage: however good the ground on which a thing is experienced as existing, and however it confirms itself in its existence in the course of further experience and lets itself be determined by experiential science, it remains forever for knowledge in a certain way mere existence-pretension. The right, however good, can turn out in the progress of experience to be insufficient and outweighed by a better right.
>
> All that belongs to the essence of appearance, or to the sense of experiential objectivity as such. We need only transpose ourselves into an experiencing and weigh the sense in which the experienced gives itself as being, and we have the evidence that experienceable being in principle does not go up in the *percipi*, but is, over against it, an in-itself. And this in-itself comes from [?] givenness. And yet it is in principle never given in an absolute way; its meaning is forever only meaning in the sense that it always needs a demonstration that can never be given conclusively. And so the being-in-itself of the thing is also, over against knowledge, forever pretension, in so far as we never really get rid of the consciousness of experience. It depends forever on the further progress of experiencing whether the experience-thesis once performed can rightfully be maintained. In experiential thinking, then, something is posited to which being experienced is in itself contingent; and yet, as soon as we cut off every further being-experienced, the experiential positing of that in-itself also hangs in the air, since it is not conclusively demonstrated and in principle not demonstrable. That is connected, however, with the fact that experience, according to its sense, posits *transcendence*.

The file also writes "An-sich" and "Ansich" side by side. The italics on *percipi* and *Transzendenz* are the file's; the second is one of the few non-Latin italics in Nr. 6, and the file cannot say whether it is Husserl's emphasis. "Existence-pretension" (*Daseinsprätention*) is built from a legal word. A *Prätention* is a claim put forward, a title asserted and not yet granted. The passage surrounds the coinage with the vocabulary of right: the thing is experienced "with ever so good a ground" (*mit noch so gutem Grund*), "the right, however good" (*Das noch so gute Recht*) can prove "insufficient and outweighed by a better right" (*durch besseres Recht überwogenes*), the thesis must be "rightfully maintained" (*rechtmässig aufrechterhalten*), and the meaning "always needs a demonstration" (*Ausweis*), the document that would make good the claim. The claim is never void; it is never settled either. "Mere" (*blosse*) and "in a certain way" (*gewissermaßen*) hedge the noun in opposite directions: the being of the thing is only a claim, and calling it a claim is only a manner of speaking. The second paragraph drops the compound and the hedges. Now the being-in-itself of the thing "is forever pretension" (*immerfort Prätention*), without qualification.

The argument turns the thing's independence against it. The paragraph before grants, "speaking in the manner of natural science", that the thing is what it is even if no one experiences it: if a geological revolution killed every living being, the earth would still be. The disadvantage is the other face of that independence. Because being experienced is "in itself contingent" (*an sich zufällig*) to what experience posits, no course of experience can include the thing's being in itself. The sentence with "and yet" (*und doch*) makes the trap explicit: cut off further experience and "the experiential positing of that in-itself hangs in the air", since it is "in principle not demonstrable". Experience posits something whose being does not depend on experience, and can confirm it only by more experience. The word that names this is the last one: experience "posits *transcendence*" (*Transzendenz setzt*). The pretension is the transcendence seen from the side of knowledge.

The evidence for the disadvantage is of a particular kind, and the passage says so. "We need only transpose ourselves into an experiencing and weigh the sense in which the experienced gives itself as being." The claim is about the sense of experience, not about how often experience errs. It is the method of § 11's rational psychology, applied to thing-experience. The next paragraph states the result as a law: to demand a complete experience of a thing in a finitely closed act "is a countersense, is something excluded by the essence of experience" (Hua XIII 146). Husserl then concedes: "That is admittedly here only an assertion, whose full grounding we cannot give here, but which you can see …" In a later revision he struck the sentence up to that point (note 46), so that only the appeal to insight remains. The later text no longer admits that the essence law is asserted without proof.

The pretension is the foil for the privilege. The section opened with the thesis that a lived-experience "is what it is, even if the talk of a spatio-temporal nature … were a meaningless fancy", and that its being has "a tremendous privilege" (*einen gewaltigen Vorzug*) over the existence of a natural object (Hua XIII 144). The privilege is the absence of pretension: the feeling grasped "purely in itself" is what it is "whether the whole of nature is or is not" (Hua XIII 147). The two perceptions are told apart by what they claim. Empirical perception posits more than it gives; the perception of the pure lived-experience posits only what it gives.

The word returns at the reduction, as a verb. § 15 (Hua XIII 148) says what the phenomenological attitude takes into its realm: every experiential act and judgment "as pure existence (*reines Dasein*) that it itself is", while there "wholly fails to occur all co-positing of that existence which it, as experience, pretends of itself to posit" (*das es als Erfahrung von sich aus zu setzen prätendiert*). The noun of § 14 becomes the act of § 15. The experience is kept, its pretension is not honoured, and the existence it claims is not co-posited. § 17's critical note, probably of 1921, uses the participle once more for the judgments of the natural sciences as "pretended, hypothetical truths" (*prätendierter, hypothetischer Wahrheiten*, Hua XIII 153). The term stays on the side of nature throughout. § 24 will test whether the reduced perception has none, and § 25 will find that even it reaches beyond the now through retention (rows 18–19).

*Ideas* I keeps the thought and changes the word. § 46 concludes that "everything which is there for me in the thing-world is in principle only presumptive actuality" (*prinzipiell nur präsumptive Wirklichkeit*), while "I myself … or my lived-experience-actuality is absolute actuality, given through an unconditional, simply unannullable positing" (Hua III/1 98; Kersten's translation, CW II, agrees). "Presumptive" is the logician's word for what *Prätention* said in the language of legal claims, and it loses the edge of the 1910 coinage, which made the thing's being something asserted against knowledge, a title that experience keeps presenting and can never make good.

## §15, Hua XIII 148: the bracket and the appropriation of experience

Reads [§15.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-phenomenological-attitude-delimitation-of-the-phenomenological-beholding-or-perception-of-the-pure-lived-experience-over-against-the-inner-perception-of-the-psychic-lived-experience), first paragraph under § 15, with its source in §14.7.

The phenomenological attitude takes experience over by declining to take it up. Every empirical act is put "as it were in brackets": it is still there, it becomes an object, but what it offers as being is "in no way" accepted. Husserl names the result with a word of possession: "so we appropriate all experience". The appropriation is the reverse of the refusal on which it rests. One paragraph earlier, he said of the empirical apprehension that he makes it an object "but I do not now make it my own". The phenomenologist owns every experience as his object because he disowns each experience's positing. The play on *zu eigen machen* and *sich zueignen* is the formula of the epoché in the course, and the commentary, which renders the two halves in different blocks, does not bring them together.

> In this way we can now proceed with regard to all lived-experiences. We can give ourselves quite generally a new kind of attitude, which switches off every kind of empirical, transcendent attitude. So from now on we accept no single object posited in empirical attitude as actuality, we let ourselves be given no single one that is to be given in empirical attitude. We now no longer "perform" any empirical attitude, no natural, naive positing of things, of nature in the widest sense; every empirical act that may so to speak press itself upon us, or that we have momentarily performed, we set as it were in brackets; what it offers us as being we accept in no way. Instead of living in its performance, and instead of naively holding fast, after its performance, its positing with its sense, we look at it itself, make it itself, and what it may offer us in itself, into an object, into an object that is no nature and contains nothing of nature-positing any more. So we appropriate all experience to ourselves. Not that we make experience and, living in experience, pass experiential judgments, pursue experiential theories and experiential sciences; rather we take every experiential act, every experiential judgment, every complete or incomplete experiential cognition into our realm as pure existence that it itself is. Wholly omitted, by contrast, is all co-positing of that existence which it, as experience, pretends of itself to posit.

German:

> In dieser Art können wir nun hinsichtlich aller Erlebnisse verfahren. Wir können uns überhaupt eine neuartige Einstellung geben, welche jederlei empirische, transzendente Einstellung ausschaltet. Also wir nehmen von nun an kein einziges in empirischer Einstellung gesetztes Objekt als Wirklichkeit hin, kein einziges in empirischer Einstellung zu gebendes lassen wir uns geben. Wir „vollziehen” nunmehr keine empirische Einstellung mehr, kein natürliches, naives Setzen von Dingen, von Natur im weitesten Sinn; jeden empirischen Akt, der sozusagen sich herandrängen mag oder den wir momentan vollzogen haben, setzen wir gleichsam in Klammern, wir nehmen, was er uns als Sein bietet, in keiner Weise hin. Statt in seinem Vollzug zu leben und statt nach seinem Vollzug seine Setzung mit ihrem Sinn naiv festzuhalten, blicken wir auf ihn selbst hin, machen ihn selbst und was er uns in sich bieten mag zum Objekt, zu einem Objekt, das keine Natur ist und nichts von Natursetzung mehr enthält. So eignen wir uns alle Erfahrung zu. Nicht, dass wir Erfahrung machen und in der Erfahrung lebend Erfahrungsurteile fällen, Erfahrungstheorien, Erfahrungswissenschaften betreiben, vielmehr jeden Erfahrungsakt, jedes Erfahrungsurteil, jede vollkommene oder unvollkommene Erfahrungserkenntnis nehmen wir in unser Reich auf als reines Dasein, das es selbst ist. Völlig unterbleibt dagegen alle Mitsetzung desjenigen Daseins, das es als Erfahrung von sich aus zu setzen prätendiert.

*Note on the text.* The file has no page marker for Hua XIII 148 (it moves from 147 to 149), so the page is inferred. The sentence of § 14.7 quoted below is broken in the file by a false paragraph break after "eine Auffassung." (an apprehension.), and resumes "und Setzung" (and positing) on a new line; the reading joins the two halves, which read as one sentence.

The paragraph generalizes the result of § 14 and borrows its decisive phrase from there. In § 14.7 Husserl had grasped a feeling "in and for itself", and found one with it an apprehension that relates it to "the natural object man". He grasps and posits that apprehension too, "in a new act", and then glosses the phrase: "'In itself', that means, however: I make the empirical apprehension into an object in and for itself, but I do not now make it my own (*aber ich mache sie mir jetzt nicht zu eigen*). That is to say, that I now refrain from further positing what it posited, or from making any use of what it posited as actuality" (Hua XIII 147). Making something one's own (*sich zu eigen machen*) is to adopt it: to take over its claim and stand behind it. Here the apprehension is made an object and refused adoption. § 15 repeats the structure for every empirical act and closes it with the counterword: "So we appropriate all experience" (*So eignen wir uns alle Erfahrung zu*). The verb is from the same root. What was not made one's own in § 14 is appropriated in § 15, and the two are the same operation. The appropriation is of the act as an object; the refusal is of the act's positing. "So" (*So*) says that the first follows from the second.

The bracket enters under a double hedge, "we set as it were in brackets" (*setzen wir gleichsam in Klammern*), and it does not come alone. It is one of four formulas for one refusal, and each says something the others do not. The first is accounting: we "accept" (*nehmen … hin*) no posited object "as actuality", and we "let ourselves be given" nothing. The second is performance: we no longer "perform" (*„vollziehen”*, in quotation marks) any empirical attitude. The third is the bracket, which keeps the act present and marks it as not in force. The fourth is the gaze: "instead of living in its performance … we look at it itself". The bracket, which became the lasting name, is the only one that says the bracketed remains in the text. The relative clause about the act that "may so to speak press itself upon us" (*sich herandrängen mag*) concedes that the natural positing goes on offering itself. The refusal is not done once; it has to be kept up against an attitude that returns of itself.

The second half of the paragraph says what the appropriation is not. It is not that "we make experience and, living in experience, pass experiential judgments". Whoever does that has adopted experience in the sense § 14 refused. The phenomenologist takes "every experiential act … every complete or incomplete experiential cognition into our realm as pure existence that it itself is" (*als reines Dasein, das es selbst ist*). The word for the act's being is *Dasein*, existence, the same word the course uses for the being of things. The reduced act is posited as existing; it is not merely considered. That is the premise of the course's experiencing phenomenology (row 1). The last sentence ties the paragraph to § 14 by its verb: "Wholly omitted is all co-positing of that existence which it, as experience, pretends of itself to posit" (*zu setzen prätendiert*). The "existence-pretension" (*Daseinsprätention*) of the thing in § 14 (row 10) becomes the pretension of the act, and the omission of "co-positing" (*Mitsetzung*) is exact: we posit the act, and we do not posit along with it what it posits.

The paragraph sets the terms on which the whole course runs. The next paragraph gives the attitude its name, "phenomenological attitude", and its giving act a name, "phenomenological beholding or seeing" (*Schauung oder Erschauung*), against "experience", the title for every giving consciousness of the natural attitude (Hua XIII 149). The bracket then extends to one's own empirical I, which is what separates phenomenological perception from "Lockean reflection". Appropriation returns where the course tests how far the reduction reaches. § 27 claims that "we can appropriate to ourselves the entire domain of the empirical, I mean experiential positing of every kind, in the same sense by phenomenological reduction, admittedly with the same result" (*uns zueignen können, freilich mit demselben Resultat*, Hua XIII 164), and the section's heading speaks of an appropriation that is "phenomenological, but not absolute". The "same result" is the one §§ 24–26 had reached for retention and recollection: the appropriated experience is posited, and it can deceive. The word that began as the reverse of a refusal ends as the name for the course's whole extension of phenomenological experience over the empirical, and with it the risk returns that the refusal was meant to exclude.

*Ideas* I keeps the bracket and drops the appropriation. § 31 describes the modification of the thesis: "while it remains in itself what it is, we put it as it were 'out of action', we 'switch it off', we 'bracket it'. It is still there, like the bracketed in the bracket", and "we make 'no use' of it" (*wir machen von ihr aber „keinen Gebrauch“*, Hua III/1 63; Kersten's translation, CW II, agrees). The three verbs, the "as it were", the persistence of what is bracketed and the formula of no use all come from §§ 13–15 of the 1910/11 course. What is missing is the possession. *Ideas* I says what the epoché does to the thesis. It does not say, as the lecture did, that by refusing every experience's claim the phenomenologist makes every experience his own.

## §16, Hua XIII 150–51: Descartes's reduction and the indubitability that is "not the main thing"

Reads [§16.1–2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#descartes-fundamental-consideration-and-the-phenomenological-reduction), first and second paragraphs under § 16.

Husserl credits Descartes with the method and takes away its purpose. Descartes's fundamental consideration was a phenomenological reduction, "staged" and then given up, and its correlate, the *cogitatio*, is what the reduction yields. But Descartes wanted from it an indubitable foundation for absolute science, and that, Husserl says, "is for us not the main thing". The reduction of the course is the Cartesian one with its aim changed: not certainty, but a science within the phenomenological attitude. The separation matters later, because the course will admit into phenomenological experience givens that can be doubted.

> The first philosopher who carried out a phenomenological reduction, admittedly carried it out in order to give it up again at once, was Descartes. It is a most remarkable fact that the fundamental consideration which opens the whole course of development of modern philosophy was nothing other than the staging of a phenomenological reduction. It is remarkable because herein lies in fact the beginning of all genuine scientific philosophy and the springing-point of all genuine philosophical problems. The correlate of phenomenological perception is the *cogitatio* in the Cartesian sense; we can say for it: the pure, in contrast to the empirical, consciousness. A *cogitatio*, a consciousness, is every kind of sensing, presenting, perceiving, remembering, expecting, every kind of judging, inferring, every kind of feeling, desiring, willing and so on. These are well-known matters, all that which each person sees immediately "in himself", as he says, and so that he cannot doubt it at all. However, all that which the empirical psychologist claims as psychic lived-experience of the respective human or animal I-consciousness becomes *cogitatio* in the absolute sense, in the sense of pure phenomenological givenness, only through phenomenological reduction, and only then is the givenness pure and absolute also in the sense that the simple positing as a This, as being, leaves open possible doubt in no direction, indeed so that doubt here would lose every sense. Just that was what mattered to Descartes, while for us it is not the main thing. The intention toward a reform of all sciences, which would make possible their construction as absolutely valid science and would exclude every deceptive appearance, every establishment as pseudo-sciences, is significant enough, after all. In the last ground philosophy is surely nothing other than intention toward absolute cognition. But whether and how cognition in the phenomenological attitude can serve to ground absolute cognition at all, absolute cognition in the sphere of experience too, that cannot be settled, indeed cannot even be understood, from the outset. And Descartes's procedure itself failed because, without an investigation of the sense of absolute science and without establishing systematic phenomenology, of whose existence he had no inkling, he believed he could venture on the grounding of absolute science.

German:

> Der erste Philosoph, der eine phänomenologische Reduktion vollzogen hat, freilich sie vollzogen hat, um sie alsbald wieder preiszugeben, war Descartes. Es ist eine höchst merkwürdige Tatsache, dass die fundamentale Betrachtung, welche den ganzen Entwicklungsgang der neueren Philosophie eröffnet, nichts anderes als Inszenierung einer phänomenologischen Reduktion war. Es ist merkwürdig, weil hierin in der Tat der Anfang aller echten wissenschaftlichen Philosophie und der Springpunkt aller echten philosophischen Probleme ist. Das Korrelat der phänomenologischen Wahrnehmung ist die *cogitatio* im Cartesianischen Sinn, wir können dafür sagen: das reine, im Gegensatz zum empirischen Bewusstsein. Eine *cogitatio*, ein Bewusstsein, ist jederlei Empfinden, Vorstellen, Wahrnehmen, Sich-erinnern, Erwarten, jederlei Urteilen, Schliessen, jederlei Fühlen, Begehren, Wollen usf. Es sind allbekannte Sachen, all das, was jeder „in sich“, wie er sagt, unmittelbar sieht, und so, dass er daran gar nicht zweifeln kann. Indessen, all das, was der empirische Psychologe als psychisches Erlebnis des jeweiligen menschlichen oder tierischen Ichbewusstseins in Anspruch nimmt, wird erst zur *cogitatio* im absoluten Sinn, im Sinn reiner phänomenologischer Gegebenheit, durch phänomenologische Reduktion, und erst dann ist die Gegebenheit auch in dem Sinn rein und absolut, dass die schlichte Setzung als ein Dies, als sein, nach keiner Richtung möglichen Zweifel offenlässt, ja so, dass Zweifel hierbei jeden Sinn verlöre. Gerade darauf kam es Descartes an, während es für uns nicht die Hauptsache ist. Im letzten Grund ist sicherlich Philosophie nichts anderes als Intention auf absolute Erkenntnis. Aber ob und wie Erkenntnis in phänomenologischer Einstellung dazu dienen kann, absolute Erkenntnis überhaupt, auch absolute Erkenntnis in der Erfahrungssphäre zu begründen, das kann nicht von vornherein ausgemacht, ja auch nur verstanden werden. Und Descartes’ Vorgehen selbst scheiterte, weil er ohne eine Untersuchung des Sinnes absoluter Wissenschaft und ohne Etablierung systematischer Phänomenologie, von deren Existenz er keine Ahnung hatte, an Begründung absoluter Wissenschaft sich glaubte wagen zu dürfen.

*Note on the text.* The page changes from Hua XIII 150 to 151 inside "dazu | dienen". The file reads "als ein Dies, als sein" (as a This, as being), with lower-case *sein*; it is quoted as it stands. The italics on *cogitatio* are the file's Latin italics.

The credit is given in two verbs that undercut each other. Descartes "carried out" (*vollzogen*) a phenomenological reduction, "admittedly carried it out in order to give it up again at once" (*um sie alsbald wieder preiszugeben*). The final clause makes the surrender the purpose of the performance: the reduction was done in order to be left. The next sentence names it "the staging (*Inszenierung*) of a phenomenological reduction". A staging is a performance with a script, done for an end beyond itself. Descartes's doubt, on this reading, put the reduction on stage for the sake of the certainty it would deliver, and once the certainty was had the reduction had done its part. The praise that follows is as large as the qualification is sharp: "the beginning of all genuine scientific philosophy and the springing-point of all genuine philosophical problems". The method is what Husserl keeps; the drama is what he rejects.

The *cogitatio* changes sense between two sentences. First it is the familiar list, all that "each person sees immediately 'in himself', as he says, and so that he cannot doubt it at all". The quotation marks around "in himself" (*„in sich“*) and the tag "as he says" (*wie er sagt*) give this as the self-description of the natural attitude, the "inner perception" § 15 has just set apart from phenomenological perception. "However" (*Indessen*) then reserves the name. What the empirical psychologist claims as a psychic experience "becomes *cogitatio* in the absolute sense … only through phenomenological reduction" (*wird erst … durch phänomenologische Reduktion*). The everyday indubitability of what I see "in myself" is not the absoluteness Husserl means. Only after the reduction is the givenness absolute "also in the sense" that the simple positing of it "as a This" leaves doubt open in no direction, "indeed so that doubt here would lose every sense". The "also" (*auch*) is the hinge of the paragraph. Indubitability is one consequence of the purity the reduction gives, not its definition.

That is why the next sentence can grant Descartes his aim and set it aside: "Just that was what mattered to Descartes, while for us it is not the main thing" (*während es für uns nicht die Hauptsache ist*). The course does not deny that the reduced *cogitatio* is indubitable. It denies that indubitability is the point. The paragraph then concedes the Cartesian ideal in full, "in the last ground philosophy is surely nothing other than intention toward absolute cognition", and withholds the inference Descartes drew. "Whether and how" cognition in the phenomenological attitude can ground absolute cognition, "absolute cognition in the sphere of experience too", "cannot be settled, indeed cannot even be understood, from the outset". The verdict on Descartes is causal and sweeping: his procedure "failed because" he ventured on absolute science without investigating its sense and "without establishing systematic phenomenology, of whose existence he had no inkling". The second reason makes the failure a lack of what the lecture is about to supply. It explains Descartes by the course, and it is the weakest step in the passage.

The restriction is stated in the paragraph that follows: "We are interested here not in absolute universal science, but in science within the phenomenological attitude", and whether such science may be called "absolute" (in quotation marks) is left "to other considerations" (Hua XIII 151). The course needs this restriction because of where it is going. If indubitability were the main thing, phenomenological experience would have to stop at the reduced perception of the present *cogitatio*. The course goes further. § 24 (Hua XIII 161) still argues in the Cartesian manner, that doubt presupposes the givenness of the meaning doubted, so that for the reduced perception "every doubt loses its sense" (row 18), the phrase of § 16. But § 25 admits retention as a "transcendence" within the phenomenological attitude (row 19), § 26 admits recollection with "its possibility of deception", and § 27 appropriates the whole domain of experience in a phenomenological "but not absolute" way. The sentence "for us it is not the main thing" is what makes room for a phenomenological experience that can err. § 29 (Hua XIII 169) draws the consequence for natural science too: no one demands of the natural scientist that his modes of givenness be absolute, since that would be "foolish, indeed countersensical", and since strict science does not need it.

The *Cartesian Meditations* repeat the double verdict and move its centre. § 10, "Descartes's failure to make the transcendental turn", grants that Descartes "stands before the greatest of all discoveries, has in a certain way already made it, and yet does not grasp its proper sense" (Hua I 64). The fault is now named differently: the "inconspicuous but fateful turn" that makes the *ego* into "*substantia cogitans*, into the separated human *mens sive animus*", and the prejudice that the *ego cogito* is "an apodictic axiom" from which a deductive science of the world could be built (Hua I 63). In 1929 Descartes failed by keeping a piece of the world, the human mind, inside the reduction; in 1910/11 he failed by wanting certainty from it. The two charges meet at one point, the axiom of indubitability, which the lecture had already declared "not the main thing".

## §17, Hua XIII 152–53: independence of judging, not of truths

Reads [§17.4–5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#independence-of-the-phenomenological-judgment-from-the-natural-judgment), fourth and fifth paragraphs under § 17, and the later note attached to them.

The phenomenological judgment is independent of the natural judgment as an act, not as a truth. The 1910 lecture claimed both: the propositions the phenomenologist does not use are "without influence" on his findings, and so his findings remain "in their truth" untouched by whether those propositions hold. Husserl's later note, probably of 1921, keeps the first claim and withdraws the second. Truths in the two spheres are bound by "essence-correlations", so that a thing posited as existing prescribes a rule to every possible experiencing consciousness. What remains independent is the judging, "the going-out after truth". The note marks the layer on which the course's independence thesis must be read, and it moves the course, in passing, from an experiencing to an eidetic phenomenology.

> It is evident that judgments of which I make no use, or better, that propositions which in principle I take as premises for no findings within a scientific circuit, are without influence for such findings. That, then, is absolutely certain: that my findings remain untouched in their truth by whether those judgments hold or do not hold.

German:

> Es ist evident, dass Urteile, von denen ich keinen Gebrauch mache, oder besser gesprochen, dass Sätze, die ich prinzipiell für keine Feststellungen innerhalb eines wissenschaftlichen Umkreises als Prämissen nehme, für solche Feststellungen ohne Einfluss sind. Das also ist absolut gewiss, dass meine Feststellungen in ihrer Wahrheit davon unberührt bleiben, ob jene Urteile gelten oder nicht gelten.

Husserl's note to these two sentences, which the editor calls a "later critical supplement, probably from 1921" (*Spätere kritische Ergänzung, wohl aus 1921*), begins:

> One cannot say it so. One could indeed object: If in a geometrical domain I make use only of certain propositions and do not judge about other domains of propositions, it is not thereby said that the ones and the other propositions are, as truths (actual or hypothetical), independent of one another. So it is first the question in what way phenomenological and ontological judging are independent, or what the independence rightfully means and can mean. Certain dependencies do exist. If I posit that a thing exists, or in a general way posit the possible existence of a thing as such, then a rule is thereby prescribed for every I for its possible experiencing consciousness.

German:

> So kann man nicht sagen Man konnte ja einwenden Wenn ich in einem geometrischen Gebiet nur von gewissen Sätzen Gebrauch mache und über andere Satzgebiete nicht urteile, so ist damit nicht gesagt, dass die einen und anderen Sätze als Wahrheiten (wirkliche oder hypothetische) voneinander unabhängig sind. Es ist also erst die Frage, inwiefern phänomenologisches und ontologisches Urteilen unabhängig sind bzw. was die Unabhängigkeit rechtmässig bedeutet und bedeuten kann. Gewisse Abhängigkeiten bestehen ja. Setze ich an, dass ein Ding existiert, oder in allgemeiner Weise, setze ich die mögliche Existenz eines Dinges überhaupt an, so ist damit für jedes Ich eine Regel für sein mögliches erfahrendes Bewusstsein vorgezeichnet.

*Note on the text.* The two body sentences run across the page break from Hua XIII 152 to 153 ("ohne Einfluss | sind"). The note is numbered "[^1]" in the file, one of the reused numbers inside Nr. 6; it is the note attached at "nicht gelten" on Hua XIII 153. Its first two sentences lack their punctuation in the file ("So kann man nicht sagen Man konnte ja einwenden Wenn"), and "konnte" may stand for *könnte* (could); the quotation keeps the file's text and the rendering supplies the breaks. The angle-bracketed words later in the note are the editor's supplements.

The body argues from use to truth in one step, and the particles carry the step. The first sentence is a claim about inference. It corrects itself from "judgments" to "propositions" (*oder besser gesprochen … Sätze*), so that what is excluded is a premise, not a mental act: propositions that are taken as premises for no finding have no influence on the findings. That much is a truism of logic. The second sentence draws from it, with "then" (*also*), a claim about truth: "my findings remain untouched *in their truth*" (*in ihrer Wahrheit*). And it raises the certainty to the top: "absolutely certain" (*absolut gewiss*). The next sentences apply the claim to the phenomenologist, whose statements stay untouched whether he as a natural man trusts natural science again or as "a dogged skeptic" rejects its judgments. For phenomenology these are "private affairs that do not concern it" (Hua XIII 154). The slide from "without influence" to "untouched in their truth" is exactly where the note intervenes.

The note answers with a counter-case from geometry and a distinction of senses. A geometer who uses only some theorems does not thereby make the theorems he leaves aside independent "as truths" of those he uses. Non-use is a fact about the judge, not about the propositions. "So it is first the question in what way phenomenological and ontological judging are independent." The word that changes is the noun: the body spoke of findings and judgments, the note speaks of "judging" (*Urteilen*), the activity. The note then states the dependency that the body missed. Positing a thing, or the possible existence of a thing as such, "prescribes a rule for every I for its possible experiencing consciousness". This is the index doctrine of § 35 (row 24) in its later form: the thing and the regulation of consciousness correspond, so that a truth about one has its correlate in a truth about the other. The note's conclusion is exact: "But independence of the truths themselves does not exist, by virtue of the connecting essence-correlations" (*vermöge der verbindenden Wesenskorrelationen*). And then: "Independence of the judging doing, of the going-out after truth, independence of cognition itself, does not mean independence of the judgments in question themselves as truths or as pretended, hypothetical truths." The independence that survives belongs to the act of cognition. In any closed domain, arithmetic for example, "I am independent in cognition" wherever I reach a proof.

The note also changes what phenomenology is. Its example of independent phenomenological judgment is a construction: I can treat a thing I now experience "purely as intentional object", construct a perfectly concordant connection of experience for it, and describe that system "in evident truth" without judging about the thing; and "likewise I can eidetically" consider "a thing as such" as a pure intentional possibility. Its conclusion names the two realms that are independent of the existence of an objective world: "the realm of ontological possibilities" and "that of pure eidetic phenomenology". The 1910 course had set out to test a phenomenology "that is not a doctrine of essence" (row 1). The note, written about ten years later, defends the independence thesis only for eidetic phenomenology, and that is a second correction folded into the first. Its last sentences state the residue in the formula of *Ideas* I: pure consciousness remains, "even if modified", "as my *ego cogito*, even if the world did not exist", and "I cannot strike out my *ego*".

The thesis the note qualifies is the one the next chapter needs. § 18 asks whether phenomenological research is solipsistic (row 14), § 19 whether the reduction can switch off the I, and § 20 whether a phenomenological science is possible and can ground natural science. Each objection presupposes the independence of § 17, and the answers lean on it. § 42, at the course's end, turns the correlation the note names into a theme: the knowledge of the consciousness-connections in which nature "expresses itself" is "in a certain way equivalent to the knowledge of nature and conversely" (Hua XIII 194). The 1910 course thus reaches, in its last section, the dependency that its § 17 had denied, and the note of about 1921 brings the denial into line with the ending.

Husserl returned to the note once more. Beilage XXIII, "probably from 1924 or somewhat later", is headed as a "reworking of the note" and begins: "The original note … as well as the passage in the text of the lectures itself still contains unclarities" (*enthält noch Unklarheiten*, Hua XIII 200). It restates the method in the terms of the note, "phenomenologically judging I make 'no use' of the objectivities", and adds that the phenomenologist judges "simply" (*schlechthin*) about consciousness, so that judging simply means judging thematically. Three layers lie on this page of the course: the 1910 claim of untouched truth, the 1921 distinction between independence of judging and of truths, and the 1924 recasting of that distinction as a difference of theme. The close reading of § 17 has to cite all three, and the body's "absolutely certain" holds only in the narrowest of them.

## §18, Hua XIII 154: two immanences and the reversed *solus ipse*

Reads [§18.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-objection-of-solipsism), the only paragraph under § 18.

The charge of solipsism rests on confusing two immanences. Psychological immanence is the inner life of a human being in the world, my states and acts as mine; phenomenological immanence is what remains when every positing of nature, my own person included, is switched off. The solipsist keeps the first and switches off the rest of the world. The phenomenologist switches off the first along with the world. So the reduction is not a narrowed solipsism but its reversal, and the first division of immanence in the course is drawn to say so. The question that the reversal leaves, whether after it there are other phenomenological I's, is the one the course answers in § 39.

> Is phenomenological research, then, solipsistic research? Does it restrict research to the individual I, and more closely to the domain of its individual psychic phenomena? Nothing less than that. *Solus ipse*: that would mean, I alone am, or, I switch off all the rest of the world, only not myself and my psychic states and acts. On the contrary, however, as phenomenologist I switch off myself just as much as all the world, and no less my psychic states and acts, which as mine are precisely nature. One can say that the countersensical theory of knowledge of solipsism grows out of this: that one, ignorant of the radical principle of the phenomenological reduction, but with the same aim of switching off transcendence, confuses psychological and psychologistic immanence with the genuine phenomenological one. One can also say that a misunderstanding of the proper sense of transcendence and of its switching-off leads to confusing psychological immanence (that is precisely solipsistic immanence) and phenomenological immanence. But let us leave epistemological matters aside here.

German:

> Ist also phänomenologische Forschung solipsistische Forschung? Schränkt sie die Forschung auf das individuelle Ich und näher auf das Gebiet seiner individuellen psychischen Phänomene ein? Nichts weniger als das. *Solus ipse* — das hiesse, ich allein bin, oder, ich schalte alle übrige Welt aus, nur nicht mich selbst und meine psychischen Zustände und Akte. Im Gegenteil schalte ich aber als Phänomenologe mich selbst genauso aus wie alle Welt, und nicht minder meine psychischen Zustände und Akte, die als meine eben Natur sind. Man kann sagen, dass die widersinnige Erkenntnistheorie des Solipsismus daraus erwächst, dass man unkundig des radikalen Prinzips der phänomenologischen Reduktion, aber in gleichem Absehen auf Ausschaltung der Transzendenz, die psychologische und psychologistische Immanenz mit der echten phänomenologischen verwechselt. Man kann auch sagen, dass ein Missverständnis des eigentlichen Sinnes der Transzendenz und ihrer Ausschaltung dahin führe, psychologische Immanenz (das ist eben solipsistische) und phänomenologische zu verwechseln. Doch lassen wir hier Erkenntnistheoretisches beiseite.

*Note on the text.* The section heading stands in angle brackets in the file (*<§ 18. Der Einwand des Solipsismus>*), so it is the editor's, as is the heading of the Third Chapter above it. The footnote markers 50 and 51 are left out of the quotation; the notes record changes of 1924 or later. Note 49, which inserts "(world)", belongs to the last sentence of § 17 ("the existence of the phenomenologist himself … as member of nature"), not to § 18. The file prints notes 49–51 twice.

The passage turns the objection's formula against it. *Solus ipse* is unfolded into two paraphrases, "I alone am" and "I switch off all the rest of the world, only not myself and my psychic states and acts". The second puts solipsism into the vocabulary of the reduction, so that solipsism becomes a reduction with one exception. "On the contrary" (*Im Gegenteil*) then removes the exception: "as phenomenologist I switch off myself just as much as all the world". The relative clause gives the reason in two words, "which *as mine* are precisely nature" (*die als meine eben Natur sind*). Possession is what makes them nature. My states are mine as states of this human being, ordered by his body into objective time; that relation is the empirical apprehension § 14 refused to make its own (row 11). In 1924 or later Husserl inserted after "as mine" the words "of this human person" (*dieser Menschenperson*, note 50), which spells out what "mine" had implied.

The diagnosis grants the solipsist the right aim. He acts "with the same aim of switching off transcendence" (*in gleichem Absehen auf Ausschaltung der Transzendenz*), and fails by ignorance of "the radical principle". The error is a confusion of immanences, and the adjectives mark two degrees of it: "psychological and psychologistic immanence". The first is the immanence of the psychologist's object, the inner life of a person; the second is the theory that makes that inner life the ground of knowledge. Husserl's later revision (note 51) moves the second adjective from the immanence to the confusing: the solipsist "confuses psychological immanence with the genuine phenomenological one psychologistically", "with the same aim of switching off all worldly transcendence". Psychologism is now the manner of the mistake, and "worldly" (*weltlich*) specifies the transcendence. Both changes sharpen the 1910 contrast without altering it. The second sentence restates the diagnosis from the side of transcendence: a misunderstanding of its "proper sense" leads to the confusion. The course has not yet given that sense, and the parenthesis "(that is precisely solipsistic)" equates psychological immanence with the solipsist's in a gloss. "But let us leave epistemological matters aside" closes the paragraph on a promise.

The confusion is named here, but the distinction was drawn in § 15. There Husserl had warned against identifying phenomenological perception with "Lockean reflection" or inner perception, which stays empirical as long as "not only every foreign but also one's own empirical I" has not been switched off; only then, in the "immanent description" of the psychic lived-experience, does one gain "the pure lived-experience as object of phenomenological perception" (Hua XIII 149–50). § 18 gives the two sides of that warning their names and their quarrel. § 30 then supplies what § 18 left over, the "proper sense" of transcendence. It sets out three concepts of immanence and transcendence (Hua XIII 170–71), and the third, the division of individual objects into those given in absolute self-presence and those given only through appearances, defines "the field of phenomenology" as the immanent (row 21). Psychological immanence turns out to be a transcendence in that third sense, since the person whose states it contains is given through a body that appears.

The objection returns as a question about others. Switching myself off does not by itself yield other I's. § 36 asks whether the reduction restricts research to a single consciousness, and § 38 begins: "Do we ever come to another phenomenological I? … On the way so far, naturally not" (Hua XIII 187). § 39 answers through the double reduction of empathy, and with a law: "from the empathized stream no channel leads into the stream to which the empathizing itself belongs" (Hua XIII 189; row 29). The reply to solipsism in 1910/11 thus has two steps. § 18 removes the solipsist's exception by bracketing the empirical self. §§ 38–39 then show that the reduced field contains more than one stream. The first step is a correction of a confusion; the second is the course's doctrine of monads.

The *Cartesian Meditations* raise the objection at the level the first step does not reach. § 42 asks: "If I, the meditating I, reduce myself through the phenomenological *epoché* to my absolute transcendental *ego*, have I not become the *solus ipse*?", and whether phenomenology must then be branded "transcendental solipsism" (Hua I 121). The 1910 reply, that the phenomenologist switches off himself too, does not answer this form of the objection, because what remains after the switching-off is still an ego. The lecture's distinction of immanences disposes of psychological solipsism. The solipsism of the reduced ego is left to the second step, and in 1929 Husserl begins the Fifth Meditation from it.

## §21, Hua XIII 156–57: "empirical" phenomenology and the deferred apodictic question

Reads [§21.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-motivationlessness-of-the-phenomenological-reduction), the only paragraph under § 21, and Husserl's note to its first sentence.

Husserl's note to § 21 calls the course's phenomenology an "empirical science on the ground of the phenomenological reduction". In the note the adjective has changed sides. In the body of §§ 20–21, "empirical" names what the reduction switches off: empirical positing, the universal science of experience, nature. In the note it names what the reduced field allows, a science that posits individual being, as against an eidetic one, and that does not make apodictic warrant its condition. The note thereby defines the science that § 21 says needs no motive to be founded. Its only test is whether, once nature is switched off, there is anything left to investigate. The note also narrows "beholding" to perception, and so fixes where the absoluteness at issue in §§ 20–24 is claimed.

> Before we ask about the grounds of doubt against the absolute character of phenomenological beholding, we would like to answer what was said hypothetically. One need not impute to phenomenology any motives at all for why it switches off experiential positing. As phenomenology it has no such motives; the phenomenologist concerned may have them, and those are private matters. Phenomenology switches off empirical positing and restricts itself to what then remains. The only question then is what there then is to investigate, whether room then remains for a science. One may not say: our only interest is nature. The natural scientist may say that, and even that is his private view. The phenomenologist's interest is precisely not nature as the existence posited in experience and in the universal experiential science. Whether and how phenomenological research can mean something, or nothing, for the knowledge of nature itself is naturally no question that precedes the establishment of phenomenology.

German:

> Ehe wir nach den Zweifelsgründen gegen den absoluten Charakter der phänomenologischen Erschauung fragen, möchten wir auf das hypothetisch Gesagte antworten. Der Phänomenologie braucht man gar keine Motive zu unterschieben, warum sie Erfahrungssetzung ausschaltet. Als Phänomenologie hat sie keine solchen Motive; diese mag der betreffende Phänomenologe haben, und das sind Privatsachen. Die Phänomenologie schaltet empirische Setzung aus und beschränkt sich auf das, was dann übrig bleibt. Die einzige Frage ist dann die, was es dann zu erforschen gibt, ob dann Raum bleibt für eine Wissenschaft. Man darf nicht sagen, unser einziges Interesse ist Natur. Das mag der Naturforscher sagen, und selbst das ist seine Privatsicht. Des Phänomenologen Interesse ist eben nicht Natur als das in der Erfahrung und in der universalen Erfahrungswissenschaft gesetzte Dasein. Ob und wie phänomenologische Forschung für Naturerkenntnis selbst etwas bedeuten oder nicht bedeuten kann, das ist natürlich keine Frage, die der Etablierung der Phänomenologie vorangeht.

Husserl's note at "beholding":

> "Beholding" is here an expression for a reduced perception. "Phenomenology" thought as empirical science on the ground of the phenomenological reduction, thus not as eidetic phenomenology, and without question of "apodictic" justification.

German:

> „Erschauung“ ist hier ein Ausdruck für eine reduzierte Wahrnehmung. „Phänomenologie“ als empirische Wissenschaft auf dem Boden der phänomenologischen Reduktion gedacht, also nicht als eidetische Phänomenologie, und ohne Frage nach „apodiktischer“ Rechtfertigung.

*Note on the text.* The footnote markers 61–65 are left out of the body. Notes 63 and 65 are dated by the editor "1924 or later": the first changes "whether room then remains for a science" to "whether a field really opens up for an independent science" (*ob dann wirklich ein Feld sich eröffnet für eine eigenständige Wissenschaft*), the second adds "and in particular for the psychological" after "knowledge of nature itself". Notes 61, 62 and 64 carry no date and no editor's sign. They are in Husserl's voice, and the file cannot say when he wrote them. The reference mark of note 61 stands before the page marker for 157, so the note belongs to Hua XIII 156 (with the file's usual margin of one page), and the rest of the paragraph to 157.

The first sentence of the note restricts a word the course had introduced broadly. In § 15, phenomenological "seeing or beholding" (*Schauung oder Erschauung*) was "the title that comprises the giving acts of the phenomenological attitude", the counterpart of "experience" as the title for every giving consciousness of the natural attitude (Hua XIII 149). That is the wide sense, which covers whatever the reduced field gives. § 20's objector takes up the word for the claim of absoluteness: phenomenological beholding "is supposed to be free of this defect. What it gives is not mere appearance, but being itself" (Hua XIII 156). The note says that "here" (*hier*) the word means "a reduced perception". The restriction matters, because the claim to absoluteness holds only of perception. The body confirms it two sections later. § 23 speaks of "the absolute givenness which we provisionally called phenomenological beholding, namely phenomenological perception", and announces that the concept will have to be extended "so that it runs parallel to empirical experience, thus becomes as it were phenomenological experience" (Hua XIII 159). The note anticipates that "provisionally" (*vorläufig*). It keeps the reader from carrying the wide title of § 15 into the doubt of § 20, where only perception can win.

The second sentence has no finite verb. "'Phenomenology' thought as empirical science" (*gedacht*) says how the course conceives its subject; it does not assert that such a science exists. The adjective carries the weight. Two pages earlier, another undated note of Husserl's fixes the terms: "Experience = objective-natural experience; empirie = objective experience" (*Erfahrung = objektiv-natürliche Erfahrung; Empirie = objektive Erfahrung*, note 56, Hua XIII 156). In § 21's body, phenomenology "switches off empirical positing", and its interest is not nature as posited "in the universal experiential science". Measured by these uses, an "empirical" phenomenology would be a contradiction. The note avoids the contradiction with "thus" (*also*): empirical, "thus not as eidetic phenomenology". The inference is a definition. "Empirical" now takes its sense from a new contrary, and the contrary is no longer the reduced but the eidetic: a science of individual being as against a science of essences. In the body the word marks the line between nature and consciousness. In the note it marks the line between fact and essence, and it falls on the other side of the first line. The later Husserl felt the strain. Where § 22 speaks of the knowledge "that switches off all empirie", a revision of 1924 or later inserts "objective" (*alle objektive Empirie*, note 67, Hua XIII 158). Once there is an empirical science of the reduced field, the reduction switches off only objective empirie. The critical supplement to § 17, probably of 1921, qualifies the noun in the same way: the opponent is "empiricism (that means here, objectivist empiricism, which acknowledges only 'positive' experiential sciences, objective ones)" (Hua XIII 153). Note 68, also undated, glosses "phenomenology" in § 22 with "always in the sense defined here" (*Immer in dem hier definierten Sinn*). Note 61 is the nearest definition it can point back to, though the file does not say so.

The third clause, "without question of 'apodictic' justification", puts its adjective in quotation marks, and the body of Nr. 6 never uses the word. The course speaks of "absolute character" and of "doubtlessness" (*Zweifellosigkeit*). In 1924 or later Husserl rewrote § 20's "absolute character" as "claim to absolute evidence" (note 57). The note thus uses the later vocabulary, and on its face it misdescribes the course, since §§ 20 and 22–24 ask precisely whether phenomenological givenness is absolute. Note 71, attached to the title of the Fourth Chapter, removes the conflict. "Here the idea of an apodictic critique of phenomenological experience according to its basic forms, perception, retention, recollection etc., first comes into appearance", and "first pure phenomenological experience must be gained at all, only then can apodictic critique be exercised" (Hua XIII 159). The file prints the second half of note 71 inside the body of § 24, after the point where the note breaks off; joined to the note's opening it reads continuously, but the join is this reading's own. "Without question", then, means "not as a condition". The science does not wait for its warrant before it begins. Beilage XXIV, which note 71 cites and which the editor dates "probably from 1924", sets the same order: the problem of the unity of phenomenological experience "really precedes the critique" (*eigentlich der Kritik vorangeht*, Hua XIII 212).

This is why the note sits on § 21 and not on § 20. § 20 raised two doubts: whether phenomenological givenness is absolute, and, even if it were, "what could that help us much?", since nature "is after all everywhere our interest" (Hua XIII 156). § 21 answers the second doubt first, by refusing its premise. Motives belong to the phenomenologist, not to phenomenology, and they are "private matters", as the natural beliefs of the phenomenologist were "private affairs that do not concern it" in § 17 (Hua XIII 154). The paragraph's thrice-repeated "then" (*dann*) sets the order: switch off, then look at what remains, then ask whether a science fits it. An empirical science in the note's sense is one that needs nothing more to begin: neither a purpose outside itself nor a proof of its warrant in advance. Note 62, undated, says it in the later word: phenomenology "can begin with the epoché and need not ask for further motives". Within Nr. 6, "epoché" occurs only there and in note 86, which the editor dates "later". The weak point is the open question. The body asks "whether room then remains for a science", and the note answers it by naming the science. The 1924 revision of the question (note 63) sharpens it to "whether a field really opens up", and does not settle it either. § 22 then argues by parity with the natural scientist, whose knowledge is valuable without being absolute, and ends with the possibility of several phenomenological disciplines, a sentence Husserl later recast as "some eidetically …, the others empirically" and marked for deletion (note 70; row 16).

The body states the note's conception once in its own words, and hedges it. § 29 concludes that nothing stands in the way of attempting "a transcendental psychology, a science of the lived-experiences in phenomenological reduction": "Why then should there not be, over against the naturalistic experiential science, a phenomenological one! So at least one would like to think at first, taking it as self-evident that to every experience an experiential science must be able to correspond" (Hua XIII 169). A later note glosses "phenomenological experience" there as "singular-individual research of consciousness within the phenomenological epoché" (note 86), which is note 61's "empirical" spelled out. The hedge, "at least … at first", is the doubt that § 41 states openly (row 32).

The *Cartesian Meditations* keep both of the note's qualifications, but as stages of method. § 13 divides the work in two. First, the realm of transcendental self-experience is to be traversed "in mere devotion to the evidence inherent in its concordant course, thus with the questions of a final critique, concerned with apodictic principles of range, set aside" (*unter Zurückstellung der Fragen einer letzten, auf apodiktische Prinzipien der Tragweite bedachten Kritik*), "similarly to the natural scientist in his devotion to the evidence of natural experience". The second stage would be the critique of transcendental experience (Hua I 68). § 34 then says that the analyses so far were meant to work "at first in the simpler garb of a merely empirical (though running only in the transcendental sphere of experience) description" (*bloß empirischen (obschon nur in der transzendentalen Erfahrungssphäre verlaufenden) Deskription*), before the method of eidetic description carries them "into a new, a principled dimension" (Hua I 103). The parenthesis does the work of the note's "on the ground of the phenomenological reduction": it keeps "empirical" from meaning "objective". In the *Meditations* the empirical and the uncritiqued are where the exposition starts. The 1910/11 course, as note 61 describes it, had tried to make that starting point a science in its own right.

## §22, Hua XIII 158: phenomenology as the title of a method, and its two recastings

Reads [§22.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#preliminary-reflection-for-the-discussion-of-the-objections-against-the-absoluteness-of-phenomenological-cognition), third paragraph under § 22, with Husserl's later revision of it (note 70).

In 1910 "phenomenology" is more the name of a method than of a discipline. The method is the reduction. The field it opens is graded by modes of givenness, some absolutely doubtless and some not, so the one method may house several disciplines. The grading runs entirely within the individual life of consciousness: the present perception against what is given less than absolutely. In 1924 or later Husserl rewrote the sentence. Method and discipline now weigh equally ("as much … as"), and the grading becomes a difference of kind, "eidetically" against "empirically". Then he marked the rewritten sentence for deletion. Three positions lie on this page: the 1910 grading by givenness, the 1924 grading by essence and fact, and the withdrawal of both.

> Perhaps matters also stand somewhat otherwise. Perhaps within the phenomenological reduction itself one must again distinguish between different modes of givenness, among them such as are absolutely doubtless and such as are not. And perhaps the title phenomenology is more the title of a method than of a discipline: perhaps there are different phenomenological disciplines, the ones related, say, to absolute givennesses, the others to "incomplete" givennesses.

German:

> Vielleicht verhält es sich auch etwas anders. Vielleicht ist innerhalb der phänomenologischen Reduktion selbst wieder zwischen verschiedenen Gegebenheitsweisen zu unterscheiden, darunter solchen, die absolut zweifellos sind, und solchen, die es nicht sind. Und vielleicht ist der Titel Phänomenologie mehr Titel einer Methode als einer Disziplin: Vielleicht ist es verschiedene phänomenologische Disziplinen, die einen etwa auf absolute Gegebenheiten bezogen, die anderen auf „unvollkommene“ Gegebenheiten.

The editor's note 70:

> The two preceding sentences were changed in 1924 or later as follows and provided with a deletion mark: "And perhaps the title phenomenology is as much the title of a method as of a discipline, perhaps there are different phenomenological disciplines, the ones, say, eidetically on absolutely evident givennesses, the others empirically on 'incomplete' givennesses".

German:

> Die beiden vorangehenden Sätze wurden 1924 oder später folgendermaßen verändert und mit einem Deleaturzeichen versehen: „Und vielleicht ist der Titel Phänomenologie ebensosehr Titel einer Methode als einer Disziplin, vielleicht gibt es verschiedene phänomenologische Disziplinen, die einen etwa eidetisch auf absolut evidente Gegebenheiten, die anderen empirisch auf „unvollkommene“ Gegebenheiten“. — Anm. d. Hrsg.

*Note on the text.* The file splits note 70 across the heading of the Fourth Chapter, after "folgendermaßen ver-"; the quotation joins the two pieces at the hyphen. The body's "Vielleicht ist es verschiedene … Disziplinen" is ungrammatical (a singular verb with a plural subject); the revision reads "gibt es" (there are), and the rendering of the 1910 text follows that sense, but the file's wording is quoted as it stands. The note does not say which "two preceding sentences" it means. Since the revised text begins with "And perhaps the title", they are most likely the two halves on either side of the colon; if they were the sentence on modes of givenness and the sentence on the title, the revision would also have dropped the first of them. The file cannot settle this.

The paragraph is a third "perhaps" laid on the two before it. § 22 has argued by parity. Natural science has its "doubtless right" without being absolute knowledge, so phenomenology need not be absolute to be valuable. And "perhaps" it is absolute after all in its givenness, and fallible only in its "scientific working-up", so that it stands "nearer to the Idea of absolute science" than any other science (Hua XIII 158). "Perhaps matters also stand somewhat otherwise" (*etwas anders*) breaks with both hypotheses at once. The parity argument treated phenomenology as one science, either absolute or not. The new hypothesis divides it. The word that does this is "again" (*wieder*). The reduction was introduced to separate a giving that is final from one that is not: experience is "a giving act, but in principle no finally giving one" (§ 20, Hua XIII 156). Now the same separation is to be made "again", inside the field that the first separation left over. The measure is still the body's word, doubtlessness (*absolut zweifellos*), and not yet evidence.

The quotation marks around "incomplete" (*„unvollkommene“*) carry the paradox. The word belongs to natural experience. § 7 called the evidence of the experience-thesis "an evidence, but, generally speaking, an incomplete one", and § 14 said that every finite series of experiences gives the thing "in in-principle incomplete appearance", one-sided and never all-sided (Hua XIII 121, 146). The reduction was meant to leave that incompleteness behind with nature. To find it again in the reduced field is to say that something like the defect of thing-experience returns there, and the quotation marks hold the word at a distance while using it. The Fourth Chapter's title states the result: "The Going-Beyond of Phenomenology beyond the Sphere of the Absolutely Given". § 23 names the incomplete modes. Interwoven with phenomenological perception are other modes of givenness "whose absolute character can no longer be vindicated in quite the same sense (namely as doubtlessness)", and the concept of beholding must be extended until it becomes "as it were phenomenological experience" (Hua XIII 159). The "disciplines" of § 22 are therefore not sciences of different objects. They are the researches the one reduced field supports at different grades of warrant, perception on one side and retention, recollection and expectation on the other.

That is why the title is "more the title of a method than of a discipline". A discipline is fixed by its domain; a method by its procedure. If the reduced field divides by grades of warrant, then what unifies phenomenology is the procedure that opens the field, and "phenomenology" names that procedure first and its results second. Husserl had said nearly as much in the 1907 lectures *The Idea of Phenomenology*: "Phenomenology: that designates a science, a connection of scientific disciplines; but phenomenology designates at the same time and above all a method and an attitude of thought" (*zugleich und vor allem eine Methode und Denkhaltung*, Hua II 23). There, however, the disciplines are those of a critique of knowledge that clarifies "the essence of knowledge", and so of essence. In 1910/11 the disciplines are distinguished by their grade of givenness within an experiencing phenomenology, the course's premise (rows 1 and 15). The formula of 1907 survives, and its content has changed.

The revision of 1924 or later changes the formula and its content in three words. "More … than" (*mehr … als*) becomes "as much … as" (*ebensosehr … als*): phenomenology is now no less a discipline than a method. "Absolute givennesses" becomes "absolutely evident givennesses", the vocabulary of evidence that the same layer of revision introduces in § 20 ("claim to absolute evidence", note 57) and in the heading of § 22 ("absoluteness of the evidence of phenomenological cognition", note 66). And two adverbs enter, "eidetically" and "empirically". With them the grading by warrant is aligned with the difference between essence and fact: the eidetic disciplines on the absolutely evident, the empirical ones on the "incomplete". The alignment does not fit the course it revises. The absolutely given of the Fourth Chapter is the perception of an individual *cogitatio* in its duration (§ 24, Hua XIII 160–61), a fact and not an essence. And the incomplete modes, recollection and expectation, give individual being too. The 1910 text drew its line inside the individual field; the revision draws it between the individual and the eidetic, and so lays the distinction of § 41 over that of § 22. § 41 asks whether what the course found "does not *au fond* concern pure essence-cognition", on the model of nature, which has "an apriori of nature and associated apriori disciplines" beside empirical natural science (Hua XIII 193). The revised sentence answers that question at the hinge of the course, before the course has asked it. The deletion mark may record that misfit, but the file does not say why Husserl struck the sentence.

The revision belongs to the same rereading as Beilage XXIV, which the editor dates "probably from 1924" and which reconstructs the leading thoughts of the Fourth and Fifth Chapters. Its first sentence makes the same doubling: through the epoché the *ego cogito* seems to open up as a "realm" of phenomenological experience "(and eidetically as a realm of phenomenological essence-intuition)", and "with it a realm of sciences" (Hua XIII 211–12). In 1924 Husserl read the course as founding two kinds of phenomenological science at once, one experiential and one eidetic. The rewritten § 22 put that reading into the text, and the deletion mark took it out again. What remains in the course is the 1910 sentence: one method, a field graded by warrant, and disciplines that differ in how far their givenness can be trusted, not in whether they deal with facts or essences.

## §23, Hua XIII 159: phenomenological experience

Reads [§23.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-problem-of-the-absolute-character-of-phenomenological-givenness), the single paragraph of § 23.

Phenomenological experience is the name § 23 gives to everything the reduced field gives, once it is granted that not all of it is given beyond doubt. The concept is built by parallel. Natural experience reaches from perception to memory and expectation; the phenomenological giving acts are to reach in the same way from the reduced perception to the presentifications of what is reduced. The name crosses a line the course had drawn. In § 15 "experience" was the title for the giving acts of the natural attitude, and "beholding" the title for those of the phenomenological attitude. § 23 gives the reduced field the natural word, hedged with an "as it were", and the sections that follow drop the hedge. With the word comes what experience has always had, the possibility of being wrong. Every mode of the extended concept stays inside the phenomenological attitude, and of absoluteness only one sense, doubtlessness, is given up.

> What sort of reservations, then, are these that would rise against the absolute character of phenomenological givenness? Let us now pursue them a little. That will already be useful, because through it we can cast some glances into the kinds of such givenness. In fact it will turn out that the phenomenological reduction leads us first to the absolute givenness which we provisionally called phenomenological beholding, to phenomenological perception precisely, whose absolute and at the same time doubtless character can indeed be vindicated. But, interwoven with it in a certain way, other modes of givenness soon emerge (and always within the phenomenological attitude) whose absolute character can no longer be vindicated entirely in the same sense (namely as doubtlessness). In this respect we shall have to extend the concept of phenomenological beholding so that it runs parallel to empirical experience, and so becomes as it were phenomenological experience: phenomenological *Vergegentwärtigung* [garbled; see the note] and presentification.

German:

> Was sind das nun für Bedenken, die sich gegen den absoluten Charakter phänomenologischer Gegebenheit erheben wollen? Gehen wir jetzt ihnen ein wenig nach. Das wird schon nützlich sein, weil wir dadurch einige Blicke in die Arten solcher Gegebenheit werfen können. In der Tat wird es sich zeigen, dass die phänomenologische Reduktion uns zuerst auf die absolute Gegebenheit führt, die wir vorläufig phänomenologische Erschauung nannten, auf eben phänomenologische Wahrnehmung, deren absoluter und dabei zweifelloser Charakter sich allerdings vertreten lässt. Aber mit ihr in gewisser Weise verflochten stellen sich alsbald andere Gegebenheitsweisen heraus (und zwar immer innerhalb der phänomenologischen Einstellung), deren absoluter Charakter sich nicht mehr durchaus in demselben Sinn vertreten lässt (nämlich als Zweifellosigkeit). Wir werden in dieser Hinsicht den Begriff der phänomenologischen Erschauung so erweitern müssen, dass er der empirischen Erfahrung parallel läuft, also gleichsam zur phänomenologischen Erfahrung wird: phänomenologische Vergegentwärtigung und Vergegenwärtigung.

*Note on the text.* The file ends the paragraph with "phänomenologische Vergegentwärtigung und Vergegenwärtigung". The first noun is not a German word, and it is quoted as the file has it. The pairing asks for a contrary of presentification (*Vergegenwärtigung*). The likeliest is presentation (*Gegenwärtigung*), which Husserl sets against presentification in an earlier text of the volume ("in original presentation, and not through presentification", Text Nr. 2, Hua XIII 25) and in the time-consciousness lectures, whose § 16 is headed "Perception as presentation in distinction from retention and recollection" (Hua X 38). But the same non-word occurs once more in the volume, in Beilage XLIV of 1914 or 1915 (Hua XIII 344), where the context fits presentification at least as well. The garble may therefore come from either word. The main commentary prints "presentation (*Gegenwärtigung*)" without marking that it has supplied the word. Nothing below depends on which word stands here.

The first sentence turns the objections of § 20 into a programme. There the objector asked whether absolute givenness is ever to be reached, and Husserl added that "even if phenomenological givenness could vindicate its absolute character, against which we shall soon express well-founded doubts", it would not help much (Hua XIII 156). § 23 picks up the verb, "vindicate" (*vertreten*), and the promise. The reservations are now Husserl's own, and they are useful "because through it we can cast some glances into the kinds of such givenness". Doubt about the reduced field becomes a means of sorting it. The defence of absoluteness and the typology of givenness are the same work.

The grammar of the negation decides what is given up. Of perception Husserl says that its character is "absolute and at the same time doubtless" (*absoluter und dabei zweifelloser*), two predicates joined, not one predicate restated. Of the other modes he says that their absolute character can "no longer be vindicated entirely in the same sense", and the parenthesis names the sense, "namely as doubtlessness". The negation falls on "entirely" (*durchaus*) and on "the same sense", not on "absolute". Read strictly, the sentence leaves open that retention or recollection keeps an absolute character in some other sense. The parenthesis cuts absoluteness loose from doubtlessness, which Descartes's demand for certainty had joined; § 16 had already said that indubitability "for us is not the main thing" (row 12). § 23 does not say what the other sense would be. § 25 repeats the hedge once, for "some things that can no longer be claimed as 'absolutely given' in the same sense as what was named before", with the quotation marks now on "absolutely given" (Hua XIII 162). When § 29 asks whether one may admit "those kinds of phenomenological experience that do not have absolute character" (Hua XIII 169), the qualification has gone: these modes are now simply not absolute.

The positive sentence carries three marks of reserve. "Extend" (*erweitern*) presents the new concept as the old one stretched, not replaced. The verb "runs parallel" (*parallel läuft*) borrows its structure from empirical experience and claims no more than a correspondence of forms. "As it were" (*gleichsam*) holds back from calling the result experience outright. Row 15 showed that "provisionally" (*vorläufig*) retrospectively narrows "beholding" to perception, against § 15's wider title. Here the narrowing pays off. Once beholding means perception alone, a second word is needed for the rest of the field, and the course takes it from the side of the line that § 15 had closed: "That the realm of phenomenological objectivities is a completely separate one over against the realm of nature, I need not say" (Hua XIII 149). The objects stay separate. The word for giving them is shared.

The next sections remove each reserve in turn. § 28 is headed "The phenomenological experience. Its 'transcendence in immanence' and the possibility of deception" (Hua XIII 165), with no "as it were". Its body still hedges once: the reduction yields intuitive connections that are beheld not as phenomenological perception beholds, "but so to speak in various other modes of phenomenological experience" (Hua XIII 166). § 29 sets the term in quotation marks and gives it a gloss that names what it reaches: "'phenomenological experience', transcendent phenomenological reflection of various kinds" (Hua XIII 167). The parallel becomes an argument. No one asks the natural scientist for absolute modes of givenness, so "phenomenological experience may often be no better, at any rate it is also no worse than empirical experience" (Hua XIII 169). § 33 ends the hedging in four words: "But experience is experience" (*Aber Erfahrung ist Erfahrung*), "it has its value as such" (Hua XIII 176). The word that § 23 borrowed "as it were" now grounds its own right, and the parallel with natural science licenses the "transcendental psychology" of § 29 (row 20).

The later layer redraws the line that § 23 left open. Where § 29 speaks of modes without "absolute character", Husserl later added "the character of bodily givenness, and givenness not through mere presentification" (note 82, Hua XIII 169). The lost absoluteness is now defined by the pair with which § 23 ends: bodily presence on one side, presentification on the other. That is not where the 1910 text draws it. In §§ 24–25 the just-past phase of the perception is "given", and a revision of 1924 or later makes it "absolutely" given (note 75, Hua XIII 162), although retention is not bodily presence of the now. And the later note on § 29's term puts it in quotation marks once more and defines it by scope, not by warrant: "'phenomenological experience' = singular-individual research of consciousness within the phenomenological epoché" (note 86, Hua XIII 169). For the later Husserl the term names the study of individual being in the reduced field, set against eidetic research, which is the reading row 16 found in the revision of § 22.

The parallel has a history on both sides of the course. In 1907 presentified lived-experiences already counted as givennesses. They can stand "'as it were' before the eyes" (*„gleichsam“ vor Augen*) without being "actual presences", and "even then they are in a certain sense givennesses", because in looking at them one can bring out their essence (Hua II 32). There presentification serves the seeing of essences, and its "as it were" is the as-if of phantasy. In § 23 presentification serves the experience of individual being, and the "as it were" qualifies the experience. The *Cartesian Meditations* repeat the list and change the parallel again. The epoché opens "a new kind of infinite sphere of being as sphere of a new kind of experience, the transcendental", with its modes "perception, retention, recollection etc.", and to each corresponds "a pure phantasy, an experience as-if with parallel modes", from which an a priori science is expected (§ 12, Hua I 66). In 1929 the parallel runs from experience to phantasy and founds an eidetic science. In 1910/11 it runs from natural to phenomenological experience and founds a science of fact. § 23 is the point where the course first calls what the reduced field gives experience, and it grants that experience its fallibility in the same sentence.

## §24, Hua XIII 160–61: doubt presupposes givenness

Reads [§24.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-absolute-givenness-of-the-phenomenologically-perceived-the-senselessness-of-a-switching-off-in-phenomenological-perception), second paragraph under § 24.

Doubt has sense only where a meaning and the being that would correspond to it can come apart. To doubt whether something really is or only seems to be is to doubt whether a seeming is valid, and that presupposes the seeming as given. Taken purely as what the pointing "This!" posits, with nothing added, the reduced perception is nothing but such a seeming: there is no further being behind it that might fail to correspond. Doubt therefore loses its sense there, and the perception is given absolutely. What § 24 claims besides is that it is given so "in its duration". The argument is built to stop a slide that the first paragraph of § 24 had set off, in which the demand for strict givenness shrank the perception to a now that is gone before it can be judged.

> Meanwhile, let us not be led astray! Directed upon the perception, we grasp it as a wholly immediate This! and as unity of a duration; and if we add nothing further and purely accept the positing that is performed with this This!, if we take this perception there purely as this enduring thing there, then every doubt loses its sense. When we doubt whether something only seems to be or really is, that plainly says: we doubt whether the "seeming to be" in question, the appearing in the manner of perception or memory or indication, the "seeming to be" in the manner of judgment and the like, is valid or not, whether perhaps nothing in truth corresponds to it. But precisely therewith this appearing, this perceiving, remembering, judging and so on is presupposed as given, as it is in fact given (perhaps we say better still: one must distinguish what appears as such, the perceived, remembered or thought etc. as such, in a word "mere meaning", and the being that corresponds to it in case of its validity). In any case, then, doubt presupposes givenness, the doubtless givenness of the meaning that is put in doubt. Thus this perception, this phenomenon of enduring empirical givenness, is given in its own-being and in its duration, and absolutely given.

German:

> Indessen, lassen wir uns nicht beirren! Auf die Wahrnehmung eingestellt erfassen wir sie als ein ganz unmittelbares Dies! und als Einheit einer Dauer, und wenn wir weiter nichts dazutun und rein die Setzung annehmen, die mit diesem Dies! vollzogen ist, wenn wir diese Wahrnehmung da rein als dieses Dauernde da nehmen, so verliert jeder Zweifel seinen Sinn. Wenn wir zweifeln, ob etwas nur zu sein scheine oder wirklich sei, so besagt das offenbar: Wir zweifeln, ob das betreffende „zu sein Scheinen“, das Erscheinen in der Art der Wahrnehmung oder Erinnerung oder Anzeige, das „zu sein Scheinen“ in der Art des Urteils u.dgl. gültig sei oder nicht, ob ihm in Wahrheit vielleicht nichts entspreche. Aber eben damit ist dieses Erscheinen, dieses Wahrnehmen, Erinnern, Urteilen usf. als gegeben vorausgesetzt, wie es in der Tat gegeben ist (vielleicht sagen wir noch besser, es sei zu unterscheiden das Erscheinende als solches, das Wahrgenommene, Erinnerte oder Gedachte etc. als solches, mit einem Worte, „blosse Meinung“, und das im Falle ihrer Gültigkeit entsprechende Sein). Jedenfalls setzt also der Zweifel Gegebenheit voraus, die zweifellose Gegebenheit der Meinung, die in Zweifel gesetzt ist. Somit ist diese Wahrnehmung, dieses Phänomen dauernder empirischer Gegebenheit in seinem Eigensein und in seiner Dauer gegeben, und absolut gegeben.

*Note on the text.* Between "als dieses Dauernde da" and "nehmen, so verliert jeder Zweifel seinen Sinn" the file prints seven lines that do not belong to the body: "Wahrnehmung (ihrer intentionalen Struktur gemäss) phänomenologische Reduktion geübt und eine phänomenologisch reine Wahrnehmung gewonnen wird …", ending with a reference to Beilage XXIV in the editor's angle brackets. They read as the continuation of note 71 on the title of the Fourth Chapter, which breaks off in the file at "wie an einer" (how on a): joined there, the note runs on without a gap ("how on a perception … phenomenological reduction is exercised"), and the body, read without the seven lines, runs on as well. The quotation leaves them out and joins the body at "da | nehmen". The join is an inference from the file and has not been checked against the edition. The page marker for Hua XIII 161 falls in the same gap, so the paragraph begins on 160 and ends on 161.

"Every doubt loses its sense" (*so verliert jeder Zweifel seinen Sinn*) answers a sentence of the paragraph before. There the regress of strict givenness reached its end: "Now, however, this whole undertaking of switching off loses its sense" (*Nun verliert aber dieses ganze Unternehmen des Ausschaltens seinen Sinn*), because the switching-off has become "so radical that we find nothing at all left to judge" (Hua XIII 160). The two sentences share their verb and object. The switching-off loses its sense by excess: it removes the ground on which it was to judge. Doubt loses its sense by deficiency: it finds nothing beyond the given to doubt. The section's heading joins the two in one word, "the senselessness of a switching-off in phenomenological perception", and § 16 had already used both the phrase and the positing: in pure givenness "the simple positing as a This, as being, leaves possible doubt open in no direction, indeed such that doubt would here lose every sense" (Hua XIII 150). § 16 stated it. § 24 says what doubt is, and shows from that why it finds no place.

The analysis turns on a phrase set in quotation marks, "seeming to be" (*„zu sein Scheinen“*), a nominalized infinitive that makes the appearing itself an object. Doubt is always doubt about its validity: whether "nothing in truth corresponds to it". The doubt has two terms, a seeming and a being that might fail to match it. The parenthesis then corrects the first term. The body had said that "this appearing, this perceiving, remembering, judging" is presupposed, which names acts. "Perhaps we say better still" moves from the act to its correlate: "what appears as such, the perceived, remembered or thought etc. as such", and Husserl gives it a name in quotation marks, "mere meaning" (*„blosse Meinung“*). The following sentence keeps the corrected term. What doubt presupposes is "the doubtless givenness of the meaning (*Meinung*) that is put in doubt". The distinction between the meant as such and the being that would answer to it "in case of its validity" enters the course here, as the anatomy of doubt.

The conditional that opens the paragraph carries the argument, and the doubt analysis only explains it. Husserl does not say that the perception is beyond doubt. He says that it is so "if we add nothing further and purely accept the positing that is performed with this This!". The exclamation marks on "This!" (*Dies!*) and the repeated "there" (*da*) in "this perception there … this enduring thing there" make the positing a pointing, not a predication. A pointing that adds no sense posits nothing that could fail to correspond, and so offers doubt no second term. The same perception, taken as perception of a thing, does offer one. The thing it gives may not be. That is why Husserl can call what is absolutely given "this phenomenon of enduring empirical givenness". The absolutely given is the giving of something dubitable. What is beyond doubt is that it gives, not what it gives.

The inference is weaker at "thus" (*Somit*). The doubt analysed is the doubt whether something seems to be or really is, which attacks the object. The doubt of the first paragraph was different. It attacked the perception's own past: "Perhaps that is deception. Memory, after all, often deceives. Perhaps I mean it was given, and it only begins in the now" (Hua XIII 160). Applied to that doubt, the analysis yields only the givenness of the meaning "it was given", and leaves open whether the past phase was. "In its duration" therefore rests on the opening clause, that the perception is grasped "as unity of a duration", and not on the analysis of doubt. Husserl's later insertion marks the pressure from the other side. To "the past of the perception is no longer given" a revision of 1924 or later adds "now" (*nicht mehr jetzt gegeben*, note 73, Hua XIII 160), so that the past is denied present givenness only, not givenness as such. And an undated later insertion restricts the senselessness of the switching-off: it holds "if the epistemological interest is the determining one" (note 74, Hua XIII 160).

§ 25 takes up the gap. "From there one will also find the right position" toward the just-past perception "co-included in the givenness of the enduring perception": the "been" is "a given, a This, but as just-been" (Hua XIII 162). The word "This" now covers a past phase. A paragraph later § 25 looks back: "we could not give in" to the inclination "to let only the now of perception count", because the now is "the eternally fleeing boundary-point" that could not even be asserted without retention. And it calls the admission of retention a "transcendence" within the phenomenological attitude (Hua XIII 162; row 19). § 24 claims the duration as part of the absolute; § 25 grants that holding it means reaching beyond the now. § 29 then states the limit of the "This!" from the side of science: restricted to perceptual givenness "we could only ever say 'this'", and no scientific knowledge would come of it (Hua XIII 167). The pointing that ends the doubt also ends what can be said.

The earlier formulations show what § 24 has to defend. In 1907 the reflectively beheld lived-experience was "given as a being, as a This-there, whose being it makes no sense at all to doubt" (*ein Dies-da, dessen Sein zu bezweifeln gar keinen Sinn gibt*), and its duration was simply asserted: "this perception is and remains, as long as it lasts, an absolute, a This-there" (Hua II 32). The course of 1910/11 keeps the "This" and the senselessness of doubt but has to win the duration back against a regress that 1907 did not face. The time-consciousness lectures of 1905, as printed in Hua X, had supplied the description on which it relies. The act "built of now-consciousness and retentional consciousness is adequate perception of the temporal object", and of each tone "only a punctual phase is in each case present as now, while the others attach as a retentional tail" (§ 16, Hua X 39). There the extension of perception over retention is a finding about how temporal objects are perceived. In § 24 it is the condition of there being anything to judge in the reduced field. The doubt analysis makes the reduced perception absolute by taking it as mere meaning. The duration it adds is what § 25 has to call a transcendence.

## §25, Hua XIII 162: retention as a "transcendence" within the phenomenological attitude

Reads [§25.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-retention-implied-in-phenomenological-perception-as-a-transcendence-within-the-phenomenological-attitude), second paragraph under § 25.

Retention is a present phase of consciousness whose object is not present. Every now of retention is retention "of a not-now". To admit retention as phenomenological being is harmless; to admit what it is retention of is to admit, inside the reduced field, an object that the act does not contain. Husserl calls this a "transcendence" and puts the word in quotation marks. The word belongs to what the reduction was defined against: in § 14 experience "by its sense posits transcendence" (Hua XIII 146), and § 18 names the aim that the reduction shares with its solipsistic misreading as the "switching-off of transcendence" (Hua XIII 154). The quotation marks hold the word at the distance of a borrowed term. Retention reaches beyond the now as experience reaches beyond its appearances, but what it reaches is not nature. The course needs the admission because without it not even the now could be asserted.

> We could not give in to the inclination to let only the now of perception (of phenomenological beholding) count. The now is the eternally fleeing boundary-point between past and future, and even that it is this could not be asserted if we wanted to switch off retention. Therewith we have admitted a "transcendence" within the phenomenological attitude, insofar as we admit not only retention itself as phenomenological being, but also that of which it is retention. Every now of retention is retention of a not-now, of a just-having-been, and this been is, we said, given. We could easily make it clear to ourselves that to mistrust such givenness would be as much as to throw oneself into the arms of absolute skepticism; that such retentional evidence is presupposed in empirical perception too; and that therefore at any rate the natural scientist who builds on empirical perception, and with him every philosopher who trusts natural knowledge, may not suddenly play the hypercritic when it is a matter of phenomenology.

German:

> Der Neigung, bloss das Jetzt der Wahrnehmung (der phänomenologischen Erschauung) gelten zu lassen, konnten wir nicht nachgeben. Das Jetzt ist der ewig fliehende Grenzpunkt zwischen Vergangenheit und Zukunft, und selbst, dass er das ist, dürfte nicht behauptet werden, wenn wir die Retention ausschalten wollten. Damit haben wir eine „Transzendenz“ innerhalb der phänomenologischen Einstellung zugelassen, sofern wir nicht nur die Retention selbst als phänomenologisches Sein, sondern auch das, wovon sie Retention ist, zulassen. Jedes Jetzt der Retention ist Retention von einem Nicht-Jetzt, von einem Eben-Gewesenen, und dieses Gewesene sei, sagten wir, gegeben. Wir könnten uns leicht deutlich machen, dass solcher Gegebenheit misstrauen soviel hiesse wie sich dem absoluten Skeptizismus in die Arme werfen; dass solche retentionale Evidenz auch in der empirischen Wahrnehmung vorausgesetzt ist und dass somit jedenfalls der Naturforscher, der auf empirische Wahrnehmung baut, und damit auch jeder der Naturerkenntnis vertrauende Philosoph nicht plötzlich den Hyperkritischen spielen darf, wenn es sich um Phänomenologie handelt.

The argument for retention runs through the now. The inclination refused is the one § 24 had followed to its end, the demand to let "only the now" count. Husserl does not answer it by adding the past to the now. He shows that the now itself is a relational term. It is a "boundary-point between past and future", and a boundary cannot be asserted without what it bounds. "Eternally fleeing" (*ewig fliehende*) adds that the point is never there to be held. So the reduction that would keep only the now has to keep retention in order to keep the now, and the "even" (*selbst*) of "even that it is this" makes the minimal claim depend on the larger one. The verb is in the subjunctive, "could not be asserted" (*dürfte nicht behauptet werden*). The point is about what can be asserted, and the course's interest throughout is a judgment-ground, as § 25 has just said: phenomenological seeing "opens a ground on which thinking can establish itself" (Hua XIII 162).

The admission is limited by "insofar as" (*sofern*). Retention "itself as phenomenological being" is a lived-experience among others and raises no problem. The "transcendence" lies in the second object, "that of which it is retention". The next sentence gives the structure in four nouns: a now, a retention, a not-now, a just-having-been. The act is now; its object is not. That is the sense of "transcendence" that § 30 will list second among its three: what is meant without being "in the strictest sense bodily present" to the act. § 30 places on that side "all that is not present, though conscious as object", and adds: "even when a phenomenologically reduced recollection or even retention reproduces something that was beheld, what is remembered would be transcendent to the consciousness of remembering" (Hua XIII 171). In § 30's third sense, the division of individual objects into those given in absolute self-presence and those given only "through appearances", the retained is immanent and nature transcendent. Read against that list, the quotation marks in § 25 mark the second sense used where the course had so far known only the third (row 21).

The fourth sentence reports the givenness of the been instead of asserting it: "this been is, we said, given" (*dieses Gewesene sei, sagten wir, gegeben*). The subjunctive of reported speech and the "we said" cite § 25's first paragraph, which had called the "been" "a given, a This, but as just-been" (Hua XIII 162). Husserl does not repeat the claim in his own voice at the point where he names its cost. A revision of 1924 or later goes the other way and makes the first paragraph's "given" "absolutely given" (note 75). The 1910 text grants the given and calls it a transcendence; the later text makes it absolute.

The defence of the admission is announced, not carried out: "We could easily make it clear to ourselves" (*Wir könnten uns leicht deutlich machen*). The three clauses that follow are a programme. The first is a reduction to absurdity: mistrust of retention would be "absolute skepticism". The second is a premise: retentional evidence is presupposed in empirical perception. The third draws from it only an argument *ad hominem*, marked by "at any rate" (*jedenfalls*): whoever trusts natural knowledge "may not suddenly play the hypercritic". That binds the natural scientist and the philosopher who trusts him. It does not show that retentional evidence is good, only that its critics already rely on it. The course argues by parity again in § 29, where no one demands absolute modes of givenness of the natural scientist (Hua XIII 169), and the argument has the same limit there. It shifts the burden; it does not vindicate. Note 71, on the title of the Fourth Chapter, says what the vindication would need: "an apodictic critique of phenomenological experience according to its basic forms: perception, retention, recollection etc." (Hua XIII 159), which the course postpones (row 15).

The word then spreads. § 26 finds in phenomenological recollection "new 'transcendences' in phenomenological immanence" and asks whether they are justified "as absolute doubtlessnesses". "By no means!", everyone will object: "Memory deceives" (Hua XIII 163). § 28 puts the phrase into its heading, "'transcendence in immanence' and the possibility of deception", and its body pairs the two: "everywhere 'transcendence in phenomenological immanence' and everywhere possibility of deception" (Hua XIII 165–66). With recollection the transcendence carries a risk that retention did not; § 25 had kept retention on the side of the given. Only § 30 drops the quotation marks, looking back on the phrase "transcendence in immanence", which "I have recently spoken of repeatedly", and explaining that it is to be "understood rightly" because the terms are ambiguous (Hua XIII 170). The later layer shows the same need from the other end. Where § 18 spoke of the "switching-off of transcendence", a revision of 1924 or later writes "switching-off of all worldly transcendence" (note 51, Hua XIII 154), and in § 19 "all empirical transcendence" receives "worldly" (note 52, Hua XIII 155). Once § 25 had admitted a transcendence the reduction keeps, the one it switches off needed an adjective.

The *Cartesian Meditations* draw the line between the given and the presumed at a different place. In transcendental self-experience only "a core of what is properly adequately experienced" is offered, "the living self-presence", while beyond it reaches "a presumptive horizon" of what is "not properly experienced, but necessarily co-meant", to which belongs "the mostly completely dark self-past" (§ 9, Hua I 63). The section recalls that adequacy and apodicticity of an evidence "need not go hand in hand". In 1929 the past belongs to the presumptive horizon and falls under a critique of the "range" of apodictic evidence. In 1910/11 the just-past is granted as given, and its cost is named in a single word in quotation marks. The admission of § 25 is what lets the course go on judging: it keeps the now by keeping what the now is not.

## §29, Hua XIII 168–69: the "transcendental-psychic"

Reads [§29.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-going-beyond-the-domain-of-absolute-givenness-as-necessary-condition-of-the-possibility-of-a-phenomenological-science), second paragraph under § 29.

The reduced field is the same field the psychologist claims, taken under another apprehension. The psychologist's right to it depends on apprehending it as the experience of an empirical I. Once that I is switched off, what remains is "no psychological lived-experience". Husserl lets the word "psychic" survive only on that condition, in quotation marks, and splits it with two prefixes into the "transcendental-psychic" and the "empirical-psychic". The adjective is new to the course. Apart from the synopsis, which Husserl wrote after the course, "transcendental" occurs in the text of Nr. 6 only in this sentence, in the "transcendental psychology" of the next paragraph, and in Husserl's marginal note on that phrase. The course's later name for its field enters as a qualifier on a word the course had given to nature.

> The psychologist will say: well, all that is psychic phenomena, present or past psychic phenomena, my own, or, if I assume them on the basis of empathy, those of the other. Now certainly, all that we have marked off there as a field of objectities of their own kind the psychologist claims by right, namely when it is considered not purely in phenomenological reduction but apprehended as I-experience, as phenomenon of an empirical I; though the psychologist, to be sure, has no inkling what great difficulties this naturalistic apprehension of lived-experiences, the concept of psychological lived-experience, brings with it. Still, so far everything is in order. We, however, insist on switching off the empirical subject as well, and then of course phenomenological-singular being is no psychological lived-experience. If one wants to speak of the "psychic", one would have to speak, say, of a transcendental-psychic over against the empirical-psychic.

German:

> Der Psychologe wird sagen: Nun all das sind psychische Phänomene, gegenwärtige oder vergangene psychische Phänomene, meine eigenen, oder wenn ich sie auf Grund der Einfühlung annehme, die des Andern. Nun gewiss, all das, was wir da als Feld von Objektitäten eigener Art abgegrenzt haben, nimmt der Psychologe mit Recht in Anspruch, wenn es nämlich nicht rein in phänomenologischer Reduktion betrachtet, sondern als Icherlebnis, als Phänomen eines empirischen Ich aufgefasst wird, wobei der Psychologe freilich keine Ahnung hat, wie grosse Schwierigkeiten diese naturalistische Auffassung der Erlebnisse, der Begriff des psychologischen Erlebnisses, mit sich führt. Immerhin, soweit ist alles in Ordnung. Wir aber bestehen darauf, das empirische Subjekt ebenfalls auszuschalten, und dann ist natürlich das phänomenologisch-singuläre Sein kein psychologisches Erlebnis. Will man von „Psychischem“ sprechen, so müsste man etwa von einem Transzendental-Psychischen gegenüber dem Empirisch-Psychischen sprechen.

*Note on the text.* Husserl later struck "the following passage" (*Der folgende Abschnitt*) lightly in pencil (note 80, at the paragraph's first word). The file does not say where the strike ends. The next paragraph opens after a page marker with a question of its own, so the strike most likely covers this paragraph alone, but the file cannot settle it.

The concession turns on one clause. "Now certainly" (*Nun gewiss*) grants the psychologist the field "by right" (*mit Recht*), and "namely when" (*wenn es nämlich*) makes the right conditional on an apprehension. The object of the concession is "all that we have marked off", the same "all that" (*all das*) the psychologist named in his first sentence. So the field does not differ between the two parties. What differs is whether it is "considered purely in phenomenological reduction" or "apprehended" (*aufgefasst*) as the phenomenon of an empirical I. Husserl adds a reservation about psychology's own concept, its "naturalistic apprehension" and its "concept of psychological lived-experience", and leaves it undeveloped: "Still, so far everything is in order" (*Immerhin, soweit ist alles in Ordnung*). He tolerates the psychologist's right instead of contesting it, because he does not need to contest it. He needs only the step that takes the apprehension away.

That step is a decision, not an argument: "We, however, insist" (*Wir aber bestehen darauf*). "As well" (*ebenfalls*) points back to § 15, where the switching-off was extended past the body to the empirical I, the foreign one and one's own, and only then did the "pure lived-experience" appear, with "lived-experience" (*Erlebnis*) put in quotation marks for the state of an experiencing I (Hua XIII 149–50). "Of course" (*natürlich*) claims that the result follows at once, and given the decision it does: being with no empirical subject is no state of one. Husserl names that being "phenomenological-singular" (*phänomenologisch-singulär*). The compound gathers the two results of § 29.1, the reduced givenness and the individual "this-here" (Hua XIII 168), into one hyphenated word.

The last sentence carries three hedges: "if one wants to" (*Will man*), "would have to" (*müsste*), and "say" (*etwa*). The quotation marks around "psychic" mark a borrowed word, as the marks around "transcendence" did in § 25 (Hua XIII 162). The two compounds keep the same hyphenated form as "phenomenological-singular", but here the head noun is the borrowed one. Grammatically, "transcendental-psychic" and "empirical-psychic" are two species of one genus, the psychic. The preceding sentence has just denied that the reduced being falls under that genus. Here the wording claims more than the argument shows. The paragraph gives no sense of "psychic" common to both sides, and the hedges admit that it cannot give one. "Transcendental" does the dividing work and is left unexplained. It marks the side opposite to nature without saying what it qualifies.

The next paragraph turns the concession into a programme. Since no one demands absolute modes of givenness of the natural scientist, "nothing at all stands in the way of attempting a transcendental psychology, a science of lived-experiences in phenomenological reduction" (*eine transzendentale Psychologie, eine Wissenschaft von den Erlebnissen in phänomenologischer Reduktion*; Hua XIII 169). The compound of § 29.2 was conditional; here the noun phrase names a science to be attempted, and the apposition defines it without using "psychic". Husserl wrote in the margin "phenomenology as transcendental psychology" (*Phänomenologie als transzendentale Psychologie*; note 84). The note has no editor's sign and no date. In 1924 or later he changed "science" to "experiential science" (*Erfahrungswissenschaft*; note 85), which ties the name to the parity with empirical experience that the paragraph argues. The paragraph hedges its result too: "So one would at first at least think" (*So möchte man zunächst wenigstens denken*). If note 80's strike ends where the paragraph does, Husserl lightly struck the derivation of the coinage and kept its use, with a marginal title. The later layer also changes the psychologist's side. After "I-experience" (*Icherlebnis*) a note of probably 1921 inserts "in the world" (note 81), as revisions of 1924 or later turned the transcendence switched off in §§ 18–19 into "worldly" transcendence (notes 51–52, Hua XIII 154–55). In both places the later Husserl marks the empirical side as worldly.

The coinage does not return in the body. Elsewhere in Nr. 6 "psychic" belongs to nature: in § 18 "my psychic states and acts" are switched off because, as mine, they "are nature" (Hua XIII 154). After § 29, "psychological" names only the apprehension the reduction starts from. § 36 asks whether the reduction restricts us to pure consciousness that "in empirical-psychological apprehension" belongs to one empirical I (Hua XIII 184). In § 37 the phenomenologist sets out from "my psychologically inner or outer experiences" (Hua XIII 187). The reduced side is called "phenomenological" throughout: phenomenological data, the phenomenological I. "Transcendental" comes back only in the retrospective synopsis, and there it qualifies subjectivity and reduction, not the psychic: "objectivity as index for transcendental subjectivity", "in the subjective (transcendental-subjective)", "transcendental reduction (switching-off of physical nature)" (Hua XIII 111). In hindsight Husserl gave the adjective to the subject, not to the word he had borrowed from the psychologist.

Husserl's writings of these months show the borrowed word in use. In the ethics lectures of 1911, "transcendental-psychological" names Kant's confusion: Kant left the problems of geometrical cognition "in complete confusion in his transcendental-psychological doctrines" (*in seinen transzendental-psychologischen Doktrinen*; Hua XXVIII 195). "Philosophy as Rigorous Science", drafted in the Christmas break of 1910/11 according to Husserl's note on his copy (Hua XXV editors' report), draws the line of § 29.2 without the compound. It names "a science of consciousness and yet not psychology, a phenomenology of consciousness over against a natural science of consciousness", and it expects the two to be closely related, "since this will hardly be an accidental equivocation" (Hua XXV 17). Psychology deals with "empirical" consciousness, phenomenology with "pure" consciousness. Both texts hold the same consciousness under two attitudes, as § 29.2 does, but the essay does not call the pure side "psychic".

The Freiburg inaugural lecture withdraws both halves of § 29. "Psychology is science of psychic nature", and the psychic is given in psychological experience "as occurrence in the connection of nature" (Hua XXV 75). So the word goes wholly to nature. "Pure phenomenology was not founded as experiential science" (Hua XXV 79), so the parity of § 29.3 with empirical experience goes as well. The *Cartesian Meditations* restore a psychological side, but as a parallel, not a species. Transcendental self-explication has as "its parallel" the psychological self-explication of "my purely psychic being" (*meines rein seelischen Seins*), apperceived as part of my psychophysical reality and of the world (§ 16, Hua I 76). The hyphenated genus of 1910/11 has become two disciplines, set side by side. In § 29.2 the field is already the one that later becomes transcendental, and "psychic" holds it only in quotation marks and under a "would have to". The adjective that will name the whole of phenomenology first appears here as a prefix that keeps a borrowed word apart from nature, and the course itself goes on calling that field phenomenological.

## §30, Hua XIII 171: immanence as a division of objects

Reads [§30.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#immanence-and-transcendence-the-ambiguity-of-these-terms-and-the-sense-of-immanence-and-transcendence-in-the-field-of-phenomenology), fourth paragraph under § 30.

In its third sense, immanence is not a relation between an act and its object. It is a class of objects, sorted by the kind of givenness they admit in principle. Some individual objects can be given in absolute self-presence. Others can only appear as self-present, through appearances. The first class is immanent even when it is remembered and so not present to the act that intends it. The second class is transcendent even when it stands bodily before perception. On this division, the transcendence of § 25, the been that retention holds, falls on the side of immanence, and the reduction's switching-off of "transcendence" turns out to have meant one class only, nature. The definition lets the course keep a field larger than the present without giving up the line the reduction drew.

> 3) Another concept of immanence and transcendence arises when we see in it a division of objects, and specifically of individual ones. According to it, individual objects fall into those which are beholdable, to be given in absolute self-presence, and individual objects which can only appear as self-present, which can be given only through appearances, presentations. In this case every phenomenological consciousness relates to immanence; the immanent is the field of phenomenology, provided that we now understand phenomenology as a possible science of individual objects brought to immanence by any switching-off of nature. On the side of transcendence there then belongs nature. For nature is just a title that spans the totality of objectivities presenting themselves through appearances. Phenomenology therefore does not want to switch off transcendence in every sense. From the outset it was, after all, defined by the switching-off of nature, of transcendence in a determinate sense, of transcendence in the sense of what appears.

German:

> 3) Ein anderer Begriff von Immanenz und Transzendenz ergibt sich, wenn wir darin eine Einteilung der Gegenstände sehen, und zwar der individuellen. Danach zerfallen individuelle Gegenstände in solche, welche schaubar, in absoluter Selbstgegenwart zu geben sind, und individuelle Gegenstände, die nur als selbstgegenwärtig erscheinen können, die nur durch Erscheinungen, Darstellungen gegeben sein können. In diesem Fall bezieht sich jedes phänomenologische Bewusstsein auf Immanenz; das Immanente ist das Feld der Phänomenologie, wofern wir jetzt Phänomenologie als eventuelle Wissenschaft von individuellen, durch jede Ausschaltung von Natur zur Immanenz gebrachten Gegenständen verstehen. Auf der Seite der Transzendenz gehört dann die Natur. Denn Natur ist eben ein Titel, der die Gesamtheit durch Erscheinungen sich darstellender Gegenständlichkeiten umspannt. Die Phänomenologie will also nicht Transzendenz in jedem Sinn ausschalten. Von vornherein war sie ja definiert durch Ausschaltung der Natur, von Transzendenz in einem bestimmten Sinn, von Transzendenz im Sinn von Erscheinendem.

The first sentence moves the concept from acts to objects. The first two senses of § 30 are relations. In the first, every object is transcendent to the act that intends it, which is why no one calls that transcendence. In the second, only what is "bodily present in the strictest sense" to a determinate act is immanent, and "everything not present, though conscious as object", is transcendent (Hua XIII 170–71). The third sense is "a division of the objects" (*eine Einteilung der Gegenstände*), and "fall into" (*zerfallen*) is the verb of classification. The modal forms carry it. The objects of the first class are "beholdable" (*schaubar*) and "to be given" (*zu geben sind*). The others "can only appear" (*nur … erscheinen können*) and "can be given only" through appearances. Neither class is defined by how an object is given now. Each is defined by what its kind of givenness can be at best. § 30's opening paragraph had said the same of the objects of the projected science: they are "in principle absolutely perceivable, and accordingly also absolutely intuitable in recollection" (Hua XIII 169). A remembered lived-experience is transcendent in the second sense and immanent in the third.

The second clause of the division concedes something to the transcendent class: its objects can "appear as self-present" (*als selbstgegenwärtig erscheinen*). The thing in perception presents itself as there in person. What it lacks is self-presence that is not appearance. The second sense, bodily presence to the act, could not draw this line, since perception gives the thing as bodily there too. The third sense draws it by the means of presence, "through appearances, presentations" (*durch Erscheinungen, Darstellungen*). § 32 makes the contrast explicit: the object of thing-perception "also presents itself as self-present existence, but it is given through mere appearance". The phenomenological present "is not appearing presence but self-presence in the absolute sense", and the very phenomenon of the thing's "presenting-itself-externally-as-self-present" is itself "an absolutely given immanent being" (Hua XIII 175). The appearing of self-presence is immanent. What appears through it is not.

The definition of the field is hedged. "Provided that we now understand" (*wofern wir jetzt … verstehen*) makes the field relative to a conception of phenomenology, and the conception is that of the course: a science "of individual objects". "Possible" (*eventuelle*) keeps that science a possibility, as § 29 had offered transcendental psychology as something "to attempt" (Hua XIII 169). The field is defined for an experiential science of individuals. When § 41 asks whether the course's results concern "pure essence-cognition" after all (Hua XIII 193), it puts this proviso in question, not the division.

The last two sentences claim that nothing has changed. "Therefore" (*also*) draws the consequence: phenomenology "does not want to switch off transcendence in every sense". "After all" (*ja*) and "from the outset" (*von vornherein*) say that it never did. The referent supports the claim. § 15 introduced the new attitude as giving up "every natural, naive positing of things, of nature in the widest sense" (Hua XIII 148). The wording supports it less. The same sentence of § 15 says that the attitude switches off "every empirical, transcendent attitude" (*jederlei empirische, transzendente Einstellung*), and § 18 places the reduction, and the solipsist's mistake, under the aim of "switching-off of transcendence" with no qualifier (Hua XIII 154). § 30 does not withdraw those phrases. It restricts them retrospectively, with three appositions that narrow the word step by step: "of nature, of transcendence in a determinate sense, of transcendence in the sense of what appears". After § 30 the body itself qualifies the word when it uses it. § 32 switches off "all transcendence in the sense of natural existence-positing" (*alle Transzendenz im Sinne natürlicher Existenzsetzung*; Hua XIII 174). A revision of 1924 or later does the same for § 18, which now reads "switching-off of all worldly transcendence" (note 51). The 1910/11 course made the correction first.

Husserl's notes on the section show that the division needed a second criterion. A note on the paragraph itself, undated and Husserl's own, sorts the two classes by repetition and by being: the immanent "can be given only once in their originary becoming, the others repeatedly"; the immanent "can only have been as having been perceived", while the others "can have been before all perception" (Hua XIII 171). A note on the previous paragraph says the same thing from the side of perception: "external transcendences are repeatedly perceivable, immanent objects are not repeatedly perceivable", only units of repeated recollection (Hua XIII 170). On the first paragraph Husserl wrote that the talk of "absolutely intuitable in recollection" is "very misleading": immanent data are "objects only as enduring unities of manifold actual and possible reproductions" (Hua XIII 169). The body's criterion is the possibility of absolute self-presence. The notes add that an immanent object is one-time in its givenness and has no being before it is perceived, and that it becomes an object only through repetition in presentification. That is the problem of the identity of the phenomenological object across acts, which § 31 takes up next. Where nature enters the division, a revision of probably 1921 inserts "the 'objective world'" (note on "Natur", Hua XIII 171).

The division of § 30 revises a doubled concept from 1907. *The Idea of Phenomenology* found transcendence "ambiguous" (*doppelsinnig*). It is either the object's not being "really contained" in the act, so that "immanent here means really immanent in the cognitive lived-experience", or the going-beyond "absolute and clear givenness, self-givenness in the absolute sense" (II. Vorlesung, Hua II 35). The lecture warned that the two "run confusedly into one another" (Hua II 36). In 1910/11 the first 1907 sense returns as § 30's first and is dismissed. "Even the phenomenologically beheld is not in the proper sense in the act" (Hua XIII 170), so a transcendence defined as not being in the act holds of every object, and "immanence" would lose its sense. The second 1907 sense survives as § 30's second sense. The third is new: in 1907 both senses were relations of a cognition to its object, and in 1910/11 immanence becomes a property of a kind of object. That is what lets the reduced field include a past. The transcendence in immanence that §§ 25–28 admitted is transcendence in the second sense and immanence in the third. The field of the course is fixed by the third sense, and nature, given only through appearances, is the one class that the reduction switches off.

## §32, Hua XIII 174: the reduction is not the turn to the apriori

Reads [§32.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#repetition-and-new-presentation-the-phenomenological-reduction-to-the-pure-consciousness-as-individual-being-and-the-problems-of-the-reach-of-the-reduced-consciousness-world-and-of-the-possibility-of-phenomenological-science), third paragraph under § 32, with the paragraph before it.

The reduction excludes every judgment founded on natural experience, and it does not thereby make phenomenology apriori. Husserl separates two contrasts that § 9 had run together. One is between natural and reduced positing. The other is between general validity, which marks the apriori, and individual validity, which marks the aposteriori. The reduction works on the first contrast only. What it leaves is still individual being, posited as such, so the judgments that describe it are aposteriori. Phenomenology is non-empirical, because it uses no natural experience, and it is still a science of individuals. The course's programme of an experiencing phenomenology rests on this distinction.

> When we begin our reflection, we find ourselves in the attitude of natural world-consideration, as human beings in a determinate environment, performing manifold and in each case determinate psychic acts: perceiving, phantasying, judging, and so on. We now carry out an all-embracing phenomenological reduction: we switch off all transcendence in the sense of natural existence-positing. We will make no use in judgment of any existence that comes to us in natural givenness, without, by the way, doubting it or casting suspicion on it in any way. For our present investigation we exclude in principle every empirically founded judgment, as premise and as theoretical establishment.
>
> Understood in the ordinary way of speaking, the last sentence would mean that from now on we wanted to judge *a priori*. But that was by no means the contrast for us. For the genuine apriori, the apriori that is to carry necessity and unconditioned universal validity with it in the judgment, has nothing to do with individual facts. Apriori judgments are generally valid ones. Aposteriori judgments are individually valid; they posit individual being, even when they are universal ones. What remains to us through the all-embracing phenomenological reduction, however, is a world of individual being, the world of phenomenological data, the world of pure consciousness.

German:

> Beginnen wir unsere Reflexion, so finden wir uns in der Einstellung der natürlichen Weltbetrachtung als Menschen in einer bestimmten Umgebung, mannigfaltige und jeweils bestimmte psychische Akte vollziehend: wahrnehmend, phantasierend, urteilend usw. Wir führen nun eine allumfassende phänomenologische Reduktion aus, wir schalten alle Transzendenz im Sinne natürlicher Existenzsetzung aus. Wir wollen von keinem Dasein, das uns zu natürlicher Gegebenheit kommt, irgendwelchen Urteilsgebrauch machen: ohne daran übrigens zu zweifeln, es irgendwie zu verdächtigen. Für unsere jetzige Untersuchung schliessen wir prinzipiell jedes empirisch fundierte Urteil als Prämisse und theoretische Feststellung aus.
>
> In der gewöhnlichen Redeweise verstanden, besagte der letzte Satz, wir wollten von nun ab *a priori* urteilen. Aber das war keineswegs für uns der Gegensatz. Denn das echte Apriori, dasjenige, das im Urteil Notwendigkeit und unbedingte Allgemeingültigkeit mit sich führen soll, hat es nicht mit individuellen Fakten zu tun. Apriorische Urteile sind generell gültige. Aposteriorische sind individuell gültige, sie setzen, auch wenn sie allgemeine sind, individuelles Sein. Uns aber verbleibt durch die allumfassende phänomenologische Reduktion eine Welt individueller Seins, die Welt der phänomenologischen Data, die des reinen Bewusstseins.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "eine Welt individueller Seins". The adjective has the wrong ending for the neuter genitive "Seins", where "individuellen" would be expected. The file cannot settle whether the slip is Husserl's, the edition's or the conversion's. The sense is not in doubt, and the rendering gives "individual being". The italics of *a priori* are the file's; its loss of emphasis elsewhere (see the foreword) means that nothing is argued from them.

Every exclusion in the first paragraph carries a qualifier. The reduction switches off "transcendence in the sense of natural existence-positing" (*im Sinne natürlicher Existenzsetzung*), existence that comes "in natural givenness", and judgments "empirically founded" (*empirisch fundiert*). None of these phrases excludes positing as such. The paragraph after the passage says what survives: phenomenological perception "is absolute positing of a now and, in the now, of a determinate not-now" (*absolutes Setzen eines Jetzt*; Hua XIII 175). The reduced field is posited. Its positing is no longer natural.

The second paragraph turns on that point. "Would mean" (*besagte*) is a subjunctive: the apriori reading is what the sentence would say "in the ordinary way of speaking". The ordinary way is the course's own. § 9 distinguished "natural or empirical attitude" from "non-empirical, apriori attitude": in the one, objects of existence are given, and in the other, objects of essence (Hua XIII 126). On that pairing, a judgment that is not empirically founded must be apriori. "But that was by no means the contrast for us" (*Aber das war keineswegs für uns der Gegensatz*) breaks the pairing. The past tense "was" claims that it never held for the reduction, much as § 30 says that phenomenology was "from the outset" defined by switching off nature (Hua XIII 171). The synopsis of the course supports the claim, since it announced an attempt at "an experiencing phenomenology that is not a doctrine of essence" (Hua XIII 111). The body of the course had not stated the break until now.

The definitions that follow give the apriori and the aposteriori a criterion of validity, not of origin. The "genuine apriori" (*das echte Apriori*) carries "necessity and unconditioned universal validity". § 9 had given another mark, existence-freedom: "the purity of the apriori, that is, its freedom from existence" (*die Reinheit des Apriori, das ist seine Daseinsfreiheit*; Hua XIII 127), against the "impure apriori" of a mathematics bound to experience (Hua XIII 128). § 32 now defines the aposteriori by what it posits. Aposteriori judgments "posit individual being, even when they are universal ones" (*auch wenn sie allgemeine sind*). The concession matters. A universal judgment about the stream, such as § 31's claim that expectation necessarily precedes perception "with the same phenomenological content" (Hua XIII 173), is aposteriori so long as it bears on individual being. The definition does not rule out such generality. It rules out only generality without positing. The passage asserts the classification and does not argue for it. Whether a law like § 31's posits individual being or holds of essence is exactly what § 41 will ask.

The last sentence names what remains with the word "world". The natural attitude has one world, and § 8 had denied "two separate worlds, called things and souls" (Hua XIII 124). § 32 calls the remainder "a world of individual being" and then "the reduced consciousness-world" (*reduzierte Bewusstseinswelt*; Hua XIII 175). The next paragraph, later struck (note 95, Hua XIII 174), tries to reconcile the two uses. "Certainly" (*Gewiss*) the natural world "spans all individual being", since everything can be ordered into it. But "a part of this world, called consciousness, has the peculiarity that it lets itself be phenomenologically reduced". What remains is "something that in this positing is not nature" (*in dieser Setzung nicht Natur*), neither self-appearing being nor co-posited with nature, as it is when consciousness is taken as causally bound up with the body. Husserl wrote beside the clause about a part that lets itself be reduced: "Can one speak like that?" (*Kann man so sprechen?*; note 96, his own and undated). The paragraph needs consciousness to be a part of the one world so that the reduction can start from it, and it needs the remainder to be no part of nature so that the remainder counts as a field of its own. The qualifier "in this positing" carries both claims, and Husserl did not let the reconciliation stand. The unstruck text keeps only the "world of individual being".

The course holds the distinction to its end and then doubts it. § 33 says of retention and recollection, which lack the indubitability of perception: "But experience is experience; it has its value as such" (Hua XIII 176). § 41 looks back: "we thought of phenomenology as a kind of parallel to natural science, both occupied with individual objectivities". It then asks whether what the course found about time-consciousness and the motivations of thing-consciousness concerns "*au fond* pure cognition of essence" and bears "from the outset the stamp of apriori cognition" (Hua XIII 193). Its last sentence takes experience "as positing of individual being" (*als Setzung von individuellem Sein*), the very phrase § 32 used to define the aposteriori, and doubts only whether a science "as real matter-of-fact science" can be founded on it (Hua XIII 193). Husserl's later glosses read the course in § 32's terms. "Eidetic reduction has not been performed. The investigation considers the phenomenologically reduced consciousness in its individual flow" (undated note on § 25, Hua XIII 162). "Phenomenological experience" is "singular-individual research into consciousness within the phenomenological epoché" (note 86, Hua XIII 169). Probably in 1921, he narrowed § 41's parallel to natural science to an "insofar as": phenomenology is parallel only insofar as both deal with individual objectivities (note 121, Hua XIII 193).

*Ideas* I keeps the distinction and reverses the programme. Its note to § 62 grants that "the specifically phenomenological exclusions … are independent of the eidetic exclusion of individual existence" (*unabhängig … von der eidetischen Ausschaltung individueller Existenz*; Hua III/1 119). The reduction is still not the eidetic turn. But the note then calls "any attempt to begin naively with a phenomenological fact-science, before carrying out the phenomenological doctrine of essence, a nonsense". It denies that there can be a phenomenological fact-science "parallel and coordinate" (*parallele und gleichgeordnete*) with the others. Such a science would be in large part the "phenomenological conversion" of the ordinary fact-sciences, and only eidetic phenomenology makes that possible (Hua III/1 119; Kersten's translation, cw2 file, reads the same). "Parallel" is the word § 41 used for the course's plan. In 1910/11 § 32 made the independence of the two reductions the ground for an experiencing phenomenology. In 1913 the same independence makes a question that only eidetic phenomenology can decide, and it decides against that phenomenology.

## §34, Hua XIII 178–79: the double phenomenological reduction

Reads [§34.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#lifting-of-an-artificial-restriction-the-attainment-of-the-phenomenological-stream-of-consciousness-starting-from-the-natural-reflection-on-the-stream-of-consciousness-and-the-double-phenomenological-reduction), second and third paragraphs under § 34.

The double reduction is two reductions performed on one experience. The first reduces the experience itself, as a lived-experience now going on, and brings it to absolute beholding. The second is performed inside the experience, on what it intends. In a recollection, this means that I can reduce the remembered perception, which was a natural perception when it occurred and was never reduced. The second reduction is new. It does not remember an earlier reduction. That is what lets the reduction reach past the present: the whole of past natural life becomes phenomenological material, since every remembered experience can be reduced after the fact. Husserl names the second reduction here and makes it the method for the rest of the course. The heading claims the same for "every experience", but the passage shows it only for recollection.

> Here there showed itself the highly remarkable fact that every experience admits a double phenomenological reduction: on the one hand the one that brings the experience itself to pure immanent beholding, and on the other the one that is exercised on its intentional content and object. So there is a phenomenological reduction that is exercised on the intentional content and object of the recollection. For just as in recollection we can "subsequently" attend to the background of the remembered object, which in the original perception was an unattended background of perception, so in recollection we can exercise on foreground and background a phenomenological reduction that was not performed in the original perception, and that is therefore not itself the recollection of an earlier reduction.
>
> Considered in themselves, the phenomena of the reflections in memory, in presentification of every kind, are of the very greatest interest, and their exact description and analysis is a fundamental piece of all phenomenology. To be sure, <they have> so far not even been seen by anyone. Here they come into consideration for the sake of a determinate, highly wonderful achievement that they make possible: namely, the all-embracing turning of all natural experience, not only according to what in it is *cogitatio*, but also according to what lies in it intentionally.

German:

> Da zeigte sich nun das höchst Merkwürdige, dass jede Erfahrung eine doppelte phänomenologische Reduktion zulässt, einmal diejenige, die sie selbst zum reinen immanenten Schauen bringt, und das andere Mal diejenige, die an ihrem intentionalen Inhalt und Objekt geübt wird. So gibt es eine phänomenologische Reduktion, die am intentionalen Inhalt und Objekt der Wiedererinnerung geübt wird. Nämlich so wie wir in der Wiedererinnerung „nachträglich“ auf den Hintergrund des erinnerten Objektes achten können, der in der ursprünglichen Wahrnehmung unbeachteter Wahrnehmungshintergrund war, so können wir in der Wiedererinnerung eine phänomenologische Reduktion an Vordergrund und Hintergrund üben, die in der ursprünglichen Wahrnehmung nicht vollzogen war, die also nicht selbst Wiedererinnerung einer früheren Reduktion ist.
>
> An sich betrachtet sind die Phänomene der Reflexionen in der Erinnerung, in der Vergegenwärtigung jeder Art von allergrösstem Interesse, ihre genaue Deskription und Analyse ein Fundamentalstück aller Phänomenologie. Allerdings <sind sie> bisher von niemand auch nur gesehen worden. Hier kommen sie in Betracht um einer bestimmten, höchst wunderbaren Leistung willen, die sie ermöglichen: nämlich der allumfassenden Wendung aller natürlichen Erfahrung nicht nur nach dem, was in ihr *cogitatio* ist, sondern auch nach dem, was in ihr *intentio*nal liegt.

*Note on the text.* The angle brackets in "<sind sie>" are the editor's supplement. The file italicizes only the first syllables of "intentional" (*intentio*nal). That may be a trace of emphasis or a conversion artefact, and the reading draws nothing from it. The opening sentence of § 34 (Hua XIII 177–78) is split in the file by the stray tail of an editor's note ("der Horizonte unter kontinuierlichem Vollzug der phänomenologischen Reduktion von jeder Stelle aus" [of the horizons, with continuous performance of the phenomenological reduction from every point]). Read without that line and without the full stop the file puts after "beseitigen", the sentence runs on ("die wir beseitigen" + "müssen" [which we must remove]). The note's beginning is not in the file. The quoted paragraphs are not affected.

The sentence that states the doctrine sets two relative clauses in parallel: "on the one hand" (*einmal*) and "on the other" (*das andere Mal*). The first reduction "brings" the experience "itself" (*sie selbst*) to beholding. The second "is exercised on" (*an … geübt*) its "intentional content and object". The opening paragraph of § 34 had prepared the second preposition: we perform the reduction "on them and in them" (*an ihnen und in ihnen*), on the experiences found in natural reflection (Hua XIII 178). The first reduction takes the experience as an object. The second works through it. The verb is "admits" (*zulässt*): the second reduction is a possibility of every experience and has to be carried out. The consequence follows in "that is therefore not itself the recollection of an earlier reduction" (*die also nicht selbst Wiedererinnerung einer früheren Reduktion ist*). The phenomenological past need not have been phenomenological when it was present. That removes the "artificial restriction" of the heading. Until now, the course had started from "an already phenomenological beholding" and then exercised retention and recollection on it (Hua XIII 178).

The analogy that grounds the claim is weaker than it looks. Attending "subsequently" (*„nachträglich“*, in Husserl's quotation marks) to a remembered background finds something that was there in the original perception and went unnoticed. A reduction performed in recollection finds nothing of the kind: the original perception contained no reduction to be noticed. The analogy shows only that recollection can do with its content what the original act did not do. Whether the reduced past it yields is the past as it was is a further question. § 26 had already shown that two recollections, once reduced, can contradict each other about whether two perceptions were simultaneous (Hua XIII 163–64). The second reduction inherits that fallibility. § 29 had said so when it first described the procedure: a "second reflection and reduction, which runs, so to speak, in the recollection", gives a remembered lived-experience "as phenomenological having-been, but no longer to absolute givenness, which excludes every doubt" (Hua XIII 168). "There showed itself" (*Da zeigte sich*) is in the past tense because § 34 restates that parenthesis of § 29 and makes it a principle.

The example is recollection, and in recollection the intentional object is itself an experience: the remembered perception, with its foreground and background. Reducing it yields another *cogitatio*, a past one. The thesis, however, is about "every experience", and the last sentence names the "turning of all natural experience" by "what lies in it intentionally" as well as by what in it is *cogitatio*. For an outer perception, what lies in it intentionally is a thing, and reducing the thing does not yield a lived-experience. The passage does not say what it yields. § 35 does: the bracketed thing "serves as index of determinate pure connections of consciousness", which can be shown "through phenomenological reduction in these experience-positings" (Hua XIII 179). The extension from presentification to experience of every kind is asserted in § 34 and carried out in § 35. It changes what the second reduction delivers. In recollection it delivers a remembered experience. In outer experience it delivers a system of possible experiences, for which the thing is an index.

The rest of the course uses the doctrine where the intentional object is again an experience. § 39 names it in its heading, "the attainment of other phenomenological I's through double phenomenological reduction", and applies it to empathy, "an experience that we can reduce phenomenologically like any other": "and here too the double kind of phenomenological reduction". First comes empathy itself, given in phenomenological perception and set in my one stream (Hua XIII 189). The second reduction then works on what empathy presentifies, the other's lived-experience, and so yields a second stream. § 38 had placed empathy among presentifications (Hua XIII 188). The reduction "in" a presentification is the one form of the double reduction that the course works out in full. It is the form that reaches the past and the other I.

Husserl's later retrospect describes it in exactly that narrower form. A passage reproduced in the editor's introduction to *Erste Philosophie* II says that the reduction to the stream of consciousness "might seem" to yield a new solipsism, and that the difficulty dissolves because every thing of experience is, "as it was put in 1910", an "*Index*" for an infinite manifold of possible experiences. "The further execution rested on the proof of the double reduction that all presentifications can undergo, namely reduction of the presentifications as present lived-experiences and reduction 'in' the presentifications" (*Reduktion der Vergegenwärtigungen als gegenwärtiger Erlebnisse und Reduktion „in“ den Vergegenwärtigungen*; Hua VIII 434–35). The retrospect assigns the double reduction to presentifications, not to "every experience". It also places the reduction "in" (*„in“*, Husserl's quotation marks) where § 34 had placed the reduction "on" the intentional content. In this account the index is a result of the double reduction, not a second application of it to perception. The file of Hua VIII is on the list of damaged conversions, and the page before the retrospect ends at a seam that loses both the reference and the opening words, so the passage's own source cannot be identified from the file. The quoted sentences run without a break. Read with § 34, the retrospect confirms what the example already shows: the double reduction belongs to presentification, which holds an experience within an experience, and its extension to the whole of natural experience goes through the index.

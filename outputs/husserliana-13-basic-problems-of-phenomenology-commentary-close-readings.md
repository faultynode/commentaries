# Husserl, *Grundprobleme der Phänomenologie* (1910/11): close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md) works through the lecture course at equal depth, section by section. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding goes in the rest of the course and in Husserl's other works.

The text is Text Nr. 6 of Husserliana XIII (*Zur Phänomenologie der Intersubjektivität*, first part, 1905–1920, edited by Iso Kern), "From the lectures *Basic Problems of Phenomenology*, winter semester 1910/11". It is quoted from the converted German file in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-13-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-ersterteil-1905-1920.md`). Passages are cited by § and by Hua XIII page. The main commentary cites § numbers only, and unit references such as "§10.7" name the seventh paragraph of the commentary under § 10. All English renderings of Husserl and of the editor's notes are my working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows.

The text has two layers. The body is the manuscript of 1910/11. The footnotes record what Husserl changed later, mostly "probably in 1921" and "in 1924 or later", when he reread the manuscript and Ludwig Landgrebe's transcription of it. Several readings turn on the difference between the layers, and each says which layer it quotes.

The file has limits that bear on more than one reading. Its page numbers come from page markers that sometimes trail mid-line or are missing, so a page can be one out. It has lost Husserl's emphasis: the italics it keeps are almost all Latin, and no reading argues from emphasis or its absence. Footnote numbers restart across the volume, so every note cited here is a note of Nr. 6. Four places are damaged and are not repaired: a later-struck paragraph of § 10 runs into footnote text, so that its opening cannot be quoted; note 70, on the 1924 revision of the end of § 22, is split across the heading of the Fourth Chapter; note 71, on the first appearance of an apodictic critique, breaks off mid-sentence; and the opening sentence of § 23 has a garbled word. The readings concerned say what the file has at that point.

## §1, Hua XIII 111: an experiencing phenomenology

Reads [§1.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-13-basic-problems-of-phenomenology-commentary.md#the-i-in-the-natural-attitude), third paragraph under § 1.

The course sets out to test whether phenomenology can be an experiencing science: one that posits individual being in the field the reduction opens, as natural science posits individual being in nature, and that is not, or not first, a doctrine of essence. The synopsis does not assert that such a phenomenology exists. It frames it as a question to be weighed. Everything later in the course that speaks of "phenomenological experience" works inside that question, and the last chapter answers it with a doubt.

> Starting from the natural concept of the world. The natural concept of the world as the starting point of a theory of knowledge. Possibility of a phenomenology. "Phenomenology" is here not taken from the outset as a phenomenological doctrine of essence; rather, the attempt is made to weigh whether an experiencing phenomenology, one that is not a doctrine of essence, is possible.
> The evidence of the *ego cogito* with regard to the evidence of the unity of the stream of consciousness, hence givenness of the phenomenological field. The transcendence in the immanence and the various concepts of transcendence. The right of transcendent positing in the immanence. The right of recollection and of expectation. Especially important is the right of the intentional relations, of the expectation-intentions, which yields the turning of an empirical transcendent positing into systematic connections of consciousness. Objectivity as index for transcendental subjectivity, and the right of "empirical" cognition in the sphere of consciousness. The phenomenological (not eidetic) reduction thus yields the possibility of going beyond the actually impressional within the subjective (transcendental-subjective). The same applied to empathy. First elaborations of it. Transcendental reduction (switching-off of physical nature) yields, besides the *ego*, also the other *ego* and its stream. Doctrine of monads. Connection of the monads. Recollection gives the self! Empathizing presentification, present-memory does not give the self.
> Especially important: the essence of the unified phenomenological I: how my stream of consciousness closes itself off phenomenologically against every other. Principle of unity.

German:

> <Inhaltsangabe:> Ausgang vom naturlichen Weltbegriff. Der natürliche Weltbegriff als Ausgangspunkt einer Erkenntnistheorie. Möglichkeit einer Phänomenologie. „Phänomenologie“ wird hier nicht von vornherein als phänomenologische Wesenslehre bezogen, sondern der Versuch gemacht, zu erwägen, ob eine erfahrende Phänomenologie, die nicht Wesenslehre ist, möglich ist.
> Die Evidenz des *ego cogito* in Hinsicht auf die Evidenz der Einheit des Bewusstseinsstromes, also Gegebenheit des phänomenologischen Feldes. Die Transzendenz in der Immanenz und die verschiedenen Begriffe von Transzendenz. Das Recht der transzendenten Setzung in der Immanenz. Das Recht der Wiedererinnerung und der Erwartung. Besonders wichtig ist das Recht der intentionalen Beziehungen, der Erwartungsintentionen, die die Umwendung einer empirischen transzendenten Setzung in systematische Bewusstseinszusammenhänge ergibt. Objektivität als Index für transzendentale Subjektivität und das Recht „empirischer“ Erkenntnis in der Bewusstseinssphäre. Die phänomenologische (nicht eidetische) Reduktion ergibt so die Möglichkeit, im Subjektiven (Transzendental-subjektiven) über das aktuell Impressionale hinauszugehen. Dasselbe angewendet auf Einfühlung. Erste Ausführungen darüber. Transzendentale Reduktion (Ausschaltung der physischen Natur) ergibt ausser dem *ego* auch das andere *ego* und seinen Strom. Monadenlehre. Verbindung der Monaden. Wiedererinnerung gibt das Selbst! Einfühlende Vergegenwärtigung, Gegenwartsereinnerung gibt nicht das Selbst.
> Besonders wichtig: das Wesen des einheitlichen phänomenologischen Ich: wie sich mein Bewusstseinsstrom gegenüber jedem anderen phänomenologisch abschliesst. Prinzip der Einheit.

*Note on the text.* The file has "naturlichen" without its umlaut in the first sentence and "Gegenwartsereinnerung" for *Gegenwartserinnerung* (present-memory, the memory of a present); both are quoted as the file has them and neither affects the reading. The angle brackets around "Inhaltsangabe" (summary of contents) mark an editorial supply, as they do elsewhere in Nr. 6.

The synopsis is not part of the lecture. It stands in Husserl's own note on the title, directly after the remark that only the sheets of the first weeks survive and that "later I lectured freely, without lecture notes". It is therefore written after the course, looking back on it, and its vocabulary shows it. "Eidetic" (*eidetisch*) occurs nowhere in the 1910/11 body of Nr. 6. It appears only here and in notes added in 1921 or later (notes 61 and 70, and the critical supplement to § 17). Where the body of the course speaks of the matter, it says "apriori" (*apriorisch*) and, in § 41, "pure essence-cognition" (*reine Wesenserkenntnis*). The synopsis translates the course into the terms of *Ideas* I, and in those terms it marks what the course did not do: its reduction was "not eidetic".

The decisive word is a participle. An "experiencing phenomenology" (*erfahrende Phänomenologie*) is not a phenomenology *of* experience, which any phenomenology would be. The phenomenology itself does the experiencing. Husserl uses the same participle for natural consciousness: the "experiencing consciousness" (*erfahrendes Bewusstsein*) of the critical supplement to § 17, probably of 1921 and the "experiencing eye" (*erfahrendes Auge*) before which nature stands in § 35. The phrase transfers to phenomenology the posture of natural experience, which posits what it sees as individual and existing. The relative clause then defines it by negation, "that is not a doctrine of essence", and the parenthesis repeats the negation at the reduction: "phenomenological (not eidetic)". The positive content of "experiencing" is left to the course.

The syntax keeps the matter open. "Not taken from the outset" (*nicht von vornherein*) restricts the denial to the start: the course does not presuppose that phenomenology is a doctrine of essence, and it leaves room for the possibility that it will end as one. The attempt is "to weigh whether" (*zu erwägen, ob*) such a phenomenology "is possible". It is an experiment, not a programme. The connective in the middle of the synopsis claims more. The reduction "thus" (*so*) yields the possibility of going beyond the actually impressional. "Thus" rests on the preceding clauses about the right of recollection, expectation and intentional relations. Those clauses name problems, and none of them is yet a result. The note's one exclamation mark, "Recollection gives the self!", marks where the later Husserl saw the decisive asymmetry: memory gives my own past itself, empathy never gives the other's present itself.

The lecture's first paragraph follows the note and sharpens the problem. "Phenomenology is by no means psychology, it lies in a new dimension", and it requires an attitude "essentially different" from that of "every science of spatio-temporal existence". If phenomenology were a science of essences, the distance from psychology would be easy to state, as the distance of geometry from physics. An experiencing phenomenology cannot use that distance. It posits individual being as psychology does, and so it must find its difference from psychology wholly in the attitude, in the reduction, and in nothing else. The course spends its first two chapters drawing that line, and §§ 15 and 29 return to it twice.

The understanding is carried through the course in three stages. Husserl's note to § 21 (note 61), added later, states it as the course's premise: "'Phenomenology' thought as empirical science on the ground of the phenomenological reduction, thus not as eidetic phenomenology, and without the question of 'apodictic' justification." § 32 (Hua XIII 174) states it in the body of the course: what remains after the reduction is "a world of individual being". § 41 (Hua XIII 193) takes it back. Husserl asks whether what the course has found "does not *au fond* concern pure essence-cognition", and whether "something like an experiential phenomenology" (*erfahrungsmässige Phänomenologie*) is "still entirely questionable, indeed perhaps impossible". The question of the synopsis returns in the last chapter, unchanged and now in doubt. When Husserl reread the manuscript in 1924 or later, he narrowed that doubt by inserting "as rational-empirical science" (note 122) after "perhaps". The later Husserl, it seems, no longer doubted that phenomenology could experience. He doubted only that it could be an empirical science of a rational kind.

*Ideas* I gives the answer the course withheld. Its introduction (Hua III/1 6, first edition 4) announces that "pure or transcendental phenomenology will be grounded not as a science of matters of fact, but as a science of essences (as an 'eidetic' science)" (*nicht als Tatsachenwissenschaft, sondern als Wesenswissenschaft (als „eidetische“ Wissenschaft)*; Kersten's translation, CW II, agrees). The synopsis's "not eidetic" names the alternative that *Ideas* I excludes. A disposition of the 1910/11 course that Husserl wrote "in one of the following years", printed as Beilage XXI of Hua XIII, confirms the direction: the right introduction, he says there, would first "lay free the way to the eidetic" and then distinguish rational psychology "as eidetic of the mind" from phenomenology, which "corresponds on the whole to the way of 1912 (summer)". The 1910/11 course is the one place where Husserl tried the other route to the end, and the synopsis, written from the far side of *Ideas* I, names that route by what it was not.

# Husserl, *Ideen I*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md) covers §§111–153 of the First Book at equal depth. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds that meaning, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the book and in his other works. Each heading links to the section of the main commentary that holds the block it reads; unit references such as "§114.3" name the third paragraph of the commentary under § 114.

The German is quoted from Husserliana III/1, *Ideen zu einer reinen Phänomenologie und phänomenologischen Philosophie, Erstes Buch*, edited by Karl Schuhmann, in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-3-1-ideen-i-allgemeine-einfuhrung-in-die-reine-phanomenologie-recheck.md`). The commentary cites § numbers only. Headings here add the Hua III/1 page, taken from the running heads in the file. The file also carries the pages of the first edition of 1913 in brackets, and Husserl's own cross-references ("S. 233") use those; where a reading cites one, it says "1913 p.". All English renderings of Husserl are working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows. They are not Kersten's or Boyce Gibson's. Kersten's translation (Collected Works II) is in the corpus and was used to check the sense of every passage quoted; it is cited only where it departs from the German in a way that matters.

The file is an unreviewed conversion, and three cautions hold for every reading. First, Hua prints Husserl's emphasis in spaced type, and the conversion has dropped almost all of it. No reading argues from emphasis or from its absence. Quotation marks survive, in mixed forms (`,,…''`, `„…“`), and are quoted as the file has them. Second, OCR has damaged the text in places, most seriously at §114 (words lost at a page marker) and §129 ("von" [from] for *vor* [before], which reverses the sense). Each reading says where damage touches its passage, and none repairs the wording. Third, several § headings are demoted, promoted or split in the file; the readings cite the sections by Husserl's numbering, not by the file's heading levels.

## §111, Hua III/1 250–252: two universalities of "merely thinking to oneself"

Reads [§111.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#neutrality-modification-and-phantasy).

"Merely thinking to oneself" (*sich-bloß-denken*) names an attitude that posits nothing. Husserl's claim in §111 is that the phrase covers two modifications that differ in their range. Phantasy is a neutrality modification, but of one kind of positing only, re-presentation (*Vergegenwärtigung*), and it can be applied to every experience whatever, because every experience has its possible memories and so its possible phantasies. The neutrality modification proper is not tied to one kind of positing: it follows every kind, perception, memory, doubt, negation, and has as many forms as there are kinds. Both are universal, but in different dimensions. Phantasy is universal over experiences; neutralization is universal over positings. The confusion of the two is the confusion of these two universalities.

> But yet another dangerous equivocation of the expression "merely-thinking-to-oneself" comes into question, or rather, a very natural confusion is to be warded off, namely that between neutrality modification and phantasy. What is confusing here, and really not easy to disentangle, lies in this: that phantasy itself is in fact a neutrality modification; that despite the particularity of its type it is of universal significance, applicable to all experiences; that it also plays its role in most formations of thinking-to-oneself; and that it must nonetheless be distinguished from the general neutrality modification with its manifold formations, which follow all kinds of positing.
>
> Stated more precisely, phantasizing in general is the neutrality modification of "positing" re-presentation, hence of memory in the widest conceivable sense.

German:

> Aber noch eine gefährliche Äquivokation des Ausdruckes ,,sich-bloß-denken'' kommt in Frage, bzw. einer sehr naheliegenden Verwechslung ist zu wehren, nämlich der zwischen Neutralitätsmodifikation und Phantasie. Das Verwirrende und wirklich nicht leicht Auseinanderzuwirrende liegt hier darin, daß die Phantasie selbst in der Tat eine Neutralitätsmodifikation ist, daß sie trotz der Besonderheit ihres Typus von universeller Bedeutung ist, anwendbar auf alle Erlebnisse, daß sie bei den meisten Gestaltungen des Sich-denkens auch ihre Rolle spielt und dabei doch von der allgemeinen Neutralitätsmodifikation mit ihren mannigfaltigen, allen Setzungsarten folgenden Gestaltungen unterschieden werden muß.
>
> Näher ausgeführt, ist das Phantasieren überhaupt die Neutralitätsmodifikation der ,,setzenden'' Vergegenwärtigung, also der Erinnerung im denkbar weitesten Sinne.

"Yet another" (*noch eine*) counts. §110 had already found two equivocations in the same family of words: the "mere thought" (*bloßer Gedanke*) that asserts nothing against the assumption (*Ansetzen*) that can serve as the antecedent of a hypothesis and be judged right or wrong, and the word "thinking" (*denken*) itself, which means now logical, conceptual thinking and now "the positional as such" (Hua III/1 250). §111 opens a third, and this one lies inside the neutral sphere: once assumption is set aside, "merely thinking to oneself" still covers two different modifications. The phrase is the vernacular name for neutrality, and Husserl takes it apart in three cuts.

The second sentence states the difficulty in four clauses with "that" (*daß*), and their order carries the argument. The first three draw phantasy toward neutralization: it "is in fact" a neutrality modification; it is "of universal significance, applicable to all experiences"; it plays its role in most forms of thinking-to-oneself. The fourth turns on "and … nonetheless" (*und dabei doch*): it must be told apart from "the general neutrality modification". The adjectives match across the turn. Phantasy has universal significance "despite the particularity of its type"; the neutrality modification is "general", with formations "which follow all kinds of positing". The particularity is the type: phantasy modifies one kind of positing, re-presentation. The generality is the range of positings modified. The wordplay on confusing and disentangling (*das Verwirrende … Auseinanderzuwirrende*) names the knot: two universalities, one phrase.

The definition that follows says which positing phantasy modifies, and its scare quotes matter. Phantasy is the neutrality modification of "positing" (*,,setzenden''*) re-presentation. The quotation marks distance the participle, since the next paragraph leaves the word "re-presentation" deliberately unmarked "as to whether the associated 'positing' is genuine or neutralized" (Hua III/1 250). Re-presentation thus divides into memories of every kind and their neutrality modifications, phantasies. A footnote at once denies that this is "a genuine classification" and refers to "the demonstrations about essence and counter-essence, p. 233" (1913 p., footnote 109). That page is §114's statement that "to the originary essence there corresponds its counter-essence as a 'shadow' of the same essence" (Hua III/1 259). Phantasy is not a species beside memory; it is memory's shadow.

The universality of phantasy is then derived, and the derivation is shorter than it looks. Every experience can be reflected on and found present; "accordingly" (*Demgemäß*) every experience has a series of ideally possible memories; "hence also" (*somit auch*), as their neutrality modifications, phantasies. The last step adds nothing but the definition. Phantasy reaches every experience because memory does, and memory reaches every experience because reflection does. Phantasy's range is borrowed through two links, and the second is analytic.

This is where the two modifications part. The modification that would turn "every experience, even the phantasizing one itself" into mere phantasy applies to anything, including its own product. The neutrality modification is "counterposed to every 'positing' experience" (Hua III/1 251), and memory is only one of these, beside perception, the consciousness of possibility, doubt, negation and assumption. Husserl then needs an example of neutralization that is not phantasy, and the Dürer engraving supplies it. The image-object, the "little grey figurines", is the neutrality modification of perception, not of re-presentation: it is "conscious as existing, but as quasi-existing" (*gleichsam-seiend*), and this is "no privation, but a modification" (Hua III/1 252). Aesthetic contemplation is a neutralized seeing, not a phantasy.

§112 draws the consequence from the reflexive clause. Phantasy, "as re-presentation", is iterable: there are "phantasies 'in' phantasies". The "operation" of neutralization cannot be repeated (Hua III/1 253). The scare quotes on "operation" pick up §111's last warning, that neutralization need not be pictured as "a transforming operation attached to a prior positing". What cannot be repeated is not a step performed on an act; it is a way of having the act at all. Neutralizing a neutralized act yields nothing new, because neutralization is already the counter-essence of positing as such.

The pair is older than *Ideas I*, and Husserl says so. His note at the end of §112 (note 110) claims that the *Logical Investigations* had already reached "the right view" on the main points, "especially in §39 the contrast of 'qualitative' and 'imaginative modification', the former of which had the sense of the neutrality modification discussed here". (In the file the note breaks off mid-sentence and its remainder is spliced into the body of §113, Hua III/1 254; it is quoted from both places.) The *Investigations* bear the claim out in the opposition of ranges and qualify it in the iterable member. There (§39, Hua XIX/1 506) representing objectivation was "iterable *in infinitum*", the qualitative modification not, and the first applied "to all objects whatever", the second only to acts. (The file prints the same note marker five times in that sentence, but the text is whole.) In 1901 the iterable member was representation in general, an act-form coordinate with the qualitative modification. In 1913 it is phantasy, one neutralized kind of re-presentation, so that the iterable modification is itself a case of the non-iterable one. That is why the old contrast now needs a warning against confusion: two modifications that were coordinate are now nested, and "merely thinking to oneself" covers both because one sits inside the other. The same note names who confused them. Meinong's *Über Annahmen* (1902), Husserl writes, brought no real advance over the *Investigations*, and "the confusions made clear in our last discussions form the very core of Meinong's conception of assumptions" (Hua III/1 254). The target of §§110–111 is thus a theory that let assumption, mere thought and phantasy run together under one name.

## §112, Hua III/1 253: the "of" that no thinning produces

Reads [§112.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#iterability-of-the-phantasy-modification-non-iterability-of-the-neutrality-modification).

The difference between a sensation and the corresponding phantasm is, for Husserl, a difference in consciousness: the phantasm is phantasy *of* the sensation-datum. It is not a weaker, paler specimen of the same kind of datum. The claim is aimed at a psychology that describes experiences by their contents and grades those contents by intensity and fullness, and it accuses that psychology of a reification it believes it has given up. The accusation and the claim stand or fall together with §112's thesis that phantasies can be iterated. Levels of phantasy within phantasy are levels of "of". A scale of fading contents has no levels, only degrees.

> Our assertion of the possibility of iterated reproductive (and also depicting) modifications is likely to meet with fairly general contradiction. That will change only when practice in genuine phenomenological analysis is more widespread than is the case at present. As long as one treats experiences as "contents" or as psychic "elements", which, despite all the fashionable disputing of atomizing and reifying psychology, are after all regarded as a kind of little things; as long as one accordingly believes one can find the difference between "sensation-contents" and corresponding "phantasy-contents" only in material marks of "intensity", "fullness" and the like, it cannot get better.
>
> One would first have to learn to see that what is at issue here is a difference of consciousness; that the phantasm, therefore, is not a mere anaemic sensation-datum, but by its essence phantasy of the corresponding sensation-datum; further, that this "of" cannot come in through any thinning, however extensive, of the intensity, the fullness of content and so on, of the sensation-datum in question.

German:

> Unsere Behauptung der Möglichkeit iterierter reproduktiver (sowie abbildender) Modifikationen dürfte auf ziemlich allgemeinen Widerspruch stoßen. Das wird sich erst ändern, wenn Übung in der echten phänomenologischen Analyse verbreitet sein wird, als es zurzeit noch der Fall ist. So lange man Erlebnisse als „Inhalte” behandelt oder als psychische „Elemente”, die trotz aller modischen Bestreitung der atomisierenden und verdinglichenden Psychologie eben doch als eine Art Sächelchen angesehen werden; solange man demgemäß den Unterschied zwischen „Empfindungsinhalten” und entsprechenden „Phantasieinhalten” nur in sachlichen Merkmalen der „Intensität”, „Fülle” u. dgl. finden zu können glaubt, kann es nicht besser werden.
>
> Man müßte eben erst sehen lernen, daß es sich hier um einen Bewußtseinsunterschied handelt, daß also das Phantasma nicht ein bloßes bleichsüchtiges Empfindungsdatum ist, sondern seinem Wesen nach Phantasie von dem entsprechenden Empfindungsdatum; ferner, daß dieses „von” durch keine noch so ausgiebige Verdünnung der Intensität, der Inhaltsfülle usw. des betreffenden Empfindungsdatums hereinkommen kann.

Six words in the first paragraph stand in quotation marks: "contents", "elements", "sensation-contents", "phantasy-contents", "intensity", "fullness". Husserl quotes a whole vocabulary, not a single term he disputes. The marks hold the opponent's descriptive language at arm's length before any thesis is stated against it. One word is left unquoted because it is Husserl's own: "little things" (*Sächelchen*). The diminutive does the polemical work. The opponent no longer calls experiences things; he calls them contents and elements, and he joins the "fashionable disputing" (*modische Bestreitung*) of an atomizing, reifying psychology. Husserl answers that the contents are "after all" (*eben doch*) regarded as a kind of little thing. The critique of atomism has shrunk the atoms without changing what they are. "Fashionable" dates the disputing and doubts it: a rejection taken up as a fashion has not been carried out in description.

The second "as long as" (*solange … demgemäß*) gives the consequence. Whoever treats experiences as contents must look for the difference between sensing and phantasizing in "material marks" (*sachlichen Merkmalen*) of the content. Intensity and fullness are the only marks a content has to offer, so the difference must become a difference of degree. Husserl's adjectives turn the degree into a picture of illness and dilution. The phantasm on this view is an "anaemic" (*bleichsüchtiges*) sensation, pale from loss of blood, and phantasy is obtained by "thinning" (*Verdünnung*) the sensation. Both images keep the phantasm and the sensation of one stuff. The counter-thesis is stated through a preposition: the phantasm is "by its essence phantasy of" (*Phantasie von*) the sensation-datum, and Husserl puts the "of" in quotation marks and makes it the subject of the next clause. No thinning, "however extensive" (*noch so ausgiebige*), lets it "come in" (*hereinkommen*). The verb is spatial: the "of" is not a further part that enters the content when enough has been subtracted. It is the way the content is had.

The two paragraphs do not demonstrate the thesis. They explain why it will be contradicted and what the contradicting party must first learn. "One would first have to learn to see" (*Man müßte eben erst sehen lernen*), and in the next sentence whoever is "practised in reflections on consciousness" will see the levels of phantasy "just without further ado" (*eben ohne weiteres*). The opening sentence had set the same condition: the contradiction will cease only when "practice in genuine phenomenological analysis" has spread. The argument appeals to a trained seeing, and the conditional mood of *müßte* concedes that the opponent does not yet see it. The claim rests on what the practised reflector will find, not on an inference the opponent could check without the practice. That is the strength and the weak point of the passage. It names the presupposition, the content-model, that makes the opponent blind to the levels; it does not show that the levels are there.

What links the passage to its section is the iterability thesis it defends. If a phantasm were an anaemic sensation, a phantasy within a phantasy would be a still paler sensation, and the levels would collapse into a continuum of fading. Husserl's description requires discrete levels: a phantasy of the second level can be "freely converted into a direct phantasy" of what it phantasizes at one remove, while no free passage leads from phantasy to perception. There "is a gulf for spontaneity" that only "realizing action and creation" can cross (Hua III/1 253). A gulf is the opposite of a gradient. On the content-model the step from the faintest sensation to the most vivid phantasm would be one more degree; on Husserl's it is a change in the kind of consciousness, and no increase of intensity crosses it. §111 had shown that neutralization cannot be iterated. §112 defends the other half, that re-presentation can, and the defence rests on the "of" being a structure that can be stacked.

The book has already conceded intensity a place, and the place shows the limit of the concession. In §68 (Hua III/1 143) Husserl allows that intuitiveness "admits, under the title of clarity, of continuous intensity-like differences", rising from zero to a fixed upper limit. Intensity-like grades exist, but inside one mode of givenness, as the clarity of a colour that is given. §112 denies that any such grade runs from one mode to another. The same distinction governs the later analyses of "fullness": §132 speaks of the noematic kernel "in the mode of its fullness", and there fullness is a mode of the sense, not a quantity of content.

The formula is older than *Ideas I*. A research text of 1909 in Hua XXIII proposes "to set impression and phantasm against each other", the phantasm being the impression's "phantasy modification: hence phantasy of it" (*also Phantasie von ihm*; Text Nr. 9, Hua XXIII 273; the file is not yet in the corpus map and has not been reviewed). What is a tentative question in 1909 ("How would it be …?") is a thesis in 1913, and the 1913 text adds what the research note lacked: the diagnosis that the critics of element psychology repeat its error, and the iterability argument that makes the "of" decisive.

## §114, Hua III/1 259–261: the shadow that cannot deceive

Reads [§114.3–5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#more-on-potentiality-of-the-thesis-and-neutrality-modification).

Neutral consciousness, in §114, is the exact counterpart of positional consciousness that accomplishes nothing. Husserl says this through images: the neutral act is a "shadow" of an act, a "mere mirroring" of an accomplishment, a "counter-essence"; consciousness is of a double type, "prototype and shadow" (*Urbild und Schatten*). The images carry two claims at once. The shadow matches its original point for point, and it does none of the original's work. Husserl then forbids the reading the images invite in the philosophical tradition, the shadow as illusion (*Schein*). A shadow in his sense cannot deceive, because deceiving is a positing, and the shadow posits nothing.

> The relation of the parallel "acts" consists in this, that the one of the two is a "real act", the cogito a "real", "really positing" cogito, while the other is a "shadow" of an act, an improper cogito, one that does not "really" posit. The one really accomplishes, the other is a mere mirroring of an accomplishment.
>
> To this corresponds the radical difference of the correlates: on the one side the constituted noematic accomplishment, which has the character of the unmodified, real accomplishment, on the other the "mere thought" of the exactly corresponding accomplishment. The real and the modified correspond to each other ideally with absolute exactness and yet are not of the same essence. For the modification carries over to the essences: to the originary essence there corresponds its counter-essence as a "shadow" of the same essence.
>
> Naturally one must not bring into the figurative talk of shadow, mirroring, image anything of mere seeming, anything of deceptive opinion and the like, with which real acts, or positional correlates, would indeed be given. Against the other, so natural confusion of the modification in question here with the phantasy modification, which likewise for every experience — as experience — [233] present in inner time-consciousness — creates a counterpart, its phantasy image, there is no need to warn anew.

German:

> Das Verhältnis der parallelen ,,Akte" besteht darin, daß der eine von beiden ein ,,wirklicher Akt" ist, das cogito ein ,,wirkliches", ,,wirklich setzendes" cogito, während der andere ,,Schatten" von einem Akte, ein uneigentliches, ein nicht ,,wirklich" setzendes cogito ist. Der eine leistet wirklich, der andere ist bloße Spiegelung einer Leistung.
>
> Dem korrespondiert der radikale Unterschied der Korrelate: auf der einen Seite die konstituierte noematische Leistung, die den Charakter der unmodifizierten, wirklichen Leistung hat, auf der andern der ,,bloße Gedanke" der genau entsprechenden Leistung. Die wirkliche und modifizierte entsprechen sich idealiter absolut genau und sind doch nicht vom selben Wesen. Denn auf die Wesen überträgt sich die Modifikation: dem originären Wesen entspricht sein Gegenwesen als ,,Schatten" von demselben Wesen.
>
> Natürlich darf man in die bildlichen Reden von Schatten, Spiegelung, Bild nichts von bloßem Schein, nichts von trügerischer Meinung u. dgl. hineinbringen, womit ja wirkliche Akte, bzw. positionale Korrelate gegeben wären. Vor der anderen so naheliegenden Verwechslung der hier fraglichen Modifikation mit der Phantasiemodifikation, die ebenfalls zu jedem Erlebnis — als Erlebnis — [233] gegenwart im inneren Zeitbewußtsein — ein Gegenstück, sein Phantasiebild, schafft, braucht nicht von neuem gewarnt zu werden.

*Note on the text.* Words are lost at the 1913 page marker in the last sentence: "— als Erlebnis — [233] gegenwart im inneren Zeitbewußtsein —" does not construe. Kersten's translation (CW2) reads "as the present of the mental process in the consciousness of internal time", which suggests a lost phrase ending in a compound of "-gegenwart" [present]. The rendering above keeps the gap. The reading does not depend on the clause, which only recalls §111's distinction between phantasy and neutralization.

Seven words in the first two sentences stand in quotation marks, and five of them are forms of "real" (*wirklich*): "acts", "real act", "real", "really positing", not "really" positing. The quotation marks do not doubt the word. They mark it as the one the description must define. "Real" here does not mean existing, since the neutral act exists as fully as the positional one. It means doing what positing does, and the next sentence says so with the verb "accomplish" (*leisten*): "the one really accomplishes" (*leistet wirklich*). The word family of accomplishment runs through the paragraph: *leistet*, *Leistung*, "noematic accomplishment", "the 'mere thought' of the exactly corresponding accomplishment". Against it stands a family of counter-words. The cogito has a "counterpart" (*Gegenstück*), its noema a "counter-noema" (*Gegennoema*, Hua III/1 259), the essence a "counter-essence" (*Gegenwesen*), and two paragraphs later the neutral consciousness holds "no 'real' noema, but only a counter-image (*Gegenbild*) of one" (Hua III/1 261). The prefix states the exact correspondence. The images state the powerlessness: shadow, mirroring, and later "powerless mirroring of noematic accomplishing" (*kraftlose Spiegelung noematischen Leistens*, Hua III/1 260).

The third sentence of the second paragraph turns the images into an ontological thesis, and the inference is thinner than its "for" (*Denn*) suggests. Real and modified "correspond ideally with absolute exactness and yet are not of the same essence." The reason given is that "the modification carries over to the essences". That restates the conclusion as a law; it does not derive it. What the sentence secures is a classificatory point that §111 had announced. There Husserl denied that memories and phantasies form "a genuine classification" of re-presentations and sent the reader to this page for "essence and counter-essence" (1913 p. 233, §111 note 109). A counter-essence is not a species of the genus it shadows. Neutral believing is not a weaker kind of believing alongside conjecture and doubt; it is the shadow of each of them in turn.

The warning in the third paragraph then takes back what the images would bring with them. "Naturally one must not bring" into the talk of shadow, mirroring and image "anything of mere seeming, anything of deceptive opinion". The relative clause gives the reason: with seeming and deception "real acts, or positional correlates, would indeed be given" (*womit ja … gegeben wären*). Seeming is a positing that turns out false; deception is a belief held wrongly. Both belong to the side that accomplishes. The shadow cannot be an illusion because an illusion makes a claim. The warning cuts the image off from its usual epistemic sense, in which a shadow is what gets mistaken for the thing. Husserl's shadow is what nobody takes for anything. The image keeps only its formal sense: exact outline, no substance.

The section's own close shows what is left to the shadow. "Only one doxic positability remains to the neutral experiences too": the one that belongs to them "as data of immanent time-consciousness", which determines them as "modified consciousness of a modified noema" (Hua III/1 261). The neutral experience is really there as an experience; reflection can posit it with certainty. What it lacks is any positable content of its own. The shadow is real as an event and empty as a claim, and that is why it is neither an illusion nor nothing.

The images did not satisfy Husserl. Hua III/2 prints two replacements entered in his copy of the book about 1914. Beilage 58 (Hua III/2 611) rewrites the first sentence quoted above without the shadow: the one act is "a really positing act (a 'real' believing, doubting, valuing, wishing and so on)", the other "an only 'as-it-were' positing act" (*ein nur „gleichsam“ setzender Akt*), whose "'thesis' is improper, namely neutrally modified". The image gives way to §111's word "as-it-were" (*gleichsam*), the word that had described the image-object in the Dürer engraving as "quasi-existing". Beilage 59 (Hua III/2 612) replaces the "And again" paragraph (Hua III/1 261), whose "attachment", Husserl notes, "is not clear" (*dessen Anfügung nicht klar ist*). It keeps "prototype" and "shadow", now in quotation marks, and states as a "law" that the unfolding of a positional consciousness leads to real doxic acts only and that of a neutral one to neutral acts only. The revisions keep the doctrine and discipline its language: the shadow survives only as a quoted name, and the description passes to the "as-it-were". The 1913 text leans on the images; the warning against *Schein* is what keeps them from saying more than Husserl meant, and his revisions show that he found the warning alone insufficient.

## §114, Hua III/1 262: positionality as a potentiality

Reads [§114.7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#more-on-potentiality-of-the-thesis-and-neutrality-modification).

"Positional" and "neutral", as Husserl fixes them at the end of §114, do not sort experiences by what they do but by what they can yield. An experience is positional if converting it into a doxic cogito would produce real positings, neutral if it would produce only their shadows. Whether it posits anything now is beside the point. The definition lets the opposition reach every experience, including those that are not acts and those in the background of attention, and it makes neutrality a property an experience has from the start, not an operation performed on it. The cost is that the word "positional" no longer means positing, and Husserl has to readmit the performed positing by a stipulation.

> The expressions "positional" and "neutral" are to serve us from now on terminologically. Every experience, whether it has the form of the cogito, whether it is an act in any particular sense or not, falls under this opposition. Positionality thus does not mean the presence or the performance of a real position; it expresses only a certain potentiality for the performance of actually positing doxic acts. Yet we include the case in which an experience is from the outset a performed position in the concept of the positional experience, which is all the less objectionable since, by essential law, a plurality of potential positings belongs to every performed positing.

German:

> Die Ausdrücke ,,positional“ und ,,neutral“ sollen uns von nun an terminologisch dienen. Jedes Erlebnis, ob es die Form des cogito hat, ob es in irgendeinem besonderen Sinn Akt ist oder nicht, fällt unter diesen Gegensatz. Positionalität besagt also nicht Vorhandensein oder Vollzug einer wirklichen Position, es drückt nur eine gewisse Potentialität für den Vollzug aktuell setzender doxischer Akte aus. Doch befassen wir den Fall, daß ein Erlebnis von vornherein vollzogene Position ist, mit in den Begriff des positionalen Erlebnisses, was um so weniger anstößig ist, als wesensgesetzlich zu jeder vollzogenen Setzung eine Mehrheit potentieller Setzungen gehört.

The passage is a terminological act, and its verbs say so. The expressions "are to serve us" (*sollen uns … dienen*), and "we include" (*befassen wir … mit*) the performed position in the concept. The words were not new. §110 had already called mere thinking-to-oneself "no positional consciousness" (Hua III/1 250), and §113 had chosen "positional consciousness" as "the general title, which we nevertheless cannot do without", once actual and potential positing were told apart (Hua III/1 254; the file misprints *posionales*). What is new in §114 is the fixing and its ground. "Nevertheless" (*trotzdem*) in §113 already conceded that a title derived from positing would be strained once it had to cover potential positings, and §114 accepts the strain.

The second sentence states the range before the definition, and the range forces the definition. "Every experience" falls under the opposition, "whether it has the form of the cogito, whether it is an act in any particular sense or not". An experience that is not a cogito performs no position. If positionality meant performing one, background experiences and non-acts would be neither positional nor neutral, and the opposition would lose the universality §114 claims for it. Hence "thus" (*also*): positionality "does not mean" (*besagt … nicht*) the presence or performance of a position; it "expresses only a certain potentiality" (*nur eine gewisse Potentialität*). The connective presents as a consequence what is in fact a decision made for the sake of the range. The paragraph before had prepared it: what the doxic conversion of an experience "yields" (*aus sich hergibt*), real positings or neutral ones, "is absolutely predetermined" by the experience's essence, and in each consciousness there is "prefigured" a "fixed totality of potential positings of being", either "a field of possible real positings or of possible neutral 'shadow-positings'" (*Schattensetzungen*, Hua III/1 261). The definition turns that field into the criterion.

"Only" and "a certain" hedge the potentiality without saying what it is. It is not any possibility, since a neutral experience can also be converted into a cogito. It is the possibility of real, "actually positing doxic acts". The criterion is doxic: all experiences, those of feeling and will included, are classed by the beliefs they would yield. That is the bridge to §117, where every act is shown to be "potentially objectivating" because it can be turned into a doxic positing. §114.7 already measures every kind of consciousness against belief.

The last sentence repairs the gap the definition opened. A definition by potentiality alone would leave the performed position outside the positional, which is absurd for a word made from "position". Husserl includes it by fiat and defends the fiat as "all the less objectionable" (*um so weniger anstößig*): every performed positing carries a plurality of potential ones, so the actual case also satisfies the potential criterion. The defence works, but its form is telling. Husserl anticipates offence (*Anstoß*) at his own usage. The term has been redefined so thoroughly that its literal sense has to be brought back as a special case.

The literal sense returns two lines later all the same. The final paragraph of §114 recalls how the analysis began, with the difference "between positional (that is, actual, real) belief and its neutral counterpart" (*zwischen positionalem (d.i. aktuellem, wirklichem) Glauben*, Hua III/1 262). Within the doxic cogito, positional and actual coincide, and the gloss is correct there. But it glosses the term by exactly the words the definition had just excluded. The sentence shows why the definition needed saying: the vernacular sense of "positional" keeps returning, and the potentiality sense is held only by the decision.

The later chapters use the term in the defined sense, and they show what the definition secures. §117 extends the concept of thesis to all acts and with it "the concept of positionality", and repeats §114's criterion: positional theses are those that "either are actual theses or can be converted into actual ones", having "'really' positable noemata" (Hua III/1 270). §120 makes every synthesis depend for its positional character on that of its founding theses (Hua III/1 278). §124 transfers the opposition into expression, where the expressive layer "cannot have a differently qualified positional or neutral thesis" than what it expresses (Hua III/1 288). In each case the opposition applies to an experience before anything is posited, because it is fixed by potentiality. The one place where the boundary admits a passage is §140. For an essence-proposition the intuitive re-presentation of its fulfilment, "indeed the mere phantasy of an essential connection, and insight into it", are "'equivalent'": one "passes over into the other by a mere change of attitude" (Hua III/1 326). Here the boundary is crossed. A neutral experience, the phantasy, passes into a positing insight, although §114 had declared what each experience yields "absolutely predetermined" by its essence. Husserl does not reconcile the two passages. The reconciliation the text allows is that the change of attitude produces a new experience, an eidetic seeing founded on the phantasy, rather than unfolding the phantasy's own potential; the essence seen is the same in both, and so the section's title can speak of an "equivalence of positional and neutral insight". The definition by potentiality is what makes the question sharp: had positionality meant performance, the equivalence would be trivial, a matter of performing or not; measured by what an experience can yield, it marks the one region where the shadow and the act yield the same.

## §117, Hua III/1 272: all acts "objectivating", only one actually

Reads [§117.5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#the-founded-theses-and-the-conclusion-of-the-doctrine-of-the-neutralization-modification-the-general-concept-of-thesis).

In §117 "objectivating" names a potentiality before it names a performance. Every act, valuing, wishing and willing included, is "objectivating" because it carries doxic modalities that can be made actual; its correlate can then be posited as something that is, a value or a wish, an object of a new region. Only the doxic cogito objectivates in act. The thesis that all acts constitute objects is therefore true in the sense §114 gave to "positional": by what an act can yield. The restriction in the next sentence is not a qualification added afterwards. It is the thesis's own sense, and it decides how far "all acts" reaches.

> After all this it follows that all acts whatever — the acts of feeling and will too — are "objectivating" ones, originally "constituting" objects, necessary sources of different regions of being and thereby also of the ontologies belonging to them. For example: valuing consciousness constitutes the "axiological" objectivity, novel as against the mere world of things, a "being" of a new region, inasmuch as, through the essence of valuing consciousness in general, actual doxic theses are prefigured as ideal possibilities, which bring objectivities of a novel content — values — into relief as "meant" in valuing consciousness. In the act of feeling they are meant in the manner of feeling; through actualization of the doxic content of these acts they come to be meant doxically and further logically-expressly.
>
> Every act-consciousness performed non-doxically is in this way potentially objectivating; the doxic cogito alone performs actual objectivation.

German:

> Nach all dem ergibt es sich, daß alle Akte überhaupt — auch die Gemüts- und Willensakte — „objektivierende” sind, Gegenstände ursprünglich „konstituierende”, notwendige Quellen verschiedener Seinsregionen und damit auch zugehöriger Ontologien. Zum Beispiel: Das wertende Bewußtsein konstituiert die gegenüber der bloßen Sachenwelt neuartige „axiologische” Gegenständlichkeit, ein „Seiendes” neuer Region, sofern eben durch das Wesen des wertenden Bewußtseins überhaupt, aktuelle doxische Thesen als ideale Möglichkeiten vorgezeichnet sind, welche Gegenständlichkeiten eines neuartigen Gehaltes — Werte — als im wertenden Bewußtsein „vermeinte” zur Heraushebung bringen. Im Gemütsakte sind sie gemütsmäßig vermeint, sie kommen durch Aktualisierung des doxischen Gehaltes dieser Akte zu doxischem und weiter zu logisch-ausdrücklichem Gemeintsein.
>
> Jedes nicht-doxisch vollzogene Aktbewußtsein ist in dieser Art potentiell objektivierend, das doxische cogito allein vollzieht aktuelle Objektivierung.

The quotation marks in the first sentence fall on the two participles that carry the thesis, "objectivating" and "constituting", and on three words of the example, "axiological", "a 'being'", "meant". The marks on the participles distance a technical term inherited from elsewhere; the marks on "being" and "meant" signal an extended use. A value is "a 'being' of a new region" only in the sense that it can be posited as being. The sentence states the extension as a result ("it follows", *ergibt es sich*), and the example then gives its ground in a clause with "inasmuch as" (*sofern eben*): valuing constitutes a new objectivity inasmuch as "actual doxic theses are prefigured as ideal possibilities" in it. The constitution of values rests on a prefigured possibility, not on anything the valuing act does as valuing.

The last sentence of the example names three stages: values are "meant in the manner of feeling" in the act of feeling; they come "to being meant doxically" through actualization of the doxic content; and "further" to being meant "logically-expressly". The verb "come to" (*kommen … zu*) makes the stages a passage. What the feeling act means, it means already; the doxic turn changes only how it is meant. That is the claim the paragraph before had prepared with a qualification: a wish-proposition converted into a doxic proposition is "in a certain way after all both in one" (*in gewisser Weise doch beides in eins*, Hua III/1 271), and the conversion is "an 'operation' possible in principle" (*,,Operation''*). The hedges "in a certain way" and the scare-quoted "operation" mark that the doxic proposition does not replace the wish but doubles it.

The second paragraph quoted restricts what the first asserted. "In this way" (*in dieser Art*) refers back to the prefiguring, and it turns "all acts are objectivating" into "every non-doxically performed act is potentially objectivating". The second clause reserves actual objectivation to one form of act: "the doxic cogito alone" (*allein*). Read without the restriction, the first sentence would make feeling and will objectivating in their own right, independent sources of objects beside belief. Read with it, they are sources of regions only through the belief they harbour. The regions are new, since a value is not a thing, but the access to them is doxic throughout. The thesis widens the field of objects and narrows the act that reaches them.

This is where the unnamed target comes in. The Fifth Logical Investigation had made "objectivating act" the name of a class. "Every intentional experience is either an objectivating act or has such an act as its 'basis'", which it contains "as a component" whose total matter is its own (§41, Hua XIX/1 514–515). There the non-objectivating acts, feeling and will, were founded on an objectivating act beside them. *Ideas I* drops the founding layer and keeps the priority. Feeling acts are no longer built on a separate representation; they "harbour" doxic modalities within their own thetic characters, which "coincide" with those characters "in noematic sense" (Hua III/1 271). The word "objectivating" passes from a class of acts to a potentiality of every act, and the quotation marks show that Husserl knows he is reusing it. What the *Investigations* expressed as a stratum of founding becomes in *Ideas I* a convertibility. The privilege of belief survives the change of model intact: it is now "the unique privilege" (*den einzigartigen Vorzug*) of the doxic modalities that "their positional potentiality embraces the whole sphere of consciousness" (Hua III/1 271).

The passage contributes the ground for the claims that follow it at once. Husserl draws from it "the deepest of the sources" of "the universality of the logical, ultimately of predicative judgment", and the possibility, "indeed necessity", of formal and material disciplines of feeling and will (Hua III/1 272), a theme deferred by note to the Third Chapter of the Fourth Section. That chapter takes it up: §147 branches the problems of reason into formal logic, axiology and praxis, and §152 transfers the problem of constitution to the founded regions, cultural formations among them. Both depend on the restricted thesis. A formal axiology is possible because every valuing can be turned into a doxic proposition and so made the object of logic; a constitutive phenomenology of values is possible because values are constituted as "beings" of a region. If feeling objectivated on its own, axiology would need a logic of its own kind; because it objectivates only through belief, logic in the ordinary sense has authority over it. The "universality of the rule of logic itself" (*der Universalität der Herrschaft der Logik selbst*, Hua III/1 272) is the price of "all acts are objectivating", and the restriction is what pays it.

## §119, Hua III/1 276–277: nominalization as the logical face of a conversion

Reads [§119.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#conversion-of-polythetic-acts-into-monothetic-ones).

Nominalization, for Husserl, is not a grammatical device first. It is the expression in logic of a law of consciousness: whatever is constituted in a many-rayed synthesis, a collection, a disjunction, a relation, a state of affairs, can be grasped in a single ray as one object. Logic records this as the rule that every proposition and every form within it has a corresponding nominal: "that S is p", "being-P", "similarity", "plurality". Because the nominals so formed are fixed by pure form alone, they are the categories of formal ontology. The passage claims that the relation between formal logic and formal ontology turns on this conversion, and it is the claim the Third Chapter of the Fourth Section cashes.

> In logic this lawfulness manifests itself in the law of "nominalization", according to which to every proposition and to every partial form distinguishable in the proposition there corresponds a nominal: to the proposition itself, say "S is p", the nominal that-clause; for example, in the subject position of new propositions, to the "is p" the P-being, to the relational form "similar" the similarity, to the plural form the plurality, and so on. The concepts sprung from the "nominalizations", thought of as determined exclusively by the pure forms, form formal-categorial variants of the idea of objectivity in general and supply the fundamental conceptual material of formal ontology and, included in it, of all formal-mathematical disciplines. This proposition is of decisive significance for understanding the relation of formal logic, as logic of apophansis, to universal formal ontology.

German:

> In der Logik bekundet sich diese Gesetzmäßigkeit im Gesetz der „Nominalisierung“, wonach jedem Satz und jeder im Satz unterscheidbaren Partialform ein Nominale entspricht: dem Satze selbst, sagen wir „S ist p“, der nominale Daßsatz; z. B. an Subjektstelle neuer Sätze dem „ist p“ das P-sein, der Relationsform „ähnlich“ die Ähnlichkeit, der Pluralform die Mehrheit usw. Die aus den „Nominalisierungen“ entsprungenen Begriffe, ausschließlich durch die reinen Formen bestimmt gedacht, bilden formal-kategoriale Abwandlungen der Idee der Gegenständlichkeit überhaupt und liefern das fundamentale Begriffsmaterial der formalen Ontologie und darin beschlossen alle formal-mathematischen Disziplinen. Dieser Satz ist für das Verständnis des Verhältnisses der formalen Logik als Logik der Apophansis und der universellen formalen Ontologie von entscheidender Bedeutung.

The first verb sets the order of grounding. The lawfulness "manifests itself" (*bekundet sich*) in logic: the logical law is a manifestation of something that holds first in consciousness. "This lawfulness" points back to the noetic law stated just before, that to every polythetic constitution of synthetic objects "belongs the essential possibility" of converting "the many-rayed conscious" into something "conscious simply in one ray", of "making objective" in a "monothetic" act what was constituted synthetically (Hua III/1 276). The passage therefore reads the grammar of nominals off a structure of acts. The that-clause is not a linguistic transformation of the sentence; it is the sign that a state of affairs, judged in a many-rayed predication, can be taken up as one object and made the subject of new predications.

The examples are graded. A proposition yields a that-clause, a predicate form yields a being-P, a relational form yields "similarity", a plural form "plurality". The nominal is always taken "in the subject position of new propositions" (*an Subjektstelle neuer Sätze*). The phrase says what nominalization is for: it lets a form become a theme, and a theme can bear predicates, relations and further syntheses. The noetic paragraph had said the same of the collection: once made one object, "the plurality can now for its part be connected with other pluralities and other objects, put into relation with them, and so on" (Hua III/1 276). Nominalization is the hinge of iteration in the formal sphere, as re-presentation was in the sphere of intuition.

The quotation marks on "nominalization" in both sentences treat the word as a term of the logician's that Husserl is placing, not inventing. The second sentence then makes the decisive restriction: the concepts that spring from nominalizations count for formal ontology only "thought of as determined exclusively by the pure forms" (*ausschließlich durch die reinen Formen bestimmt gedacht*). Similarity of colours is a material concept; similarity as the nominalized relational form is formal. What results are "formal-categorial variants of the idea of objectivity in general". The word "variants" (*Abwandlungen*) is careful. Set, number, relation and state of affairs are not new kinds of object beside the thing; they are modifications of the empty "something in general", and they owe their objecthood to the conversion.

The section's own warning shows where the inference is thinner than the wording. Before stating the law, Husserl demands that it be "brought to complete evidence that what is simply objectified and what is synthetically unitary is really the same", and that the later thesis "invents nothing for the synthetic consciousness" (*nichts andichtet*) but "grasps what it gives" (Hua III/1 276). "Is to be brought" (*ist zu … zu bringen*) is a task, not a result. The identity of the nominal object with the synthetic one is asserted, and the "essentially different mode of givenness" is conceded in the same breath. If the identity failed, the objects of formal ontology would be products of the nominalizing act, fictions of grammar. The decisive significance claimed in the last sentence depends on an evidence the section requires rather than supplies.

§148 draws on the passage by name. Formal ontology reaches "very far beyond" the mere conversion of apophantic truths into ontic ones: "great disciplines accrue to it through those 'nominalizations' of which we spoke earlier", and the note refers to "§ 119, S. 247f." (1913 pp.). "In plural judging the plural appears as plural thesis. Through the nominalizing turn it becomes the object set, and so the basic concept of set theory arises" (Hua III/1 343). Relation and number follow. §148 adds what §119 did not yet say: the same transfers to formal axiology and practics, so that values and practical objects fall under the "formal title 'object', 'something in general'", and the concept of formal ontology "has widened". That widening rests on §117 as much as on §119. Because every act is potentially objectivating through doxic conversion, and every synthesis of feeling can be nominalized like a synthesis of belief, the forms of feeling and will can be made objects too. §148 then warns against the abuse of the parallel: Kant called the relation of willing the end to willing the means "analytic" and so "confused analogy with identity" (Hua III/1 343). The nominalized forms of feeling are analogues of the logical forms, not the same forms.

The thesis was first worked out in the *Logical Investigations*, to whose Fifth Investigation §§34–36 Husserl's note sends the reader. In the Fifth Investigation as Hua XIX/1 prints it, the general statement reads: "with all syntheses the fundamental operation of nominalization is possible, the transformation of synthetic many-rayedness into a 'nominal' one-rayedness with the corresponding matter that points back" (§38, Hua XIX/1 502). The *Investigations* speak of an "operation" and of a "matter that points back" to the original synthesis. *Ideas I* speaks of a law, grounds it in a conversion of the act, and draws from it the constitution of formal ontology's objects. The step from operation to law is the step from a fact about judging to an essential possibility that founds a science of objects in general, and it is that step §119 calls of decisive significance.

## §124, Hua III/1 285–286: *Bedeutung* kept for words, *Sinn* for every act

Reads [§124.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#the-noetic-noematic-layer-of-the-logos-meaning-and-signification).

In §124 Husserl splits two words that ordinary German, and his own earlier usage, treat as one. "Signification" (*Bedeutung*) is kept for the old concept, the meaning of an expression, and above all for "logical" or "expressive" signification. "Sense" (*Sinn*) is used for the noematic content of every intentional experience, whether or not anything is said. The decision makes expression a layer that is added to a sense already there: a perception has a sense before anyone says "this is white", and saying it adds a signification that expresses that sense. Everything §§129–133 build on "noematic sense" depends on this split, and so does the claim of §124.4 that expression is "not productive".

> We look exclusively at "meaning" and "signification". Originally these words have relation only to the linguistic sphere, to that of "expressing". But it is almost unavoidable, and at the same time an important step of knowledge, to extend the signification of these words and modify it suitably, whereby it finds application in a certain way to the whole noetic-noematic sphere: thus to all acts, whether these are interwoven with expressing acts or not. So we too have spoken all along of "sense" — a word that is after all generally used as equivalent to "signification" — in the case of all intentional experiences. For the sake of distinctness we will prefer the word *signification* for the old concept, and in particular in the complex locution "logical" or "expressive" signification. The word sense we use as before in the more comprehensive breadth.

German:

> Wir blicken ausschließlich auf "Bedeuten" und "Bedeutung" hin. Ursprünglich haben diese Worte nur Beziehung auf die sprachliche Sphäre, auf die des "Ausdrückens". Es ist aber nahezu unvermeidlich und zugleich ein wichtiger Erkenntnisschritt, die Bedeutung dieser Worte zu erweitern und passend zu modifizieren, wodurch sie in gewisser Art auf die ganze noetisch-noematische Sphäre Anwendung findet : also auf alle Akte, mögen diese nun mit ausdruckenden Akten verflochten sein oder nicht, So haben auch wir immerfort von "Sinn" - ein Wort, das doch im allgemeinen gleichwertig mit "Bedeutung" gebraucht wird - bei allen intentionalen Erlebnissen gesprochen. Der Deutlichkeit halber wollen wir das Wort Be d e u tun g für den alten Begriff bevorzugen und insbesondere in der komplexen Rede "logische" oder "ausdrückende" Bedeutung. Das Wort Sinn gebrauchen wir nach wie vor in der umfassenderen Weite.

*Note on the text.* In this stretch the file sets one print line per file line, with soft hyphens and stray marks. The quotation joins the hyphenated line-ends and drops four footnote markers that interrupt words ("modifizie- [^123]ren", "bei [^124]allen") and a stray "!" after "oder nicht,". The first sentence is promoted to a `###` heading in the file. "Be d e u tun g" is the file's garbled spaced type, the one instance of spaced type to survive in §§111–153: Husserl spaced the word whose sense he was fixing. "ausdruckenden" is the file's reading and may be an OCR slip for *ausdrückenden* [expressing]; nothing turns on it.

The passage turns on a word used to describe itself. To extend "the signification (*Bedeutung*) of these words" is to apply the word under discussion to its own case, and the second sentence does so without remark. That is the difficulty in small: the old word is unavoidable even while its range is being restricted. Husserl does not stipulate the extension as a choice. It is "almost unavoidable" and "an important step of knowledge" (*Erkenntnisschritt*): the extension follows the matter, since the noematic structures found in perception, memory and wish are of the same kind as the content of a meaning-act. The hedge "in a certain way" (*in gewisser Art*) marks that the application to all acts is analogical. The concession that follows, "we too have spoken all along of 'sense'", admits that the book has been using the extended concept since its analyses of the noema under another name.

The concession then names the problem: "sense" is "a word that is after all generally used as equivalent to 'signification'" (*doch im allgemeinen gleichwertig*). The words are synonyms in use, so the split has to be imposed. "For the sake of distinctness" (*Der Deutlichkeit halber*) gives the only reason, and it is terminological. The division of labour is lopsided. *Bedeutung* is narrowed back to its origin, "the old concept"; *Sinn* keeps the "more comprehensive breadth" it had "as before" (*nach wie vor*). The narrow word gets the spaced type. The wide word is introduced without emphasis, as the word already in use.

The split licenses the thesis that follows at once. Husserl takes a perception "with a determinate sense, posited monothetically in the determinate fullness", lets it be explicated as "This is white", and insists that "this process does not require the least of 'expression'" (Hua III/1 285). Only when we have "'thought' or asserted" it does "a new layer" come with it, "one with what is meant purely perceptually 'as such'". So every "'meant as such', every meaning in the noematic sense (namely as noematic kernel) of any act whatever is expressible through 'significations'". The general formula follows on a line of its own: "Logical signification is an expression" (Hua III/1 286). Without the two words the formula would be a tautology, meaning expressing meaning. With them it says that a signification expresses a sense it did not make, and that expression is a form "that lets itself be adapted to every 'sense' (the noematic 'kernel')" and raises it into "the realm of the 'logos', of the conceptual and thereby of the 'universal'". The sense is pre-conceptual and particular; the signification is conceptual and universal. The rest of §124 makes this precise, in the thesis that the expressive layer "is not productive" except of the conceptual form.

The decision closes a question the *Logical Investigations* had left open on purpose. In the First Investigation "signification counts for us further as equivalent with sense" (*Bedeutung gilt uns ferner als gleichbedeutend mit Sinn*). Husserl gave two reasons: parallel terms are convenient, and the "firmly rooted habit" of using the words as synonyms made it seem unwise "to differentiate their significations (as, e.g., G. Frege has proposed), using the one for signification in our sense and the other for the expressed objects" (§15, Hua XIX/1 58). *Ideas I* differentiates after all, against the same habit it once deferred to ("generally used as equivalent"), but along a different axis from Frege's. For Frege the pair split meaning from reference. For Husserl both words stay on the side of meaning, and the split runs between the meaning of expressions and the meaning of acts. The object meant is assigned to neither word. It becomes the "determinable X" of §131, the "object" in the sense, which the sense refers to but which is not itself a sense. §124's last remark on the literature, that "the great problems indicated here" are rarely "appreciated in their full deep-lying sense" (Hua III/1 287), names among them the distinction of this sense from "the signification of the expressing phenomenon itself", which is the distinction the *Investigations* had declined to fix in words.

The split holds in the chapters that follow, and the English pair cannot carry it alone. §129 makes the "content" of every noema its "sense", §130 delimits the "noematic sense", and §133 defines the sense as the "matter" to which a thetic character adds a "proposition" (*Satz*). Kersten's CW2 renders the pair "signification" and "sense" here, which keeps it apart. "Meaning" for *Bedeutung* would erase it, because "meaning" is what an English reader hears in both German words. The verb *Bedeuten*, which Husserl names alongside *Bedeutung* in the first sentence, has no counterpart on the side of *Sinn* at all: the passage names no act that would confer sense, and that asymmetry is part of the doctrine. Meaning-acts are special acts; sense belongs to every act without a special act to confer it.

## §124, Hua III/1 287–288: expression "not productive", or productive only of the concept

Reads [§124.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#the-noetic-noematic-layer-of-the-logos-meaning-and-signification).

The expressive layer, in §124, adds to what it expresses only the form of the conceptual. It brings no new object, no new sense and no thesis of its own: an expressed believing is still believing, an expressed wish still wishing, and in the coincidence of the two layers there is "only one thesis". Husserl first says that the layer is "not productive" and then corrects himself: its productivity "exhausts itself" in expressing and in the conceptual form that comes with it. The correction is the thesis. Expression is productive of exactly one thing, conceptual form, and the argument of §§125–127 depends on what that form brings with it and what it leaves out.

> The layer of expression is — this makes up its peculiarity — apart from the fact that it lends just expression to all other intentionalities, not productive. Or, if one will: its productivity, its noematic accomplishment, exhausts itself in expressing and in the form of the conceptual that comes in newly with it.
>
> At the same time the expressing layer is perfectly one in essence, as to its thetic character, with the layer that undergoes expression, and in the coincidence it takes the latter's essence into itself so fully that we call expressed representing just representing itself, expressed believing, conjecturing, doubting themselves and as a whole believing, conjecturing, doubting; likewise expressed wishing or willing just wishing, willing. That the difference between positionality and neutrality also passes over into the expressed is evident, and we mentioned it above already. The expressing layer cannot have a differently qualified positional or neutral thesis than the one undergoing expression, and in the coincidence we find not two theses to be separated, but only one thesis.

German:

> Die Schicht des Ausdruckes ist — das macht ihre Eigentümlichkeit aus — abgesehen davon, daß sie allen anderen Intentionalien eben Ausdruck verleiht, nicht produktiv. Oder wenn man will: Ihre Produktivität, ihre noematische Leistung, erschöpft sich im Ausdrücken und der mit diesem neu hereinkommenden Form des Begrifflichen.
>
> Dabei ist die ausdrückende Schicht mit der Ausdruck erfahrenden dem thetischen Charakter nach vollkommen wesenseinig, und sie nimmt in der Deckung so sehr deren Wesen in sich auf, daß wir das ausdrückliche Vorstellen eben selbst Vorstellen, das ausdrückliche Glauben, Vermuten, Zweifeln selbst und als Ganzes Glauben, Vermuten, Zweifeln nennen; desgleichen das ausdrückliche Wünschen oder Wollen, eben Wünschen, Wollen. Daß auch der Unterschied zwischen Positionalität und Neutralität in das Ausdrückliche übergeht, ist einleuchtend, und wir haben es oben schon erwähnt. Die ausdrückende Schicht kann nicht eine anders qualifizierte positionale oder neutrale Thesis haben als die Ausdruck erfahrende, und in der Deckung finden wir nicht zwei zu scheidende Thesen, sondern nur eine Thesis.

*Note on the text.* The file sets the first paragraph and half of the second as short print lines with line-end hyphens; the quotation joins them.

The first sentence states the thesis twice over, and the exception comes before the predicate. Between "is" and "not productive" stand two insertions, "this makes up its peculiarity" and "apart from the fact that it lends just expression (*eben Ausdruck*) to all other intentionalities". The exception is phrased so that it seems to subtract nothing: the layer does only what a layer of expression does. The second sentence, "Or, if one will" (*Oder wenn man will*), admits that the exception is a production after all, and names it twice, "its productivity, its noematic accomplishment" (*Leistung*). That noun matters. In §114 *Leistung* was what the positional act achieves and the neutral act only mirrors. Expression is not a shadow in that sense: it accomplishes something, but its accomplishment "exhausts itself" (*erschöpft sich*) in expressing and in "the form of the conceptual that comes in newly" (*neu hereinkommenden*). "Newly" concedes a novelty; "exhausts itself" bounds it.

The second paragraph says what the bound protects: the thesis. The expressive layer is "perfectly one in essence" with the expressed "as to its thetic character", and it "takes the latter's essence into itself" in the coincidence (*Deckung*). The evidence offered is linguistic: "we call" an expressed believing "believing itself", an expressed wishing "wishing". The argument appeals to usage, and usage is weak evidence for an essential unity. We also call a reported belief a belief. What carries the claim is the noun "coincidence", which the book has used since §117 for two layers that fill one another without remainder. The positional-neutral difference "passes over" into the expressed, and so expression cannot neutralize a belief or posit a phantasy. It has no thesis to oppose to the one it expresses, and therefore "only one thesis" is there.

Two images surround the passage, and Husserl withdraws both. Just before, expression is an "intentional medium" that "reflects, so to speak, every other intentionality in form and content", "depicts it in its own colouring" and "imprints on it its own form of 'conceptuality'"; the talk of "mirroring or depicting" is "to be taken with caution, since the imagery mediating its application could easily mislead" (Hua III/1 286). Just after, the picture of strata is restricted: "too much must not be asked of the image of a stratification", for expression "is not something like a lacquer laid over, or like a garment drawn over it; it is a mental forming" (*eine geistige Formung*) that exercises "new intentional functions" on the lower layer and undergoes intentional functions from it (Hua III/1 288). The mirror says too little production, the lacquer too little unity. "Forming" names what the correction in the first sentence allowed: expression forms the sense conceptually and does nothing else to it. The images in §114 had to be cleared of illusion; here they have to be cleared of externality.

The same page names what the new form brings: the sorts of "universality" (*,,Allgemeinheit"*), from that of every expressive moment, "is", "not", "and", "if", to that of general names like "man" against proper names like "Bruno" (Hua III/1 288). §126 draws the consequence the passage left implicit. Expression is essentially incomplete because it is general: "it lies in the sense of the universality belonging to the essence of expressing that never all the particularities of the expressed can reflect themselves in the expression". "The layer of meaning is not, and in principle not, a kind of reduplication of the lower layer". Whole dimensions of variability, the degrees of clarity among them, "do not 'express themselves' at all" (Hua III/1 291). An unproductive layer that adds a form and cannot carry all the content is not a copy. The conceptual form is the one thing expression adds, and it costs the particulars.

§127 then puts the "one thesis" under strain. If the medium of expression is "specifically doxic", and "all expressing is … a doxic act in the pregnant sense, i.e. a certainty of belief", then expression "can only express certainties". An uncertain wish cannot be expressed directly as "May S be p"; the faithful expression must be indirect, "Perhaps may S be p" (Hua III/1 293). Here the expressing layer and the expressed no longer have one thesis in any simple sense: the expression is a certainty about a modalized wish. Husserl presents this view conditionally, "if we let this conception count as correct" (*Lassen wir diese Auffassung als richtige gelten*), and leaves the question of §127's title open. The thesis of §124.4 thus holds without qualification only for direct expression of non-modalized acts. For the rest, expression's single contribution, conceptual form, turns out to be doxic form, and the thesis of the expression is the doxic thesis the expressed act harbours, the one §117 found in every act. The unproductive layer is unproductive because belief already lies in what it expresses.

## §129, Hua III/1 297–299: the noema's own "content" and "object"

Reads [§129.1–2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#content-and-object-content-as-sense).

§129 takes the distinction between the content and the object of a presentation, which psychology and Twardowski had drawn on the side of the act, and finds it again inside the noema. The noema has a "content", its "sense", and through that sense it refers to "its" object. The content is no longer a real part of the experience, a mental image or a psychic datum; it is the sense, an ideal structure on the side of the correlate. And the reference to the object is no longer only something the act does: the noema itself refers. The quotation marks around "content", "sense" and "its" show that each of the three inherited words is being redefined as it is used.

> Every noema has a "content", namely its "sense", and refers through it to "its" object.
>
> In recent times one often hears it praised as a great advance that the fundamental distinction between act, content and object has at last been gained. The three words in this combination have by now become slogans, especially since Twardowski's fine treatise. However great and undoubted the merit of this author was, in having acutely discussed certain generally current confusions and made their errors evident, it must nevertheless be said that he (which is not to be counted against him as blame) did not get substantially beyond what was well known to the philosophers of earlier generations (despite their incautious confusions) in clarifying the conceptual essences concerned. A radical advance was simply not possible before a systematic phenomenology of consciousness. With phenomenologically unclarified concepts like "act", "content", "object" of "presentations" we are not helped. What cannot be called act, and above all what cannot be called content of a presentation and presentation itself! And what can be so called must itself be known scientifically.

German:

> Jedes Noema hat einen „Inhalt“, nämlich seinen „Sinn“, und bezieht sich durch ihn auf „seinen“ Gegenstand.
>
> In neuerer Zeit hört man es oft als einen großen Fortschritt preisen, daß nun endlich die grundlegende Unterscheidung zwischen Akt, Inhalt und Gegenstand gewonnen sei. Die drei Worte in dieser Zusammenstellung sind nachgerade zu Schlagworten geworden, insbesondere seit der schönen Abhandlung Twardowskis. Indessen so groß und zweifellos das Verdienst dieses Autors war, gewisse allgemein übliche Vermengungen scharfsinnig erörtert und ihre Fehler evident gemacht zu haben, so muß doch gesagt werden, daß er (was ihm nicht etwa als Tadel anzurechnen ist) in der Klärung der zugehörigen begrifflichen Wesen nicht erheblich darüber hinausgekommen ist, was den Philosophen der früheren Generationen (trotz ihrer unvorsichtigen Vermengungen) wohlbekannt war. Ein radikaler Fortschritt war eben von einer systematischen Phänomenologie des Bewußtseins gar nicht möglich. Mit phänomenologisch ungeklärten Begriffen wie ,,Akt“, ,,Inhalt“, ,,Gegenstand“ der ,,Vorstellungen“ ist uns nicht geholfen. Was kann nicht alles Akt und zumal was nicht alles Inhalt einer Vorstellung und Vorstellung selbst heißen. Und was so heißen kann, gilt es selbst wissenschaftlich zu erkennen.

*Note on the text.* "von einer systematischen Phänomenologie" [from a systematic phenomenology] is an OCR slip for *vor* [before]: as printed, the sentence says a radical advance was impossible *from* a systematic phenomenology, the opposite of the argument. Kersten's translation (CW2) has "before", and the rendering above follows the sense; the German is quoted as the file has it. The file breaks the line "Zusammen-/stellung" at a running head; the quotation joins it and omits the note marker after "Twardowskis" (the note gives Twardowski's *Zur Lehre vom Inhalt und Gegenstand der Vorstellungen*, Vienna 1894). The last German sentence ends with a full stop where the sense is exclamatory.

The thesis is set off as "title and goal" of the discussion, and its four quotation marks fall on the words, not the structure. "Has", "namely", "refers through it" are unquoted: the structure is asserted. "Content", "sense", "its" are quoted: the terms are borrowed and provisionally assigned. "Content" is taken from "the usual equivocal talk of content of consciousness", and Husserl says so in the sentence before: "as content we take the 'sense'" (*Als Inhalt fassen wir den „Sinn“*, Hua III/1 297). The verb *fassen* marks a decision, not a discovery. "Namely" (*nämlich*) then identifies the borrowed word with the noematic sense of §124, and the identification is the point. Of all the things "content" had meant, the real parts of the experience, the sensations, the mental image, the one retained is the one that is not in the experience at all. "Its" (*„seinen“*) in quotation marks reproduces the possessive of the preceding sentence, where consciousness refers to something objective "as 'its' own". The object belongs to the noema only as the object the noema means, and the quotation marks keep that ownership intentional.

The polemic that follows does not dispute the triad. It disputes its status. The three words "have by now become slogans" (*nachgerade zu Schlagworten geworden*); the distinction is "praised as a great advance" that "has at last been gained". "At last" (*endlich*) is the eulogists' word, quoted with irony. Twardowski is praised in a concessive sentence whose concessions take up most of its length: "however great and undoubted" his merit, and "(which is not to be counted against him as blame)", he did not get "substantially beyond" what earlier philosophers knew "despite their incautious confusions". The double parenthesis softens the verdict and sharpens it: Twardowski sorted confusions, but he worked with the same unclarified concepts as those who confused them. The sentence with the OCR slip gives the reason. A radical advance "was simply not possible" before a systematic phenomenology of consciousness. The claim is general, and its exclamation, "what cannot be called act, and content, and presentation!", shows why. A distinction among words that each cover a dozen things is no distinction until the things are separated.

What separates them is the step Husserl credits to his own *Investigations*: "the phenomenological setting off of 'matter' and 'quality'", and "the idea of 'intentional essence' in its separation from 'cognitive essence'". He calls it a first, "and, as it might seem to me, necessary step" (Hua III/1 298). In the Fifth Investigation the intentional essence was "the unity of both", quality and matter, distinguished "in the descriptive content of every act" (§21, Hua XIX/1 431). §129 relocates that unity. The "one-sidedness of the noetic direction of view" in which the distinctions were drawn "overcomes itself easily" by attending to the noematic parallels. Quality becomes the thetic character of the noema, and the word "quality", "stemming from contemporary (Brentanian) psychology", now "seems to me little fitting" (*wenig passend*). Matter, "the 'what' that receives from the 'quality' its positing characterization", "corresponds" to the noematic kernel (Hua III/1 298). The self-correction is terminological and material at once: Husserl drops Brentano's word and moves the concept from the act's real content to its correlate. "Easily" (*leicht*) claims more than the passage shows, since the relocation is exactly what the rest of the chapter has to work out.

The second half of the section shows why the triad must be found again inside the noema. The full noesis relates to the full noema "as its intentional and full what", but that cannot be the relation meant when consciousness is said to refer to its object, for the noema itself contains a kernel and its thetic characters. The ego's "glance" goes "through the noematic kernel", and following it we are referred to "an innermost moment of the noema": "not the kernel itself, but something that makes up, so to speak, the necessary central point of the kernel and functions as 'bearer'" of the noematically modified properties (Hua III/1 299). So "not only for 'consciousness'", but "for the noema taken in itself" the difference between "content" and "object" must be made: the noema "possesses a 'content', 'by means of' which (*„mittels“*) it refers to the object", and the object "is the same as that of the noesis" (Hua III/1 299). The quotation marks on "by means of" leave open what kind of means this is. The chapter's next sections answer. §130 delimits the noematic sense as the noema's "fixed content". §131 names the bearer "the determinable X", the "pure X in abstraction from all predicates", and draws two concepts of object, the object simpliciter and the "object in the how of its determinations". The thesis of §129 is thus the root of the chapter: content becomes sense, and the object that the triad had placed outside consciousness reappears inside the sense as its central point, the same X for many senses. That is the analysis Husserl means by a "radical advance", and the slogan could not give it.

## §131, Hua III/1 302–303: the determinable X

Reads [§131.1–2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#the-object-the-determinable-x-in-the-noematic-sense).

The "object" in the noema, for Husserl, is not a further predicate and not the sum of the predicates. It is the "something" they are predicates of: an empty point of unity, "the pure X in abstraction from all predicates", which stays identical while the predicates change and which can be determined further. With it §131 splits the noematic concept of object in two. There is the "object simpliciter", the X, and the "object in the how of its determinations", the X with the predicates this act prescribes. The second is the sense. Because every sense has such an X, and X's of different senses can coincide, different senses can refer to the same object. The rest of the book's account of identity, verification and constitution runs on that X.

> The predicates are, however, predicates of "something", and this "something" also belongs, and evidently inseparably, to the kernel in question: it is the central point of unity of which we spoke above. It is the point of connection or "bearer" of the predicates, but by no means a unity of them in the sense in which some complex, some combination of the predicates would be called a unity. It must necessarily be distinguished from them, although not set beside them and separated from them, just as conversely they are its predicates: unthinkable without it and yet distinguishable from it. […] there separates itself out as central noematic moment: the "object", the "objective", the "identical", the "determinable subject of its possible predicates" — the pure X in abstraction from all predicates — and it separates itself from these predicates, or more precisely, from the predicate-noemata.
>
> […] Thus there lies in every noema such a pure object-something as point of unity, and at the same time we see how in noematic respect two concepts of object are to be distinguished: this pure point of unity, this noematic "object simpliciter", and the "object in the how of its determinations" — including the indeterminacies that "remain open" in each case and are co-meant in this mode. […] The "sense" of which we have spoken repeatedly is this noematic "object in the how" with all that the description characterized above can evidently find in it and express conceptually.

German:

> Die Prädikate sind aber Prädikate von ,,etwas", und dieses ,,etwas" gehört auch mit, und offenbar unabtrennbar, zu dem fraglichen Kern: es ist der zentrale Einheitspunkt, von dem wir oben gesprochen haben. Es ist der Verknüpfungspunkt oder ,,Träger" der Prädikate, aber keineswegs Einheit derselben in dem Sinne, in dem irgendein Komplex, irgendeine Verbindung der Prädikate Einheit zu nennen wäre. Es ist von ihnen notwendig zu unterscheiden, obschon nicht neben sie zu stellen und von ihnen zu trennen, so wie umgekehrt sie selbst seine Prädikate sind: ohne ihn undenkbar und doch von ihm unterscheidbar. […] dann scheidet sich evident der identische intentionale ,,Gegenstand'' von den wechselnden und veränderlichen ,,Prädikaten''. Es scheidet sich als zentrales noematisches Moment aus: der ,,Gegenstand'', das ,,Objekt'', das ,,Identische'', das ,,bestimmbare Subjekt seiner möglichen Prädikate'' — das pure X in Abstraktion von allen Prädikaten — und es scheidet sich ab von diesen Prädikaten, oder genauer, von den Prädikatnoemen.
>
> […] So liegt also in jedem Noema solch ein pures Gegenstandsetwas als Einheitspunkt, und zugleich sehen wir, wie in noematischer Hinsicht zweierlei Gegenstandsbegriffe zu unterscheiden sind: dieser pure Einheitspunkt, dieser noematische „Gegenstand schlechthin“ und der „Gegenstand im Wie seiner Bestimmtheiten“ — hinzugerechnet die jeweiligen „offen bleibenden“ und in diesem Modus mitvermeinten Unbestimmtheiten. […] Der „Sinn“, von dem wir wiederholt sprachen, ist dieser noematische „Gegenstand im Wie“ mit all dem, was die oben charakterisierte Beschreibung an ihm evident vorzufinden und begrifflich auszudrücken vermag.

*Note on the text.* The file sets most of §131 as short print lines with line-end hyphens, which the quotation joins. In "ohne ihn … von ihm" the masculine pronouns refer to the "bearer" (*Träger*) or "point of connection" (*Verknüpfungspunkt*).

The first sentence turns on a small word, and Husserl quotes it twice. Predicates are predicates "of 'something'" (*von ,,etwas"*), and "this 'something'" belongs to the kernel. The preposition does the work the "of" did in §112: it makes the something the term of a relation, not one more item in a list. The second sentence states the consequence in a negative contrast. The something is the "point of connection or 'bearer'" of the predicates, "but by no means a unity of them" in the sense in which a complex or combination of predicates is a unity. A bundle theory of the object is refused in a subordinate clause. The third sentence gives the relation its logical form: the X is "necessarily to be distinguished" from the predicates, "although not to be set beside them and separated from them", and they in turn are "unthinkable without it and yet distinguishable from it". Distinction without separation is the formula Husserl uses for dependent moments. The X is a dependent moment of the sense, and the predicates are dependent on it.

The list that names it has five members, and each is quoted: "object", "objective", "identical", "determinable subject of its possible predicates", and then, unquoted and set between dashes, "the pure X in abstraction from all predicates". The quoted names are borrowed from ordinary and logical talk; the unquoted phrase is Husserl's own formula, and it is algebraic. An X is a variable: a place that can be filled, not a thing already filled. "Determinable" (*bestimmbar*) adds the temporal dimension. The X is the subject of "possible" predicates, of the ones still open, and the second concept of object includes "the indeterminacies that 'remain open' in each case and are co-meant in this mode". The identity of the X is established in a description of change: the object "gives itself 'otherwise'" (*„anders gibt“*), "'it' shows itself only from different sides", "'it', the identical, changes", "gains in beauty, loses in use-value" (Hua III/1 302). The pronouns in quotation marks are the X caught in ordinary speech. Whatever changes, "it" is what changes.

Then comes the claim that makes the X the hinge of the chapter. "Several act-noemata have here everywhere different kernels, but in such a way that they nevertheless join together into a unity of identity, in which the 'something', the determinable, that lies in each kernel is conscious as identical." Separate acts too, two perceptions or a perception and a memory, can join into a "harmonious" unity in which "the something of the initially separate kernels", determined now one way and now another, is "conscious as the same something" (Hua III/1 302–303). Sense is "in general changing from noema to noema"; the X can be the same. Hence the final formula: "No 'sense' without the 'something' and again without 'determining content'" (*Kein „Sinn“ ohne das „etwas“*), and "through the sense-bearer belonging to the sense (as empty X)" "not only does every sense have its 'object', but different senses relate to the same object" (Hua III/1 303). The relation to the object, which §129 found inside the noema, is here given its structure: a sense refers by having an X, and two senses refer to one object when their X's coincide in a synthesis.

Husserl adds a caution that the inference needs. The X is not something "the subsequent analysis and description first puts in"; it "really lies in the correlate of consciousness" as "condition of the possibility of the evident description and before it" (Hua III/1 303). The phrase "condition of the possibility" is borrowed from transcendental philosophy, and it is the strongest claim in the section. The evidence offered for it is the description itself: the X "separates itself evidently" once the description is carried out "in pure adequation". That makes the claim depend on the reliability of noematic description, which is what the chapter is trying to establish. Husserl also says "cautiously 'sense', not 'kernel'" (*vorsichtig „Sinn“, nicht „Kern“*): the full kernel will need a further dimension, the mode of fullness of §132, which the description that defines the sense does not capture.

The later sections cash the X at every step. §132 gives each member of a synthetic act "its X with its 'determining content'", and in nominalization a ray of actuality goes to the uppermost synthetic X (Hua III/1 304). §138 describes experience as a continuous progress of appearances "with one and the same determinable X", and disappointment as the "determining otherwise" of "the X always conscious as the same" (Hua III/1 320). §142 defines the adequate reason-thesis by a sense that would leave nothing "open" for "the determinable X" (Hua III/1 329). §150 directs the constitutive inquiry at "the identical, the X of the sense", and calls every look at appearances, noeses or hyle a "reflection" because it is a deflection from the "straight direction of view upon the X" (Hua III/1 349). The X begins in §131 as an empty point of unity in one noema. By §§142–150 it is what reason must determine completely and what constitution must explain: the idea of the thing is the idea of a fully determined X. The emptiness of the X is what lets it carry that load. An X that already had its predicates could not be the same through all of them.

## §133, Hua III/1 305–306: sense narrowed, *Satz* widened

Reads [§133.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#the-noematic-proposition-thetic-and-synthetic-propositions-propositions-in-the-domain-of-representations).

§133 makes two terminological moves at once and corrects the *Logical Investigations* in both. "Sense" is narrowed to the "matter" alone: the noematic "object in the how", without the thetic character that posits it. "Proposition" (*Satz*) is widened to name the unity of a sense and a thetic character in any act whatever, so that a perception, a wish and a command have their *Satz* as a judgment does. The *Investigations* had included the quality in the sense. *Ideas I* takes it out and gives the unity of sense and thesis a name of its own, and the name it chooses is the word for what is set or posited.

> In the *Logical Investigations* they [the thetic moments] were taken from the outset (under the title quality) into the concept of sense (of "significational essence"), and thus within this unity the two components "matter" (sense in the present conception) and quality were distinguished. But it seems more fitting to define the term sense merely as that "matter", and then to designate the unity of sense and thetic character as proposition. We then have one-membered propositions (as with perceptions and other thetic intuitions) and many-membered, synthetic propositions, such as predicative doxic propositions (judgments), propositions of conjecture with predicatively articulated matter, and so on. One-membered as well as many-membered, further, are propositions of liking, wish-propositions, command-propositions and so on. The concept of proposition is thereby admittedly extended extraordinarily and perhaps disconcertingly, but still within the frame of an important unity of essence. It must constantly be kept in view that the concepts sense and proposition contain for us nothing of expression and conceptual signification, but on the other hand comprise under them all expressed propositions, or proposition-significations.

German:

> In den „Logischen Untersuchungen“ wurden sie (unter dem Titel Qualität) von vornherein in den Begriff des Sinnes (des „bedeutungsmäßigen Wesens“) genommen und somit in dieser Einheit die beiden Komponenten „Materie“ (Sinn in der jetzigen Fassung) und Qualität unterschieden. Doch scheint es passender, den Terminus Sinn bloß als jene „Materie“ zu definieren, und dann die Einheit von Sinn und thetischem Charakter als Satz zu bezeichnen. Wir haben dann eingliedrige Sätze (wie bei den Wahrnehmungen und sonstigen thetischen Anschauungen) und mehrgliedrige, synthetische Sätze, wie prädikative doxische Sätze (Urteile), Vermutungssätze mit prädikativ gegliederter Materie usw. Eingliedrig wie mehrgliedrig sind ferner Gefallenssätze, Wunschsätze, Befehlssätze usw. Der Begriff des Satzes ist damit freilich außerordentlich und vielleicht befremdlich erweitert, aber doch im Rahmen einer wichtigen Wesenseinheit. Beständig ist ja im Auge zu behalten, daß die Begriffe Sinn und Satz für uns nichts von Ausdruck und begrifflicher Bedeutung enthalten, andererseits aber alle ausdrücklichen Sätze, bzw. Satzbedeutungen unter sich befassen.

The correction is stated with a historical parenthesis that does half its work. In the *Investigations* the thetic moments were taken "from the outset" (*von vornherein*) into the concept of sense, there called "significational essence", so that the sense had two components, "'matter' (sense in the present conception) and quality". The parenthesis "sense in the present conception" already performs the redefinition before the next sentence announces it. The old whole was the sense; the old part is now the sense. The note gives the place, the Fifth Investigation §§20–21, and adds a second correction: the neutral "leaving-undecided" (*„Dahingestellt“-haben*) "now counts for us, of course, not as there as a 'quality' (thesis) beside other qualities, but as a modification that 'mirrors' all qualities and thus whole acts in general" (note 134, Hua III/1 305). The two corrections belong together. Once neutrality is no quality among qualities but the shadow of every quality (§114), the thetic character cannot be a component of sense on a level with the matter; it has to be what is added to a sense to make it a posited sense.

The verb of the decision is modest: "it seems more fitting" (*scheint es passender*). The same register appeared at §129, where the Brentanian word "quality" "seems to me now little fitting" (*wenig passend*). Husserl revises his terms as matters of fit, not of error. Yet the revision moves a boundary. In the *Investigations* the intentional essence was "the unity of both", quality and matter, distinguished "in the descriptive content of every act" (§21, Hua XIX/1 431). In *Ideas I* that unity is not abandoned but renamed: it becomes the *Satz*, and it lies in the noema, not in the act's real content. The sense, narrowed to matter, becomes the stable core that many *Sätze* can share. One and the same sense can be believed, doubted, wished or merely thought, and each time the *Satz* differs.

"Proposition" is the usual English for *Satz*, and it misses the range §133 gives the word. *Satz* means a sentence, a proposition, a theorem, and it is the noun of *setzen* [to set, to posit]. Husserl's extension draws on the last sense. The unity of sense and "thetic character", of a matter and its positing, is what has been set. That is why perceptions have one-membered *Sätze* and wishes and commands have *Sätze*: each posits its sense in its own mode. Husserl concedes the strain: the concept is extended "extraordinarily and perhaps disconcertingly" (*außerordentlich und vielleicht befremdlich*), "but still within the frame of an important unity of essence". "Admittedly" (*freilich*) and "but still" (*aber doch*) give the objection its due and keep the extension. The final sentence guards against the reading the English invites. The concepts sense and *Satz* "contain for us nothing of expression and conceptual signification", though they "comprise under them all expressed propositions". After §124 this follows: *Bedeutung* is the meaning of expressions, *Sinn* is pre-expressive, and so the *Satz* that unites a sense with a thesis is pre-expressive too. A perceptual *Satz* is not a sentence about a perception.

Kersten's translation (CW2) answers the difficulty by refusing "proposition": it renders *Satz* as "positum" ("to designate the unity of sense and posited characteristic as 'positum'"; "liking-posita, wish-posita, command-posita"). The Latin keeps the root of *setzen* and drops the linguistic sense. The cost is that the word loses the bridge to logic that Husserl wants it to keep, since §134 builds an "apophantic morphology" on exactly these *Sätze*, and judgments are *Sätze* in the logician's sense as well. Neither English word holds both. The commentary's "proposition" keeps the bridge and loses the positing; Kersten's "positum" keeps the positing and loses the bridge. The German word holds both because the logical *Satz* is, for Husserl, a special case of the posited.

The next paragraph states why the extension matters: the concepts name "an abstract layer belonging to the full tissue of all noemata", and it is "of great consequence" to see that this layer "really has its place in all act-spheres" (Hua III/1 306). Perception therefore needs the concepts "intuition-sense" and "intuition-proposition", and from "the perceived object as such" one can extract the "thing-sense of this perception", which "is a different one from perception to perception (even with regard to 'the same' thing)". Taken with its intuitive fullness this sense yields "a determinate and very important concept of appearance" (*Erscheinung*). The narrowing of sense to matter is what makes this possible. The thing-sense varies from perception to perception while the X stays the same (§131), and the thetic character, which belongs to the *Satz*, can vary independently of both. The Fourth Section's second chapter needs exactly this articulation. §136 defines the reason-character as a character that belongs to a positing when its sense is originarily fulfilled; reason is a property of *Sätze*, and it can be one only because sense and thesis have been separated and their unity named.

## §135, Hua III/1 312–313: what "actual" means for an object given through senses

Reads [§135.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#object-and-consciousness-transition-to-the-phenomenology-of-reason).

At the end of the First Chapter Husserl finds that "constitution" has been used in two senses. In the wide sense an object is constituted wherever consciousness carries the consciousness of an identical X, "whether it is actual or not". In the narrow sense, which the analyses had mostly in mind, constitution is constitution of actual objects. The turn to a phenomenology of reason is the turn to the second sense, and it is staged as a run of questions about one word, "actual" (*wirklich*). An object is given to consciousness only through senses and propositions. What, then, can its actuality mean, if not something about those senses and propositions themselves? The Second Chapter's answer, that actuality is the correlate of a certain kind of positing, reason, is prepared by the form of the question.

> If we consider more exactly what the essential connections between object and consciousness characterized in the preceding reflection say or had to say, an ambiguity makes itself felt, and following it up we notice that we stand before a great turning point of our investigations. We assign to an object manifolds of "propositions", or of experiences of a certain noematic content, and in such a way that through it syntheses of identification become a priori possible, by virtue of which the object can and must stand there as the same. The X in the different acts or act-noemata, furnished with different "content of determination", is necessarily conscious as the same. But is it really the same? And is the object itself "actual"? Could it not be non-actual, while the manifold harmonious and even intuitively fulfilled propositions — propositions of whatever essential content — ran their course in consciousness?
>
> […] In our talk of the noetic-noematic "constitution" of objectivities, for example of thing-objectivities, there lay therefore an ambiguity. We were thinking above all, at any rate, of "actual" objects, of things of the "actual world", or at least of "an" actual world in general. But what then does this "actual" mean for objects that are, after all, given in conformity with consciousness only through senses and propositions? What does it mean for these propositions themselves, for the essential kind of these noemata, or of the parallel noeses? What does it mean for the particular ways of their structure according to form and fullness? How does this structure particularize itself according to the particular regions of objects? […] When, it can be asked everywhere, is the noematically "meant" identity of the X "actual identity" instead of "merely" meant identity, and what does this "merely meant" mean everywhere?

German:

> Überlegen wir genauer, was die in der vorstehenden Betrachtung gekennzeichneten Wesenszusammenhänge zwischen Gegenstand und Bewußtsein besagen oder besagen mußten, so wird uns eine Doppeldeutigkeit fühlbar, und wir merken ihr nachgehend, daß wir vor einem großen Wendepunkt unserer Untersuchungen stehen. Wir ordnen einem Gegenstand Mannigfaltigkeiten von ,,Sätzen'', bzw. von Erlebnissen eines gewissen noematischen Gehaltes zu, und zwar so, daß durch ihn Synthesen der Identifikation a priori möglich werden, vermöge deren der Gegenstand als derselbe dastehen kann und muß. Das X in den verschiedenen Akten, bzw. Aktnoemen mit verschiedenem ,,Bestimmungsgehalt'' ausgestattet, ist notwendig bewußt als dasselbe. Aber ist es wirklich dasselbe? Und ist der Gegenstand selbst ,,wirklich''? Könnte er nicht unwirklich sein, während doch die mannigfaltigen einstimmigen und sogar anschauungserfüllten Sätze — Sätze von welchem Wesensgehalt auch immer — bewußtseinsmäßig abliefen?
>
> […] In unserer Rede von der noetisch-noematischen „Konstitution“ von Gegenständlichkeiten, z.B. Dinggegenständlichkeiten, lag also eine Zweideutigkeit. Vorzüglich dachten wir bei ihr jedenfalls an „wirkliche“ Gegenstände, an Dinge der „wirklichen Welt“ oder mindestens „einer“ wirklichen Welt überhaupt. Was besagt dann aber dieses „wirklich“ für Gegenstände, die bewußtseinsmäßig doch nur durch Sinne und Sätze gegeben sind? Was besagt es für diese Sätze selbst, für die Wesensartung dieser Noemen, bzw. der parallelen Noesen? Was besagt es für die besonderen Weisen ihres Baues nach Form und Fülle? Wie besondert sich dieser Bau nach den besonderen Gegenstandsregionen? […] Wann ist, kann überall gefragt werden, die noematisch „vermeinte“ Identität des X „wirkliche Identität“ statt „bloß“ vermeinter, und was besagt überall dieses „bloß vermeint“?

*Note on the text.* The first paragraph is set in short print lines with line-end hyphens, joined here. Kersten's translation (CW2) italicizes "is" in "is it actually the same" and "itself" in "is the object itself 'actual'", which suggests Husserl spaced both words; the file shows no emphasis, and nothing below rests on it.

The two questions at the centre use the same word twice, and the quotation marks change its job. "But is it really (*wirklich*) the same?" uses the word as an adverb, unquoted, as anyone would: is the identity real or only apparent? "And is the object itself 'actual' (*,,wirklich''*)?" quotes the word and makes it a predicate. German has one word where English needs two, "really" and "actual", and the passage moves from the first use to the second. The adverb asks whether the synthesis of identification is right. The quoted adjective asks what kind of property the object would have if it were. The first question can be asked within the analyses so far; the second cannot, since those analyses described the X as "necessarily conscious as the same" without deciding anything about its actuality.

The third question states the sceptical possibility in full and grants it the most favourable case. Could the object be non-actual "while the manifold harmonious and even intuitively fulfilled propositions" ran their course? "Even" (*sogar*) concedes that intuitive fulfilment does not by itself settle actuality, and the dash-enclosed "propositions of whatever essential content" extends the doubt to all regions. The question is not answered here, and Husserl says why: "we are interested not in the factualities of consciousness and its courses, but in the essential problems" (Hua III/1 312). The subject of consciousness "judges about actuality, asks about it, conjectures, doubts it, decides the doubt, and performs 'adjudications of reason'" (*,,Rechtsprechungen der Vernunft''*). The phrase in quotation marks returns from §110, where only non-neutralized noeses "are subject to an 'adjudication of reason'" and for the neutralized "the question of reason and unreason makes no sense" (Hua III/1 249). The turn of §135 thus redeems a phrase introduced twenty-five sections earlier. Reason was there a property only positional consciousness can have; here the right of that positing, "and correlatively the essence of 'actuality'", is to be clarified "purely phenomenologically".

The second paragraph turns the question on the book's own vocabulary. "In our talk of … 'constitution' there lay therefore an ambiguity." Husserl had been thinking "above all" of "'actual' objects", things of "the 'actual world', or at least of 'an' actual world in general". The quotation marks multiply: "actual" twice and "an" once. The quoted indefinite article concedes that the phenomenology, working after the exclusion of the natural world, could not have meant this world; it meant at most the form of a world that is actual. Then come four questions, three opening with "what does it mean" (*Was besagt*). The anaphora carries the argument. Each question moves the place where "actual" must be found one step further into the correlate: from objects, to "these propositions themselves", to their "essential kind" and their noeses, to "the particular ways of their structure according to form and fullness". The answer is assumed in the order of the questions. If objects are given "only through senses and propositions", actuality must show itself in a feature of propositions, and the feature must lie in their form and fullness.

Husserl then distinguishes the two senses explicitly. The question is how to describe the connections of consciousness that make "an object simpliciter (which in the sense of ordinary talk always means an actual object)" necessary "in its actuality". "In the wider sense", however, an object "'constitutes' itself — 'whether it is actual or not' — in certain connections of consciousness" which "carry an insightful unity in themselves" insofar as they "bring with them the consciousness of an identical X" (Hua III/1 313). The wide sense is the First Chapter's: constitution of an X. The narrow sense is the Second Chapter's task. The section ends by turning the question on the opposite term as well. When is "the noematically 'meant' identity of the X 'actual identity' instead of 'merely' meant, and what does this 'merely meant' mean everywhere?" The quotation marks now fall on "merely". The phenomenology of reason must clarify both sides of the contrast, since "merely meant" is not a privation that explains itself.

The Second Chapter answers in the terms §135 prepared. §136 defines the reason-character as belonging to a positing when its sense is fulfilled in originary givenness, and calls that givenness the positing's "original ground of right". §142 states the equivalence the questions pointed to: "to every 'truly existing' object there corresponds in principle the idea of a possible consciousness" in which the object can be grasped originarily and perfectly, and conversely (Hua III/1 329). "Actual" becomes the correlate of a perfect reason-positing, and "merely meant" becomes the case where no such positing is possible or carried out. The Fourth Section's title, "Reason and Actuality", names the pair the questions of §135 bind together. What the passage contributes is the claim that the question of actuality is a question about the structure of propositions according to form and fullness. Once that is granted, reason can only be a character of propositions, and actuality only its correlate.

## §136, Hua III/1 316: reason as motivated belonging

Reads [§136.3–4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#the-first-basic-form-of-reason-consciousness-originarily-giving-seeing).

Reason, in §136, is a character of a positing, and it belongs to the positing "then and only then" when the positing rests on a sense fulfilled in originary givenness. Husserl calls this belonging "motivation", and "rational motivation" when the motivating ground is originary: the positing of a thing "belongs" to its bodily appearing because the appearing motivates it. Evidence is then defined by the same relation, as "the unity of a reason-positing with what essentially motivates it". The definition does not add a feeling or a mark to the positing. It names a relation between the positing and the way its sense is given, and it changes what "belong" means.

> A specific reason-character, however, is proper to the positing-character as a distinction that accrues to it essentially then and only then when it is positing on the ground of a fulfilled, originarily giving sense, and not only of a sense in general.
>
> Here, and in every kind of reason-consciousness, the talk of belonging takes on a meaning of its own. For example: to every bodily appearing of a thing the positing belongs; it is not only in general one with this appearing (say even as a mere general fact — which is out of the question here), it is one with it in a peculiar way, it is "motivated" by it, and yet again not merely in general, but "rationally motivated". The same is said by: the positing has in the originary givenness its original ground of right. In other givennesses the ground of right need not be lacking, but the privilege of the original ground is lacking, which plays its distinguished role in the relative estimation of grounds of right.
>
> […] Insight, evidence in general, is therefore a quite distinguished occurrence; as to its "kernel" it is the unity of a reason-positing with what essentially motivates it, where this whole situation can be understood noetically, but also noematically.

German:

> Ein spezifischer Vernunftcharakter ist aber dem Setzungscharakter zu eigen als eine Auszeichnung, die ihm wesensmäßig dann und nur dann zukommt, wenn er Setzung auf Grund eines erfüllten, originär gebenden Sinnes und nicht nur überhaupt eines Sinnes ist.
>
> Hier und in jeder Art von Vernunftbewußtsein nimmt die Rede vom Zugehören eine eigene Bedeutung an. Zum Beispiel: Zu jedem Leibhaft-Erscheinen eines Dinges gehört die Setzung, sie ist nicht nur überhaupt mit diesem Erscheinen eins (etwa gar als bloßes allgemeines Faktum — das hier außer Frage ist), sie ist mit ihm eigenartig eins, sie ist durch es „motiviert“, und doch wieder nicht bloß überhaupt, sondern „vernünftig motiviert“. Dasselbe besagt: Die Setzung hat in der originären Gegebenheit ihren ursprünglichen Rechtsgrund. In anderen Gegebenheiten braucht der Rechtsgrund nicht etwa zu fehlen, es fehlt aber der Vorzug des ursprünglichen Grundes, der in der relativen Schätzung der Rechtsgründe seine ausgezeichnete Rolle spielt.
>
> […] Einsicht, überhaupt Evidenz ist also ein ganz ausgezeichnetes Vorkommnis; dem „Kerne“ nach ist es die Einheit einer Vernunftsetzung mit dem sie wesensmäßig Motivierenden, wobei diese ganze Sachlage noetisch, aber auch noematisch verstanden werden kann.

The definition is a biconditional, and the noun before it is modest. The reason-character is a "distinction" (*Auszeichnung*) "proper to" the positing-character, accruing "essentially then and only then" (*wesensmäßig dann und nur dann*) when the positing rests on a fulfilled, originarily giving sense "and not only on a sense in general". The final clause sets the contrast class. Every positing rests on some sense, since that is what §133 meant by a *Satz*. Reason distinguishes those whose sense is given in the mode of originary fulfilment, "bodily" (*leibhaft*) in perception, "originarily" in the seeing of essences. The paragraph before had prepared the vocabulary: in the noema "the character of bodiliness (as originary fulfilledness) is fused with the pure sense" (Hua III/1 315), and the sense with this character underlies the positing. Reason is thus defined entirely within the structure of the *Satz*: a thesis, a sense, and the mode of that sense's fullness. §135's question, what "actual" can mean for objects given only through senses and propositions, gets its first answer in this form.

The second paragraph says that "belonging" (*Zugehören*) "takes on a meaning of its own" here, and the example shows the shift in three steps. The positing "belongs" to the bodily appearing. It is "not only in general one with" it, and the parenthesis rules out the weakest reading, "say even as a mere general fact", as "out of the question here". It is "one with it in a peculiar way": it is "'motivated'" by it. And again "not merely in general", but "'rationally motivated'". Each step narrows the relation. Co-occurrence, even lawful co-occurrence, is excluded; motivation is admitted; rational motivation is the case defined. In earlier sections "belong" stated essential connections, as when to every positing "belongs" a field of potential positings (§114). Here belonging acquires a normative sense: the positing belongs to the appearing as a claim belongs to its ground. The quotation marks on both "motivated" and "rationally motivated" mark the words as terms of art.

The term has a history in the book, and the file shows it only in damaged form. At §47 (Hua III/1 101–102) the experiential world is a "nexus of 'motivation'", and a note calls this "phenomenological basic concept of motivation" one that arose for Husserl with the separation of the purely phenomenological sphere in the *Logical Investigations*. The file sets the note as broken footnote lines and loses at least one line between "in contrast to the concept of" and "of that concept of motivation according to which we can say, for example, that willing the end motivates willing the means" (note 73). What the lost words said about the relation of the two concepts cannot be recovered from the file. What survives is enough to show that "motivation" names, from §47 onward, a relation of sense between experiences, the relation of the act that motivates to the act motivated, not a causal one. §136 sharpens it into the relation of right.

The legal vocabulary does the sharpening. "The same is said by" (*Dasselbe besagt*): "the positing has in the originary givenness its original ground of right" (*ursprünglichen Rechtsgrund*). The phrase continues the "adjudications of reason" of §§110 and 135. A ground of right is something a claim can appeal to, and the next sentence makes room for degrees: in other givennesses the ground of right "need not be lacking", but "the privilege of the original ground" is, and this privilege "plays its distinguished role in the relative estimation of grounds of right" (*relativen Schätzung der Rechtsgründe*). Reason is no longer all or nothing. Memory has a ground of right, but not the original one, and the next sections build on the comparison: §138 sets out the "weight" and "force" of motivations and their increase in harmonious fulfilment (Hua III/1 320), and §141 traces mediate reason back to immediate. The biconditional of the first sentence defines the original case; the second paragraph opens the scale on which all other cases are placed.

Evidence is then defined from the same elements. "Insight, evidence in general, is therefore (*also*) a quite distinguished occurrence"; "as to its 'kernel'" it is "the unity of a reason-positing with what essentially motivates it". "Therefore" claims that the definition follows from the analysis of rational motivation. It does follow, but only because evidence has been defined as the case in which motivation is rational and originary. Husserl adds that motivation "fits above all" the relation between the noetic positing and the "noematic proposition in its mode of fulfilledness", and that "'evident proposition'" is "immediately intelligible" in its noematic sense (Hua III/1 316). Evidence is a property of propositions as much as of acts.

The definition carries the rest of the chapter. §137 restricts "insight" (*Einsicht*) to apodictic evidence and takes "evidence" (*Evidenz*) for the genus. §138 divides evidence into adequate and inadequate by whether the motivating fulfilment can be increased. §145 uses §136 as its weapon. If evidence is the unity of a positing with what motivates it, it cannot be "some index of consciousness attached to a judgment" that calls to us "like a mystical voice" (Hua III/1 334), and Husserl names the targets "index- and feeling-theory": an index or a feeling would be a further item beside the positing, and §136 has defined evidence as a relation within it. The polemic of §145 needs no further argument than the change in "belong". What belongs to a positing by motivation is its ground, not a sign attached to it.

## §137, Hua III/1 317–318: *Evidenz* for the genus, *Einsicht* for apodicticity

Reads [§137.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#evidence-and-insight-originary-and-pure-assertoric-and-apodictic-evidence).

In ordinary philosophical usage "evidence" (*Evidenz*) and "insight" (*Einsicht*) are synonyms, and both mean apodictic seeing, the grasp of a necessity. §137 separates them. "Evidence" becomes the name of the whole genus of reason-consciousness that is related to originary givenness, and it covers the "assertoric" seeing of an individual thing as much as the "apodictic" seeing of an essence. "Insight" is kept for apodicticity alone. The separation follows a claim Husserl calls a phenomenological discovery "of the greatest importance": that perceiving a thing and seeing an essential law are "really of one essential genus". The terminology records that claim. Once it is made, perception counts as evidence, though not as insight.

> Evidence and insight are, in the usual pregnant sense, understood as synonymous: as apodictic insight. We want to separate the two words terminologically. We absolutely need a more general word that in its signification spans assertoric seeing and apodictic insight. It is to be regarded as a phenomenological knowledge of the greatest importance that both are really of one essential genus and that, taken still more generally, reason-consciousness in general designates a highest genus of thetic modalities, in which precisely the "seeing" (in the extremely extended sense) related to originary givenness makes up a firmly bounded species. To name the highest genus one now has the choice either (as just now, but going still much further) of extending the signification of the word seeing, or that of the words "insight", "evidence". It may well be most fitting to choose the word evidence for the most general concept; for every reason-thesis characterized by a relation of motivation to originariness of givenness the expression originary evidence would then offer itself. One would further have to distinguish between assertoric and apodictic evidence and leave to the word insight the particular designation of this apodicticity.

German:

> Evidenz und Einsicht werden, im gewöhnlichen prägnanten Sinne, gleichbedeutend verstanden: als apodiktisches Einsehen. Wir wollen die beiden Worte terminologisch trennen. Wir brauchen durchaus ein allgemeineres Wort, das in seiner Bedeutung das assertorische Sehen und das apodiktische Einsehen umspannt. Es ist als eine phänomenologische Erkenntnis von größter Wichtigkeit zu betrachten, daß beide wirklich von einer Wesensgattung sind und daß, noch allgemeiner gefaßt, Vernunftbewußtsein überhaupt eine oberste Gattung von thetischen Modalitäten bezeichnet, in der eben das auf originäre Gegebenheit bezogene ,,Sehen'' (im extrem erweiterten Sinne) eine festbegrenzte Artung ausmacht. Man hat nun, die oberste Gattung zu benennen, die Wahl, entweder (wie soeben, aber noch weit weitergehend) die Bedeutung des Wortes Sehen, oder die der Worte ,,Einsehen'', ,,Evidenz'' auszudehnen. Da dürfte es am passendsten sein, für den allgemeinsten Begriff das Wort Evidenz zu wählen; für jede durch eine Motivationsbeziehung auf Originarität der Gegebenheit charakterisierte Vernunftthesis böte sich dann der Ausdruck originäre Evidenz dar. Es wäre ferner zwischen assertorischer und apodiktischer Evidenz zu unterscheiden und dem Wort Einsicht die besondere Bezeichnung dieser Apodiktizität zu belassen.

*Note on the text.* The file sets the passage as short print lines with line-end hyphens; the quotation joins them.

The German word family carries the problem. "Seeing" (*Sehen*), "insight" (*Einsehen*, *Einsicht*) and the Latin-derived "evidence" (*Evidenz*) all name a kind of seeing, and in German *Einsehen* is visibly a compound of *Sehen*. The passage uses all three. The assertoric is a "seeing" (*Sehen*), the apodictic an "insight" (*Einsehen*), and the genus needs a word that "spans" both. Husserl lays out "the choice" (*die Wahl*) as a choice between extending *Sehen* or extending "'insight', 'evidence'". He has already extended *Sehen* in §136's title, "originarily giving 'seeing'", and he notes that naming the genus by it would require going "still much further". He takes *Evidenz*. The reason is unstated, but the effect is visible. *Sehen* stays the name for the species of reason related to originary givenness, in quotation marks as a term of art; *Einsicht* keeps its ordinary narrow sense, apodicticity; *Evidenz*, the foreign word, loses its narrow sense and becomes the genus. The one word without a native German root is the one free to be redefined.

The mood of the decision is conditional. "It may well be most fitting" (*Da dürfte es am passendsten sein*), "the expression originary evidence would then offer itself" (*böte sich dann … dar*), "one would further have to distinguish" (*Es wäre ferner … zu unterscheiden*). The terminology is proposed, not decreed, as at §133 ("it seems more fitting"). The claim that grounds it is not conditional. "It is to be regarded as a phenomenological knowledge of the greatest importance" that assertoric seeing and apodictic insight "are really of one essential genus". "Really" (*wirklich*) asserts the unity against the usage that separates perceiving from knowing, and the second "that" clause widens it: reason-consciousness in general is "a highest genus of thetic modalities", within which "seeing" related to originary givenness is "a firmly bounded species". The order of the passage matters. The first sentences say a word is "needed" (*Wir brauchen durchaus*); the middle sentence says why, by stating the genus; the end chooses the word. The terminology follows the doctrine. What the doctrine asserts, however, is not argued in the passage. It rests on §136, where the reason-character of every positing was defined by motivation through originary givenness, whether the given was a landscape or an essence. If reason is defined that way, perception and essential insight fall under one genus by definition. The "phenomenological knowledge" is the definition seen from the other side.

What the choice decides is that perception is evidence. Ordinary usage, as the section's first paragraph records, says that in the landscape example "we have a seeing, but not an evidence in the usual pregnant sense of the word, an 'insight'" (Hua III/1 317). After §137 the perception of the landscape is "originary evidence", assertoric and inadequate, and only the arithmetical example is insight. The first paragraph had also named a second difference: the eidetic givenness is "adequate", the empirical "inadequate". The two differences, Husserl says, "may cross under circumstances" (*sich unter Umständen kreuzenden*). Essence against individual and adequate against inadequate are distinct axes. That is why §138 can follow as a section of its own on adequate and inadequate evidence, and why "insight" is bound to apodicticity and not to adequacy.

The later chapters keep the split. §138 divides "every such evidence — the word understood in our extended sense" into adequate evidence, which "in principle can no longer be 'strengthened' or 'weakened'", and inadequate evidence, which admits degrees (Hua III/1 321). The word "evidence" now applies without strain to the inadequate givenness of things. §140's title speaks of "positional and neutral insight", and its example is the essence-proposition, where the phantasy of an essential connection and the insight into it are "equivalent" (Hua III/1 326): insight, as §137 fixed it, belongs to essences, and so it can be had in phantasy. §145 finally sets the "index- and feeling-theory of evidence" against the account §§136–137 have built. A feeling that accompanies a judgment could not be the genus that spans perceiving a thing and seeing a law; a relation of motivation can.

The *Cartesian Meditations* state the crossing that §137 only allows. There adequacy is an idea of perfection that "may in principle lie in the infinite", and the scientist ranks above it "a perfection of evidence of another kind", "that of *apodicticity*", which "can eventually occur also in inadequate evidences" (§6, Hua I 55). The *Meditations* need the crossing for the "I am": §9 recalls "that *adequation* and *apodicticity* of an evidence need not go hand in hand", since transcendental self-experience offers "only a core of what is properly adequately experienced" (Hua I 63). *Ideas I* had already separated the axes and kept the word "insight" for one of them; its terminology made room for the later claim, though its examples paired apodicticity with adequacy throughout.

## §142, Hua III/1 329: true being and the idea of a perfect reason-thesis

Reads [§142.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-3-ideas-i-commentary.md#reason-thesis-and-being).

§142 states the correlation of being and reason as an equivalence. An object is "truly existing" if and only if there is, in principle, the idea of a possible consciousness in which it would be given originarily and with perfect adequacy. The claim runs in both directions, and the second direction carries its weight: if the possibility of such a consciousness is guaranteed, the object is "eo ipso" truly existing. The passage does not say that being is consciousness, or that an actual consciousness must grasp the object. It ties true being to the idea of a possible perfect evidence. Everything turns on that "idea", and §143 has to say at once what kind of idea it is when the object is a thing.

> A general insight soon results here, namely that not only "truly existing object" and "rationally positable" object are equivalent correlates, but also "truly existing" object and object positable in an originary, perfect reason-thesis. To this reason-thesis the object would not be given incompletely, not merely "one-sidedly". The sense underlying it as matter would leave nothing "open" for the determinable X on any side prefigured by the apprehension: no determinability that was not yet fixed determinacy, no sense that would not be fully determined and closed. Since the reason-thesis is to be an originary one, it must have its ground of reason in the originary givenness of what is determined in the full sense: the X is not only meant in full determinacy, but given originarily in just this. The equivalence indicated now means:
>
> In principle (in the a priori of unconditioned essential generality) there corresponds to every "truly existing" object the idea of a possible consciousness in which the object itself is graspable originarily and at the same time perfectly adequately. Conversely, if this possibility is guaranteed, the object is eo ipso truly existing.

The word for the relation is "equivalent correlates" (*äquivalente Korrelate*). Husserl does not define "truly existing" by "rationally positable", and he does not reduce one to the other. He states that the two are correlates which hold together. The first equivalence, with "rationally positable" in general, is granted in passing ("not only"); the second, stronger one is the claim. The strengthening lies in two adjectives, "originary" and "perfect". A rational positing can rest on inadequate evidence, as perception does. The positing the equivalence needs cannot: the object would be given "not incompletely, not merely 'one-sidedly'".

The description of the perfect thesis is in the subjunctive. The object "would" not be given incompletely (*wäre … nicht … gegeben*); the sense "would leave nothing 'open'" (*würde … lassen*); there would be "no sense that would not be fully determined and closed". The mood is required by the content. §131's X was the "determinable" X, defined by its open predicates; §142's perfect thesis leaves nothing open "on any side prefigured by the apprehension", so its X would be determinable no longer. The perfect reason-thesis is the limit at which the determinable X becomes the determined X, "not only meant in full determinacy, but given originarily in just this". The terms of §131 and §136 meet here: fullness of sense and originarity of givenness, carried to their end together.

The formula that follows is introduced as a gloss: "The equivalence indicated now means" (*besagt nun*). It states the principle in the indicative and qualifies it twice at the start. "In principle" (*Prinzipiell*) and "(in the a priori of unconditioned essential generality)" place it as an eidetic law, not a claim about any actual course of experience. What corresponds to the truly existing object is not a consciousness but "the idea of a possible consciousness". Three modal layers stand between the object and any act: idea, possibility, graspability (*erfaßbar*). The converse removes one of them. "If this possibility is guaranteed" (*gewährleistet*), the object is truly existing "eo ipso". The verb is legal, like the "ground of right" of §136, and it leaves open who or what guarantees. The passage gives no criterion for when the possibility is guaranteed. It states what would follow if it were.

Here the inference is weaker than the "eo ipso" makes it sound. The forward direction is plausible on the book's own terms: whatever truly exists must be capable of being given, since nothing but givenness could justify positing it. The converse makes the possibility of perfect evidence sufficient for true being, and it is the converse that turns the correlation into a transcendental idealism. Husserl's support for it is the general insight that "results very soon" (*ergibt sich … sehr bald*), with no argument beyond the analyses of §§136–141. The commentary is right that this is the book's most direct statement of the correlation of being and reason. It is also its most compressed.

The passage sets up the problem §143 resolves, and the next paragraph prepares the resolution. Every "category of apprehension", correlate of a category of objects, prefigures "how an imperfect apprehension is to be perfected, how its sense is to be completed", and every category of objects is itself an essence that can be brought "in principle to adequate givenness". In that adequate givenness it "prescribes an insightful general rule" for how any object under it "would be" brought to full determinacy (Hua III/1 330). The example is the thing: its unseen determinations are necessarily spatial, and this gives "a lawful rule for possible spatial completions of the invisible sides", "a rule which, fully unfolded, is called pure geometry". Husserl adds that how much lies in these "short sentences" will become clear only "in the final chapter (from § 149 on)". The equivalence therefore has two faces. For the individual thing, adequate givenness is impossible, as §§138 and 144 say. For the category Thing, adequate givenness of the rule is possible. §143 joins the two: the perfect givenness of the thing is "prefigured as 'Idea' (in the Kantian sense)", and the idea of a possible perfect consciousness in §142 is that Idea. The subjunctives of §142 were the grammar of an Idea before the Kantian word arrived.

The *Cartesian Meditations* restate the correlation without the equivalence and with a source of right. §26, "Actuality as correlate of evident verification", says that "truth or true actuality of objects is to be drawn only from evidence", and that "every right stems from there, stems from our transcendental subjectivity itself; every conceivable adequation arises as our verification, is our synthesis, has in us its ultimate transcendental ground" (Hua I 95). Where *Ideas I* places two correlates side by side and calls them equivalent, the *Meditations* say where the right comes from. The 1913 formula leaves the direction of grounding unstated, and the converse of §142 is the place where the later direction is already implied.

# Husserl, *Analyses Concerning Passive and Active Synthesis*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md) works through Anthony Steinbock's English edition at equal depth. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding goes in the rest of the lectures and in Husserl's other texts.

Steinbock's edition (Collected Works IX, 2001) assembles four Husserliana volumes, and so do these readings. Part 2, Supplementary Section 1 and most of the appendices and essays are Husserliana XI, *Analysen zur passiven Synthesis*, edited by Margot Fleischer (1966), quoted from `sources/husserl/husserl-hua-11-analysen-zur-passiven-synthesis.md` in `faultynode/sources`. Part 1 is the 1920/21 introduction printed in Husserliana XVII, quoted from `husserl-hua-17-formale-und-transzendentale-logik-…md`. Part 3 and Appendices 29–39 are Husserliana XXXI, *Aktive Synthesen*, edited by Roland Breeur (2000), quoted from `husserl-hua-31-aktive-synthesen-…md`. Hua XI's textual apparatus and editor's introduction are quoted from their split-off files (`…-textkritischer-anhang.md`, `…-einleitung-des-herausgebers.md`). Steinbock's English, including his Translator's Introduction, is quoted from `husserl-cw9-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis.md`, and he is named wherever he is cited. The CW9, Hua XXXI and split-off Hua XI files entered the corpus after the key-passage list was made. They are not in the corpus map and have not been reviewed, and each was checked at the passage before it was quoted.

Passages are cited by Steinbock's § numbers and by the page of the Husserliana volume they come from ("Hua XI 148"), recovered from the files' running heads and therefore uncertain by a page in places. The Translator's Introduction is cited by CW9 page. Unit references such as "§32.1" name the first paragraph of the main commentary under §32. Every English rendering of the German is my working translation, kept close enough to show the structure under discussion. Where Steinbock's rendering bears on a reading it is quoted from CW9 and named as his.

Four limits of the files bear on more than one reading. First, almost all of Hua XI's § headings, and the titles of the supplementary texts, were formulated by the editor and set in angle brackets in the print (Hua XI, editor's introduction); no reading treats a heading's wording as Husserl's. Second, Hua XI renders Husserl's spaced type as italics, so the file shows emphasis but cannot tell spaced from italic type, and some stretches are hard-wrapped with the print's marginal line numbers fused into the text; I drop those numbers and the line-break hyphens when quoting. The file also has two dropped words, at §4 and §25, discussed where they occur, and it lacks Text A §20 on Kant, which survives in the corpus only in Steinbock's English. Third, the Hua XXXI file is a raw OCR text layer that garbles umlauts and ß ("Aktivitlit", "bloBen"). In quoting it I restore those letters and nothing else, and I say so at each quotation. Fourth, CW9 is also an OCR text layer: it has lost italics, carries its own margin line numbers and the German page numbers in square brackets, and garbles some letters. No reading argues from emphasis in Steinbock's English or in Hua XXXI. Other damage is noted where it occurs, and none of it is repaired.

## Translator's Introduction, CW9 xliv–xlvi: *Reiz* as "allure"

Reads [Translator's Introduction, Section 3.B, third paragraph](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md#b-affection-and-association).

Steinbock's claim is that Husserl's *Reiz* names a motivational pull, so that an English word with no causal ring has to translate it. The claim has two parts, a history of the word and a ruling on its sense, and the ruling rests on one sentence of *Ideas II*. Read against that sentence and against Hua XI itself, "allure" turns out to translate an attitude, not a word: it keeps what Husserl says *Reiz* becomes for the personal subject, and it drops the dimension of strength and degree that the lectures on affection are built on.

> It was not uncommon for Husserl to appropriate terms from other contexts and give to them a new phenomenological sense. This is the case with the expression, Reiz (translated here as "allure"). The expression "Reiz" gained its currency in the discipline of medicine and physiology in the 18th and 19th centuries and referred primarily either to mechanistic muscle contractions or sensations caused by the stimulation of nerves, and latter interpreted as the principle of association. […] Rather than confining Reiz to a quantitatively defined psycho-physical relation, or defining it as a causal connection between a physical agitation by the outside world upon the central nervous system, or as naturalistically determining internal psychic life, Husserl gave to "the concept of Reiz a fundamentally new sense"; he did this by bringing the concept of Reiz into the motivational relation between the lived-body [Leib] and intentional objectlike formations within a lived context, and by also implicating it in the process of apperception and formations of associative connection between one givenness and another […]. Since the draw or affective tendency on the part of the objectlike formation is motivational and not causal, and to avoid mechanistic, reductionistic, or behavoristic connotations, I translate the term Reiz as "allure," rather then, say, as "excitation" or "stimulus." By affection, then, Husserl does not mean a contextless power or a third person force; rather, he understands an "enticement to be" on the part of the "object," a motivational solicitation or pull to attentiveness, eventually to respond egoically and epistemically […].

*Note on the text.* CW9 is an OCR text layer. The file reads "UJAffective Allure", "18,h" and "19lh" for "(1) Affective Allure", "18th" and "19th"; these and the footnote numbers are dropped silently. "Rather then" and "behavoristic" stand as the file has them. The omissions cover sentences on German physiology, on Wundt and Fechner, and on normality.


The paragraph's grammar makes Husserl the agent of a semantic act: he "gave" the concept a new sense, "by bringing" it into the motivational relation. The quoted phrase, though, comes from *Ideas II* (Steinbock's note cites the English page, 199), and there the change has a different subject. Husserl sets out the physiological use first, with the stimulus that travels down the nerves and ends as a "'stimulus' for the soul", and then writes a conditional: "But if we place ourselves on the ground of the intentional subject–object relation, the relation between person and surrounding world, the concept of stimulus gains a fundamentally new sense" (*so gewinnt der Begriff des Reizes einen fundamental neuen Sinn*, Hua IV 190). The concept "gains" its sense when the attitude changes. Husserl does not retire the physiological concept. He leaves it valid for the naturalistic attitude and shows a second sense arising in the personalistic one. Steinbock's "rather than confining Reiz to…" turns a doubling of attitudes into a correction of a mistake.

The same page of *Ideas II* shows how Husserl handles the word in its new sense: he keeps the old vocabulary and puts it in quotation marks. "Stimuli" (*„Reize“*) go out from the intended things; the thing-unities are the starting points of more or less "'strong' tendencies"; and "with sufficient 'strength of stimulus' the ego 'follows' the stimulus, 'gives way' and turns toward it" (*bei hinreichender „Reizstärke“ „folgt“ das Ich dem Reiz, es „gibt nach“ und wendet sich zu*, Hua IV 190). The quotation marks transpose the physiology; they do not delete it. Strength, sufficiency, yielding and resistance all survive the change of attitude as the grammar of motivation. Rojcewicz and Schuwer accordingly render the sentence "the concept of stimulus acquires a fundamentally new sense" (CW3, *Ideas II* 199), and Steinbock records that choice in a note. "Allure" translates the new sense correctly, but it removes the trace of the old one that Husserl's quotation marks preserve.

What it removes matters for the lectures. Steinbock lists, as the reductionism Husserl overcame, the reduction of normality and pathology "to mere quantitative variations of intensity" by Wundt and Fechner. Yet degree is the structure of affection in Hua XI. Sensible data "send, as it were, affective rays of force toward the ego-pole" (*senden gleichsam affektive Kraftstrahlen auf den Ichpol*), and some are too weak to reach it and do not become "an awakening *Reiz*" (§32, Hua XI 149). §33 gives laws for the propagation of affective force, and §35 a gradation down to a zero. Husserl's correction of Fechner is not that affection lacks magnitude but that its magnitude is a magnitude of motivation, measured by its pull on an ego and not by a physical cause. "Allure" in English names a property of the attractive thing, its charm, and has no natural comparative of force. Steinbock's own gloss shows the strain. To explain "allure" he writes of an "enticement to be", which is the vocabulary of §11's *Anmutung*, the modal pull of a possibility toward belief (see the reading of §11 below). The word chosen to avoid physiology has to borrow its sense from the theory of modalities.

The paragraph then sets up the definition that §32 gives: affection is "the *Reiz* as given to consciousness, the peculiar pull that an object of consciousness exercises on the ego" (*den bewußtseinsmäßigen Reiz, den eigentümlichen Zug, den ein bewußter Gegenstand auf das Ich übt*, Hua XI 148). Steinbock renders it "the allure given to consciousness" and gives the German in a note (CW9 196). Husserl's own qualifier, "as given to consciousness" (*bewußtseinsmäßig*), does the work that Steinbock assigns to the English word: it marks the *Reiz* as a consciousness-relative one, as *Ideas II*'s attitude did. Where Husserl has a neutral noun and a qualifier, Steinbock has a coloured noun and no qualifier. The reading of §32 below tests the cost of that exchange against the heading "affection as the effect of a *Reiz* on the ego" (*Affektion als Reizwirkung auf das Ich*), which turns out to be the editor's wording, not Husserl's. Steinbock's ruling stands as a ruling on attitude. It holds that the pull is motivational, but it cannot hold "allure" to the measured, propagating force that the analyses of affection need.

## Part 1 §2, Hua XVII 356–357: the meanings of *logos* as a guide

Reads [Part 1, §2, first paragraph](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md#thinking-as-the-theme-of-logic-speaking-thinking-what-is-thought).

Husserl's inventory of the meanings of *logos* is not an etymology that grounds the theme of logic. It is a guide (*Leitung*), and what it guides is a route: from the senses in which *logos* already carries a norm of reason back to the senses that carry none, and so to speaking, thinking and what is thought, taken "before all distinction of the rational and the irrational". The root meanings of the Greek verb are named once and never used again. What organizes Part 1 is the order in which the groups of meanings are visited.

> The word logos, from which the name logic is derived, has a great multiplicity of meanings, which have arisen through quite intelligible transfers from the more original meanings of λέγω, namely the meanings to lay together, to lay out, then to lay out by means of the word, of speech. In developed language λόγος means now word and speech itself, now that of which there is speech, the matter under discussion. […] All these meanings of the word logos take on, however, especially wherever scientific interests are in play, a pregnant sense, in that the idea of a norm of reason enters into them. […] If we now take this manifold of evidently belonging-together meanings of the word logos as a guide for forming the first idea of a science of logos, rich and connected themes open up for theoretical research and normative use. A natural course of research is easy to find here. If we start from the second and third groups of meanings, then the theme of reason […] leads us at once to the question what sort of acts the "rational" acts of thinking in question here are. But before what is specific to this rationality can come into consideration, what is specific to thinking itself must naturally become the theme, before all distinction of the rational and the irrational. […] So we come to the first group of meanings of the word logos. For this first group of meanings of the logical, [these are to be brought] under three titles: speaking, thinking, what is thought. […] Only the human being has language and reason, only he is able to carry out psychic acts of the kind that are subject to a normative regulation by reason, as at least the general conviction is.

German:

> Das Wort Logos, von dem der Name Logik abgeleitet ist, hat eine große Vielheit von Bedeutungen, die durch wohl verständliche Übertragungen aus den ursprünglicheren Bedeutungen von λέγω entsprungen sind, also den Bedeutungen zusammenlegen, darlegen, dann mittels des Wortes, der Rede darlegen. In der entwickelten Sprache heißt λόγος bald Wort und Rede selbst, bald das, wovon die Rede ist, die in Rede stehende Sache. […] Alle diese Bedeutungen des Wortes Logos nehmen aber, insbesondere überall wo wissenschaftliche Interessen im Spiele sind, einen prägnanten Sinn an, indem in diesen die Idee einer Vernunftnorm eintritt. […] Nehmen wir nun diese Mannigfaltigkeit sichtlich zusammengehöriger Bedeutungen des Wortes Logos als Leitung für die Bildung der ersten Vorstellung von einer Wissenschaft vom Logos, so eröffnen sich damit reiche und zusammenhängende Themata für eine theoretische Forschung und normative Verwendung. Dabei ist ein natürlicher Forschungsgang leicht zu finden. Knüpfen wir an die zweite und dritte Bedeutungsgruppe an, so führt uns das Thema der Vernunft […] alsbald auf die Frage, was für Akte die hier fraglichen „vernünftigen“ Denkakte sind. Bevor nun aber das Spezifische dieser Vernünftigkeit zur Erwägung kommen kann, muß natürlich das Spezifische des Denkens selbst zum Thema werden, vor aller Unterscheidung von Vernünftigem und Unvernünftigem. […] Wir kommen also auf die erste Gruppe von Bedeutungen des Wortes Logos. Für diese erste Gruppe der Bedeutungen vom Logischen sind diese auf drei Titel bringen: Reden, Denken, Gedachtes. […] Nur der Mensch hat Sprache und Vernunft, nur er vermag psychische Akte zu vollziehen derart, die einer Vernunftnormierung unterliegen, wie wenigstens die allgemeine Überzeugung ist.

*Note on the text.* The Hua XVII file is on the unsafe list (machine-converted, unreviewed), but it reads cleanly here. Its sentence "sind diese auf drei Titel bringen" lacks a word; Steinbock renders "can be brought under three headings" (CW9 10), and my bracket marks the gap. The file has no emphasis in this stretch. Steinbock notes (CW9 9, note 6) that in the omitted clause on habitual faculties he reads *begründen* (ground) for the printed *bedürfen* (need), following a correction of a transcription error by the Husserl Archives.

The verb that carries the paragraph is "take as a guide" (*als Leitung nehmen*). The meanings are not evidence for a thesis about logic. They supply the "first idea" (*erste Vorstellung*) of a science, which the inquiry will then replace. Even the derivation of the senses is called "intelligible transfers" (*wohl verständliche Übertragungen*), a phrase that claims only that the semantic history can be followed, not that it is authoritative. The verb's root meanings, "to lay together, to lay out" (*zusammenlegen, darlegen*), are listed and dropped. Nothing in Part 1 returns to gathering. The main commentary's remark that Husserl here excavates "sedimented layers of meaning" credits the passage with a method it does not use. Sedimentation, in this volume, is a matter of passive genesis (§48), not of word history.

The argument lies in the order of the groups. Husserl numbers them implicitly: the senses of word, matter, thought and act; the "pregnant sense" (*prägnanten Sinn*) in which a norm of reason enters; and the narrower "scientific" coinage. The "natural course of research" begins with the second and third groups, because a logic is a normative science. It is at once turned back. The specific character of rationality cannot be considered until the specific character of thinking "before all distinction of the rational and the irrational" has been made a theme. The regress is driven by the priority of the unnormed over the normed, and that priority is the programme of the whole course. §9 calls it the regression from the theoretical logos to pre-theoretical sense-giving life, and Part 2 carries it below thinking altogether, into perception. Read this way, the triad "speaking, thinking, what is thought" is not the root of the word but the point where the regress first comes to rest. It is the plainest group of meanings, the one in which *logos* is not yet reason.

The last sentence of the stretch shows how provisional that resting place is. "Only the human being has language and reason," Husserl writes, "as at least the general conviction is" (*wie wenigstens die allgemeine Überzeugung ist*). The hedge takes the anthropological frame as a common opinion, the frame in which the course begins, not one it endorses. A transcendental logic cannot rest on what humans and animals are, and §§3–4 duly turn from the human speaker to the ideality of the word and to the sense-constituting experience. The main commentary reports the sentence without its hedge (P1 §2.5) and so loses the sign that the frame is already being held at a distance.

*Formal and Transcendental Logic* (1929) opens with the same inventory, rewritten, and the rewriting confirms what the 1920/21 version is doing. The verb becomes the infinitive λέγειν, its meanings are put in quotation marks ("„zusammenlegen“, „darlegen“"), and the groups are numbered "1." and "2.", with the scientific coinage dropped as a separate group (FTL §1, Hua XVII 22–23). The sense of *logos* now leads "to asserting, in the ordinary sense of the word judging, thinking" (*auf behauptendes, im gewöhnlichen Wortsinn urteilendes Denken*), where 1920/21 had "conceptual thinking" (*begriffliches Denken*), and the sentences on humans and animals are gone. The book's revision fits its object, a formal logic of judgment whose transcendental grounding comes after; the lecture's fits its own, a descent below judgment into passivity. Both keep the regress from normed to unnormed thinking, and both keep the inventory as a guide. In neither does the history of the word decide anything, so the main commentary's claim that "the argument turns on the word's history" (as the list's reason puts it) has to be narrowed: it turns on the order in which the word's meanings are taken up.

## Part 1 §3, Hua XVII 358–359: the word's "spiritual corporeality"

Reads [Part 1, §3, second paragraph](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md#the-ideality-of-linguistic-phenomena).

The ideality of language, for Husserl, is not first the ideality of meaning. Before the word expresses anything, the word itself, as sound pattern and grammatical form, is one and the same across its utterances and copies. It is a body, but a body of the cultural world, and so already "spiritual". The passage places ideality on the side of the sign, which is the side a theory of sense might have expected to leave to physics.

> Language as a system of signs, by which, in contrast to other kinds of signs, an expressing of thoughts takes place, offers in general and in many respects subtle and wondrous problems. One of them is the ideality of language that we have just met, which is usually entirely overlooked. We can also designate it thus: language has the objectivity of the objectlike formations of the so-called spiritual world or cultural world, and not that merely of physical nature. As an objective spiritual formation, language has the same properties as spiritual formations otherwise: so we also distinguish, from the thousands of reproductions of an engraving, the engraving itself, and this engraving, that is, the engraved image itself, is seen out of every reproduction and is given in each in the same way as an identical ideal. Again the same when we speak of the Kreutzer Sonata as against its arbitrary reproductions. However much it consists of tones, it is an ideal unity, and its tones are no less so; they are not, say, the physical tones or the tones of outer acoustic perception: the sense-thingly tones that are really present only in an actual reproduction and the intuiting of it. […] It is the same, then, with all linguistic formations; and indeed what is in question here does not concern them with respect to what they express (however large a role this may also play). Certainly it concerns them also as speeches filled with sense, taken as concrete unities of linguistic body and expressed sense, but it concerns them already with respect to linguistic corporeality itself, which is, so to speak, also already a spiritual corporeality. The word itself, the grammatical sentence itself is, as we saw, an ideal unity that does not multiply with its thousandfold reproductions.

German:

> Die Sprache als ein System von Zeichen, mit denen sich im Gegensatz zu anderen Arten von Zeichen ein Ausdrücken von Gedanken vollzieht, bietet überhaupt und in vieler Hinsicht feine und wundersame Probleme. Eins davon ist die uns soeben entgegengetretene Idealität der Sprache, die völlig übersehen zu werden pflegt. Wir können sie auch so bezeichnen: Die Sprache hat die Objektivität der gegenständlichen, der sogenannten geistigen Welt oder Kulturwelt und nicht die bloß der physischen Natur. Als objektives geistiges Gebilde hat die Sprache dieselben Eigenschaften wie geistige Gebilde sonst: So scheiden wir ja auch von den tausenden Reproduktionen eines Stichs den Stich selbst, und dieser Stich, das ist das gestochene Bild selbst, wird aus jeder Reproduktion herausgeschaut und ist in jeder in gleicher Weise als ein identisches Ideales gegeben. Wieder ebenso wenn wir von der Kreutzer-Sonate sprechen gegenüber ihren beliebigen Reproduktionen. So sehr sie selbst aus Tönen besteht, ist sie eine ideale Einheit und ihre Töne sind es nicht minder; sie sind nicht etwa die physikalischen Töne oder auch die Töne der äußeren akustischen Wahrnehmung: die sinnendiglichen Töne, die eben nur in einer wirklichen Reproduktion und ihrem Anschauen real vorhanden sind. […] Ebenso verhält es sich also mit allen sprachlichen Gebilden; und zwar, was hier in Frage kommt, betrifft sie nicht bezüglich dessen, was sie ausdrücken (eine wie große Rolle dieses auch mit spielen mag). Sicherlich betrifft es sie auch als sinnerfüllte Reden, als konkrete Einheiten von sprachlichem Leib und ausgedrücktem Sinn genommen, aber es betrifft sie schon hinsichtlich der sprachlichen Leiblichkeit selbst, die sozusagen auch schon eine geistige Leiblichkeit ist. Das Wort selbst, der grammatische Satz selbst ist, sahen wir ja, eine ideale Einheit, die sich mit ihren tausendfältigen Reproduktionen nicht vervielfältigt.

*Note on the text.* The Hua XVII file (unsafe list) is hard-wrapped here; line-break hyphens are dropped. "Sinnendiglichen" is the file's reading, a misprint or OCR slip for the "sense-thingly" (*sinnen-dinglichen*) of the parallel in *Formal and Transcendental Logic*. "Der gegenständlichen" lacks its noun; Steinbock reads *Gegenständlichkeiten* (objectivities), following a correction by the Husserl Archives (CW9 11, note). The omitted sentence repeats the sonata's reproduction tone by tone.

The paragraph argues by a pair of analogies, and both are chosen to remove meaning from the case. An engraving and a sonata express no thoughts. If they are ideal, ideality cannot be a property of what is expressed, and the analogies prepare the sentence that matters: what is in question "does not concern them with respect to what they express". The sentence then turns with a concession and a "but". Certainly ideality concerns words as concrete unities of "linguistic body and expressed sense" (*sprachlichem Leib und ausgedrücktem Sinn*), but it concerns them "already" (*schon*) in their corporeality. "Already" fixes an order of founding: the body of the word is ideal before sense animates it, and the unity of body and sense rests on an ideality that the body has on its own.

The key term pairs *Leib* with *geistig* and so declines the vocabulary of the *Logical Investigations*, where the expression's physical side is a sign-body set against the meaning-giving act. Husserl writes "linguistic body" (*sprachlicher Leib*), not "physical sign", and the word for body is the one he uses for the lived body, not for the physical thing (*Körper*). The coinage "spiritual corporeality" (*geistige Leiblichkeit*) carries a double hedge: "so to speak, also already" (*sozusagen auch schon*). "So to speak" admits that the phrase joins what normally stands opposed. "Also already" says that this spirituality is not added when sense arrives. The tones of the sonata are the model. They are "no less" ideal than the sonata, and they are set against "the physical tones" and against "the tones of outer acoustic perception", the sensory tones that exist only in a performance. Husserl distinguishes three tones, not two: the physical, the perceived and the ideal. The word's body belongs with the third.

This is why the passage sits where it does, at the first of the three titles of §2. Before thinking and what is thought can be separated, speaking has to be shown to be more than an event. The following paragraphs separate the speaker's practical intention from a meaning formed first and clothed afterwards, and the last paragraph restricts the logician to "language in its ideality". The logician's object is therefore never the noise or the ink. Part 3 returns to ideality on the other side of the expression, as the omni-temporality of the judicative proposition (§56), where what is ideal is the sense. The early insistence on the body is what keeps that later ideality from being simply the ideality of meaning in the *Investigations*' sense: the word, too, is a cultural object constituted in a community of repetition.

*Formal and Transcendental Logic* rewrites the sentence in a way that weakens it. Its §2 has "this ideality is not only one of what is expressed in them" (*nicht nur eine solche des in ihnen Ausgedrückten*, Hua XVII 25), and the body is "so to speak a spiritual corporeality" without the "also already" (*sozusagen eine geistige Leiblichkeit*). The 1920/21 text denied that the ideality in question was that of the expressed. The book allows that it is, and adds the body. At the same time the book adds a genesis the lecture lacks: language is now a system of "habitual signs" that has "grown up in a people's community, transforms itself, persists in the manner of tradition" (*ein in einer Volksgemeinschaft erwachsendes, sich umbildendes, in der Weise der Tradition verharrendes System von habituellen Zeichen*), and the engraving "has existence in the real world only in the form of reproduction". The lecture states the ideality of the linguistic body more sharply; the book says more about how it comes to be. The main commentary's "not only as expressing something" follows the book's wording, not the lecture's that Steinbock translates ("does not concern what they express", CW9 11), and so loses the 1920/21 text's sharper point that the ideality at issue lies in the word's body first.

## Part 1 §9, Hua XVII 373–374: going back before thinking

Reads [Part 1, §9, fifth and sixth paragraphs](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md#the-regression-from-theoretical-logos-to-the-pre-theoretical-sense-giving-life-of-consciousness).

The programme of the lectures rests on a claim about what expression does to the life it expresses. Thinking can only be understood by going back before it, Husserl argues, *because* the broad life beneath it is pre-linguistic and stops being what it was the moment it is put into words. The descent is required by the fragility of its object. The same passage then frees "sense" from words, so that this pre-linguistic life can be described as sense-giving without being described as thought.

> We cannot possibly understand the specific thinking, which is such a highly built accomplishment in order to be expressible through language and general words and to deliver a science, a theory, if we do not go back before this thinking, back to those acts and accomplishments that make up the broadest part of our life. For in its breadth this is not only a pre-theoretical but also a pre-linguistic life, and one that with every utterance at once ceases to be in its original primitive peculiarity.
>
> And with this I set the further lectures the task of opening up for you this wide great world of the interiority of consciousness, and, under the guiding viewpoint of a theory of science, and ascending from below, of showing how genuine thinking emerges there at all its levels, how it is motivated and built in its founded accomplishment.
>
> […] So we distinguish meaning [as act] and meaning [as meant], the sense-giving act and the sense itself that is thematically conscious in it. That holds generally. Where a thematic act is attached to words, what is meant in the act is called the sense of the word or also, because the word points to it, its signification. But regardless of whether an act stands in such a function, of lending signification to words and perhaps being able to lend it, it has in itself a content of sense. We must therefore free the concept of sense from its relation to expressions.

German:

> Wir können das spezifische Denken, das eine so hoch gebaute Leistung ist, um durch Sprache und allgemeine Worte ausdrückbar zu sein und eine Wissenschaft, eine Theorie zu liefern, unmöglich verstehen, wenn wir nicht vor dieses Denken zurückgehen, zurück auf diejenigen Akte und Leistungen, die den breitesten Teil unseres Lebens ausmachen. Denn in seiner Breite ist das nicht nur ein vortheoretisches, sondern auch ein vorsprachliches und ein solches, das mit jeder Aussprache sofort aufhört, in seiner ursprünglichen primitiven Eigenheit zu sein.
>
> Und damit stelle ich den weiteren Vorlesungen die Aufgabe, Ihnen diese weite große Welt der Bewußtseinsinnerlichkeit zu eröffnen, und unter dem leitenden Gesichtspunkt einer Wissenschaftslehre, und von unten aufsteigend zu zeigen, wie da das eigentliche Denken in allen seinen Stufen auftaucht, wie es in seiner fundierten Leistung motiviert und gebaut ist.
>
> […] Wir scheiden also Meinen und Meinung, den sinngebenden Akt und den Sinn selbst, der in ihm thematisch bewußt ist. Das gilt allgemein. In der Anknüpfung eines thematischen Aktes an Worte heißt das im Akt Gemeinte Sinn des Wortes oder auch, weil das Wort darauf deutet, seine Bedeutung. Aber gleichgültig, ob ein Akt in solcher Funktion steht, Worten Bedeutung zu verleihen und vielleicht verleihen zu können, hat er in sich selbst einen Sinnesgehalt. Den Begriff des Sinnes müssen wir also von der Beziehung auf Ausdrücke befreien.

*Note on the text.* The Hua XVII file (unsafe list) is clean in this stretch. A page marker for Hua XVII 374 falls between the first and second paragraphs. The file shows no emphasis here.

The first sentence is built on a purpose clause that makes thinking an instrument. Thinking is "so highly built an accomplishment *in order to* be expressible" (*um … ausdrückbar zu sein*). Its height is measured by its fitness for language and for science, and that is the measure the regress gives up. "Before" (*vor*) is a spatial preposition used for an order of founding, and "back" (*zurück*) is repeated to make the movement felt. Then the ground clause, introduced by "for" (*denn*), gives a reason that cuts against the task. The broad life is pre-theoretical, which only says that theory has not yet worked on it, and pre-linguistic, which says the same of words. But it is also a life that "with every utterance at once ceases to be in its original primitive peculiarity". Every description of passivity is an utterance. The lectures that follow can therefore never present this life as it is, only as it was before the description reached it. Husserl states this without drawing it as a difficulty, and the main commentary passes over it. It is the methodological premise of every later "we must abstract from" and "for the time being we disregard" in Part 2: what is described is reached by subtracting the description's own contribution.

The task in the second paragraph runs in the opposite direction to the reason in the first. The reason goes "back" (*zurück*); the task goes "ascending from below" (*von unten aufsteigend*). The commentary takes both as one "ascending genetic" method. They are two movements. The regress is the order of inquiry, from the finished theory down to experience, as the previous paragraph had put it: phenomenology "inquires back" (*fragt … zurück*) from ready-made propositions. The ascent is the order of exposition, from the lowest accomplishments up to genuine thinking "at all its levels". Part 2 begins at the bottom, with the perceived thing and its horizons (§1), because the regress has already been made here, in Part 1. The phrase "how it is motivated and built" names the two relations the ascent will trace: motivation, which Part 2 develops as the passive pull of possibilities and affections, and building, which §48 calls the "graded structure" of accomplishments.

The paragraph that follows gives the ascent its material. Husserl distinguishes "meaning as act" from "meaning as meant" (*Meinen und Meinung*), and says that the meant is called a word's "sense" or "signification" (*Bedeutung*) only when an act is joined to words. The word "regardless" (*gleichgültig*) carries the step. Whether or not an act lends signification to words, "it has in itself a content of sense". The conclusion, introduced by "therefore" (*also*), is a demand: "we must free the concept of sense from its relation to expressions". The freeing does what the ground clause of the first paragraph required. If pre-linguistic life ceases with utterance, it cannot have its sense from utterance, and the concept of sense has to reach below words if the lectures are to describe that life as sense-giving at all. *Bedeutung* is kept for verbal sense, and *Sinn* becomes the general term, the step *Ideas I* had taken for the noema and the 1920/21 course now uses to open the field of passive synthesis.

One sentence just before the passage keeps this descent from being a descent into the irrational. Experience, Husserl writes, already performs a sense-giving accomplishment that stands "under a widely taken title, reason and unreason" (*unter einem weit gefaßten Titel Vernunft und Unvernunft*, Hua XVII 373). The pre-theoretical is not pre-rational. The distinction §2 postponed, "before all distinction of the rational and the irrational", returns below thinking in a wider form, and Part 2 gives it content as the difference between concordant and conflicting experience, confirmation and crossing out. That is how a course on passive synthesis can still be a course on transcendental logic. Its lowest level is already the first form of the norm that logic articulates at the top.

## Part 1 §10, Hua XVII 377–378: the two concepts of sense

Reads [Part 1, §10, sixth paragraph](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md#perception-and-perceptual-sense).

"Sense", introduced as the intentional object, splits in two at the moment the modalities of being come into view. In one concept the sense is the meant object together with its being "existent", "doubtful" or "null". In the other it is the bare content that persists while those characters change, the old "matter" of the *Logical Investigations* set against their "quality". Husserl's note records the split as a flaw in his own introduction of the term. Its importance is that the lectures resolve it by moving the modal characters from the act to the object's sense, which is what makes a passive genesis of negation and doubt possible.

> The introduction of the concept of sense is unclear. Sense is introduced at first as intentional object, the meant as such. That is ambiguous, as is object in quotation marks too. When I practise the phenomenological reduction, I have for every "act" its meant, the intentional, which includes all modalities of being in itself, is "existent".
>
> But then it comes to this separation between the intentional content and the intentional modal character, which at first looks like a distinction of two components. The intentional content in this sense, the "matter" of the "quality", is also the "intentional object", the merely presented, that which would be modally qualified there.
>
> That is an entirely different concept of sense and intentional object. All this also emerges in the further exposition, but one must proceed in the right way from the start, and the distinctions must be made, even if as provisional ones.

German:

> Unklar ist die Einführung des Begriffs Sinn. Zunächst eingeführt wird Sinn als intentionaler Gegenstand, das Vermeinte als solches. Das ist mehrdeutig, wie auch Gegenstand in Anführungszeichen. Wenn ich phänomenologische Reduktion übe, habe ich für jeden ,,Akt“ sein Vermeintes, das Intentionale, das alle Seinsmodalitäten in sich schließt, ,,Seiendes“ ist.
>
> Aber dann kommt es 〈zu〉 dieser Scheidung zwischen dem intentionalen Inhalt und dem intentionalen Modalcharakter, was zunächst wie eine Unterscheidung zweier Bestandstücke aussieht. Der intentionale Inhalt in diesem Sinn, die „Materie” der „Qualität” ist auch der „intentionale Gegenstand”, das bloß Vorgestellte, dasjenige, was da modal qualifiziert sei.
>
> Das ist ein ganz anderer Begriff von Sinn und intentionalem Gegenstand. Das alles tritt auch in der weiteren Darstellung hervor, aber es muß in der rechten Weise von vorn vorgegangen und die Unterscheidungen 〈müssen〉, sei es auch als vorläufige, gemacht werden.

*Note on the text.* The Hua XVII file (unsafe list) shows the editor's insertions in pointed brackets, which I translate without marking. Its "die „Materie” der „Qualität”" reads as a genitive, "the matter of the quality". Steinbock renders "the 'material,' the 'quality,'", a list (CW9 38), and the main commentary follows him ("the intentional content or 'material' and the 'quality'"). The file cannot settle whether the print has *der* or a comma; without Hua XVII's apparatus in the corpus, the reading below does not rest on the genitive. The note's clipped style and the editor's two supplied words suggest a note added to the lecture manuscript rather than delivered prose; the file does not say so.

The note works with two pairs of quotation marks. In the first paragraph, the meant includes every modality of being and so "is 'existent'" (*„Seiendes“ ist*): the quotation marks give being as a character of the sense, not as a judgment about the tree. In the second, every key word is quoted: "matter", "quality", "intentional object". These are the *Investigations*' terms, quoted as borrowed and under suspicion. What makes them suspect is the phrase "at first looks like" (*zunächst … aussieht*). The separation of content and modal character "looks like a distinction of two components" (*zweier Bestandstücke*), as if a perception were a presented content plus an added belief. That was the *Investigations*' analysis of the act into matter and quality, and the note doubts it without yet replacing it. The subjunctive "what would be modally qualified" (*was da modal qualifiziert sei*) keeps the bare content hypothetical: no perception has it without some mode.

The three-part distinction just before the note had treated sense as the "object in quotation marks", the identical pole of changing appearances. That concept answers the question of identity across aspects. The note sees that it does not answer the question of identity across modalities, the same tree first believed, then doubted. Part 2 is built on that second question. Negation, doubt and possibility (§§5–13) are all changes in the mode of a sense whose content stays the same, and the lectures need both concepts at once to describe them.

The "further exposition" the note promises arrives in the 1920/21 text that Hua XI prints as Appendix 2 (Beilage II), which takes up the same tree and the same reduction. After the waxwork figure that turns out to be a doll, Husserl writes that "two concepts of sense separate" (*zwei Begriffe von Sinn treten auseinander*). Sense can mean "the full What of the perception, and that says, the intentional object with its mode of being (proposition)" (*der intentionale Gegenstand mit seinem Seinsmodus (Satz)*), or "the bare intentional object … (the propositional matter), the bare, unqualitative objective sense" (*(die Satzmaterie) der bloße, unqualitative gegenständliche Sinn*, Hua XI 352). The vocabulary has changed. "Matter" and "quality" have become the *Ideas I* terms "proposition" and "propositional matter", and the doubt about "two components" has been answered: the modes of being "belong to the objective sense and not, say, to the contents of appearance, to the thematic regard" (*daß diese Seinsmodi dem gegenständlichen Sinn zugehören*, ibid.). In the *Investigations* quality was a moment of the act. Here it is a character of the meant, and that is the condition of the lectures' main thesis on modalization: a sense can be crossed out, can become "null", without any act of judgment, because the null is a character the sense takes on.

The note's last sentence asks for provisional distinctions made "from the start" (*von vorn*). The main course does not start there. Its §5 begins with disappointment, and the word "quality" returns only at §14 (Hua XI 54–56), where the traditional "qualities" of affirmation and denial are rejected as not two "equal-ranking qualities like red and blue". The reading of that passage below follows the second half of the resolution. Beilage II moves being-characters into the sense; §14 then shows that the ego's yes and no are position-takings on those characters and not two species of one genus. Read with both, the 1920/21 note turns out to name the precise point where the *Investigations*' act analysis has to give way for a theory of passive modalization to begin.

## §1, Hua XI 3: perception as a constant pretension

Reads [§1, first paragraph](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md#original-consciousness-and-the-perspectival-adumbration-of-spatial-objects).

External perception, for Husserl, claims more than it can give. Its own sense is to present the thing itself, the whole thing, and its own structure lets it present only sides. The word for this claim is *Prätention*, which holds both "claim" and "pretence", and the lectures keep both senses. The claim is legitimate, since the thing really is perceived; it is also always in excess, since the thing is never exhausted. Husserl calls this "as it were a contradiction" and hedges the word at once, because the conflict lies between an intention and its fulfilment, not between two propositions.

> External perception is a constant pretension to accomplish something that by its own essence it is unable to accomplish. So, as it were, a contradiction belongs to its essence. What is meant by this will soon become clear to you when you watch intuitively how the objective sense presents itself as unity in the infinite manifolds of possible appearances, and what the continuous synthesis looks like more closely that, as a unity of coinciding, lets the same sense appear, and how, over against the factual, limited courses of appearance, there constantly persists a consciousness of further, ever new possibilities of appearance reaching beyond them.

German:

> Die äußere Wahrnehmung ist eine beständige Prätention, etwas zu leisten, was sie ihrem eigenen Wesen nach zu leisten außerstande ist. Also gewissermaßen ein Widerspruch gehört zu ihrem Wesen. Was damit gemeint ist, wird Ihnen alsbald klarwerden, wenn Sie schauend zusehen, wie sich der objektive Sinn als Einheit in den unendlichen Mannigfaltigkeiten möglicher Erscheinungen darstellt und wie die kontinuierliche Synthese näher aussieht, welche als Deckungseinheit denselben Sinn erscheinen läßt, und wie gegenüber den faktischen, begrenzten Erscheinungsabläufen doch beständig ein Bewußtsein von darüber hinausreichenden, von immer neuen Erscheinungsmöglichkeiten besteht.

The apparatus shows that this opening was not written as one. Before "Die äußere" Husserl struck out in pencil "Ich sagte zu Eingang unserer ganzen Darlegungen über Transzendenz" (I said at the outset of our whole exposition on transcendence), and the manuscript had the subjunctives of reported speech: perception "sei" a pretension, a contradiction "gehöre" to its essence, and what "war" meant would soon be clear (Hua XI 446, notes to 3,5–8). The pencil revision turned a reminder of earlier lectures, which Hua XI does not print, into a thesis stated in the present tense. Hua XI's main text begins by recalling a claim made elsewhere, and the editor's revision made the recollection invisible. The present tense is Husserl's own correction, so the thesis stands as his, but its first form was a summary, which explains its compression.

The sentence sets an accomplishment against an incapacity. "To accomplish" (*leisten*) is repeated in both halves, and the second half supplies the bar: "by its own essence" (*ihrem eigenen Wesen nach*). Perception does not fail by accident. The phrase rules out the reading on which a better perceiver could close the gap, the reading §4 will reject in its "theological turn". *Prätention* fits this because it does not decide between legitimacy and pretence. It names the claim a perception makes simply by being a perception, its sense of giving the object "in the flesh". "Constant" (*beständig*) places the claim in every phase, not at the start of a process, so no later fulfilment discharges it. Steinbock's "it harbors an essential contradiction, as it were" (CW9 39) turns "belongs to its essence" into an adjective; the main commentary quotes "essential contradiction" as if it were Husserl's phrase.

"So, as it were" (*Also gewissermaßen*) joins an inference to a hedge. "So" presents the contradiction as following from the first sentence, and it does: a claim to accomplish what one cannot accomplish is self-defeating. "As it were" warns that "contradiction" is used beyond its logical sense. Nothing in perception asserts *p* and not-*p*. What conflicts is a meaning-intention, aimed at the whole thing, and the fulfilment that the aspects provide. The next sentence explains it by three movements to be watched "intuitively" (*schauend*): the sense as unity across infinite appearances, the synthesis of coinciding, and the persisting consciousness of further possibilities. Each describes a structure, and none describes a contradiction. The hedge marks that the paradox will be dissolved into the description of horizons, emptiness and fulfilment.

§3 restates the thesis in those terms. Every appearance carries a *plus ultra* in its empty horizon, "and since with each the perception does pretend to give the object in the flesh, it in fact constantly pretends to more than it can accomplish by its own essence" (*so prätendiert sie in der Tat beständig mehr, als sie ihrem eigenen Wesen nach leisten kann*, Hua XI 11). The noun has become a verb, the hedge has gone, and "in fact" (*in der Tat*) replaces it. What follows is no longer a contradiction but "a constant mixture of familiarity and unfamiliarity". Steinbock adds "[completely]" to "give the object in the flesh" (CW9 48), which supplies the excess the German leaves to "more". §4 then grants the pretension its truth: what we grasp of the object "pretends to be its essence; and it is that too, but always only imperfect approximation" (*prätendiert sein Wesen zu sein; es ist es auch*, Hua XI 21). The claim is honoured and exceeded at once. That is the sense of *Prätention* the opening sentence already held.

The word then changes its subject. In §24 the time-form is "the form of objects that, as objects, pretend to have their in-itself" (*die als Gegenstände ihr Ansich zu haben prätendieren*, Hua XI 110), and "all talk of objects thus leads back to remembering". The pretension no longer belongs to outer perception but to objectivity as such, immanent objects included. This is the problem of Division 2: if every object claims an in-itself that no single givenness redeems, the question is what could redeem it, and the lectures answer it with the idea of a remembering that cannot be crossed out (§25) and with the "primordial transcendence" of the past (§45). The opening sentence's restriction to "external" perception is therefore provisional. §1 contrasts outer perception with immanent perception, where *esse* and *percipi* coincide (§4), and Divisions 2 and 4 show that the past of immanent life raises a claim of the same form. The "as it were a contradiction" of §1 becomes the structure of transcendence in general.

## §1, Hua XI 5–6: the object's address and the halo of emptiness

Reads [§1, fourth paragraph](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md#original-consciousness-and-the-perspectival-adumbration-of-spatial-objects).

The horizon of a perceived thing is, for Husserl, a set of references that are also tendencies: each aspect points to further aspects and pushes toward them. To make the pushing felt he lets the thing speak, in the imperative, to the perceiver. The figure is marked as a figure and then translated back into the vocabulary of referral, and the translation is where its claim lies. What the thing "says" is a rule for the course of possible perception and an invitation to get to know it. It is not yet a pull on the ego, and the lectures will need another eighty pages to say what that would be.

> The perceived in its mode of appearance is what it is, in every moment of perceiving, as a system of references, with a core of appearance on which they have their hold, and in these references it calls out to us, as it were: There is still more to see here, turn me around to all sides, run through me with your gaze, step closer, open me, cut me apart. Ever anew carry out looking around and turning on all sides. So you will get to know me in all that I am, all my surface properties, my inner sensible properties, and so on. You understand what this indicating talk is meant to say. […] But this identical thing is a constant x, a constant substrate of actually appearing table-moments, but also of references to ones not yet appearing. These references are at the same time tendencies, referring tendencies, which drive on toward the appearances not given. […] In other words, everything that genuinely appears is a thing-appearing only by being intertwined with and permeated by an intentional empty horizon, by being surrounded by a halo of appearance-like emptiness. It is an emptiness that is not a nothing, but an emptiness to be filled out; it is a determinable indeterminacy.

German:

> Das Wahrgenommene in seiner Erscheinungsweise ist, was es ist, in jedem Momente des Wahrnehmens, <als> ein System von Verweisen, mit einem Erscheinungskern, an dem sie ihren Anhalt haben, und in diesen Verweisen ruft es uns gewissermaßen zu: Es gibt hier noch Weiteres zu sehen, dreh mich doch nach allen Seiten, durchlaufe mich dabei mit dem Blick, tritt näher heran, öffne mich, zerteile mich. Immer von neuem vollziehe Umblick und allseitige Wendung. So wirst du mich kennenlernen nach allem, was ich bin, all meinen oberflächlichen Eigenschaften, meinen inneren sinnlichen Eigenschaften usw. Sie verstehen, was diese andeutende Rede besagen soll. […] Aber dieses Identische ist ein beständiges x, ist ein beständiges Substrat von wirklich erscheinenden Tisch-Momenten, aber auch von Hinweisen auf noch nicht erscheinende. Diese Hinweise sind zugleich Tendenzen, Hinweistendenzen, die zu den nicht gegebenen Erscheinungen forttreiben. […] Mit andern Worten, alles eigentlich Erscheinende ist nur dadurch Dingerscheinendes, daß es umflochten und durchsetzt ist von einem intentionalen Leerhorizont, daß es umgeben ist von einem Hof erscheinungsmäßiger Leere. Es ist eine Leere, die nicht ein Nichts ist, sondern eine auszufüllende Leere, es ist eine bestimmbare Unbestimmtheit.

*Note on the text.* The first two paragraphs are hard-wrapped in the file; line-break hyphens are dropped. "<als>" is the editor's insertion. "Vollziehe Umblick" lacks an article, as the file has it.

The address changes its grammar as it goes. It begins "it calls out to us" (*ruft es uns … zu*), with the listeners in the plural, and turns into the singular of intimacy: "turn me", "so you will get to know me" (*dreh mich*, *so wirst du mich kennenlernen*). The thing speaks in the first person and the perceiver is a "you". The imperatives run in an order of increasing intimacy, from turning and scanning to stepping closer and finally to "open me, cut me apart" (*öffne mich, zerteile mich*), and the last two reach the "inner sensible properties" that no surface view yields. The figure thus covers the whole range of the horizon, outer and inner. Its goal is stated in the future tense, "so you will get to know me", and so the address is an invitation to knowledge (*Kenntnis*), the term the heading of §2 takes up.

Two signals frame the figure. Before it stands "as it were" (*gewissermaßen*), the hedge that §1's opening put on "contradiction". After it Husserl steps out of the figure and addresses his own audience in the formal second person: "You understand what this indicating talk is meant to say" (*Sie verstehen, was diese andeutende Rede besagen soll*). The shift from the thing's "du" to the lecturer's "Sie" marks the end of the image, and "indicating talk" (*andeutende Rede*) classifies it: it points to a matter without stating it. The statement follows in three steps. The thing is a constant *x*, a substrate of appearing moments and of "references" (*Hinweise*) to moments not appearing. "These references are at the same time tendencies" (*zugleich Tendenzen*), and the compound "referring tendencies" (*Hinweistendenzen*) fuses the two. The tendencies "drive on" (*forttreiben*). The imperatives of the address have become a force in the horizon itself.

The figure's last translation defines the horizon by a double negation. It is a "halo of appearance-like emptiness" (*Hof erscheinungsmäßiger Leere*), and the emptiness "is not a nothing, but an emptiness to be filled out" (*eine auszufüllende Leere*). The gerundive carries the claim. The emptiness has a direction, and the next sentence calls it a "prefiguring" (*Vorzeichnung*) that prescribes a rule. The formula "determinable indeterminacy" (*bestimmbare Unbestimmtheit*) restates the gerundive as a modality. What the address voiced as a demand, "there is still more to see", is the same structure seen from the thing's side: the empty horizon is a lack that is already oriented toward its fulfilment.

The main commentary reads the address as the first trace of affective allure. The wording supports a narrower claim. What calls in §1 is the horizon, and it calls in every perception, however inattentive, because every aspect has its references. Affection in §32 is "the peculiar pull that an object of consciousness exercises on the ego", a pull that depends on prominence and contrast, comes in degrees, and may fail to reach the ego. Husserl separates "actual affection" from a mere "tendency toward affection" there (Hua XI 148–149). The referring tendencies of §1 belong to neither; they are tendencies of the intention, not of the ego. What §32 does take from §1 is the goal. Affection "is relaxed in turning toward and continues in striving after self-giving intuition … that is, after taking note, after closer examination of the object" (*nach Kenntnisnahme, nach näherer Betrachtung*, Hua XI 148–149). The thing's "so you will get to know me" names the striving that follows on affection, not affection itself. The two are joined in the lectures as the passive and active sides of the same course: the horizon prescribes where knowledge could go, and affection decides whether the ego goes there.

*Ideas II* had already used the same figure for that second relation. There the object "presses itself on the subject", exerts stimuli on it, and "wants, as it were, to be the object of turning toward, knocks at the gate of consciousness …, it attracts" (*es will gleichsam Objekt der Zuwendung sein, klopft an die Pforte des Bewußtseins*, Hua IV 219–220). The personification carries the same hedge, "as it were" (*gleichsam*), but its addressee is the ego's attention, and its vocabulary is that of *Reiz*. Hua XI moves the speaking object to the opening of the theory of perception and lets it speak for the horizon. The difference is the one the lectures depend on. Before affection can be explained by prominence and force, the object's call has to be shown to belong to the structure of every perception, so that what affection awakens is a sense already prefigured in the empty halo.

## §3, Hua XI 11: "association of similarity" and the acquisition of an inner tradition

Reads [§3, second paragraph](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-11-analyses-concerning-passive-and-active-synthesis-commentary.md#the-possibility-of-our-acquired-knowledge-being-freely-at-our-disposal).

Knowledge acquired of one thing passes, without any act, to a second thing that looks like it: the new thing arrives with the first one's unseen sides already prefigured. Husserl calls the result an "acquisition of an inner tradition", a handing-down within one consciousness from what it learned before to what it now sees. He names the mechanism with the psychologists' word, "association of similarity", but sets it in quotation marks and inside a parenthesis. The eidetic law stands in the main clause; the borrowed name waits in brackets for Division 3 to give it a phenomenological sense.

> But one more thing must be added as essential. If we have got to know a thing, and a second thing enters our field of vision that agrees with the earlier, familiar one in the side actually seen, then by an essential law of consciousness (by virtue of an inner coinciding with the earlier thing awakened through "association of similarity") the new thing receives the whole prefiguring of knowledge from the earlier. It is, as they say, apperceived with the same unseen properties as the old one. And this prefiguring too, this acquisition of an inner tradition, is at our free disposal in the form of actualizing perception.

German:

> Doch noch ein weiteres ist als wesentlich beizufügen. Haben wir ein Ding kennengelernt und tritt ein zweites Ding in unseren Gesichtskreis, das nach der eigentlich gesehenen Seite mit dem früheren und bekannten übereinstimmt, so erhält nach einem Wesensgesetz des Bewußtseins (vermöge einer inneren Deckung mit dem durch „Ähnlichkeitsassoziation“ geweckten früheren) das neue Ding die ganze Kenntnisvorzeichnung vom früheren her. Es wird, wie man sagt, apperzipiert mit gleichen unsichtigen Eigenschaften wie das alte. Und auch diese Vorzeichnung, dieser Erwerb innerer Tradition ist zu unserer freien Verfügung in Form aktualisierender Wahrnehmung.

The sentence ranks its terms by syntax. Its main clause has an essential law and a transfer: "by an essential law of consciousness … the new thing receives the whole prefiguring of knowledge from the earlier" (*so erhält nach einem Wesensgesetz des Bewußtseins … das neue Ding die ganze Kenntnisvorzeichnung vom früheren her*). The parenthesis supplies the mechanism in two layers. Its own term is "inner coinciding" (*innere Deckung*), the word §1 used for the synthesis that holds one thing together across its aspects. Its borrowed term is "association of similarity", in quotation marks and in the passive participle's agent phrase: the earlier thing is "awakened through" it (*durch „Ähnlichkeitsassoziation“ geweckten*). Husserl's account of the transfer is thus a coinciding, which is his concept, prompted by an awakening, which will be his concept, and the psychologists' name stands only for what does the awakening. The next sentence adds a second distancing, "as they say" (*wie man sagt*), to the technical "apperceived". Both marks report a vocabulary without endorsing its theory, which in the psychology of the time was a causal law of reproduction.

The phrase the main commentary sets in quotation marks, "acquisition of an inner tradition" (*dieser Erwerb innerer Tradition*), is unmarked in the source, and it is Husserl's own. It is placed in apposition to "this prefiguring" (*diese Vorzeichnung*), so it names a result, not a process. "Acquisition" continues the economic vocabulary of §§2–3: perception "acquires knowledge" and with it "a lasting property of what was acquired, a possession available at any time" (*ein für die Dauer bleibendes Eigentum des Erworbenen, einen jederzeit verfügbaren Besitz*, Hua XI 10–11). What §3 had shown for one thing, that the emptied retention of its seen sides can always be refilled by perceiving it again, the passage extends to its kind. "Tradition" says how. A tradition hands something down from earlier to later, and the earlier thing hands its prefiguring down to the later one. "Inner" keeps the handing-down inside one stream of consciousness. The word appears nowhere else in Hua XI. It anticipates, in a single phrase, the genetic thesis that every apperception has a history.

The passage sits at the hinge of §3. Before it, free disposal over acquired knowledge rests on the possibility of re-perceiving the same thing, "the basic character of transcendent perception through which alone a lasting world can be there for us" (Hua XI 10–11). After it, Husserl asks what that freedom looks like more closely and turns to the systems of kinaesthetic and aspectual possibilities. The insertion widens the claim. A lasting world needs not only re-perception of the same but anticipation of the similar, and the second is not under the ego's control. The transfer happens "by an essential law"; the freedom lies only in being able to check it by perception.

The quotation marks are redeemed at §26, where Husserl takes back the title for phenomenology. "For us the title association designates a form and lawfulness of immanent genesis that belongs constantly to consciousness as such, but not, as for the psychologists, a form of objective, psychophysical causality" (*Uns bezeichnet der Titel Assoziation … nicht aber, wie den Psychologen, eine Form objektiver, psychophysischer Kausalität*, Hua XI 117). The opposition "for us" against "for the psychologists" is the one §3's quotation marks only implied, and §26 adds that the old theory leaves behind a "core of phenomenological facts" once reduced (Hua XI 118–119). Division 3 then supplies what §3 assumed: awakening (*Weckung*) as the basic associative relation, and coinciding of similars as its condition. The reading of §26 below follows that redefinition. Measured against it, §3 is the first use of the word under a reservation, placed so that the transfer of sense from thing to thing could be described before its theory was available. The phrase that carries the description, "inner tradition", is the one place in the main text where that transfer is named as a history.

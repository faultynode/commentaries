# Husserl, *Ideen II*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) maps the argument of the whole book at equal depth. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries instead: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds that meaning, what work the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the book and in his other works.

The text is the German of Husserliana IV, *Ideen zu einer reinen Phänomenologie und phänomenologischen Philosophie, Zweites Buch*, edited by Marly Biemel. Passages are cited by § and printed page, "Hua IV n". Unit references such as "§4.12" name the twelfth paragraph of the main commentary under § 4. All English renderings are my working translations, close enough to show the structure under discussion. They are not the published translation by Rojcewicz and Schuwer. Other Husserl volumes are cited by Husserliana volume and page where the corpus file carries the page, and otherwise by section.

Biemel's edition rests on Ludwig Landgrebe's typescript of 1924/25, into which Husserl kept writing until about 1928. Her text-critical appendix reports every substantive insertion "von H. in L." [by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript], that is, everything entered after 1924 (Hua IV, editor's introduction). Several passages read here turn out to belong to that late layer, and the readings say so where it changes what the wording can carry.

The German comes from a converted file that is not wholly reliable. In this file, running heads are sometimes stacked or placed a few lines early: the heads for pp. 37 and 38 stand together, and the head for p. 25 stands before the start of § 11, which belongs to p. 24. Pages here follow the apparatus where it settles them. The file keeps the print's margin line numbers inside the text in places. These are dropped from quotations without comment, and so are footnote markers. Line-end hyphens are joined, a hyphen splitting a word in mid-line is removed, and a missing space between two words is restored. One Greek word that the file renders as LaTeX is given in Greek. Every other oddity is quoted as the file has it and discussed in a note where it matters. Pages 192–242 are missing from the file altogether (see the list's header). Where a reading refers to those pages, it relies on the main commentary and says so.

## §4, Hua IV 9–10: value-taking

Reads [§4.12](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), twelfth paragraph under § 4.

For Husserl, value-taking (*Wertnehmung*) is the way a value is originally there for consciousness. It is a feeling in which the ego is present at the valued object itself, as perception is the ego's presence at the perceived. Value is not inferred from feeling, nor added to a perceived thing by judgment. It is given in feeling, and the feeling that gives it is enjoyment. The word is a coinage built on *Wahrnehmung* [perception], and the passage asks the coinage to carry the analogy that justifies it.

> The most original constitution of value takes place in the heart, as that pre-theoretical (in a wide sense of the word) enjoying devotion of the feeling ego-subject for which I used the expression *value-taking* in lectures decades ago. The expression thus designates an analogue, belonging to the sphere of feeling, of perception, which in the doxic sphere means the original (self-grasping) being-there of the ego at the object itself. So in the sphere of the heart it is that feeling in which the ego lives in consciousness of being there feelingly at the object "itself", and precisely that is what the talk of enjoying means. But just as there is, so to speak, a presenting from afar, an empty presenting meaning and not being-there oneself, so there is an empty object-related feeling; and as the former is fulfilled in intuitive presenting, so the empty feeling is fulfilled by enjoying. On both sides we have intentions striving in parallel: a presenting striving (cognizing, tending toward cognition) and a valuing one, tending toward *expectation*, toward enjoying. The parallel of expressions perceiving — value-taking was meant to express the similarity.

German:

> Die ursprünglichste Wertkonstitution vollzieht sich im Gemüt als jene vortheoretische (in einem weiten Wortsinne) genießende Hingegeben des fühlenden Ichsubjektes, für die ich den Ausdruck Wertnehmung schon vor Jahrzehnten in Vorlesungen verwendet habe. Der Ausdruck bezeichnet also ein der Gefühlssphäre zugehöriges Analogon der Wahrnehmung, die in der doxischen Sphäre das ursprungliche (selbsterfassende) Dabeisein des Ich bei dem Gegenstande selbst bedeutet. Also in der Gemütssphäre dasjenige Fühlen, in dem das Ich im Bewußtsein lebt, bei dem Objekte ,selbst‘ fühlend dabei zu sein, und eben das meint die Rede vom Genießen. Wie es aber sozusagen ein von ferne Vorstellen gibt, ein leeres vorstellendes Meinen und nicht selbst Dabeisein, so ein leeres gegenstandbezogenes Fühlen; und wie sich jenes im anschaulichen Vorstellen erfüllt, so das leere Fühlen durch das Genießen. Beiderseits haben wir parallel strebende Intentionen, vorstellendes (erkennendes, auf Erkenntnis hin tendierendes) Streben und wertendens, auf *Erwartung*, auf Genießen hin tendierendes. Der Ähnlichkeit sollte die Ausdrucksparallele Wahrnehmen — Wertnehmen Ausdruck geben.

*Note on the text.* The file has *Hingegeben* where the sense requires a noun (*Hingegebenheit*, devotion), *ursprungliche* for *ursprüngliche*, and *wertendens* for *wertendes*. The italic *Erwartung* [expectation] sits oddly beside *Genießen* [enjoying] as the goal of the valuing striving. Nothing below rests on it.

The coinage replaces one member of a compound and keeps the other. *Wahr-nehmung* becomes *Wert-nehmung*, and the *-nehmung* [taking] survives. Husserl does not explain the first member of *Wahrnehmung*. What he carries over is the taking: perception "means" the ego's "being-there at the object itself" (*Dabeisein des Ich bei dem Gegenstande selbst*), and value-taking is "that feeling" in which the ego is "there feelingly at the object 'itself'" (*bei dem Objekte ,selbst‘ fühlend dabei*). The phrase is the same, *bei … dabei*, with one adverb added. "Feelingly" names the mode, and everything else in the definition is perception's. The word "thus" (*also*) in the second sentence shows the order of explanation. The expression designates an analogue, and the analogy is the content of the term. Husserl does not first describe a feeling and then find a name for it.

The analogy is then carried into the structure of fulfilment. Perception has its empty counterpart, "a presenting from afar" (*von ferne Vorstellen*), and its fulfilment in intuition. Feeling is given the same pair: an "empty object-related feeling", fulfilled "by enjoying". The paragraph states this as a parallel, "On both sides we have intentions striving in parallel", and the final sentence says the pair of words "was meant to express the similarity" (*Der Ähnlichkeit sollte … Ausdruck geben*). Here the inference is weaker than the wording. That an empty feeling relates to fulfilled enjoyment as an empty meaning relates to an intuition is the thesis the coinage was introduced to state. The parallel of words expresses the parallel of acts. It does not establish it. The sentence after the quotation restricts the term: "value-feeling" (*Wertfühlen*) "remains the more general expression", present in non-original modes too. *Wertnehmung* is reserved for the fulfilled, original case, as *Wahrnehmung* is reserved for the original givenness of a thing.

The passage answers a question that the preceding sentence of § 4 poses in spaced type: every consciousness that originally constitutes a value-object "as such" necessarily has "a component that belongs to the *sphere of the heart*" (*Gemütssphäre*, Hua IV 9). The analysis just before has shown that a picture can be "enjoyed" or judged "beautiful" by the critic. The second is a theoretical act, and its object is "a new 'theoretical' objectivity", "an object of higher level" (Hua IV 9). Value-taking names the lower act that the higher one presupposes. The paragraphs after it go on to the inadequate case: the "beauty of an old Gothic building" grasped "in one glance" and anticipated "on indications" without being given, and yet "this anticipation of feeling already suffices for a doxic turn and predication" (Hua IV 10). So a value-judgment can outrun the value-taking that would justify it. The concept that § 4 needs here is a standard of originality for feeling. Without it, the distinction between a value given and a value merely asserted could not be drawn.

The apparatus changes how the passage must be dated. The whole stretch from "Die ursprünglichste Wertkonstituierung" to "Prädikation" (Hua IV 9,26–10,20) is a supplement "von H. in L.", entered after 1924. The text it replaced said only that "this component of the heart is not an act in which we live performingly" (apparatus to 9,26–10,20). The term, the claim to have used it "decades ago", and the whole doctrine of empty and fulfilled feeling therefore belong to the late revision, not to the 1912 draft. "Decades ago" is a retrospect from the 1920s.

The understanding returns once in the book, in the personalistic analysis of the surrounding world, and its frame has changed. In § 50 a valuing built on intuitive presenting "plays the role of a *value-'perception'* (in our speech: value-taking)" (*Wert-„Wahrnehmung“ (in unserer Rede: Wertnehmung)*, Hua IV 186, :3270). The scare quotes now fall on *Wahrnehmung*, and the coinage is marked as Husserl's own idiom. The example is the tone of a violin whose beauty is "originally given" when it moves the heart. The new word in the passage is "motivating": the details of the violin's form "exercise their motivating force" in the unity of the intuition. In § 4 value-taking served a theory of attitudes, the feeling act that a theoretical turn presupposes. In § 50 it serves a theory of the person's world: the originally felt value is one of the things that motivate a person. The concept is the same, but it has passed from the analysis of acts into the analysis of motivation that governs Section III.

The corpus shows the coinage in two other places. In the logic course first given in Göttingen in 1910/11, the English translation reads the term as still being proposed: "a precisely parallel, originary presentive act that we could, for example, call value-taking, the act of direct value-givenness" (*Logic and General Theory of Science*, §65 a, whose heading reads "Mere Value-Takings (*Wertnehmungen*) as Grounded Acts"). There, value-takings already "legitimize": "Judgments draw justification from perceptions (*Wahrnehmungen*) or value-takings (*Wertnehmungen*)" (§67). In *Erste Philosophie* II (1923/24) the noun has become a verb: "The value itself in its value-truth is not perceived but as it were value-taken; what perception does for the mere thing, value-taking does for the value. It is the mode of fulfilment of valuing feeling" (*Der Wert selbst in seiner Wertwahrheit wird nicht wahrgenommen, sondern gleichsam wertgenommen; … Sie ist der Erfüllungsmodus des wertenden Fühlens*, Hua VIII 105). That is the doctrine of the Hua IV supplement in a single sentence, and it is close in date to the supplement. The "decades" of Hua IV 9 are consistent with the logic course. The two late texts agree that the word says no more than its analogy. Value-taking is to value what perception is to the thing, and that is the whole of its definition.

## §6, Hua IV 15: beauty is no reflection-predicate

Reads [§6.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 6.

The claim is that predicates such as "beautiful", "pleasing" and "sad" are predicates of the object, found at the object, and not predicates about my acts. They arise through a change of attitude: I stop living in the pleasure and look at what it is pleasure in. The acts remain presupposed but are no longer performed in the distinguished sense. This turn differs in kind from reflection, which can find only predicates relative to consciousness. Husserl's own footnotes then concede that such predicates are "subjective" in a sense that the main text had denied.

> The beauty I intuit at the object, though not like its colour or shape in simple sensuous perception; but at the object itself I find the beautiful. "Beautiful" is anything but a *reflection-predicate*, such as when I say it is one that pleases me. The "pleasing", the "gladdening", the "sad" and all predicates of the object on the same level are, in their objective sense, *not relation-predicates referred to the acts*. They arise through the change of attitude we have described: the acts in question are co-presupposed.

German:

> Die Schönheit schaue ich am Gegenstand an, freilich nicht wie seine Farbe oder Gestalt in schlichter sinnlicher Wahrnehmung; aber am Gegenstand selbst finde ich das Schöne. Das Schön sagt nichts weniger als ein *Reflexionsprädikat*, wie etwa, wenn ich sage, er sei ein mir gefallender. Das „gefällig”, das „erfreulich”, das „traurig” und alle gleichstehenden Gegenstandsprädikate sind ihrem objektiven Sinne nach *nicht Relationsprädikate*, *bezogen auf die Akte*. Sie erwachsen durch die von uns beschriebene Einstellungsänderung: die betreffende Akte sind dabei mit vorausgesetzt.

*Note on the text.* "Das Schön" is probably *Das „Schön“* with its quotation marks lost: the next sentence puts "gefällig", "erfreulich" and "traurig" in quotation marks. The sentence before the passage ends in "seine Schönheitsrichtung", which does not construe. The sense needs a verb, perhaps *richten* [to direct], and the reading does not use it.

The decisive phrase is an idiom. *Nichts weniger als* means "anything but", not "nothing less than". "Beautiful" is anything but a reflection-predicate, and the next sentence repeats the denial: such predicates are "*not relation-predicates*". The main commentary renders the phrase literally ("says nothing less than a reflection-predicate") and so states the opposite of the text. It goes on correctly from the following sentence, so the error is local, but it stands in the one place where the passage says what kind of predicate beauty is. It is logged under the list's corrections.

The positive claim is carried by a preposition and a verb. "At the object" (*am Gegenstand*) occurs twice in the first sentence, the second time strengthened by "itself" (*selbst*). The verb is *anschauen* [to intuit], the word for the most original seeing. The concession comes between the two: "though not like its colour or shape in simple sensuous perception" (*freilich nicht wie … in schlichter sinnlicher Wahrnehmung*). Beauty is intuited, and it is intuited at the object, but not sensuously. This is the place that § 4 prepared with "axiologically intuited" (*axiologisch Angeschautes*, Hua IV 9). The predicates "arise" (*erwachsen*) through the change of attitude, and the acts are "co-presupposed" (*mit vorausgesetzt*). The prefix *mit-* is exact. The feeling acts found the predicate without being its topic. The rest of the paragraph names the result a "layer": the correlates of the acts of the heart are found "at the object" as "an objective layer laid over the layer of the sensuous predicates" (*eine objektive Schicht, übergelagert über die Schicht der sinnlichen Prädikate*). The contrast with reflection closes the paragraph: "In the theoretical attitude of reflection I can find no objective predicates, only predicates relative to consciousness" (*bewußtseinsrelative*).

The footnotes to this sentence and to the next paragraph are Husserl's later marginal notes in Landgrebe's typescript (the apparatus: "15 Der Text der Fußnoten = Randbemerkungen von H. in L."). They retract part of what the main text says: "But it ought to be worked out that and why such predicates of the heart are, after all, merely subjective in a special sense, referring to valuing subjects and thereby to acts of these subjects, in which they are constituted for them and not for everyone" (Hua IV 15 n. 1). The second note reconciles the two claims. Predicates of the heart are "objective" in the general sense of determining the object. They are nonetheless "rightly called 'subjective' in a good sense too", since "in their sense itself they refer back to valuing subjects". They differ in this from "merely natural, purely factual predicates, which in their own sense indicate nothing of the subject and its acts" (n. 2). The main text had denied that beauty is a relation-predicate. The notes accept that its sense refers to a subject, and they move the difference from relation to sense. Beauty is not a predicate about my present act, but its meaning points to valuers. That is a weaker claim than the main text's, and a more defensible one. Here the inference was weaker than the wording made it look. That the predicate "arises" through a turn of attitude does not show that it is free of reference to the subject, and Husserl saw this when he reread the passage.

The passage gives § 7 its premise. Because value-predicates are objective in this sense, every act is "implicitly also objectivating" (*implizite zugleich objektivierend*, Hua IV 17), and the theoretical subject can either let the new strata "remain out of action" and grasp "*mere things*", or take them in and have "values, goods" (Hua IV 17). The second note's phrase, predicates that "indicate nothing of the subject", is the definition that § 11 needs for nature as "a sphere of 'mere things'" (Hua IV 24). Nature is exactly what is left when the predicates whose sense points to valuers are set aside.

Section III changes what the footnotes concede. When the person becomes the theme, its surrounding world contains things "as our surroundings and not, as in natural science, as 'objective' nature" (Hua IV 183, :3244), and value-characters belong to that world as experienced. The reference of value-predicates to valuing subjects is then no longer a qualification on their objectivity. It is what gives them their place: the person is "the subject of a surrounding world" (*Subjekt einer Umwelt*, Hua IV 185, :3260), and a predicate that points to valuers points to persons. In § 6 the footnotes treat this "subjective in a good sense" as a correction to the main text. By the end of the book it has become the characteristic of everything that belongs to the spiritual world. The understanding the passage reaches is that a value-predicate belongs to the object and refers to a subject in the same act. The main text of § 6 states the first half, and the late notes add the second.

## §9, Hua IV 18–20: aesthetic synthesis

Reads [§9.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §9.4, third and fourth paragraphs under § 9.

By "aesthetic synthesis" Husserl means the synthesis that makes one thing out of the many partial apprehensions of a perception without any act of connecting. "Aesthetic" here means sensory, in the sense of *aisthēsis*. The unity of a perceived thing is synthetic, built from a manifold of theses, but it is not produced by the ego. Categorial synthesis is a spontaneous doing. Aesthetic synthesis is not. The distinction lets the first constituted objects be unities without being products of thought.

> Thus every simple perception of a thing (that is, an originally giving consciousness of the present existence of a thing) leads us back intentionally. It demands of us *single considerations, single runnings-through, transitions in series of perception*, which are indeed spanned by the unity of a continuous thesis, but evidently in such a way that the many single theses are in no way united in the form of a categorial synthesis. What gives these single theses unity is a synthesis of a wholly different kind: we will call it the *aesthetic* synthesis. If we try to delimit the two against each other in their peculiarity, we find as a first distinguishing mark that categorial synthesis, as synthesis, is a spontaneous act, and *sensuous* synthesis is not. The connecting is in the one case itself a spontaneous doing, an activity of its own, in the other not.

German:

> So führt uns jede schlichte Dingwahrnehmung (also ein originär gebendes Bewußtsein vom gegenwärtigen Dasein eines Dinges) intentional zurück, sie fordert von uns *Einzelbetrachtungen, Einzeldurchlaufungen, Übergänge in Wahrnehmungsreihen*, die zwar von der Einheit einer kontinuierlichen Thesis umspannt sind, aber offenbar so, daß die vielerlei einzelnen Thesen keineswegs in Form einer kategorialen Synthesis geeinigt sind. Was diesen Einzelthesen Einheit verleiht, ist eine Synthesis ganz anderer Art: wir wollen sie die *ästhetische* Synthesis nennen. Suchen wir beide in ihrer Eigentümlichkeit gegeneinander abzugrenzen, so finden wir als ein erstes Unterscheidungsmerkmal, daß die kategoriale Synthesis als Synthesis ein spontaner Akt ist, die *sinnliche* Synthesis dagegen nicht. Die Verknüpfung ist einmal selbst ein spontanes Tun, eine eigene Aktivität, das andere Mal nicht.

The emphasis moves within two sentences. The term is introduced with the adjective italicized, "the *aesthetic* synthesis", and the distinguishing mark italicizes a different adjective: "*sensuous* synthesis is not" spontaneous. The section's heading already joins the two, "aesthetic ('sensuous') synthesis", and the passage treats them as one word in two languages. This matters because "aesthetic" has meant something else for the five preceding pages. In §§ 4–6 it is the adjective of "aesthetic pleasure" and "aesthetic value". In § 9 it is the adjective of Kant's Transcendental Aesthetic, the doctrine of sensibility. Husserl uses both senses on the same pages and never marks the switch. The heading's gloss is the only marker. A reader who takes "aesthetic synthesis" to concern beauty misreads the section.

The verbs keep the ego in view while denying its agency. Perception "demands of us" (*fordert von uns*) single considerations and runnings-through: these are things we do, phases of a looking. What is not done is their unity. The single theses are "spanned" (*umspannt*) by a continuous thesis, "but evidently" (*aber offenbar*) not united categorially. The "evidently" carries the claim that the unity of perceptual phases is not a categorial synthesis, and nothing in the passage argues it. The definition of the new term is a stipulation, "we will call it" (*wir wollen … nennen*), and its content is first only negative, "a synthesis of a wholly different kind". The one positive mark is spontaneity, and it is introduced as "a first distinguishing mark" (*ein erstes Unterscheidungsmerkmal*). No second mark follows. Two paragraphs later Husserl says the indications "do not suffice for an exhaustive description" and that the task would need "a large investigation of its own" (Hua IV 20).

The apparatus dates the last sentence of the passage. "Die Verknüpfung …" through "sinnlichen Merkmale" is a supplement "von H. in L." (apparatus to 19). The sentence that defines the difference as the difference between a doing and a not-doing, "a spontaneous doing, an activity of its own, in the other case not", was added after 1924. So were the footnotes on p. 19, which the apparatus also gives as marginal notes in Landgrebe's typescript. One of them asks whether "synthesis" should not be split into two concepts: "synthesis as connection proper, combination, an expression that points to what is *separate*", and "continuous synthesis as continuous fusion" (*kontinuierliche Verschmelzung*). It concludes that every aesthetic synthesis of the first kind "leads to ultimate elements", and that the sensuous features from which the thing is built "stem in their turn from continuous synthesis" (Hua IV 19 n.). The "ultimate sensory features" that the main text calls elements "which have not themselves come about through aesthetic synthesis" are thus, by the note, themselves synthetic, in the sense of fusion. The late Husserl sharpens the definition of aesthetic synthesis and at the same time doubts its lower limit.

A further footnote (Hua IV 20 n.) names the opponent: aesthetic synthesis "as aesthetic-causal" is also at work "in the higher strata of thing-constitution", and it is "the only one Kant has in view in his concept of synthesis". The claim is sharp against Kant's text. In the second-edition Deduction, all combination "can never come into us through the senses"; it is "an act of the spontaneity of the power of representation", and "all combination … is an act of the understanding, to which we would give the general name synthesis" (*Kritik der reinen Vernunft*, B, § 15, *Von der Möglichkeit einer Verbindung überhaupt*). Kant makes every synthesis spontaneous. Husserl's first distinguishing mark makes the synthesis of perception the one that is not, and his note then says that this non-spontaneous synthesis is what Kant was really describing. The note does not argue the point. It reassigns Kant's synthesis from the understanding to sensibility, which is the move the whole section depends on.

In the section, the passage stands between the categorial objects of § 9's opening and the "primitive" objects of § 10. Its third function, the synthesis that links "moments of the 'thing-appearance'" with "the correlative 'circumstances of perception' (e.g. the state of the eye in seeing, the position of arm, hand and finger in touching)" (Hua IV 20), is the seed of two later analyses. The first is the dependence of the material thing on "circumstances" in § 15 (the footnote itself sends the reader to "S. 41 ff."). The second is the body's kinaesthetic "if–so" in § 18 a (Hua IV 58), where the same pairing of appearance and circumstance becomes "motivation". The understanding is carried furthest in Section III. There the commentary reports, from pages missing in the corpus file (§56 c, Hua IV 225), that the apprehensions of things are "webs of motivation". In § 61 the "underground" of sense, where associations "'make' the constitution of nature", runs on according to "'blind' rules" (Hua IV 277). The book ends where § 9 began: the sensuous unity that § 9 calls synthesis without spontaneity is renamed motivation without the ego, and that is the name it keeps.

## §11, Hua IV 24–27: a kind of reduction

Reads [§11.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 11.

Nature, as the correlate of natural science, is reached by a principled restriction of the experiencing subject. Husserl calls it "a kind of 'reduction'" and later "a kind of epoché". The subject brackets all apperceptions that stem from feeling and lets only doxic, objectivating consciousness constitute objects. What remains is a world of "mere things". The operation is not arbitrary abstraction, because it is fixed a priori by the essence of the constituting consciousness. The passage presents it as method. Section III later redescribes the same operation as a form of the person's self-forgetfulness.

> But this in the sense described earlier. We accordingly carry out a kind of "reduction". All our intentions of the heart, and all apperceptions stemming from the intentionality of the heart, we bracket as it were. It is by virtue of these that spatio-temporal objectivities constantly appear to us, before all thinking, in immediate "intuitiveness", as endowed with certain value-characters and practical characters, all of them characters that go beyond the stratum of mere factuality. In this "pure" or purified theoretical attitude, then, we no longer experience houses, tables, streets, works of art. We experience merely material things, and of such value-laden things only their stratum of spatio-temporal materiality; and likewise, for human beings and human societies, only the stratum of psychic "nature" bound to spatio-temporal "bodies".

German:

> Das aber in dem früher beschriebenen Sinn. Wir vollziehen danach eine Art „Reduktion“. Alle unsere Gemütsintentionen und alle aus der Intentionalität des Gemüts herstammenden Apperzeptionen klammern wir gleichsam ein, vermöge deren uns beständig die raumzeitlichen Gegenständlichkeiten vor allem Denken, in unmittelbarer „Anschaulichkeit“ mit gewissen Wertcharakteren, praktischen Charakteren behaftet erscheinen — lauter Charakteren, die über die Schicht bloßer Sachlichkeit hinausgehen. Wir erfahren also in dieser ,,reinen“ oder gereinigten theoretischen Einstellung nicht mehr Häuser, Tische, Straßen, Kunstwerke, wir erfahren bloß materielle Dinge und von solchen wertbehafteten Dinge eben nur ihre Schicht der räumlich-zeitlichen Materialität und ebenso für Menschen und menschliche Gesellschaften nur die Schicht der an räumlich-zeitliche „Leiber“ gebundenen seelischen „Natur“.

*Note on the text.* The file's running head for p. 25 stands before the § 11 heading, but the apparatus places the opening of § 11 at Hua IV 24,37. At the end of the section the file renders the Greek *epoché* as LaTeX (`$\varepsilon\pi o\chi\eta$`, :887). The word is legible, and the reading uses it.

Three hedges control the passage, and each is placed with care. "A kind of" (*eine Art*) limits the noun. The quotation marks around "reduction" keep the term at a distance. "As it were" (*gleichsam*) qualifies the bracketing. Husserl uses the vocabulary of *Ideas I* and marks it three times as borrowed. The reason appears when the operations are compared. In *Ideas I* the epoché puts out of action "the general thesis belonging to the essence of the natural attitude" and is limited deliberately, by "a definitely restricted" bracketing (*Ideen I* § 32, Hua III 65; the corpus file of Hua III/1 is an unreviewed conversion, but this page reads cleanly). In § 11 the general thesis stays in force. What is bracketed is the heart, and the doxic positing of a spatio-temporal world is the one thing kept. The section's last paragraph says so: "the whole of consciousness we take along as object, but we let ourselves have 'objects constituted' only by the doxic objectivating consciousness, not by the valuing one", and then, "we thereby carry out a kind of exclusion, a kind of epoché" (*eine Art Ausschaltung, eine Art ἐποχή*, Hua IV 27). Both operations bracket something. The phenomenological epoché brackets the thesis and keeps the world as phenomenon. The naturalistic "reduction" keeps the thesis and brackets the world's values. The "kind of" marks an inversion.

The word "pure" carries the same double sense. The attitude is "'pure' or purified" (*,,reinen“ oder gereinigten*). The quoted adjective is the one *Ideas I* gives to pure consciousness, and the gloss "purified" turns it into a past participle, the product of an act of cleaning. The list of what disappears is concrete: "houses, tables, streets, works of art". What remains is named twice in terms of strata: "only their stratum of spatio-temporal materiality", "only the stratum of psychic 'nature'". The quotation marks on "bodies" and "nature" in the last clause are the first sign in the book that the human being as nature is a reduced object. Human beings and societies survive the reduction, but only as nature in quotation marks.

The paragraph before the passage claims that the demarcation is not "arbitrary abstraction" but "prescribed a priori in the essence of the constituting consciousness" (Hua IV 24). The paragraphs after it qualify the subject that performs it. That subject still values: it "values the knowledge of appearing being", values the "so it is", and "produces changes practically in the experiment" for the sake of knowledge (Hua IV 26). What is abstracted from is only "all other values as against the *values of knowing*" (*Wissenswerten*). The feeling acts themselves are not excluded from nature. "They belong entirely to the sphere of things" (*gehören … durchaus der Sachsphäre zu*), taken along as objects (Hua IV 27). The main commentary's last paragraph on § 11 says these acts "co-determine the full intentional situation". That is not what the source says, and the point is logged under corrections. What the source says is the harder claim: the valuing acts of the scientist are themselves natural facts in the nature his reduction constitutes.

The apparatus shows how late and how unfinished the section is. Its opening, from "Gehen wir nun wieder zurück" to "seelischen ‚Natur'" (Hua IV 24,37–25,40), the whole passage read here included, is "a supplementary sheet by H. in L.". A pencil note follows it: "The whole of § 11 is still not sufficient and does not connect with the deeper discussions of the preceding investigations, nor does it yet conclude them. It also needs the concept of aesthetic (sensuous) experience and a clarification of the correlate of knowledge from experience" (apparatus to 24,37–25,40). At the start of p. 26, where the qualification about the valuing scientist begins, Husserl wrote: "That is misleading as it stands" (*Das ist, so wie es da steht, irreführend*, apparatus to 26,1ff.). The "kind of reduction" is thus a formulation of the 1920s, written by a Husserl who judged the section inadequate. The concept he says it lacks is aesthetic experience, the subject of § 9.

The understanding is taken up again in the opening of Section III, under another name. § 49 d sets beside it "changes of attitude of equal birth (*ebenbürtig*) with the natural or, as we now say more distinctly, the *naturale* attitude" (Hua IV 180, :3224). In § 49 e its independence is said to arise "through an abstraction or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal ego", by which it "illegitimately absolutizes its world, nature" (Hua IV 184, :3248). The operation is the same: an abstraction from everything the person's heart contributes. The verdict differs. In § 11 the abstraction is a method that yields "an a priori closed idea of nature". In § 49 the same abstraction becomes a forgetting, legitimate as an abstraction and illegitimate as soon as its result is taken for the whole. "A kind of reduction" was already the right description, since a reduction that keeps the thesis of the world is the attitude of the natural scientist and not of the phenomenologist. What § 11 calls method, § 49 calls habit, and the text of § 11 reaches toward the second description only in Husserl's later notes in its margins.

## §12, Hua IV 28–29: extension, rightly understood

Reads [§12.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), fourth paragraph under § 12.

Extension, "rightly understood", is for Husserl not the essence of material being but the dividing line between the two kinds of nature. The full essential attribute of the material is materiality. Extension marks the boundary because everything that belongs to a material thing is a priori referred to its extension, while the psychic excludes extension by its essence. Animal nature is therefore a complex: a lower stratum that is extended and an upper stratum that is not. Husserl adopts Descartes' attribute and corrects it in the same sentence.

> Not without reason does Descartes designate *extensio* as the essential attribute of the material thing, which is therefore also called simply corporeal, as against psychic or spiritual being, which in its spirituality as such has no *extensio* but rather excludes it by its essence. Indeed, it must above all be brought to insight that *extensio*, rightly understood, separates nature in the first sense from nature in the second sense, although the all-encompassing essential attribute of material being is not mere *extension* but materiality, insofar as this in itself requires spatial as well as temporal extension. What matters is the recognition of the distinctive way in which everything else that belongs to a material thing is referred a priori (that is, always essentially) to its extension. Spiritual nature, understood as animal nature, is a complex consisting of a lower stratum of material nature with the essential mark of *extensio* and an inseparable upper stratum, which is of a fundamentally different essence and above all excludes extension.

German:

> Nicht ohne Grund bezeichnet Descartes die *extensio* als Wesensattribut des materiellen Dinges, das darum auch schlechthin körperliches heißt, gegenüber dem seelischen oder geistigen Sein, das in seiner Geistigkeit als solcher keine extensio hat, sie wesensmäßig vielmehr ausschließt. In der Tat muß vor allem zur Einsicht gebracht werden, daß die extensio, richtig verstanden, die Natur im ersten Sinn von derjenigen im zweiten Sinn scheidet, obschon das vollumfassende Wesensattribut des materiellen Seins nicht die bloße *Ausdehnung* sondern die Materialität ist, sofern diese in sich selbst räumlich wie zeitliche Ausdehnung fordert. Worauf es aber ankommt, ist die Erkenntnis der ausgezeichneten Weise, wie alles, was sonst einem materiellen Dinge zukommt, a priori (das heißt immer wesensmäßig) auf seine Extension bezogen ist. Die geistige Natur, als animalische Natur verstanden, ist ein Komplex, bestehend aus einer Unterschicht materieller Natur mit dem Wesensmerkmal der extensio und einer unabtrennbaren Oberschicht, die von grundverschiedenem Wesen ist und vor allem Extension ausschließt.

*Note on the text.* In the file a page break and footnote rule interrupt the first sentence after "Dinges, das" (:903–:911), and "extensio" is split by a hyphen in mid-line ("exten-sio"). Both are joined here.

The opening is a litotes. "Not without reason" (*Nicht ohne Grund*) grants Descartes a reason and withholds full agreement, and the next sentence supplies the qualification: "rightly understood" (*richtig verstanden*). Husserl keeps the Cartesian vocabulary in three registers. *Extensio*, in Latin and italic, is Descartes' word. *Ausdehnung* and *Extension* are his own. The Latin is used where the thesis is Descartes', and where the thesis is corrected, the word changes: the full attribute is "not mere *extension* (*Ausdehnung*) but materiality". What remains of Descartes is a function. Extension is no longer the essence of matter. It is the mark that "separates" (*scheidet*) nature in the first sense from nature in the second.

The third sentence explains why that mark is decisive, and its key word is Cartesian too. Everything else that belongs to a material thing is "referred a priori … to its extension" (*a priori … auf seine Extension bezogen*). Descartes says the same of the principal attribute: "each substance has one principal property which constitutes its nature and essence, and to which all its other properties are referred", so that "everything else which can be attributed to body presupposes extension" (*Principles of Philosophy* I, art. 53, Cottingham et al., vol. 1). Husserl keeps the relation of reference and denies that the term referred to is the essence. He can then say what Descartes could not: the thing is extended according to "everything whatever that it is" (§13.3, Hua IV 30), and yet its essence is a relation to circumstances that § 15 calls materiality. The apparatus records that "the whole of § 12 was expanded according to H.'s instruction in L. by going back to the manuscript" (apparatus to 27ff.). The section stands as Husserl wanted it in the 1920s.

The last sentence gives the passage its consequence and its weak point. "Spiritual nature, understood as animal nature" is "a complex" (*ein Komplex*) of "a lower stratum" and "an inseparable upper stratum". The vocabulary is geological: *Unterschicht*, *Oberschicht*. The strata are of "fundamentally different essence", and the upper one "excludes extension". The adjective "inseparable" (*unabtrennbar*) does all the work of union, and nothing in the passage says what kind of inseparability it is. A complex of two essentially different strata, one extended and one not, is the Cartesian problem in Husserl's terms, and the word "complex" does not solve it. It postpones it.

The neighbouring sections take the problem in two directions. § 14 turns the dividing mark into a test. Material things are "fragmentable" parallel to their extension; human beings and animals are not (Hua IV 33). Some of the psychic "has something like a spreading", but "not a spreading in space" (§14.2). Section II then puts a third term between extension and its absence: the sensings (*Empfindnisse*) of the body are "localized" without being extended. "Localization of *Empfindnisse* is in fact something principally different from extension of all material thing-determinations", which "characterize the *res extensa*" (§ 37, Hua IV 149, :2825). The Cartesian term returns at the exact place where Husserl finds something it cannot describe. The non-extended layer is not absent from space. It is there in another way.

Section III takes back the image of the complex. In § 49 the soul is "not spread out over the body in genuine localization", and yet the psychic is "experientially one, really one, with the body"; Husserl calls this "the admittedly misleading expression *introjection*" (Hua IV 176, :3205). Once consciousness is posited "purely as itself", it is "absolutely unspatial" (§49 b). In the personalistic attitude the relation is neither stratum nor attachment. Body and spirit are "no connection of two things", and the commentary reports, from pages the corpus file lacks, that a state or a church likewise contains "two kinds of unity" but not "two things" (§56 h.13, Hua IV 243). The last sentence of the book contrasts nature, "the X", which "determines itself through general determinations", with spirit, which "is no X but what is given itself" (Hua IV 302, :4295). That is still Descartes' division between the extended and the thinking, but it is no longer drawn between two strata of one complex. It is drawn between the correlates of two attitudes. The understanding that § 12 gives extension, a boundary rather than an essence, is what lets the book keep the Cartesian line while abandoning the Cartesian picture of what lies on either side of it.

## §15 b, Hua IV 36–37: phantom and schema

Reads [§15 b.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §15 b.3, second and third paragraphs under § 15 b.

A "phantom" is a body that appears in space, filled with colour and texture, but without any sense of materiality. Questions about its weight or elasticity have no foothold in what is seen. A "schema" is the same filled spatial shape as it belongs to every thing: "this basic framework". In perception a thing shows nothing beyond its schema, so materiality is apprehended without being given. Husserl draws the conclusion in terms of dependence. The phantom can be given without materiality, but materiality cannot be given without a phantom. The relation is "one-sided detachment", and it becomes one of the book's standing tools.

> A mere phantom is present, for example, when in the stereoscope we learn to bring suitable groupings into *bodily* fusion. We then see a *spatial body* about which meaningful questions can be asked with respect to its shape, its colour, its smoothness or roughness and similar ordered determinations, questions that can therefore be answered according to the truth, as in the words: this is a red, rough pyramid. On the other hand, what appears can be so given that the question whether it is heavy or light, elastic, magnetic and so on has no sense, or better, no foothold in the sense of the perception. We simply do not see a material thing.
>
> We say that to the essence of a thing belongs a sensuous schema, and by this we understand this basic framework, this bodily ("spatial") shape with the fullness extended over it. The thing appearing as at rest and qualitatively unchanged "shows" us no more than its schema, or rather the apparency, while it is at the same time apprehended as material. In this respect, however, it does not "show" itself, does not properly come into view, not to original givenness. In what is "properly" given nothing would change if the whole stratum of materiality were struck out of the apperception. This is in fact conceivable. In original experience, perception, a "body" is inconceivable without sensuous qualification, but the phantom is originally given and hence also conceivable without the components of materiality, while these for their part are non-independent (one-sided detachment).

German:

> Ein bloßes Phantom liegt z. B. vor, wenn wir im Stereoskop lernen, passende Gruppierungen zu *körperlicher* Verschmelzung zu bringen. Wir sehen dann einen *Raumkörper*, für den hinsichtlich seiner Gestalt, hinsichtlich seiner Farbe, auch hinsichtlich seiner Glätte oder Rauhigkeit und ähnlichen geordneten Bestimmungen sinnvolle Fragen zu stellen sind, die also der Wahrheit gemäß Beantwortung finden können, wie etwa in den Worten: dies ist eine rote, rauhe Pyramide. Andererseits kann das Erscheinende so gegeben sein, daß die Frage, ob es schwer ist oder leicht, ob elastisch, magnetisch usw. gar keinen Sinn besser: keinen Anhalt am Wahrnehmungssinn hat. Wir sehen eben kein materielles Ding.
>
> Wir sagen, zum Wesen eines Dinges gehöre ein sinnliches Schema, und wir verstehen darunter dieses Grundgerüst, diese körperliche (,,räumliche“) Gestalt mit der über sie extendierten Fülle. Das als ruhend und qualitativ unverändert erscheinende Ding „zeigt“ uns nicht mehr als sein Schema oder vielmehr die Apparenz, während es doch zugleich aufgefaßt ist als materielles. In dieser Hinsicht „zeigt“ es sich aber nicht, kommt es uns nicht eigentlich zu Gesicht, nicht zu originaler Gegebenheit. Im ,,eigentlichen“ Gegebenen würde sich nichts ändern, wenn die ganze Schicht der Materialität aus der Apperzeption weggestrichen würde. Das ist in der Tat denkbar. In der originalen Erfahrung, der Wahrnehmung, ist „Körper“ undenkbar ohne sinnliche Qualifiziertheit, das Phantom aber ist original gegeben und somit auch denkbar ohne die Komponenten der Materialität, während diese ihrerseits unselbständig sind (einseitige Ablösung).

*Note on the text.* In the file the running heads for pp. 37 and 38 stand together. The apparatus places "Das ist in der Tat …" at Hua IV 37,21–26, so the second paragraph is on p. 37. Shortly after the passage, p. 40 breaks off in mid-word ("Index zu sein für eine Mannigfaltigkeit möglicher Er", :1079), and the file goes straight on to p. 41. The end of § 15 b's argument from the agreement of several senses is therefore incomplete in the file.

The stereoscope comes with a verb that the rest of the analysis cannot use: "we learn" (*lernen*) to bring the images into "*bodily* fusion". The phantom is an achievement of practice, a seeing that has to be acquired. It is not the pure given that the definition of the schema will need. The apparatus records that Husserl later put "lernen …. zu bringen" in deletion brackets in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 36,16–17). Whatever the clause was replaced with in his intention, the deletion removes the hint that the example is a product of training. The example the section keeps is the series that follows: "a rainbow, the blue sky, the sun" (Hua IV 37). In these the phantom is simply what is seen.

The decisive test is a question about sense. Of the stereoscopic body we can ask about "shape", "colour", "smoothness" and get answers "according to the truth": "this is a red, rough pyramid". The question "whether it is heavy or light, elastic, magnetic" has "no sense, or better, no foothold in the sense of the perception" (*gar keinen Sinn besser: keinen Anhalt am Wahrnehmungssinn*). The self-correction is exact. Such a question is not absurd in itself, but it finds nothing to attach to in what the perception means. A little further on, in spaced type, Husserl distinguishes this from ordinary indeterminacy. The back of a thing is open to determination, but here "essential groups of features are not represented in the apprehension at all" (*wesentliche Gruppen von Merkmalen in der Auffassung überhaupt nicht vertreten*, Hua IV 36–37). A phantom is not an indeterminate thing. It is a different sense of object.

The second paragraph turns the phantom from a special case into a structure of every thing. The word *Schema* is glossed by *Grundgerüst* [basic framework], and the gloss is architectural. "Shows" is put in quotation marks twice, first for what the thing does show ("no more than its schema"), and then for what it does not ("it does not 'show' itself"). Husserl corrects himself in mid-sentence: "its schema or rather the apparency" (*oder vielmehr die Apparenz*). In a marginal note in Landgrebe's typescript he later says that this distinction is the one the passage lacks: "the difference between schema and apparency as what is given of the schema, the side of the schema" (apparatus to 36,35ff.). The thought experiment then sets the terms: if "the whole stratum of materiality" were "struck out", nothing in what is "properly" given would change. Materiality is apprehended but not given. A few lines on, the paragraph sums this up in a formula: materiality "can be apprehended from the outset and yet not be given" (*mit aufgefaßt und doch nicht mit gegeben*, Hua IV 37–38).

The parenthesis that ends the passage turns the result into a relation of founding. Body is "inconceivable without sensuous qualification", the phantom is "conceivable without the components of materiality", and these components are "non-independent" (*unselbständig*). The vocabulary is that of the third *Logical Investigation*, and the parenthesis names the asymmetry: "(one-sided detachment)". According to the apparatus, the sentences from "Das ist in der Tat denkbar" to "(einseitige Ablösung)" are themselves an insertion "von H. in L." (apparatus to 37,21–26). The formula that makes the phantom the independent base of the thing belongs to the late layer.

In the section the passage closes a deliberate abstraction. § 15 b holds the thing "for itself, without regard to the context of things", and it ends by conceding that under this assumption we can find "no possibility of deciding" whether the experienced material thing is real or "a mere phantom" (Hua IV 41). § 15 c removes the assumption. Materiality lies in the relation to circumstances (row 7). The phantom is thus the limit at which the analysis of the isolated thing stops.

The formula of one-sided detachment returns when Husserl analyzes the living body. The aesthesiological stratum is "the substrate for the stratum 'freely movable'"; "the movable is already presupposed as aesthesiological, but the lower stratum is one-sidedly detachable" (*einseitig ablösbar*, § 62, Hua IV 284, :3983). The commentary reports, from pages missing in the corpus file, that § 51 uses it for the egoistic and the communicative surrounding world (§51.6, Hua IV 193). The tool the passage invents for thing and phantom thus serves for body and will, and for the solitary and the social world. In each case the lower stratum can stand alone and the higher one cannot.

The term is older than *Ideen II*. In a supplement to the 1907 lectures *Ding und Raum*, the "temporal-spatial schema together with its sensuous fullness" is already called "the thing-phantom (also the full schema, also the sensuous schema)". Husserl adds that he "would like to say the sensuous 'image', which would recall Kant well, if the word image were not so seductive" (Hua XVI, Beilage II, 343; the file misprints Kant as "Krant"). Two pages later the supplement puts the thesis of § 15 c as a question: "Is the thing not substance exclusively insofar as it is bearer of causality? The mere phantom is not yet a thing" (Hua XVI 345). In 1907 phantom and schema are kept as names because "image" is too dangerous. In *Ideen II* they become the stratum below the thing. What comes from the late revision is the precise relation between the strata, one-sided detachment, which later sections of the book reuse.

## §15 c, Hua IV 41: materiality as relation to circumstances

Reads [§15 c.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 15 c.

What distinguishes a material thing from a phantom is not an added content but a relation. Materiality, "the actual reality" of the thing, lies in the dependence of its schemata on "real circumstances" and in the mode of apprehension that corresponds to this dependence. The thing is the unity that manifests itself in schemata that change, or stay the same, in determinate functional dependence on circumstances. A real property is such a unity of dependence, and so it is causal by definition.

> The actual reality, which here is called materiality, does not lie in the merely sensuous schema, not in what could belong to the perceived if for it there were nothing of the kind, if nothing of the kind had a sense, as relation to "circumstances". Rather it lies precisely in this relation and in the mode of apprehension corresponding to it. Under changing illumination, that is, in relation to another luminous thing, the thing looks different again and again, and not arbitrarily but determinately. Evidently functional connections obtain here, which set the schematic modifications on the one side in relation to those on other sides. It lies in the sense of the apprehension of a thing as thing (and not of the mere phantom) that such schemata, running off in determinate series of modification and now changing, now not changing in a determinate way, are experienced as manifestations of one and the same. But we experience them as that insofar as they run off as "dependents" of belonging "real circumstances".

German:

> Die eigentliche Realität, die hier Materialität heißt, liegt nicht im bloß sinnlichen Schema, nicht in dem, was dem Wahrgenommenen zukommen könnte, wenn es für dasselbe nichts dergleichen gäbe, nichts dergleichen einen Sinn hätte wie Beziehung auf „Umstände“, vielmehr liegt es eben in dieser Beziehung und der dieser entsprechenden Auffassungsweise. Das Ding sieht bei wechselnder Beleuchtung, also mit Beziehung auf ein anderes Leuchtendes immer wieder anders aus, und das nicht beliebig sondern bestimmt. Es bestehen hier offenbar funktionelle Zusammenhänge, welche die schematischen Modifikationen auf der einen Seite zu solchen auf anderen Seiten in Beziehung setzen. Im Sinne der Auffassung eines Dinges als Ding (und nicht des bloßen Phantoms) liegt es, daß solche Schemata, und zwar in bestimmten Modifikationsreihen ablaufend und sich in bestimmter Weise bald ändernd, bald nicht ändernd, als Bekundungen eines und desselben erfahren werden. Wir erfahren sie aber als das, wofern sie als „Abhängige“ zugehöriger „realer Umstände“ ablaufen.

The first sentence names and relocates at once. "The actual reality, which here is called materiality" (*die hier Materialität heißt*): the qualifier "here" restricts the name to this level of constitution, and § 17 will give the general aspect its own name (row 8). The sentence then denies two locations and affirms one. Reality is not "in the merely sensuous schema". It is not in what the perceived would have if "nothing of the kind had a sense, as relation to 'circumstances'". "Rather it lies precisely in this relation and the mode of apprehension corresponding to it" (*vielmehr liegt es eben in dieser Beziehung und der dieser entsprechenden Auffassungsweise*). The pronoun *es* does not agree with *Realität*, a slip of the dictation or of the copy. The phrase it governs joins two things: a relation among appearances and a manner of taking them. Reality is placed in the correlation between the two and not in either term alone.

The quotation marks mark the terms of art. "Circumstances" appears in quotation marks at its first use in § 15 c, and again here. At the end of the passage the related terms appear: the schemata run off as "dependents" of "real circumstances". The vocabulary is that of functions: "functional connections" (*funktionelle Zusammenhänge*), modifications "on the one side" related "to those on other sides". The thing "looks different again and again" under changing light, "not arbitrarily but determinately". The step from determinate variation to unity is carried by "manifestations" (*Bekundungen*). § 15 d sharpens the word into "certification" (*„Beurkundung“*) of a real property (Hua IV 44), a word from the language of documents and witnesses. The schema witnesses to the property.

The example that follows shows how much the inference rests on. A steel spring "has the real property 'elasticity'". "Like circumstances, like consequences" (*Unter gleichen Umständen gleiche Folgen*), and "the general rule … is not conscious as such": "it expresses a form belonging to the apperception of the real property" (Hua IV 43). The rule that founds the real property is not known, only apprehended. It is the form of the perception, and § 15 d concludes that causalities are here "not merely supposed but 'seen', 'perceived'" (Hua IV 44). Husserl's later marginal note picks out the phrase "Gesehene Kausalität" [seen causality] (apparatus to 39,21). The claim is strong and the passage does not argue for it. That the steadily varying schema is seen as the steady behaviour of one thing under circumstances is offered as a description of the sense of thing-apprehension, and the "evidently" (*offenbar*) of the third sentence is the only bridge from variation to function.

In the section the passage turns the abstraction of § 15 b into a definition. § 15 b ended unable to tell the isolated thing from a phantom. § 15 c names what the isolation had removed. § 15 e draws the consequence: "reality or, what here is the same, substantiality and causality belong inseparably together" (§15 e.2). § 16 adds that dependencies can themselves change in dependence on circumstances, so that unities of higher level are formed (Hua IV 46–49). § 18 g states the result as a definition of the thing: it "is a reality as unity of a manifold of regulated appearances that belong together", and its "properties express capacities, they are causal properties, referred to an 'if–so'" (Hua IV 86, :1811).

The definition is carried through the rest of the book, and it decides the treatment of the soul and of spirit. In §§ 30–33 the soul is a "unity of attestation" whose "modes of behaviour" are referred to "circumstances", so the analogy with the thing is formally complete. But "the soul … does not schematize itself" (Hua IV 127, :2462), so the analogy holds only for the form of the relation and not for the way the relation is given. In § 62 the definition becomes the premise of the argument for the priority of spirit: "Causality is the relation of a reality to its correlative circumstance-realities. The reality of spirit, however, is not related to real circumstances lying within nature but to real circumstances that consist in the 'surrounding world' and in other spirits" (Hua IV 283, :3971). Spirit has circumstances too, but they are of another kind. The relational concept of reality that § 15 c establishes for the thing is what lets § 62 say that spirit is real and yet not causal in the sense of nature. And § 64 turns the relation against the thing: "No thing has its individuality in itself" (Hua IV 299, :4251). An identity made of relations to circumstances is individuated only by the here and now of a subject.

Husserl had put the thesis as a question in the 1907 supplement already cited in the previous reading: "Is the thing not substance exclusively insofar as it is bearer of causality? The mere phantom is not yet a thing. … Does not everything in the end lead back to causality, every objective determination of a thing …? … already the unity of the objective property colour is a causal concept, a property of force" (Hua XVI, Beilage II, 345). The illumination example is already there, colour depending "on the course of the illumination by the sun, modified by clouds". *Ideen II* answers the question in the affirmative and adds the qualification of the 1907 text's last phrase: a "property of force" is the thing's "capacity" under circumstances, and the "if–so" of § 18 g is its form. What the passage contributes is the word "lies". Reality lies in a relation and in a way of apprehending it, and nowhere else.

## §17, Hua IV 53–54: reality, substance and their equivocations

Reads [§17.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 17.

Here Husserl fixes a word. The general aspect of thinghood, which § 15 found in the relation to circumstances, would best be called "reality". Tradition calls it "substance", and both words are equivocal. Husserl therefore stipulates "substantial reality" for this narrow sense, against the widest sense in which every individual temporal being is "real". From here on "substance" and "thing" mean this. Extensive substance is only a particular case, and the extension-determinations themselves belong to the schema, not to substance.

> In our considerations we have intentionally let the general aspect of thinghood as such, "reality", stand out more strongly than what belongs to materiality as such, the specific feature of extensive reality. That general aspect, which would surely best be designated as reality, is called *substance*. (Unfortunately all these philosophical terms are burdened with equivocations and lack any deeper clarification.) As against this, we have here drawn from intuition a firm essential stock that is evident in its fundamental significance. To separate these genuine "realities" from the widespread *widest* sense of the word, which comprises **any** individual (or temporal) being, we say *substantial reality*; but henceforth, wherever we speak simply of substantiality, or of substance or thing, this is what is meant. Extensive substance shall count for us only as a particularization. Of the role of extension (corporeality) we have already spoken. Accordingly it is clear that determinations such as position, figure and so on, as belonging to extension, are not substantial properties but rather thoroughly causal ones. As regards their mode of givenness, they are not unities of "certification"; rather they already belong to the *schema*.

German:

> In unseren Betrachtungen haben wir mit Absicht das Allgemeine der Dingheit überhaupt, die „Realität“ stärker hervorreten lassen gegenüber dem, was zur Materialität als solcher als dem Spezifischen der extensiven Realität gehört. Jenes Allgemeine, das sicherlich am besten als Realität zu bezeichnen wäre, heißt *Substanz*. (Leider sind alle diese philosophischen Termini mit Aequivokationen behaftet und entbehren jeder tieferen Klärung). Dem gegenüber haben wir hier einen festen Wesensbestand aus der Intuition geschöpft, der in seiner grundlegenden Bedeutung evident ist. Zur Abscheidung dieser echten „Realitäten“ von dem verbreiteten *weitesten* Wortsinne, der **jedwedes** individuelle (oder zeitliche) Sein befaßt, sagen wir *substantielle Realität*; doch soll im weiteren, wo immer wir einfach von Substantialität, bzw. von Substanz oder Ding sprechen, eben dies gemeint sein. Hierbei soll uns die extensive Substanz nur als eine Besonderung gelten. Von Rolle der Extension (Körperlichkeit) haben wir schon oben gesprochen. Danach ist es klar, daß Bestimmtheiten wie Lage, Figur usw. als zur Extension gehörig nicht substantielle Eigenschaften sind, vielmehr durchaus kausale. Was ihre Gegebenheitsweise anbelangt, so sind sie nicht Einheiten der „Beurkundung“, sie gehören vielmehr schon zum *Schema*.

*Note on the text.* *Hervorreten* is a misprint for *hervortreten* [stand out]. More seriously, "nicht substantielle Eigenschaften sind, vielmehr durchaus kausale" [are not substantial properties but rather thoroughly causal ones] contradicts the definition the paragraph has just given: substantial reality is the reality of causal properties (§ 15 e; row 7). It also contradicts the next sentence, which concedes that shape and position "are dependent on circumstances and experienceable in this causal dependence". That concession makes sense only if the preceding clause denied causality, not affirmed it. The file may have lost a word or garbled one. The reading needs the print and does not build on the clause. The main commentary's version ("they belong rather already to the schema") silently omits it.

The paragraph has two verb moods, and the difference between them is the point. Reality is what the general aspect "would surely best be designated as" (*am besten als Realität zu bezeichnen wäre*), in the subjunctive. "Substance" is what it "is called" (*heißt*), in the indicative. The better name and the received name come apart, and the parenthesis gives the reason: "Unfortunately all these philosophical terms are burdened with equivocations and lack any deeper clarification." Against the words the sentence sets a thing: "a firm essential stock drawn from intuition". The terminological decision that follows is a compromise. The new term combines the two old ones, *substantielle Realität*, and the next clause legislates for the rest of the book: "henceforth, wherever we speak simply of substantiality, or of substance or thing, this is what is meant".

The emphasis marks what the stipulation excludes. In the file the widest sense is set off twice: the *widest* sense, which comprises "**any**" (*jedwedes*) individual being. The genuine realities are put in quotation marks, "*echten „Realitäten“*". In the widest sense, anything individual and temporal is real: an experience, a tone-datum, a pure ego. In the narrow sense only what has properties that attest themselves under circumstances is real. The quotation marks around "realities" show that the narrow sense is the one the word has to be brought to. The next sentence shows what is at stake: "Extensive substance shall count for us only as a particularization" (*Besonderung*). If substance is defined by the relation to circumstances and not by extension, there can be non-extended substances. The definition is cut so that the soul can fall under it.

The passage looks back across § 15 and forward to § 31. Behind it lies the analysis in which reality was found in the relation to circumstances. Ahead of it, Section II needs a formal concept of reality under which both the material thing and the soul fall. § 31 states it: "In formal generality the concepts real substance (concretely understood: thing in a widest sense), real property, real state (real behaviour) and real causality are essentially connected concepts" (§31.1, Hua IV 126). That is the same stipulation raised to formal generality, and §§ 32–33 then measure the soul against it. The soul "does not schematize itself" (Hua IV 127). The "historyless" material thing is contrasted with the soul, which has a history (Hua IV 136–37). By § 33 it has only "a quasi-nature and a quasi-causality" (§33.3). The careful definition of § 17 is what produces these prefixes. Because "substance" means "substantial reality", the soul can be measured against it and found to be a substance only in a modified sense.

The apparatus adds context to the parenthesis. A marginal note in Landgrebe's typescript at Hua IV 48,30 says: "Up to p. 54 no proper text" (*Bis S. 54 kein eigentlicher Text*, apparatus to 48,30ff.). § 17 stands at the end of a stretch that the late Husserl regarded as unfinished. A supplementary sheet at the start of § 18 summarizes it: "Up to p. 54, aesthetic analysis of the material thing as *aistheton*, as objective sense, as matter of the doxic positing" (apparatus to 55,2). The "firm essential stock" is thus the yield of an aesthetic analysis in the sense of § 9 (row 3), the sense of the thing as perceived. Husserl did not take it to be a metaphysics of substance.

The corpus offers a precedent for the parenthesis. Descartes opens his definition of substance with the same complaint: "What is meant by 'substance' — a term which does not apply univocally to God and his creatures" (*Principles of Philosophy* I, art. 51, Cottingham et al., vol. 1). Descartes' equivocation runs between infinite and finite substance. Husserl's runs between the individual in general and the bearer of causal properties. The response is different in kind. Descartes distinguishes senses of the word. Husserl draws "an essential stock from intuition" and lets the word follow. The equivocation he regrets in the parenthesis is the one his compound term is built to fix. In Section III the narrow sense returns in the argument about spirit, which has "circumstances" too, but in "the surrounding world and in other spirits" (Hua IV 283, :3971). The stipulation of § 17 is what makes it possible to ask whether spirit is a reality at all, and § 64 gives the answer: it is a reality whose individuality, unlike the thing's, is "absolute".

## §18 a, Hua IV 55–58: the body as zero point and organ

Reads [§18 a.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) through §18 a.5, third to fifth paragraphs under § 18 a.

The body enters the constitution of things as the freely moved organ of perception and as the bearer of the zero point of orientation. Every appearance of a thing is motivated by kinaesthetic courses that run off "freely": if the eye moves thus, the image changes thus. That is why every thing that appears, and every thing that could appear, has an orientation toward the body. Here the possibility of experience is made to depend on a spontaneity, and the spontaneity belongs to the moving body. The word "motivation" enters the book here.

> But to the *possibility of experience belongs the spontaneity of the courses* of the presenting acts of sensation, which are accompanied by kinaesthetic series of sensation and motivated as dependent on them. With the *localization of the former in the movable member of the body* concerned, it is given that in all perception and perceptual verification (experience) the body is involved as freely moved sense-organ, as freely moved whole of the sense-organs, and that therefore, on this original ground, everything thingly-real in the ego's surrounding world has its relation to the body. And the distinction is evidently connected with this, that the body becomes the bearer of the zero point of orientation, Here and Now, from which the pure ego intuits space and the whole world of the senses. Thus every thing that appears has *eo ipso* a relation of orientation to the body, and not only the thing that actually appears but every thing that is to be able to appear.

German:

> Zur *Möglichkeit der Erfahrung gehört aber die Spontaneität der Abläufe* der von kinaesthetischen Empfindungsreihen begleiteten und von ihnen als abhängig motivierten darstellenden Empfindungsakte: mit dem *Lokalisiertsein der ersten im betreffenden beweglichen Leibesgliede* ist gegeben, daß bei aller Wahrnehmung und Wahrnehmungsausweisung (Erfahrung) der Leib als freibewegtes Sinnesorgan, als freibewegtes Ganzes der Sinnesorgane mit dabei ist, und daß somit aus diesem ursprünglichen Grunde alles Dinglich-Reale der Umwelt des Ich seine Beziehung hat zum Leibe. Und auch die Auszeichnung hängt offenbar hiermit zusammen, daß der Leib zum Träger der Orientierungspunkte Null wird, Hier und Jetzt, von dem aus das reine Ich den Raum und die ganze Sinnenwelt anschaut. So hat also jedes Ding, das erscheint, *eo ipso* Orientierungsbeziehung zum Leib, und nicht nur das wirklich erscheinende, sondern jedes Ding, das soll erscheinen können.

*Note on the text.* "Träger der Orientierungspunkte Null" is ungrammatical. The sense, confirmed by "von dem aus" [from which] in the same sentence, is a single point: "bearer of the zero point of orientation". The file's text is quoted as it stands.

The first clause sets a Kantian formula against a Kantian thesis. "To the possibility of experience belongs" (*Zur Möglichkeit der Erfahrung gehört*) is the language of the transcendental deduction, and what it introduces is "the spontaneity of the courses". In Kant spontaneity is the understanding's. Here it is the freedom of a body that moves its eyes and fingers. The passage keeps the doctrine of § 9 intact. The synthesis of perception remains non-spontaneous, but its condition now includes a doing, the free running-off of kinaesthetic series. The italics in the file divide the claim into two parts: "the spontaneity of the courses" and "the localization of the former in the movable member of the body". The first part is a condition of experience. The second puts it in a place, and that place is the body.

The adjective "freely moved" (*freibewegt*) is repeated twice in one clause, "as freely moved sense-organ, as freely moved whole of the sense-organs". The verb "motivated" turns up almost unnoticed in the subordinate clause: the presenting sensations are "motivated as dependent" on the kinaesthetic series. Three pages later the motivation is spelled out as a structure: "everywhere we find the 'if–so' or 'because–so'" (*das „wenn-so” oder „weil-so”*, Hua IV 58), and the apprehension of a thing "as standing so far off, so oriented, so coloured" is "not conceivable without such relations of motivation" (*Motivationsbeziehungen*, Hua IV 58). This is the first appearance in the book of the concept on which Section III will turn. Here it names a relation between two groups of sensations in one body.

The zero point is introduced through a dependence, and the dependence is marked as obvious: "evidently" (*offenbar*) "connected with this". It is not derived. The phrase "Here and Now" makes the point temporal as well as spatial, and the relative clause gives it to "the pure ego" (*das reine Ich*), who "intuits space and the whole world of the senses" from it. In the analysis of nature the pure ego is a constant presupposition, but here it already has a location, and the location is the body. The last sentence extends the orientation to possibility: "not only the thing that actually appears but every thing that is to be able to appear". The example follows at once. "If I imagine a centaur, I cannot but imagine it in a certain orientation and in a certain relation to my sense-organs: it stands 'to the right' of me" (Hua IV 56–57). Imagination does not escape the zero point. A thing that could not be oriented could not appear, even in phantasy.

The apparatus shows that the paragraph's framing was rebuilt late. At its first line Husserl inserted "*leibe*" [body], so that the dependence is on "the experiencing subject-body". Landgrebe's typescript had "subject". The marginal outline for the subsection reads: "a) orientation, a1) two kinds of 'sensations' playing together, presenting data and 'kinaesthetically' functioning 'sensations' in their different correlative relations". Husserl added later: "But up to now there has been no talk at all of sensation-data" (apparatus to 55,19). The self-criticism is exact. §§ 15–17 analysed the thing as sense, the schema and its dependencies, without the sensations in which it is presented, and § 18 a introduces both the sensations and the body that moves through them in one stroke. In Husserl's own heading for the passage (55,2), the earlier analysis concerned the thing "as *aistheton*", and "pp. 55ff." concern "the body-relatedness of this objective sense, of the *aisthesis*".

The understanding is carried furthest in Section II. The body becomes "the only object that is immediately spontaneously movable for the will of my pure ego" (§ 38, Hua IV 152, :2883). § 41 a adds that the body "bears in itself the zero point of all these orientations", the "ultimate central Here" (Hua IV 159, :2995). § 42 calls it the "conversion point" at which causal relations "are converted into conditional ones" (row 23). The "if–so" of § 18 a is the thread through all three. In Section III the person "has his 'Here'" which is "for every point of intersubjectively identical time … separate" from the other's (§52.6, Hua IV 202, a page missing in the corpus file). § 59 (Hua IV 253, :3475) turns the freedom of the moved body into a definition: "The ego as unity is a system of 'I can'". The spontaneity that § 18 a locates in the moving organ becomes, in Section III, the form of the person.

The 1907 lectures *Ding und Raum* had already described the pairing of kinaesthetic and visual series as motivation. In the analysis of oculomotor changes, the strengthening of association is said to be "a phenomenological fact", to be interpreted "phenomenologically, that is, everything within a really, seeingly performed context of motivation" (*Motivationszusammenhang*, Hua XVI § 51, 178). The structure of *Ideen II* § 18 a is already there: images "*b*" motivated by kinaesthetic "*K*". What § 18 a adds is the body as the place where this happens and the ego that is located there. In 1907 motivation connects two series of data. In *Ideen II* it connects the data to a freely moving, oriented body, and the analysis of the person later builds on that.

## §18 f, Hua IV 80–81: the solus ipse

Reads [§18 f.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §18 f.3, second and third paragraphs under § 18 f.

The solus ipse of the thought experiment is not a human being left alone. It is a subject that knows nothing of others at all, not even that others are possible. Such a subject lacks what a human being alone in the world would still have, an "objective body", a body that it apprehends as visible to others. Its "abstraction" is therefore not a subtraction of others from a world that remains the same. It removes a whole apperceptive layer, and with it the sense of objectivity. The apprehension of the body plays a "special role" for intersubjectivity, the role on which all "objective" apprehension depends.

> Strictly considered, the solus ipse knows no *objective body* in the full and proper sense, even if it had the *phenomenon* of its body and the systems of belonging manifolds of experience, and had them just as perfectly as the social human being. In other words, the solus ipse does not truly deserve its name. The abstraction that we carried out as one justified by insight does not yield the *isolated human being*, or the isolated human personality. Nor did this abstraction consist in our arranging a mass murder of the human beings and animals of our surroundings, sparing only our own human subject. The subject that would then remain as the only one would still be a human subject, that is, still the intersubjective object, still apprehending and positing itself as such.

German:

> Genau besehen kennt der Solus-ipse keinen *objektiven* *Leib* im vollen und eigentlichen Sinn, selbst wenn er das *Phänomen* seines Leibes und die Systeme zugehöriger Erfahrungsmannigfaltigkeiten hätte und sie genau so vollkommen hätte wie der soziale Mensch. M.a.W. der Solus-ipse verdient seinen Namen in Wahrheit nicht. Die Abstraktion, die wir als einsichtig berechtigte vollzogen haben, liefert nicht den *isolierten* *Menschen*, bzw. die isolierte menschliche Persönlichkeit. Diese Abstraktion bestand ja auch nicht darin, daß wir einen Massenmord der Menschen und Tiere unserer Umwelt veranstalteten, das eigene menschliche Subjekt allein verschonend. Das dann als einziges verbleibende Subjekt wäre noch immer Menschensubjekt, d.i. noch immer der intersubjektive Gegenstand, sich selbst immer noch als solches auffassend und setzend.

*Note on the text.* A footnote marker after "eigentlichen Sinn" is omitted. Shortly after the passage, p. 81 breaks off in mid-word ("Das Ding, das sich in gerege", :1663), and p. 82 begins with the stray running head "Husserl Ideen II" and "sinnen-anschaulich" (:1664–:1665). Some words of the sentence are lost. The main commentary (§18 f.4) gives it as "The thing that constitutes itself in regulated manifolds of concordant experiences for the individual subject".

The name is the argument. *Solus ipse* means "he himself alone", and Husserl says the figure "does not truly deserve its name" (*verdient seinen Namen in Wahrheit nicht*). To be alone is to be without others whom one could have, and this subject has no concept of others to be without. The opposition between "*phenomenon*" and "*objective*" makes the same point for the body. The solus ipse has "the phenomenon of its body" with all its manifolds, "just as perfectly as the social human being", and lacks only the body as something seen from elsewhere. Nothing in its experience is missing. What is missing is a sense of the body.

The mass murder is the image of what the abstraction is not, and it is drastic on purpose. The verb is theatrical, "arranged" (*veranstalteten*), and the participle is morally coloured: the own subject is "spared" (*verschonend*). A survivor of such a murder would still be a human being, "the intersubjective object", "apprehending and positing itself as such". The last of the human beings would still know itself as one among others, and its solitude would be a loss. The abstraction of § 18 is something else, and the next sentences say what it removes. The constructed subject "knows nothing of a human surrounding, knows nothing of an actuality or even the real possibility of 'others'", and so "nothing of its own body as one understandable by others" (Hua IV 81). In spaced type the conclusion follows: "the apprehension of the body plays a special role for intersubjectivity, in which all objects are apprehended 'objectively', as things in the one objective time, in one objective space" (Hua IV 81).

The paragraph before the passage contains the experiment proper, and its weak point. The solipsist's world suddenly acquires others, and their consistent experience contradicts long series of his earlier, perfectly concordant experiences. Then "my so beautifully verified actuality is called by them the hallucination of someone who until this moment was mentally ill" (Hua IV 80). The "so beautifully" (*so schön*) is ironic. The claim that "nothing changes" in this as an a priori state of affairs, however perfect the solipsistic verification, is strong, and it rests on a stipulation: the others experience "as we assume, necessarily concordantly" (*wie wir voraussetzen: notwendig einstimmig*). Why the many should be right against the one is not argued. It is built into the case. A footnote limits the case further: "This conflict may not, however, be a complete one. For a basic stock of common experiences is presupposed if communication is to take place at all" (Hua IV 80 n., :1897). Husserl's later margin to that note goes to the heart of it: "But much belongs to that, already identical nature" (*Aber dahin gehört doch sehr viel, schon identische Natur*, apparatus to 80, note). The experiment, meant to show how others make nature objective, presupposes a shared nature as the condition of their correcting the solipsist. At the head of the subsection, the apparatus records only a question mark in Husserl's hand (apparatus to 80,1).

Husserl sees the circle and names it. Right after the experiment he asks: "do we not entangle ourselves in a circle, since the apprehension of human beings presupposes the apprehension of the body and thus the apprehension of things?" (Hua IV 80). The answer is a method, not a solution: "question the thing-apprehension itself". The passage read here is the first result of that questioning. The self-forgetfulness of the analysing ego must be "lifted in a decisive point", and the decisive point is the body as something for others. The understanding reached is that objectivity is a sense, carried by the body-apprehension, and not a count of subjects. A world with one human in it is still intersubjective. A subject without the apprehension of others is not even alone.

The book follows the understanding in both directions. § 42 shows what the solipsistically constituted body lacks: "in solipsistic experience we do not attain the givenness of ourselves as a spatial thing like all others", nor the natural object "human being", to which the higher strata are "'introjected'". "To get there we must go beyond our own subject" (Hua IV 161, :3020). § 46 shows how: "only with empathy … does the closed unity 'human being' constitute itself, and this I then transfer to myself" (Hua IV 167, :3083). The circle Husserl names in § 18 f returns as the "vicious circle" of § 53, between a nature that contains persons and persons who constitute nature (row 35, on pages missing in the corpus file). And § 64 runs the experiment the other way. "If we strike out all spirits from the world, there is no more nature. But if we strike out nature … something still remains: the spirit as individual spirit; only the possibility of sociality is lost" (Hua IV 297, :4201). § 18 removes the others and keeps nature, and objectivity is lost. § 64 removes nature and keeps the subject, and sociality is lost. The two experiments mirror each other.

The image recurs in the corpus in a later work. Husserl's Paris lectures of 1929 say that the phenomenological epoché makes me "in a certain sense *solus ipse*: not in the ordinary sense, say that of a human being left over in the still-existing world after a collapse of all the stars" (*nicht im gewöhnlichen, etwa in dem <eines> bei einem Zusammensturz aller Gestirne übrig gebliebenen Menschen in der noch immer seienden Welt*, Hua I 12). The catastrophe has become cosmic, but the logic is that of the mass murder. The survivor of a catastrophe is a human in a world, and so not the solitude philosophy means. The difference is in what replaces him. In *Ideen II* it is an abstraction within the natural attitude, a subject without the apperception of others. In the Paris lectures it is the transcendental ego. The image recurs because the same misunderstanding has to be blocked in each case, the reading of an abstraction as a subtraction.

## §18 g, Hua IV 85–86: God's distorting glasses and the thing as rule

Reads [§18 g.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §18 g.4, third and fourth paragraphs under § 18 g.

The non-objectivity of secondary qualities means only that they are not exempt from the relativity of appearances to subjects. It does not mean that a mind with better access sees the qualities things really have. The proof is a reductio. A God who saw things "as they are in themselves" could share a world with us only through exchangeable appearances, and so only with a body and sense-organs of his own. Objectivity is therefore intersubjective, and the thing is "a rule of possible appearances", the identical something that answers to the regulated appearances of all who can communicate.

> Should we say that God sees things as they are in themselves, and we through sense-organs that are a kind of distorting glasses? Are things filled space with absolute quality that we just do not know? But if the things are to be the same that appear to us as they appear, then a unity of mutual understanding between God and us would have to be possible, just as between different human beings the possibility of recognizing that the things the one sees and those the other sees *are the same* exists only through mutual understanding. But how would the identification be conceivable other than through the supposed absolute spirit's seeing things, too, through sensuous appearances, which would have to be exchangeable in a mutual understanding (or at least one-sidedly) like our appearances between human beings? Then God would simply be colour-blind and so on, and we blind to his qualities. But does it make sense to dispute which are the right qualities? The new qualities would again be secondary, and would be excluded by physics in exactly the same way, physics that must be the same for all if the things are the same. Naturally the absolute spirit would also have to have a body for the purposes of mutual understanding, so the dependence on sense-organs would be there too.

German:

> Sollen wir sagen, Gott sieht die Dinge, wie sie an sich sind, und wir durch Sinnesorgane, die eine Art verzerrender Brillen sind? Die Dinge sind erfüllter Raum mit absoluter Qualität, die wir nur nicht kennen? Aber sollen die Dinge dieselben sein, die uns so erscheinen, wie sie erscheinen, dann müßte doch eine Einheit der Verständigung zwischen Gott und uns möglich sein, wie zwischen verschiedenen Menschen nur durch Verständigung die Möglichkeit besteht zu erkennen, daß die Dinge, die der eine sieht, und die, die der andere sieht, *dieselben sind*. Aber wie wäre die Identifizierung denkbar als so, daß der supponierte absolute Geist die Dinge eben auch durch sinnliche Erscheinungen sieht, die ähnlich austauschbar sein müßten in einer Wechselverständigung — oder mindestens einseitig — wie unsere Erscheinungen zwischen Menschen. Dann wäre Gott eben farbenblind usw., und wir blind für seine Qualitäten. Hat es aber einen Sinn zu streiten, welches die richtigen Qualitäten sind? Die neuen Qualitäten wären wieder sekundär und würden genau so ausgeschaltet durch die Physik, die für alle dieselbe sein muß, wenn die Dinge dieselben sind. Natürlich müßte der absolute Geist zu Zwecken der Wechselverständigung auch einen Leib haben, also wäre ja auch die Abhängigkeit von Sinnesorganen da.

*Note on the text.* According to the apparatus, the whole passage from "Sollen wir sagen" to "Sinnesorganen da" (Hua IV 85,10–30) was bracketed in Landgrebe's typescript (*in L. eingeklammert*, apparatus to 85,10–30). The apparatus does not say who set the brackets or what they meant. The edition prints the passage.

The passage is built as two questions and a reductio. The first question gives the picture to be refuted in its most familiar form: God sees things "as they are in themselves" (*wie sie an sich sind*), and our sense-organs are "a kind of distorting glasses" (*eine Art verzerrender Brillen*). The image places the distortion in an instrument between us and the thing, so that a mind without the instrument would see undistorted. The second question states the metaphysics behind the image: "filled space with absolute quality that we just do not know". The argument then goes through a conditional, "if the things are to be the same", and its premise is the one § 18 f established. The identity of the seen thing is recognized "only through mutual understanding" (*nur durch Verständigung*). If God and we see the same things, a "unity of mutual understanding" between God and us must be possible.

The reductio has two steps, and both are compressed. First: identification with God would require that God, too, sees through appearances "exchangeable" with ours. Then "God would simply be colour-blind and so on, and we blind to his qualities". The symmetry is the joke. If our qualities are distortions relative to his, his are distortions relative to ours, and the question "which are the right qualities" loses its sense. Second: physics would exclude his new qualities "in exactly the same way" as ours, because physics "must be the same for all if the things are the same". The final sentence draws the consequence with the word "Naturally" (*Natürlich*). The absolute spirit "would also have to have a body for the purposes of mutual understanding". The most important step of the argument is presented as the most obvious one. That communication needs a body was the result of § 18 f, and here it is applied to God without new argument. The inference holds only if knowing the same world requires being able to communicate about it. Husserl assumes this. A God who knew the world without ever sharing it would escape the argument, and the passage does not consider him.

What the reductio refutes is stated in the next paragraph. The non-objectivity of the secondary qualities may be understood only to mean "that they are in no way withdrawn from the relativism of appearances" (Hua IV 85). The conclusion is restrictive: no quality is privileged. It does not reverse the doctrine. Subjects may be blind to colour or tone, and their senses "may be wholly different", but they cannot be blind "with respect to *all* senses and so at the same time *space-blind, movement-blind, energy-blind*". Otherwise there would be no world of things for them (Hua IV 86). The paragraph read in the commentary's §18 g.4 states the result as a definition: "Nature is an intersubjective actuality", and "the thing is a rule of possible appearances", "a reality as unity of a manifold of regulated appearances that belong together. And this unity is an intersubjective one" (Hua IV 86, :1811). The word *Regel* [rule] is Kant's, and the hyphenation "ge-regelt" in the file marks the root. The parenthesis gives the content of the rule: "the properties express capacities, they are causal properties, referred to an 'if–so'". The "if–so" of § 18 a (row 9) and the "circumstances" of § 15 c (row 7) thus meet in the definition of the thing.

The argument's limits are visible in Husserl's own margins. Against the claim that the step to the physical thing is motivated by the plurality of differently sensing subjects, he wrote: "But I myself as an isolated one am exposed to sensuous anomaly and to variations of my originally constitutive normality; so physics does not first have a relation to intersubjectivity" (apparatus to 86,29). That reopens what § 18 e had granted and § 18 h will grant again (row 12). A solitary subject can already reach the physical thing, so intersubjectivity is not the only route to it. Read with this note, the reductio on God shows only that intersubjectivity is sufficient to deprive any quality of privilege, not that it is necessary.

The understanding runs on in two directions. The thing becomes ever emptier. Two pages later it is "a merely empty identical something", with "no sensuous-intuitive content that could be given intersubjectively as identical" (*nur ein leeres identisches Etwas*, Hua IV 88, :1825). In § 47 the natural-scientific object is "the x of 'mathematical' qualities" (§47.1, Hua IV 170). In the last paragraph of the book, "Nature is the X" (*Die Natur ist das X*, Hua IV 302, :4295). The empty something of § 18 g becomes the X that § 64 sets against the spirit, which "is no X but what is given itself". The rule, on the other hand, moves inward. In § 61 the objective thing-world, "as an intersubjectively valid rule of possible appearances, and already of possible sensation-complexes of the single spirits, points back to a lower level of all spiritual existence": "Every spirit has a 'nature-side'" (Hua IV 279, :3904). The rule of appearances that defined the thing in § 18 g becomes in § 61 an index of a level of the spirit itself.

*Ideas I* had used God against the same picture. "God, the subject of absolutely perfect knowledge and so also of all possible adequate perception", is supposed to have the "adequate perception of the thing itself" denied to us finite beings. "But this view is countersensical" (*widersinnig*). It would make the spatial thing "a really inherent constituent" of the divine stream of consciousness, an experience, and it is misled by the thought that "the transcendence of the thing is that of an image or sign" (*Ideen I* § 43, Hua III 89; the corpus file is unreviewed, and its OCR is poor at this point; the English translation, `husserl-cw2-…`, agrees). In *Ideas I* God fails as a perceiver because a thing given without appearances would no longer be transcendent. In *Ideen II* God fails as a fellow subject because a world shared without appearances and a body would not be the same world. The first argument concerns the essence of perception. The second concerns the essence of objectivity. Placed together, they show the work that *Ideen II* adds: objectivity comes to mean what can be communicated, and the "distorting glasses" become the sense-organs of a member of a community of mutual understanding.

## §18 h, Hua IV 89–90: a member of nature

Reads [§18 h.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 18 h.

The objective, physical thing can in principle be reached on either path: by a solitary subject that corrects its own anomalies, or by a community. The path through others is not necessary for physics. What only the intersubjective path gives is the subject's apprehension of itself as part of nature. A solipsistic subject could have an objective nature before it, but it could not apprehend itself as "a member of nature", a psychophysical animal. The self as natural object is constituted through others. This is the hinge on which Section I turns into Section II.

> At any rate we see: on the one hand, the *possibility* of advancing to the constitution of the "objective" (physical) *thing* exists already for the solipsistic stage. On the other hand, there is not for the intersubjective stage either the unconditional *necessity* of getting that far. But there is (apart from the fact that constitution factually takes place intersubjectively) a difference of principle between these two possible paths of building up an "objective nature". The solipsistic subject could indeed have an objective nature over against it, but it could not apprehend itself as a member of nature, could not apperceive itself as psychophysical subject, as *animal*, as happens at the intersubjective stage of experience. This can of course become evident only when one follows the constitution of animal nature. Just as, quite generally, the exhibition of the relations of dependence between experienced nature and experiencing subject has shown that for a full clarification of the sense and structure of physical nature the study of subjectivity is unconditionally required.

German:

> Immerhin sehen wir: einerseits besteht schon für die solipsistische Stufe die *Möglichkeit*, bis zur Konstitution des ,,objektiven'' (physikalischen) *Dinges* vorzudringen. Andererseits besteht auch für die intersubjektive Stufe nicht die unbedingte *Notwendigkeit*, so weit zu gelangen. Es besteht aber — abgesehen davon, daß faktisch die Konstitution sich intersubjektiv vollzieht -- ein prinzipieller Unterschied zwischen diesen beiden möglichen Wegen des Aufbaus einer „objektiven Natur“. Das solipsistische Subjekt könnte zwar eine objektive Natur sich gegenüber haben, aber es könnte nicht sich selbst als ein Glied der Natur auffassen, sich nicht als psychophysisches Subjekt, als animal, apperzipieren, so wie es auf intersubjektiver Erfahrungsstufe geschieht. Das kann natürlich erst einsichtig werden, wenn man die Konstitution der animalischen Natur verfolgt. Wie ja überhaupt der Aufweis der Abhängigkeitsbeziehungen zwischen erfahrener Natur und erfahrendem Subjekt gezeigt hat, daß zu einer vollen Klärung von Sinn und Aufbau der physischen Natur das Studium der Subjektivität unbedingt erforderlich ist.

*Note on the text.* A footnote marker after "geschieht" is omitted. Its note refers to "the sections on body-constitution, p. 158ff." (:1898). The main commentary cites the passage at Hua IV 90. The apparatus places the end of § 18 h at 89,31ff., and the passage runs across the page break.

The passage is built on a chiasm, and the emphasis in the file marks it: "*possibility*" for the solipsistic stage, "*necessity*" denied for the intersubjective one. Each modal word is the opposite of what the reader of § 18 f would expect. § 18 f had shown that the solus ipse lacks objectivity in the full sense. Here Husserl grants it the *possibility* of reaching the physical thing, and the preceding paragraph explains how. "We are free intelligent beings": even in a world without illness or illusion, "we can perform *voluntary* interventions in our own and others' bodies, and now 'anomalies' appear" (Hua IV 89). A solitary subject that experiments on itself can produce the anomalies that motivate physics. Conversely, a community of perfectly normal subjects living "passively, animal-like 'in the world'" need never reach the physical thing. The "physical world-view" is a product of freedom, not of plurality.

The concession clarifies the claim, and the parenthesis keeps fact and principle apart: "apart from the fact that constitution factually takes place intersubjectively". What remains of the difference is stated with a verb of self-relation: the solipsistic subject "could not apprehend itself as a member of nature" (*nicht sich selbst als ein Glied der Natur auffassen*). *Glied* means a member of a whole, and also a limb. The phrase is followed by two equivalents, "psychophysical subject" and "*animal*" in Latin. Of the three, the Latin word is the plainest: what the solitary subject cannot see itself as is an animal among animals.

The next sentence makes a promise: "This can of course become evident only when one follows the constitution of animal nature." The "of course" (*natürlich*) admits that the thesis has not been shown. The final sentence broadens the result into a program: the "study of subjectivity is unconditionally required" for "a full clarification of sense and structure of physical nature". The "unconditionally" (*unbedingt*) answers the "unconditional necessity" denied two sentences earlier. Physics does not need intersubjectivity unconditionally. The clarification of physics needs subjectivity unconditionally. Husserl's late margin on this paragraph is ambivalent: "That should have been said much earlier. But it would then be a mere determination of that subjectivity in which it is constituted" (apparatus to 89,31ff.). The note seems to fear that stating the program earlier would have reduced the analysis of nature to a description of its constituting subjectivity, which is the direction Section III takes.

The passage closes Section I by stating what the analysis of nature cannot do by itself. It looks back to the margin quoted in the previous reading: "I myself as an isolated one am exposed to sensuous anomaly … so physics does not first have a relation to intersubjectivity" (apparatus to 86,29). That note and this paragraph agree, and they take back the strong reading of § 18 f. Others are not required to make nature objective. They are required to make me an object in it. Section II begins from exactly this point. Its introduction asks for the "soul as natural object" (§ 19), and its first concept is the "ego-human-being", the whole human being "with body and soul" (§ 21).

The promise is kept in two steps in Section II. § 42 states the deficit in the language of § 18 h: "in solipsistic experience we do not attain the givenness of ourselves as a spatial thing like all others … and not the natural object 'human being' (animal being) … To get there we must go beyond our own subject and turn to the animalia that meet us in the external world" (Hua IV 161, :3020). § 46 supplies the mechanism: it would not occur to me in self-experience "to stuff all my psychic life into my body"; "only with empathy … does the closed unity 'human being' constitute itself, and this I then transfer to myself" (Hua IV 167, :3083). Being "a member of nature" is a status I receive from others and apply to myself afterwards. In Section III the same order governs the person. In the passage the commentary reports from the missing pages (§56 h.11, Hua IV 242–43), I first become "a social 'human being'" by taking over "the comprehending representation others have of me", and only then, "more mediately than in the case of others", a "psychic ego of natural-scientific psychology". The naturalistic self-apprehension is reached last, and § 18 h already states the reason: the self appears as a member of nature only through others.

The Cartesian Meditations, the one later work in the corpus that reruns the experiment, draw the line elsewhere. There the abstraction to the sphere of ownness leaves "a sense *mere nature*" that has lost its "*for-everyone*", and within it "I find, uniquely distinguished, *my body*", "the only object within my abstractive world-stratum" to which I ascribe fields of sensation (§ 44, Hua I 128). § 45 then goes further than *Ideen II* allows: "I, the reduced *human ego* (psychophysical ego), am thus constituted as member of the *world*" (*Ich, das reduzierte Menschen-Ich (psychophysische Ich), bin also konstituiert als Glied der Welt*, Hua I 128–29), before any other has been constituted. The word *Glied* [member] is the word of § 18 h, and the claim is its denial. The meditations can make it because their "world" is in quotation marks: an "ownness-reduced nature" in a "reduced 'world'", whose "objects", "things" and "psychophysical ego" all carry the marks of the reduction (Hua I 128–29). *Ideen II* has no such quotation marks. Its solipsistic subject faces a nature in the ordinary sense, and in that nature it cannot find itself as an animal. Read together, the two texts show what § 18 h's thesis depends on. A subject without others can be a member of a nature that is its own. It cannot be a member of the nature that is "for everyone", and that is the only nature Section I knows.

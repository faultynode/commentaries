# Husserl, *Ideen II*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) maps the argument of the whole book at equal depth. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries instead: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds that meaning, what work the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the book and in his other works.

The text is the German of Husserliana IV, *Ideen zu einer reinen Phänomenologie und phänomenologischen Philosophie, Zweites Buch*, edited by Marly Biemel. Passages are cited by § and printed page, "Hua IV n". Unit references such as "§4.12" name the twelfth paragraph of the main commentary under § 4. All English renderings are my working translations, close enough to show the structure under discussion. They are not the published translation by Rojcewicz and Schuwer. Other Husserl volumes are cited by Husserliana volume and page where the corpus file carries the page, and otherwise by section.

Biemel's edition rests on Ludwig Landgrebe's typescript of 1924/25, into which Husserl kept writing until about 1928. Her text-critical appendix reports every substantive insertion "von H. in L." [by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript], that is, everything entered after 1924 (Hua IV, editor's introduction). Several passages read here turn out to belong to that late layer, and the readings say so where it changes what the wording can carry.

The German comes from a converted file that is not wholly reliable. In this file, running heads are sometimes stacked or placed a few lines early: the heads for pp. 37 and 38 stand together, and the head for p. 25 stands before the start of § 11, which belongs to p. 24. Pages here follow the apparatus where it settles them. The file keeps the print's margin line numbers inside the text in places. These are dropped from quotations without comment, and so are footnote markers. Line-end hyphens are joined, a hyphen splitting a word in mid-line is removed, and a missing space between two words is restored. One Greek word that the file renders as LaTeX is given in Greek. Every other oddity is quoted as the file has it and discussed in a note where it matters. Pages 192–242 were missing from the file when the Section I readings were first written. They were restored on 28 October 2026 (sources commit `bca34fa`), and every reference to them has since been checked against the restored German. Two pages have no running head in the file, p. 192 (inside :3298) and p. 273 (inside :4244), so citations there span the neighbouring pages. Where the file prints spaced type as split italics, the Section III readings give the word whole and say so in a note.

## §4, Hua IV 9–10: value-taking

Reads [§4.12](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), twelfth paragraph under § 4.

For Husserl, value-taking (*Wertnehmung*) is the way a value is originally there for consciousness. It is a feeling in which the ego is present at the valued object itself, as perception is the ego's presence at the perceived. Value is not inferred from feeling, nor added to a perceived thing by judgment. It is given in feeling, and the feeling that gives it is enjoyment. The word is a coinage built on *Wahrnehmung* [perception], and the passage asks the coinage to carry the analogy that justifies it.

> The most original constitution of value takes place in the heart, as that pre-theoretical (in a wide sense of the word) enjoying devotion of the feeling ego-subject for which I used the expression *value-taking* in lectures decades ago. The expression thus designates an analogue, belonging to the sphere of feeling, of perception, which in the doxic sphere means the original (self-grasping) being-there of the ego at the object itself. So in the sphere of the heart it is that feeling in which the ego lives in consciousness of being there feelingly at the object "itself", and precisely that is what the talk of enjoying means. But just as there is, so to speak, a presenting from afar, an empty presenting meaning and not being-there oneself, so there is an empty object-related feeling; and as the former is fulfilled in intuitive presenting, so the empty feeling is fulfilled by enjoying. On both sides we have intentions striving in parallel: a presenting striving (cognizing, tending toward cognition) and a valuing one, tending toward *expectation*, toward enjoying. The parallel of expressions perceiving — value-taking was meant to express the similarity.

German:

> Die ursprünglichste Wertkonstitution vollzieht sich im Gemüt als jene vortheoretische (in einem weiten Wortsinne) genießende Hingegeben des fühlenden Ichsubjektes, für die ich den Ausdruck Wertnehmung schon vor Jahrzehnten in Vorlesungen verwendet habe. Der Ausdruck bezeichnet also ein der Gefühlssphäre zugehöriges Analogon der Wahrnehmung, die in der doxischen Sphäre das ursprungliche (selbsterfassende) Dabeisein des Ich bei dem Gegenstande selbst bedeutet. Also in der Gemütssphäre dasjenige Fühlen, in dem das Ich im Bewußtsein lebt, bei dem Objekte ,selbst‘ fühlend dabei zu sein, und eben das meint die Rede vom Genießen. Wie es aber sozusagen ein von ferne Vorstellen gibt, ein leeres vorstellendes Meinen und nicht selbst Dabeisein, so ein leeres gegenstandbezogenes Fühlen; und wie sich jenes im anschaulichen Vorstellen erfüllt, so das leere Fühlen durch das Genießen. Beiderseits haben wir parallel strebende Intentionen, vorstellendes (erkennendes, auf Erkenntnis hin tendierendes) Streben und wertendens, auf *Erwartung*, auf Genießen hin tendierendes. Der Ähnlichkeit sollte die Ausdrucksparallele Wahrnehmen — Wertnehmen Ausdruck geben.

*Note on the text.* The file has *Hingegeben* where the sense requires a noun (*Hingegebenheit*, devotion), *ursprungliche* for *ursprüngliche*, and *wertendens* for *wertendes*. The italic *Erwartung* [expectation] sits oddly beside *Genießen* [enjoying] as the goal of the valuing striving. Nothing below rests on it.

The coinage replaces one member of a compound and keeps the other. *Wahr-nehmung* becomes *Wert-nehmung*, and the *-nehmung* [taking] survives. Husserl does not explain the first member of *Wahrnehmung*. What he carries over is the taking: perception "means" the ego's "being-there at the object itself" (*Dabeisein des Ich bei dem Gegenstande selbst*), and value-taking is "that feeling" in which the ego is "there feelingly at the object 'itself'" (*bei dem Objekte ,selbst‘ fühlend dabei*). The phrase is the same, *bei … dabei*, with one adverb added. "Feelingly" names the mode, and everything else in the definition is perception's. The word "thus" (*also*) in the second sentence shows the order of explanation. The expression designates an analogue, and the analogy is the content of the term. Husserl does not first describe a feeling and then find a name for it.

The analogy is then carried into the structure of fulfilment. Perception has its empty counterpart, "a presenting from afar" (*von ferne Vorstellen*), and its fulfilment in intuition. Feeling is given the same pair: an "empty object-related feeling", fulfilled "by enjoying". The paragraph states this as a parallel, "On both sides we have intentions striving in parallel", and the final sentence says the pair of words "was meant to express the similarity" (*Der Ähnlichkeit sollte … Ausdruck geben*). Here the inference is weaker than the wording. That an empty feeling relates to fulfilled enjoyment as an empty meaning relates to an intuition is the thesis the coinage was introduced to state. The parallel of words expresses the parallel of acts. It does not establish it. The sentence after the quotation restricts the term: "value-feeling" (*Wertfühlen*) "remains the more general expression", present in non-original modes too. *Wertnehmung* is reserved for the fulfilled, original case, as *Wahrnehmung* is reserved for the original givenness of a thing.

The passage answers a question that the preceding sentence of § 4 poses in spaced type: every consciousness that originally constitutes a value-object "as such" necessarily has "a component that belongs to the *sphere of the heart*" (*Gemütssphäre*, Hua IV 9). The analysis just before has shown that a picture can be "enjoyed" or judged "beautiful" by the critic. The second is a theoretical act, and its object is "a new 'theoretical' objectivity", "an object of higher level" (Hua IV 9). Value-taking names the lower act that the higher one presupposes. The paragraphs after it go on to the inadequate case: the "beauty of an old Gothic building" grasped "in one glance" and anticipated "on indications" without being given, and yet "this anticipation of feeling already suffices for a doxic turn and predication" (Hua IV 10). So a value-judgment can outrun the value-taking that would justify it. The concept that § 4 needs here is a standard of originality for feeling. Without it, the distinction between a value given and a value merely asserted could not be drawn.

The apparatus changes how the passage must be dated. The whole stretch from "Die ursprünglichste Wertkonstituierung" to "Prädikation" (Hua IV 9,26–10,20) is a supplement "von H. in L.", entered after 1924. The text it replaced said only that "this component of the heart is not an act in which we live performingly" (apparatus to 9,26–10,20). The term, the claim to have used it "decades ago", and the whole doctrine of empty and fulfilled feeling therefore belong to the late revision, not to the 1912 draft. "Decades ago" is a retrospect from the 1920s.

The understanding returns once in the book, in the personalistic analysis of the surrounding world, and its frame has changed. In § 50 a valuing built on intuitive presenting "plays the role of a *value-'perception'* (in our speech: value-taking)" (*Wert-„Wahrnehmung“ (in unserer Rede: Wertnehmung)*, Hua IV 186, :3270). The scare quotes now fall on *Wahrnehmung*, and the coinage is marked as Husserl's own idiom. The example is the tone of a violin whose beauty is "originally given" when it moves the heart. The new word in the passage is "motivating": the details of the violin's form "exercise their motivating force" in the unity of the intuition. In § 4 value-taking served a theory of attitudes, the feeling act that a theoretical turn presupposes. In § 50 it serves a theory of the person's world: the originally felt value is one of the things that motivate a person. The concept is the same, but it has passed from the analysis of acts into the analysis of motivation that governs Section III.

The corpus shows the coinage in two other places. In the logic course first given in Göttingen in 1910/11, the English translation reads the term as still being proposed: "a precisely parallel, originary presentive act that we could, for example, call value-taking, the act of direct value-givenness" (*Logic and General Theory of Science*, §65 a, whose heading reads "Mere Value-Takings (*Wertnehmungen*) as Grounded Acts"). There, value-takings already "legitimize": "Judgments draw justification from perceptions (*Wahrnehmungen*) or value-takings (*Wertnehmungen*)" (§67). In *Erste Philosophie* II (1923/24) the noun has become a verb: "The value itself in its value-truth is not perceived but as it were value-taken; what perception does for the mere thing, value-taking does for the value. It is the mode of fulfilment of valuing feeling" (*Der Wert selbst in seiner Wertwahrheit wird nicht wahrgenommen, sondern gleichsam wertgenommen; … Sie ist der Erfüllungsmodus des wertenden Fühlens*, Hua VIII 105). That is the doctrine of the Hua IV supplement in a single sentence, and it is close in date to the supplement. The "decades" of Hua IV 9 are consistent with the logic course. The two late texts agree that the word says no more than its analogy. Value-taking is to value what perception is to the thing, and that is the whole of its definition.

## §6, Hua IV 15: beauty is no reflection-predicate

Reads [§6.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 6.

The claim is that predicates such as "beautiful", "pleasing" and "sad" are predicates of the object, found at the object, and not predicates about my acts. They arise through a change of attitude: I stop living in the pleasure and look at what it is pleasure in. The acts remain presupposed but are no longer performed in the distinguished sense. This turn differs in kind from reflection, which can find only predicates relative to consciousness. Husserl's own footnotes then concede that such predicates are "subjective" in a sense that the main text had denied.

> The beauty I intuit at the object, though not like its colour or shape in simple sensuous perception; but at the object itself I find the beautiful. "Beautiful" is anything but a *reflection-predicate*, such as when I say it is one that pleases me. The "pleasing", the "gladdening", the "sad" and all predicates of the object on the same level are, in their objective sense, *not relation-predicates referred to the acts*. They arise through the change of attitude we have described: the acts in question are co-presupposed.

German:

> Die Schönheit schaue ich am Gegenstand an, freilich nicht wie seine Farbe oder Gestalt in schlichter sinnlicher Wahrnehmung; aber am Gegenstand selbst finde ich das Schöne. Das Schön sagt nichts weniger als ein *Reflexionsprädikat*, wie etwa, wenn ich sage, er sei ein mir gefallender. Das „gefällig”, das „erfreulich”, das „traurig” und alle gleichstehenden Gegenstandsprädikate sind ihrem objektiven Sinne nach *nicht Relationsprädikate*, *bezogen auf die Akte*. Sie erwachsen durch die von uns beschriebene Einstellungsänderung: die betreffende Akte sind dabei mit vorausgesetzt.

*Note on the text.* "Das Schön" is probably *Das „Schön“* with its quotation marks lost: the next sentence puts "gefällig", "erfreulich" and "traurig" in quotation marks. The sentence before the passage ends in "seine Schönheitsrichtung", which does not construe. The sense needs a verb, perhaps *richten* [to direct], and the reading does not use it.

The decisive phrase is an idiom. *Nichts weniger als* means "anything but", not "nothing less than". "Beautiful" is anything but a reflection-predicate, and the next sentence repeats the denial: such predicates are "*not relation-predicates*". The main commentary renders the phrase literally ("says nothing less than a reflection-predicate") and so states the opposite of the text. It goes on correctly from the following sentence, so the error is local, but it stands in the one place where the passage says what kind of predicate beauty is. It is logged under the list's corrections.

The positive claim is carried by a preposition and a verb. "At the object" (*am Gegenstand*) occurs twice in the first sentence, the second time strengthened by "itself" (*selbst*). The verb is *anschauen* [to intuit], the word for the most original seeing. The concession comes between the two: "though not like its colour or shape in simple sensuous perception" (*freilich nicht wie … in schlichter sinnlicher Wahrnehmung*). Beauty is intuited, and it is intuited at the object, but not sensuously. This is the place that § 4 prepared with "axiologically intuited" (*axiologisch Angeschautes*, Hua IV 9). The predicates "arise" (*erwachsen*) through the change of attitude, and the acts are "co-presupposed" (*mit vorausgesetzt*). The prefix *mit-* is exact. The feeling acts found the predicate without being its topic. The rest of the paragraph names the result a "layer": the correlates of the acts of the heart are found "at the object" as "an objective layer laid over the layer of the sensuous predicates" (*eine objektive Schicht, übergelagert über die Schicht der sinnlichen Prädikate*). The contrast with reflection closes the paragraph: "In the theoretical attitude of reflection I can find no objective predicates, only predicates relative to consciousness" (*bewußtseinsrelative*).

The footnotes to this sentence and to the next paragraph are Husserl's later marginal notes in Landgrebe's typescript (the apparatus: "15 Der Text der Fußnoten = Randbemerkungen von H. in L."). They retract part of what the main text says: "But it ought to be worked out that and why such predicates of the heart are, after all, merely subjective in a special sense, referring to valuing subjects and thereby to acts of these subjects, in which they are constituted for them and not for everyone" (Hua IV 15 n. 1). The second note reconciles the two claims. Predicates of the heart are "objective" in the general sense of determining the object. They are nonetheless "rightly called 'subjective' in a good sense too", since "in their sense itself they refer back to valuing subjects". They differ in this from "merely natural, purely factual predicates, which in their own sense indicate nothing of the subject and its acts" (n. 2). The main text had denied that beauty is a relation-predicate. The notes accept that its sense refers to a subject, and they move the difference from relation to sense. Beauty is not a predicate about my present act, but its meaning points to valuers. That is a weaker claim than the main text's, and a more defensible one. Here the inference was weaker than the wording made it look. That the predicate "arises" through a turn of attitude does not show that it is free of reference to the subject, and Husserl saw this when he reread the passage.

The passage gives § 7 its premise. Because value-predicates are objective in this sense, every act is "implicitly also objectivating" (*implizite zugleich objektivierend*, Hua IV 17), and the theoretical subject can either let the new strata "remain out of action" and grasp "*mere things*", or take them in and have "values, goods" (Hua IV 17). The second note's phrase, predicates that "indicate nothing of the subject", is the definition that § 11 needs for nature as "a sphere of 'mere things'" (Hua IV 24). Nature is exactly what is left when the predicates whose sense points to valuers are set aside.

Section III changes what the footnotes concede. When the person becomes the theme, its surrounding world contains things "as our surroundings and not, as in natural science, as 'objective' nature" (Hua IV 183, :3244), and value-characters belong to that world as experienced. The reference of value-predicates to valuing subjects is then no longer a qualification on their objectivity. It is what gives them their place: the person is "the subject of a surrounding world" (*Subjekt einer Umwelt*, Hua IV 185, :3260), and a predicate that points to valuers points to persons. In § 6 the footnotes treat this "subjective in a good sense" as a correction to the main text. By the end of the book it has become the characteristic of everything that belongs to the spiritual world. The understanding the passage reaches is that a value-predicate belongs to the object and refers to a subject in the same act. The main text of § 6 states the first half, and the late notes add the second.

## §9, Hua IV 18–20: aesthetic synthesis

Reads [§9.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §9.4, third and fourth paragraphs under § 9.

By "aesthetic synthesis" Husserl means the synthesis that makes one thing out of the many partial apprehensions of a perception without any act of connecting. "Aesthetic" here means sensory, in the sense of *aisthēsis*. The unity of a perceived thing is synthetic, built from a manifold of theses, but it is not produced by the ego. Categorial synthesis is a spontaneous doing. Aesthetic synthesis is not. The distinction lets the first constituted objects be unities without being products of thought.

> Thus every simple perception of a thing (that is, an originally giving consciousness of the present existence of a thing) leads us back intentionally. It demands of us *single considerations, single runnings-through, transitions in series of perception*, which are indeed spanned by the unity of a continuous thesis, but evidently in such a way that the many single theses are in no way united in the form of a categorial synthesis. What gives these single theses unity is a synthesis of a wholly different kind: we will call it the *aesthetic* synthesis. If we try to delimit the two against each other in their peculiarity, we find as a first distinguishing mark that categorial synthesis, as synthesis, is a spontaneous act, and *sensuous* synthesis is not. The connecting is in the one case itself a spontaneous doing, an activity of its own, in the other not.

German:

> So führt uns jede schlichte Dingwahrnehmung (also ein originär gebendes Bewußtsein vom gegenwärtigen Dasein eines Dinges) intentional zurück, sie fordert von uns *Einzelbetrachtungen, Einzeldurchlaufungen, Übergänge in Wahrnehmungsreihen*, die zwar von der Einheit einer kontinuierlichen Thesis umspannt sind, aber offenbar so, daß die vielerlei einzelnen Thesen keineswegs in Form einer kategorialen Synthesis geeinigt sind. Was diesen Einzelthesen Einheit verleiht, ist eine Synthesis ganz anderer Art: wir wollen sie die *ästhetische* Synthesis nennen. Suchen wir beide in ihrer Eigentümlichkeit gegeneinander abzugrenzen, so finden wir als ein erstes Unterscheidungsmerkmal, daß die kategoriale Synthesis als Synthesis ein spontaner Akt ist, die *sinnliche* Synthesis dagegen nicht. Die Verknüpfung ist einmal selbst ein spontanes Tun, eine eigene Aktivität, das andere Mal nicht.

The emphasis moves within two sentences. The term is introduced with the adjective italicized, "the *aesthetic* synthesis", and the distinguishing mark italicizes a different adjective: "*sensuous* synthesis is not" spontaneous. The section's heading already joins the two, "aesthetic ('sensuous') synthesis", and the passage treats them as one word in two languages. This matters because "aesthetic" has meant something else for the five preceding pages. In §§ 4–6 it is the adjective of "aesthetic pleasure" and "aesthetic value". In § 9 it is the adjective of Kant's Transcendental Aesthetic, the doctrine of sensibility. Husserl uses both senses on the same pages and never marks the switch. The heading's gloss is the only marker. A reader who takes "aesthetic synthesis" to concern beauty misreads the section.

The verbs keep the ego in view while denying its agency. Perception "demands of us" (*fordert von uns*) single considerations and runnings-through: these are things we do, phases of a looking. What is not done is their unity. The single theses are "spanned" (*umspannt*) by a continuous thesis, "but evidently" (*aber offenbar*) not united categorially. The "evidently" carries the claim that the unity of perceptual phases is not a categorial synthesis, and nothing in the passage argues it. The definition of the new term is a stipulation, "we will call it" (*wir wollen … nennen*), and its content is first only negative, "a synthesis of a wholly different kind". The one positive mark is spontaneity, and it is introduced as "a first distinguishing mark" (*ein erstes Unterscheidungsmerkmal*). No second mark follows. Two paragraphs later Husserl says the indications "do not suffice for an exhaustive description" and that the task would need "a large investigation of its own" (Hua IV 20).

The apparatus dates the last sentence of the passage. "Die Verknüpfung …" through "sinnlichen Merkmale" is a supplement "von H. in L." (apparatus to 19). The sentence that defines the difference as the difference between a doing and a not-doing, "a spontaneous doing, an activity of its own, in the other case not", was added after 1924. So were the footnotes on p. 19, which the apparatus also gives as marginal notes in Landgrebe's typescript. One of them asks whether "synthesis" should not be split into two concepts: "synthesis as connection proper, combination, an expression that points to what is *separate*", and "continuous synthesis as continuous fusion" (*kontinuierliche Verschmelzung*). It concludes that every aesthetic synthesis of the first kind "leads to ultimate elements", and that the sensuous features from which the thing is built "stem in their turn from continuous synthesis" (Hua IV 19 n.). The "ultimate sensory features" that the main text calls elements "which have not themselves come about through aesthetic synthesis" are thus, by the note, themselves synthetic, in the sense of fusion. The late Husserl sharpens the definition of aesthetic synthesis and at the same time doubts its lower limit.

A further footnote (Hua IV 20 n.) names the opponent: aesthetic synthesis "as aesthetic-causal" is also at work "in the higher strata of thing-constitution", and it is "the only one Kant has in view in his concept of synthesis". The claim is sharp against Kant's text. In the second-edition Deduction, all combination "can never come into us through the senses"; it is "an act of the spontaneity of the power of representation", and "all combination … is an act of the understanding, to which we would give the general name synthesis" (*Kritik der reinen Vernunft*, B, § 15, *Von der Möglichkeit einer Verbindung überhaupt*). Kant makes every synthesis spontaneous. Husserl's first distinguishing mark makes the synthesis of perception the one that is not, and his note then says that this non-spontaneous synthesis is what Kant was really describing. The note does not argue the point. It reassigns Kant's synthesis from the understanding to sensibility, which is the move the whole section depends on.

In the section, the passage stands between the categorial objects of § 9's opening and the "primitive" objects of § 10. Its third function, the synthesis that links "moments of the 'thing-appearance'" with "the correlative 'circumstances of perception' (e.g. the state of the eye in seeing, the position of arm, hand and finger in touching)" (Hua IV 20), is the seed of two later analyses. The first is the dependence of the material thing on "circumstances" in § 15 (the footnote itself sends the reader to "S. 41 ff."). The second is the body's kinaesthetic "if–so" in § 18 a (Hua IV 58), where the same pairing of appearance and circumstance becomes "motivation". The understanding is carried furthest in Section III. There the apprehensions of things and of thingly connections are "'webs of motivation'" (*„Motivationsgeflechte“*, § 56 c, Hua IV 224, :3589). In § 61 the "underground" of sense, where associations "'make' the constitution of nature", runs on according to "'blind' rules" (Hua IV 277). The book ends where § 9 began: the sensuous unity that § 9 calls synthesis without spontaneity is renamed motivation without the ego, and that is the name it keeps.

## §11, Hua IV 24–27: a kind of reduction

Reads [§11.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 11.

Nature, as the correlate of natural science, is reached by a principled restriction of the experiencing subject. Husserl calls it "a kind of 'reduction'" and later "a kind of epoché". The subject brackets all apperceptions that stem from feeling and lets only doxic, objectivating consciousness constitute objects. What remains is a world of "mere things". The operation is not arbitrary abstraction, because it is fixed a priori by the essence of the constituting consciousness. The passage presents it as method. Section III later redescribes the same operation as a form of the person's self-forgetfulness.

> But this in the sense described earlier. We accordingly carry out a kind of "reduction". All our intentions of the heart, and all apperceptions stemming from the intentionality of the heart, we bracket as it were. It is by virtue of these that spatio-temporal objectivities constantly appear to us, before all thinking, in immediate "intuitiveness", as endowed with certain value-characters and practical characters, all of them characters that go beyond the stratum of mere factuality. In this "pure" or purified theoretical attitude, then, we no longer experience houses, tables, streets, works of art. We experience merely material things, and of such value-laden things only their stratum of spatio-temporal materiality; and likewise, for human beings and human societies, only the stratum of psychic "nature" bound to spatio-temporal "bodies".

German:

> Das aber in dem früher beschriebenen Sinn. Wir vollziehen danach eine Art „Reduktion“. Alle unsere Gemütsintentionen und alle aus der Intentionalität des Gemüts herstammenden Apperzeptionen klammern wir gleichsam ein, vermöge deren uns beständig die raumzeitlichen Gegenständlichkeiten vor allem Denken, in unmittelbarer „Anschaulichkeit“ mit gewissen Wertcharakteren, praktischen Charakteren behaftet erscheinen — lauter Charakteren, die über die Schicht bloßer Sachlichkeit hinausgehen. Wir erfahren also in dieser ,,reinen“ oder gereinigten theoretischen Einstellung nicht mehr Häuser, Tische, Straßen, Kunstwerke, wir erfahren bloß materielle Dinge und von solchen wertbehafteten Dinge eben nur ihre Schicht der räumlich-zeitlichen Materialität und ebenso für Menschen und menschliche Gesellschaften nur die Schicht der an räumlich-zeitliche „Leiber“ gebundenen seelischen „Natur“.

*Note on the text.* The file's running head for p. 25 stands before the § 11 heading, but the apparatus places the opening of § 11 at Hua IV 24,37. At the end of the section the file renders the Greek *epoché* as LaTeX (`$\varepsilon\pi o\chi\eta$`, :887). The word is legible, and the reading uses it.

Three hedges control the passage, and each is placed with care. "A kind of" (*eine Art*) limits the noun. The quotation marks around "reduction" keep the term at a distance. "As it were" (*gleichsam*) qualifies the bracketing. Husserl uses the vocabulary of *Ideas I* and marks it three times as borrowed. The reason appears when the operations are compared. In *Ideas I* the epoché puts out of action "the general thesis belonging to the essence of the natural attitude" and is limited deliberately, by "a definitely restricted" bracketing (*Ideen I* § 32, Hua III 65; the corpus file of Hua III/1 is an unreviewed conversion, but this page reads cleanly). In § 11 the general thesis stays in force. What is bracketed is the heart, and the doxic positing of a spatio-temporal world is the one thing kept. The section's last paragraph says so: "the whole of consciousness we take along as object, but we let ourselves have 'objects constituted' only by the doxic objectivating consciousness, not by the valuing one", and then, "we thereby carry out a kind of exclusion, a kind of epoché" (*eine Art Ausschaltung, eine Art ἐποχή*, Hua IV 27). Both operations bracket something. The phenomenological epoché brackets the thesis and keeps the world as phenomenon. The naturalistic "reduction" keeps the thesis and brackets the world's values. The "kind of" marks an inversion.

The word "pure" carries the same double sense. The attitude is "'pure' or purified" (*,,reinen“ oder gereinigten*). The quoted adjective is the one *Ideas I* gives to pure consciousness, and the gloss "purified" turns it into a past participle, the product of an act of cleaning. The list of what disappears is concrete: "houses, tables, streets, works of art". What remains is named twice in terms of strata: "only their stratum of spatio-temporal materiality", "only the stratum of psychic 'nature'". The quotation marks on "bodies" and "nature" in the last clause are the first sign in the book that the human being as nature is a reduced object. Human beings and societies survive the reduction, but only as nature in quotation marks.

The paragraph before the passage claims that the demarcation is not "arbitrary abstraction" but "prescribed a priori in the essence of the constituting consciousness" (Hua IV 24). The paragraphs after it qualify the subject that performs it. That subject still values: it "values the knowledge of appearing being", values the "so it is", and "produces changes practically in the experiment" for the sake of knowledge (Hua IV 26). What is abstracted from is only "all other values as against the *values of knowing*" (*Wissenswerten*). The feeling acts themselves are not excluded from nature. "They belong entirely to the sphere of things" (*gehören … durchaus der Sachsphäre zu*), taken along as objects (Hua IV 27). The main commentary's last paragraph on § 11 says these acts "co-determine the full intentional situation". That is not what the source says, and the point is logged under corrections. What the source says is the harder claim: the valuing acts of the scientist are themselves natural facts in the nature his reduction constitutes.

The apparatus shows how late and how unfinished the section is. Its opening, from "Gehen wir nun wieder zurück" to "seelischen ‚Natur'" (Hua IV 24,37–25,40), the whole passage read here included, is "a supplementary sheet by H. in L.". A pencil note follows it: "The whole of § 11 is still not sufficient and does not connect with the deeper discussions of the preceding investigations, nor does it yet conclude them. It also needs the concept of aesthetic (sensuous) experience and a clarification of the correlate of knowledge from experience" (apparatus to 24,37–25,40). At the start of p. 26, where the qualification about the valuing scientist begins, Husserl wrote: "That is misleading as it stands" (*Das ist, so wie es da steht, irreführend*, apparatus to 26,1ff.). The "kind of reduction" is thus a formulation of the 1920s, written by a Husserl who judged the section inadequate. The concept he says it lacks is aesthetic experience, the subject of § 9.

The understanding is taken up again in the opening of Section III, under another name. § 49 d sets beside it "changes of attitude of equal birth (*ebenbürtig*) with the natural or, as we now say more distinctly, the *naturale* attitude" (Hua IV 180, :3224). In § 49 e its independence is said to arise "through an abstraction or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal ego", by which it "illegitimately absolutizes its world, nature" (Hua IV 184, :3248). The operation is the same: an abstraction from everything the person's heart contributes. The verdict differs. In § 11 the abstraction is a method that yields "an a priori closed idea of nature". In § 49 the same abstraction becomes a forgetting, legitimate as an abstraction and illegitimate as soon as its result is taken for the whole. "A kind of reduction" was already the right description, since a reduction that keeps the thesis of the world is the attitude of the natural scientist and not of the phenomenologist. What § 11 calls method, § 49 calls habit, and the text of § 11 reaches toward the second description only in Husserl's later notes in its margins.

## §12, Hua IV 28–29: extension, rightly understood

Reads [§12.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), fourth paragraph under § 12.

Extension, "rightly understood", is for Husserl not the essence of material being but the dividing line between the two kinds of nature. The full essential attribute of the material is materiality. Extension marks the boundary because everything that belongs to a material thing is a priori referred to its extension, while the psychic excludes extension by its essence. Animal nature is therefore a complex: a lower stratum that is extended and an upper stratum that is not. Husserl adopts Descartes' attribute and corrects it in the same sentence.

> Not without reason does Descartes designate *extensio* as the essential attribute of the material thing, which is therefore also called simply corporeal, as against psychic or spiritual being, which in its spirituality as such has no *extensio* but rather excludes it by its essence. Indeed, it must above all be brought to insight that *extensio*, rightly understood, separates nature in the first sense from nature in the second sense, although the all-encompassing essential attribute of material being is not mere *extension* but materiality, insofar as this in itself requires spatial as well as temporal extension. What matters is the recognition of the distinctive way in which everything else that belongs to a material thing is referred a priori (that is, always essentially) to its extension. Spiritual nature, understood as animal nature, is a complex consisting of a lower stratum of material nature with the essential mark of *extensio* and an inseparable upper stratum, which is of a fundamentally different essence and above all excludes extension.

German:

> Nicht ohne Grund bezeichnet Descartes die *extensio* als Wesensattribut des materiellen Dinges, das darum auch schlechthin körperliches heißt, gegenüber dem seelischen oder geistigen Sein, das in seiner Geistigkeit als solcher keine extensio hat, sie wesensmäßig vielmehr ausschließt. In der Tat muß vor allem zur Einsicht gebracht werden, daß die extensio, richtig verstanden, die Natur im ersten Sinn von derjenigen im zweiten Sinn scheidet, obschon das vollumfassende Wesensattribut des materiellen Seins nicht die bloße *Ausdehnung* sondern die Materialität ist, sofern diese in sich selbst räumlich wie zeitliche Ausdehnung fordert. Worauf es aber ankommt, ist die Erkenntnis der ausgezeichneten Weise, wie alles, was sonst einem materiellen Dinge zukommt, a priori (das heißt immer wesensmäßig) auf seine Extension bezogen ist. Die geistige Natur, als animalische Natur verstanden, ist ein Komplex, bestehend aus einer Unterschicht materieller Natur mit dem Wesensmerkmal der extensio und einer unabtrennbaren Oberschicht, die von grundverschiedenem Wesen ist und vor allem Extension ausschließt.

*Note on the text.* In the file a page break and footnote rule interrupt the first sentence after "Dinges, das" (:903–:911), and "extensio" is split by a hyphen in mid-line ("exten-sio"). Both are joined here.

The opening is a litotes. "Not without reason" (*Nicht ohne Grund*) grants Descartes a reason and withholds full agreement, and the next sentence supplies the qualification: "rightly understood" (*richtig verstanden*). Husserl keeps the Cartesian vocabulary in three registers. *Extensio*, in Latin and italic, is Descartes' word. *Ausdehnung* and *Extension* are his own. The Latin is used where the thesis is Descartes', and where the thesis is corrected, the word changes: the full attribute is "not mere *extension* (*Ausdehnung*) but materiality". What remains of Descartes is a function. Extension is no longer the essence of matter. It is the mark that "separates" (*scheidet*) nature in the first sense from nature in the second.

The third sentence explains why that mark is decisive, and its key word is Cartesian too. Everything else that belongs to a material thing is "referred a priori … to its extension" (*a priori … auf seine Extension bezogen*). Descartes says the same of the principal attribute: "each substance has one principal property which constitutes its nature and essence, and to which all its other properties are referred", so that "everything else which can be attributed to body presupposes extension" (*Principles of Philosophy* I, art. 53, Cottingham et al., vol. 1). Husserl keeps the relation of reference and denies that the term referred to is the essence. He can then say what Descartes could not: the thing is extended according to "everything whatever that it is" (§13.3, Hua IV 30), and yet its essence is a relation to circumstances that § 15 calls materiality. The apparatus records that "the whole of § 12 was expanded according to H.'s instruction in L. by going back to the manuscript" (apparatus to 27ff.). The section stands as Husserl wanted it in the 1920s.

The last sentence gives the passage its consequence and its weak point. "Spiritual nature, understood as animal nature" is "a complex" (*ein Komplex*) of "a lower stratum" and "an inseparable upper stratum". The vocabulary is geological: *Unterschicht*, *Oberschicht*. The strata are of "fundamentally different essence", and the upper one "excludes extension". The adjective "inseparable" (*unabtrennbar*) does all the work of union, and nothing in the passage says what kind of inseparability it is. A complex of two essentially different strata, one extended and one not, is the Cartesian problem in Husserl's terms, and the word "complex" does not solve it. It postpones it.

The neighbouring sections take the problem in two directions. § 14 turns the dividing mark into a test. Material things are "fragmentable" parallel to their extension; human beings and animals are not (Hua IV 33). Some of the psychic "has something like a spreading", but "not a spreading in space" (§14.2). Section II then puts a third term between extension and its absence: the sensings (*Empfindnisse*) of the body are "localized" without being extended. "Localization of *Empfindnisse* is in fact something principally different from extension of all material thing-determinations", which "characterize the *res extensa*" (§ 37, Hua IV 149, :2825). The Cartesian term returns at the exact place where Husserl finds something it cannot describe. The non-extended layer is not absent from space. It is there in another way.

Section III takes back the image of the complex. In § 49 the soul is "not spread out over the body in genuine localization", and yet the psychic is "experientially one, really one, with the body"; Husserl calls this "the admittedly misleading expression *introjection*" (Hua IV 176, :3205). Once consciousness is posited "purely as itself", it is "absolutely unspatial" (§49 b). In the personalistic attitude the relation is neither stratum nor attachment. Body and spirit are "no connection of two things", and the bodily-spiritual unity "that we call human being, that we call state, church, harbours in itself two kinds of unity" (*birgt in sich zweierlei Einheiten*), but not two things (§ 56 h, Hua IV 243, :3731). The last sentence of the book contrasts nature, "the X", which "determines itself through general determinations", with spirit, which "is no X but what is given itself" (Hua IV 302, :4691). That is still Descartes' division between the extended and the thinking, but it is no longer drawn between two strata of one complex. It is drawn between the correlates of two attitudes. The understanding that § 12 gives extension, a boundary rather than an essence, is what lets the book keep the Cartesian line while abandoning the Cartesian picture of what lies on either side of it.

## §15 b, Hua IV 36–37: phantom and schema

Reads [§15 b.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §15 b.3, second and third paragraphs under § 15 b.

A "phantom" is a body that appears in space, filled with colour and texture, but without any sense of materiality. Questions about its weight or elasticity have no foothold in what is seen. A "schema" is the same filled spatial shape as it belongs to every thing: "this basic framework". In perception a thing shows nothing beyond its schema, so materiality is apprehended without being given. Husserl draws the conclusion in terms of dependence. The phantom can be given without materiality, but materiality cannot be given without a phantom. The relation is "one-sided detachment", and it becomes one of the book's standing tools.

> A mere phantom is present, for example, when in the stereoscope we learn to bring suitable groupings into *bodily* fusion. We then see a *spatial body* about which meaningful questions can be asked with respect to its shape, its colour, its smoothness or roughness and similar ordered determinations, questions that can therefore be answered according to the truth, as in the words: this is a red, rough pyramid. On the other hand, what appears can be so given that the question whether it is heavy or light, elastic, magnetic and so on has no sense, or better, no foothold in the sense of the perception. We simply do not see a material thing.
>
> We say that to the essence of a thing belongs a sensuous schema, and by this we understand this basic framework, this bodily ("spatial") shape with the fullness extended over it. The thing appearing as at rest and qualitatively unchanged "shows" us no more than its schema, or rather the apparency, while it is at the same time apprehended as material. In this respect, however, it does not "show" itself, does not properly come into view, not to original givenness. In what is "properly" given nothing would change if the whole stratum of materiality were struck out of the apperception. This is in fact conceivable. In original experience, perception, a "body" is inconceivable without sensuous qualification, but the phantom is originally given and hence also conceivable without the components of materiality, while these for their part are non-independent (one-sided detachment).

German:

> Ein bloßes Phantom liegt z. B. vor, wenn wir im Stereoskop lernen, passende Gruppierungen zu *körperlicher* Verschmelzung zu bringen. Wir sehen dann einen *Raumkörper*, für den hinsichtlich seiner Gestalt, hinsichtlich seiner Farbe, auch hinsichtlich seiner Glätte oder Rauhigkeit und ähnlichen geordneten Bestimmungen sinnvolle Fragen zu stellen sind, die also der Wahrheit gemäß Beantwortung finden können, wie etwa in den Worten: dies ist eine rote, rauhe Pyramide. Andererseits kann das Erscheinende so gegeben sein, daß die Frage, ob es schwer ist oder leicht, ob elastisch, magnetisch usw. gar keinen Sinn besser: keinen Anhalt am Wahrnehmungssinn hat. Wir sehen eben kein materielles Ding.
>
> Wir sagen, zum Wesen eines Dinges gehöre ein sinnliches Schema, und wir verstehen darunter dieses Grundgerüst, diese körperliche (,,räumliche“) Gestalt mit der über sie extendierten Fülle. Das als ruhend und qualitativ unverändert erscheinende Ding „zeigt“ uns nicht mehr als sein Schema oder vielmehr die Apparenz, während es doch zugleich aufgefaßt ist als materielles. In dieser Hinsicht „zeigt“ es sich aber nicht, kommt es uns nicht eigentlich zu Gesicht, nicht zu originaler Gegebenheit. Im ,,eigentlichen“ Gegebenen würde sich nichts ändern, wenn die ganze Schicht der Materialität aus der Apperzeption weggestrichen würde. Das ist in der Tat denkbar. In der originalen Erfahrung, der Wahrnehmung, ist „Körper“ undenkbar ohne sinnliche Qualifiziertheit, das Phantom aber ist original gegeben und somit auch denkbar ohne die Komponenten der Materialität, während diese ihrerseits unselbständig sind (einseitige Ablösung).

*Note on the text.* In the file the running heads for pp. 37 and 38 stand together. The apparatus places "Das ist in der Tat …" at Hua IV 37,21–26, so the second paragraph is on p. 37. Shortly after the passage, p. 40 breaks off in mid-word ("Index zu sein für eine Mannigfaltigkeit möglicher Er", :1079), and the file goes straight on to p. 41. The end of § 15 b's argument from the agreement of several senses is therefore incomplete in the file.

The stereoscope comes with a verb that the rest of the analysis cannot use: "we learn" (*lernen*) to bring the images into "*bodily* fusion". The phantom is an achievement of practice, a seeing that has to be acquired. It is not the pure given that the definition of the schema will need. The apparatus records that Husserl later put "lernen …. zu bringen" in deletion brackets in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 36,16–17). Whatever the clause was replaced with in his intention, the deletion removes the hint that the example is a product of training. The example the section keeps is the series that follows: "a rainbow, the blue sky, the sun" (Hua IV 37). In these the phantom is simply what is seen.

The decisive test is a question about sense. Of the stereoscopic body we can ask about "shape", "colour", "smoothness" and get answers "according to the truth": "this is a red, rough pyramid". The question "whether it is heavy or light, elastic, magnetic" has "no sense, or better, no foothold in the sense of the perception" (*gar keinen Sinn besser: keinen Anhalt am Wahrnehmungssinn*). The self-correction is exact. Such a question is not absurd in itself, but it finds nothing to attach to in what the perception means. A little further on, in spaced type, Husserl distinguishes this from ordinary indeterminacy. The back of a thing is open to determination, but here "essential groups of features are not represented in the apprehension at all" (*wesentliche Gruppen von Merkmalen in der Auffassung überhaupt nicht vertreten*, Hua IV 36–37). A phantom is not an indeterminate thing. It is a different sense of object.

The second paragraph turns the phantom from a special case into a structure of every thing. The word *Schema* is glossed by *Grundgerüst* [basic framework], and the gloss is architectural. "Shows" is put in quotation marks twice, first for what the thing does show ("no more than its schema"), and then for what it does not ("it does not 'show' itself"). Husserl corrects himself in mid-sentence: "its schema or rather the apparency" (*oder vielmehr die Apparenz*). In a marginal note in Landgrebe's typescript he later says that this distinction is the one the passage lacks: "the difference between schema and apparency as what is given of the schema, the side of the schema" (apparatus to 36,35ff.). The thought experiment then sets the terms: if "the whole stratum of materiality" were "struck out", nothing in what is "properly" given would change. Materiality is apprehended but not given. A few lines on, the paragraph sums this up in a formula: materiality "can be apprehended from the outset and yet not be given" (*mit aufgefaßt und doch nicht mit gegeben*, Hua IV 37–38).

The parenthesis that ends the passage turns the result into a relation of founding. Body is "inconceivable without sensuous qualification", the phantom is "conceivable without the components of materiality", and these components are "non-independent" (*unselbständig*). The vocabulary is that of the third *Logical Investigation*, and the parenthesis names the asymmetry: "(one-sided detachment)". According to the apparatus, the sentences from "Das ist in der Tat denkbar" to "(einseitige Ablösung)" are themselves an insertion "von H. in L." (apparatus to 37,21–26). The formula that makes the phantom the independent base of the thing belongs to the late layer.

In the section the passage closes a deliberate abstraction. § 15 b holds the thing "for itself, without regard to the context of things", and it ends by conceding that under this assumption we can find "no possibility of deciding" whether the experienced material thing is real or "a mere phantom" (Hua IV 41). § 15 c removes the assumption. Materiality lies in the relation to circumstances (row 7). The phantom is thus the limit at which the analysis of the isolated thing stops.

The formula of one-sided detachment returns when Husserl analyzes the living body. The aesthesiological stratum is "the substrate for the stratum 'freely movable'"; "the movable is already presupposed as aesthesiological, but the lower stratum is one-sidedly detachable" (*einseitig ablösbar*, § 62, Hua IV 284, :4379). § 51 uses it for the egoistic and the communicative surrounding world: "In this sense there is thus 'one-sided detachability' of the one in relation to the other surrounding world" (*„einseitige Ablösbarkeit“*, Hua IV 193, :3303). The tool the passage invents for thing and phantom thus serves for body and will, and for the solitary and the social world. In each case the lower stratum can stand alone and the higher one cannot.

The term is older than *Ideen II*. In a supplement to the 1907 lectures *Ding und Raum*, the "temporal-spatial schema together with its sensuous fullness" is already called "the thing-phantom (also the full schema, also the sensuous schema)". Husserl adds that he "would like to say the sensuous 'image', which would recall Kant well, if the word image were not so seductive" (Hua XVI, Beilage II, 343; the file misprints Kant as "Krant"). Two pages later the supplement puts the thesis of § 15 c as a question: "Is the thing not substance exclusively insofar as it is bearer of causality? The mere phantom is not yet a thing" (Hua XVI 345). In 1907 phantom and schema are kept as names because "image" is too dangerous. In *Ideen II* they become the stratum below the thing. What comes from the late revision is the precise relation between the strata, one-sided detachment, which later sections of the book reuse.

## §15 c, Hua IV 41: materiality as relation to circumstances

Reads [§15 c.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 15 c.

What distinguishes a material thing from a phantom is not an added content but a relation. Materiality, "the actual reality" of the thing, lies in the dependence of its schemata on "real circumstances" and in the mode of apprehension that corresponds to this dependence. The thing is the unity that manifests itself in schemata that change, or stay the same, in determinate functional dependence on circumstances. A real property is such a unity of dependence, and so it is causal by definition.

> The actual reality, which here is called materiality, does not lie in the merely sensuous schema, not in what could belong to the perceived if for it there were nothing of the kind, if nothing of the kind had a sense, as relation to "circumstances". Rather it lies precisely in this relation and in the mode of apprehension corresponding to it. Under changing illumination, that is, in relation to another luminous thing, the thing looks different again and again, and not arbitrarily but determinately. Evidently functional connections obtain here, which set the schematic modifications on the one side in relation to those on other sides. It lies in the sense of the apprehension of a thing as thing (and not of the mere phantom) that such schemata, running off in determinate series of modification and now changing, now not changing in a determinate way, are experienced as manifestations of one and the same. But we experience them as that insofar as they run off as "dependents" of belonging "real circumstances".

German:

> Die eigentliche Realität, die hier Materialität heißt, liegt nicht im bloß sinnlichen Schema, nicht in dem, was dem Wahrgenommenen zukommen könnte, wenn es für dasselbe nichts dergleichen gäbe, nichts dergleichen einen Sinn hätte wie Beziehung auf „Umstände“, vielmehr liegt es eben in dieser Beziehung und der dieser entsprechenden Auffassungsweise. Das Ding sieht bei wechselnder Beleuchtung, also mit Beziehung auf ein anderes Leuchtendes immer wieder anders aus, und das nicht beliebig sondern bestimmt. Es bestehen hier offenbar funktionelle Zusammenhänge, welche die schematischen Modifikationen auf der einen Seite zu solchen auf anderen Seiten in Beziehung setzen. Im Sinne der Auffassung eines Dinges als Ding (und nicht des bloßen Phantoms) liegt es, daß solche Schemata, und zwar in bestimmten Modifikationsreihen ablaufend und sich in bestimmter Weise bald ändernd, bald nicht ändernd, als Bekundungen eines und desselben erfahren werden. Wir erfahren sie aber als das, wofern sie als „Abhängige“ zugehöriger „realer Umstände“ ablaufen.

The first sentence names and relocates at once. "The actual reality, which here is called materiality" (*die hier Materialität heißt*): the qualifier "here" restricts the name to this level of constitution, and § 17 will give the general aspect its own name (row 8). The sentence then denies two locations and affirms one. Reality is not "in the merely sensuous schema". It is not in what the perceived would have if "nothing of the kind had a sense, as relation to 'circumstances'". "Rather it lies precisely in this relation and the mode of apprehension corresponding to it" (*vielmehr liegt es eben in dieser Beziehung und der dieser entsprechenden Auffassungsweise*). The pronoun *es* does not agree with *Realität*, a slip of the dictation or of the copy. The phrase it governs joins two things: a relation among appearances and a manner of taking them. Reality is placed in the correlation between the two and not in either term alone.

The quotation marks mark the terms of art. "Circumstances" appears in quotation marks at its first use in § 15 c, and again here. At the end of the passage the related terms appear: the schemata run off as "dependents" of "real circumstances". The vocabulary is that of functions: "functional connections" (*funktionelle Zusammenhänge*), modifications "on the one side" related "to those on other sides". The thing "looks different again and again" under changing light, "not arbitrarily but determinately". The step from determinate variation to unity is carried by "manifestations" (*Bekundungen*). § 15 d sharpens the word into "certification" (*„Beurkundung“*) of a real property (Hua IV 44), a word from the language of documents and witnesses. The schema witnesses to the property.

The example that follows shows how much the inference rests on. A steel spring "has the real property 'elasticity'". "Like circumstances, like consequences" (*Unter gleichen Umständen gleiche Folgen*), and "the general rule … is not conscious as such": "it expresses a form belonging to the apperception of the real property" (Hua IV 43). The rule that founds the real property is not known, only apprehended. It is the form of the perception, and § 15 d concludes that causalities are here "not merely supposed but 'seen', 'perceived'" (Hua IV 44). Husserl's later marginal note picks out the phrase "Gesehene Kausalität" [seen causality] (apparatus to 39,21). The claim is strong and the passage does not argue for it. That the steadily varying schema is seen as the steady behaviour of one thing under circumstances is offered as a description of the sense of thing-apprehension, and the "evidently" (*offenbar*) of the third sentence is the only bridge from variation to function.

In the section the passage turns the abstraction of § 15 b into a definition. § 15 b ended unable to tell the isolated thing from a phantom. § 15 c names what the isolation had removed. § 15 e draws the consequence: "reality or, what here is the same, substantiality and causality belong inseparably together" (§15 e.2). § 16 adds that dependencies can themselves change in dependence on circumstances, so that unities of higher level are formed (Hua IV 46–49). § 18 g states the result as a definition of the thing: it "is a reality as unity of a manifold of regulated appearances that belong together", and its "properties express capacities, they are causal properties, referred to an 'if–so'" (Hua IV 86, :1811).

The definition is carried through the rest of the book, and it decides the treatment of the soul and of spirit. In §§ 30–33 the soul is a "unity of attestation" whose "modes of behaviour" are referred to "circumstances", so the analogy with the thing is formally complete. But "the soul … does not schematize itself" (Hua IV 127, :2462), so the analogy holds only for the form of the relation and not for the way the relation is given. In § 62 the definition becomes the premise of the argument for the priority of spirit: "Causality is the relation of a reality to its correlative circumstance-realities. The reality of spirit, however, is not related to real circumstances lying within nature but to real circumstances that consist in the 'surrounding world' and in other spirits" (Hua IV 283, :4367). Spirit has circumstances too, but they are of another kind. The relational concept of reality that § 15 c establishes for the thing is what lets § 62 say that spirit is real and yet not causal in the sense of nature. And § 64 turns the relation against the thing: "No thing has its individuality in itself" (Hua IV 299, :4647). An identity made of relations to circumstances is individuated only by the here and now of a subject.

Husserl had put the thesis as a question in the 1907 supplement already cited in the previous reading: "Is the thing not substance exclusively insofar as it is bearer of causality? The mere phantom is not yet a thing. … Does not everything in the end lead back to causality, every objective determination of a thing …? … already the unity of the objective property colour is a causal concept, a property of force" (Hua XVI, Beilage II, 345). The illumination example is already there, colour depending "on the course of the illumination by the sun, modified by clouds". *Ideen II* answers the question in the affirmative and adds the qualification of the 1907 text's last phrase: a "property of force" is the thing's "capacity" under circumstances, and the "if–so" of § 18 g is its form. What the passage contributes is the word "lies". Reality lies in a relation and in a way of apprehending it, and nowhere else.

## §17, Hua IV 53–54: reality, substance and their equivocations

Reads [§17.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 17.

Here Husserl fixes a word. The general aspect of thinghood, which § 15 found in the relation to circumstances, would best be called "reality". Tradition calls it "substance", and both words are equivocal. Husserl therefore stipulates "substantial reality" for this narrow sense, against the widest sense in which every individual temporal being is "real". From here on "substance" and "thing" mean this. Extensive substance is only a particular case, and the extension-determinations themselves belong to the schema, not to substance.

> In our considerations we have intentionally let the general aspect of thinghood as such, "reality", stand out more strongly than what belongs to materiality as such, the specific feature of extensive reality. That general aspect, which would surely best be designated as reality, is called *substance*. (Unfortunately all these philosophical terms are burdened with equivocations and lack any deeper clarification.) As against this, we have here drawn from intuition a firm essential stock that is evident in its fundamental significance. To separate these genuine "realities" from the widespread *widest* sense of the word, which comprises **any** individual (or temporal) being, we say *substantial reality*; but henceforth, wherever we speak simply of substantiality, or of substance or thing, this is what is meant. Extensive substance shall count for us only as a particularization. Of the role of extension (corporeality) we have already spoken. Accordingly it is clear that determinations such as position, figure and so on, as belonging to extension, are not substantial properties but rather thoroughly causal ones. As regards their mode of givenness, they are not unities of "certification"; rather they already belong to the *schema*.

German:

> In unseren Betrachtungen haben wir mit Absicht das Allgemeine der Dingheit überhaupt, die „Realität“ stärker hervorreten lassen gegenüber dem, was zur Materialität als solcher als dem Spezifischen der extensiven Realität gehört. Jenes Allgemeine, das sicherlich am besten als Realität zu bezeichnen wäre, heißt *Substanz*. (Leider sind alle diese philosophischen Termini mit Aequivokationen behaftet und entbehren jeder tieferen Klärung). Dem gegenüber haben wir hier einen festen Wesensbestand aus der Intuition geschöpft, der in seiner grundlegenden Bedeutung evident ist. Zur Abscheidung dieser echten „Realitäten“ von dem verbreiteten *weitesten* Wortsinne, der **jedwedes** individuelle (oder zeitliche) Sein befaßt, sagen wir *substantielle Realität*; doch soll im weiteren, wo immer wir einfach von Substantialität, bzw. von Substanz oder Ding sprechen, eben dies gemeint sein. Hierbei soll uns die extensive Substanz nur als eine Besonderung gelten. Von Rolle der Extension (Körperlichkeit) haben wir schon oben gesprochen. Danach ist es klar, daß Bestimmtheiten wie Lage, Figur usw. als zur Extension gehörig nicht substantielle Eigenschaften sind, vielmehr durchaus kausale. Was ihre Gegebenheitsweise anbelangt, so sind sie nicht Einheiten der „Beurkundung“, sie gehören vielmehr schon zum *Schema*.

*Note on the text.* *Hervorreten* is a misprint for *hervortreten* [stand out]. More seriously, "nicht substantielle Eigenschaften sind, vielmehr durchaus kausale" [are not substantial properties but rather thoroughly causal ones] contradicts the definition the paragraph has just given: substantial reality is the reality of causal properties (§ 15 e; row 7). It also contradicts the next sentence, which concedes that shape and position "are dependent on circumstances and experienceable in this causal dependence". That concession makes sense only if the preceding clause denied causality, not affirmed it. The file may have lost a word or garbled one. The reading needs the print and does not build on the clause. The main commentary's version ("they belong rather already to the schema") silently omits it.

The paragraph has two verb moods, and the difference between them is the point. Reality is what the general aspect "would surely best be designated as" (*am besten als Realität zu bezeichnen wäre*), in the subjunctive. "Substance" is what it "is called" (*heißt*), in the indicative. The better name and the received name come apart, and the parenthesis gives the reason: "Unfortunately all these philosophical terms are burdened with equivocations and lack any deeper clarification." Against the words the sentence sets a thing: "a firm essential stock drawn from intuition". The terminological decision that follows is a compromise. The new term combines the two old ones, *substantielle Realität*, and the next clause legislates for the rest of the book: "henceforth, wherever we speak simply of substantiality, or of substance or thing, this is what is meant".

The emphasis marks what the stipulation excludes. In the file the widest sense is set off twice: the *widest* sense, which comprises "**any**" (*jedwedes*) individual being. The genuine realities are put in quotation marks, "*echten „Realitäten“*". In the widest sense, anything individual and temporal is real: an experience, a tone-datum, a pure ego. In the narrow sense only what has properties that attest themselves under circumstances is real. The quotation marks around "realities" show that the narrow sense is the one the word has to be brought to. The next sentence shows what is at stake: "Extensive substance shall count for us only as a particularization" (*Besonderung*). If substance is defined by the relation to circumstances and not by extension, there can be non-extended substances. The definition is cut so that the soul can fall under it.

The passage looks back across § 15 and forward to § 31. Behind it lies the analysis in which reality was found in the relation to circumstances. Ahead of it, Section II needs a formal concept of reality under which both the material thing and the soul fall. § 31 states it: "In formal generality the concepts real substance (concretely understood: thing in a widest sense), real property, real state (real behaviour) and real causality are essentially connected concepts" (§31.1, Hua IV 126). That is the same stipulation raised to formal generality, and §§ 32–33 then measure the soul against it. The soul "does not schematize itself" (Hua IV 127). The "historyless" material thing is contrasted with the soul, which has a history (Hua IV 136–37). By § 33 it has only "a quasi-nature and a quasi-causality" (§33.3). The careful definition of § 17 is what produces these prefixes. Because "substance" means "substantial reality", the soul can be measured against it and found to be a substance only in a modified sense.

The apparatus adds context to the parenthesis. A marginal note in Landgrebe's typescript at Hua IV 48,30 says: "Up to p. 54 no proper text" (*Bis S. 54 kein eigentlicher Text*, apparatus to 48,30ff.). § 17 stands at the end of a stretch that the late Husserl regarded as unfinished. A supplementary sheet at the start of § 18 summarizes it: "Up to p. 54, aesthetic analysis of the material thing as *aistheton*, as objective sense, as matter of the doxic positing" (apparatus to 55,2). The "firm essential stock" is thus the yield of an aesthetic analysis in the sense of § 9 (row 3), the sense of the thing as perceived. Husserl did not take it to be a metaphysics of substance.

The corpus offers a precedent for the parenthesis. Descartes opens his definition of substance with the same complaint: "What is meant by 'substance' — a term which does not apply univocally to God and his creatures" (*Principles of Philosophy* I, art. 51, Cottingham et al., vol. 1). Descartes' equivocation runs between infinite and finite substance. Husserl's runs between the individual in general and the bearer of causal properties. The response is different in kind. Descartes distinguishes senses of the word. Husserl draws "an essential stock from intuition" and lets the word follow. The equivocation he regrets in the parenthesis is the one his compound term is built to fix. In Section III the narrow sense returns in the argument about spirit, which has "circumstances" too, but in "the surrounding world and in other spirits" (Hua IV 283, :4367). The stipulation of § 17 is what makes it possible to ask whether spirit is a reality at all, and § 64 gives the answer: it is a reality whose individuality, unlike the thing's, is "absolute".

## §18 a, Hua IV 55–58: the body as zero point and organ

Reads [§18 a.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) through §18 a.5, third to fifth paragraphs under § 18 a.

The body enters the constitution of things as the freely moved organ of perception and as the bearer of the zero point of orientation. Every appearance of a thing is motivated by kinaesthetic courses that run off "freely": if the eye moves thus, the image changes thus. That is why every thing that appears, and every thing that could appear, has an orientation toward the body. Here the possibility of experience is made to depend on a spontaneity, and the spontaneity belongs to the moving body. The word "motivation" enters the book here.

> But to the *possibility of experience belongs the spontaneity of the courses* of the presenting acts of sensation, which are accompanied by kinaesthetic series of sensation and motivated as dependent on them. With the *localization of the former in the movable member of the body* concerned, it is given that in all perception and perceptual verification (experience) the body is involved as freely moved sense-organ, as freely moved whole of the sense-organs, and that therefore, on this original ground, everything thingly-real in the ego's surrounding world has its relation to the body. And the distinction is evidently connected with this, that the body becomes the bearer of the zero point of orientation, Here and Now, from which the pure ego intuits space and the whole world of the senses. Thus every thing that appears has *eo ipso* a relation of orientation to the body, and not only the thing that actually appears but every thing that is to be able to appear.

German:

> Zur *Möglichkeit der Erfahrung gehört aber die Spontaneität der Abläufe* der von kinaesthetischen Empfindungsreihen begleiteten und von ihnen als abhängig motivierten darstellenden Empfindungsakte: mit dem *Lokalisiertsein der ersten im betreffenden beweglichen Leibesgliede* ist gegeben, daß bei aller Wahrnehmung und Wahrnehmungsausweisung (Erfahrung) der Leib als freibewegtes Sinnesorgan, als freibewegtes Ganzes der Sinnesorgane mit dabei ist, und daß somit aus diesem ursprünglichen Grunde alles Dinglich-Reale der Umwelt des Ich seine Beziehung hat zum Leibe. Und auch die Auszeichnung hängt offenbar hiermit zusammen, daß der Leib zum Träger der Orientierungspunkte Null wird, Hier und Jetzt, von dem aus das reine Ich den Raum und die ganze Sinnenwelt anschaut. So hat also jedes Ding, das erscheint, *eo ipso* Orientierungsbeziehung zum Leib, und nicht nur das wirklich erscheinende, sondern jedes Ding, das soll erscheinen können.

*Note on the text.* "Träger der Orientierungspunkte Null" is ungrammatical. The sense, confirmed by "von dem aus" [from which] in the same sentence, is a single point: "bearer of the zero point of orientation". The file's text is quoted as it stands.

The first clause sets a Kantian formula against a Kantian thesis. "To the possibility of experience belongs" (*Zur Möglichkeit der Erfahrung gehört*) is the language of the transcendental deduction, and what it introduces is "the spontaneity of the courses". In Kant spontaneity is the understanding's. Here it is the freedom of a body that moves its eyes and fingers. The passage keeps the doctrine of § 9 intact. The synthesis of perception remains non-spontaneous, but its condition now includes a doing, the free running-off of kinaesthetic series. The italics in the file divide the claim into two parts: "the spontaneity of the courses" and "the localization of the former in the movable member of the body". The first part is a condition of experience. The second puts it in a place, and that place is the body.

The adjective "freely moved" (*freibewegt*) is repeated twice in one clause, "as freely moved sense-organ, as freely moved whole of the sense-organs". The verb "motivated" turns up almost unnoticed in the subordinate clause: the presenting sensations are "motivated as dependent" on the kinaesthetic series. Three pages later the motivation is spelled out as a structure: "everywhere we find the 'if–so' or 'because–so'" (*das „wenn-so” oder „weil-so”*, Hua IV 58), and the apprehension of a thing "as standing so far off, so oriented, so coloured" is "not conceivable without such relations of motivation" (*Motivationsbeziehungen*, Hua IV 58). This is the first appearance in the book of the concept on which Section III will turn. Here it names a relation between two groups of sensations in one body.

The zero point is introduced through a dependence, and the dependence is marked as obvious: "evidently" (*offenbar*) "connected with this". It is not derived. The phrase "Here and Now" makes the point temporal as well as spatial, and the relative clause gives it to "the pure ego" (*das reine Ich*), who "intuits space and the whole world of the senses" from it. In the analysis of nature the pure ego is a constant presupposition, but here it already has a location, and the location is the body. The last sentence extends the orientation to possibility: "not only the thing that actually appears but every thing that is to be able to appear". The example follows at once. "If I imagine a centaur, I cannot but imagine it in a certain orientation and in a certain relation to my sense-organs: it stands 'to the right' of me" (Hua IV 56–57). Imagination does not escape the zero point. A thing that could not be oriented could not appear, even in phantasy.

The apparatus shows that the paragraph's framing was rebuilt late. At its first line Husserl inserted "*leibe*" [body], so that the dependence is on "the experiencing subject-body". Landgrebe's typescript had "subject". The marginal outline for the subsection reads: "a) orientation, a1) two kinds of 'sensations' playing together, presenting data and 'kinaesthetically' functioning 'sensations' in their different correlative relations". Husserl added later: "But up to now there has been no talk at all of sensation-data" (apparatus to 55,19). The self-criticism is exact. §§ 15–17 analysed the thing as sense, the schema and its dependencies, without the sensations in which it is presented, and § 18 a introduces both the sensations and the body that moves through them in one stroke. In Husserl's own heading for the passage (55,2), the earlier analysis concerned the thing "as *aistheton*", and "pp. 55ff." concern "the body-relatedness of this objective sense, of the *aisthesis*".

The understanding is carried furthest in Section II. The body becomes "the only object that is immediately spontaneously movable for the will of my pure ego" (§ 38, Hua IV 152, :2883). § 41 a adds that the body "bears in itself the zero point of all these orientations", the "ultimate central Here" (Hua IV 159, :2995). § 42 calls it the "conversion point" at which causal relations "are converted into conditional ones" (row 23). The "if–so" of § 18 a is the thread through all three. In Section III every person "has his 'Here', and for the same phenomenal Now it is another than mine" (*Jeder hat sein „Hier“ und das ist für dasselbe phänomenale Jetzt ein anderes als das meine*, § 52, Hua IV 202, :3378). § 59 (Hua IV 253, :3871) turns the freedom of the moved body into a definition: "The ego as unity is a system of 'I can'". The spontaneity that § 18 a locates in the moving organ becomes, in Section III, the form of the person.

The 1907 lectures *Ding und Raum* had already described the pairing of kinaesthetic and visual series as motivation. In the analysis of oculomotor changes, the strengthening of association is said to be "a phenomenological fact", to be interpreted "phenomenologically, that is, everything within a really, seeingly performed context of motivation" (*Motivationszusammenhang*, Hua XVI § 51, 178). The structure of *Ideen II* § 18 a is already there: images "*b*" motivated by kinaesthetic "*K*". What § 18 a adds is the body as the place where this happens and the ego that is located there. In 1907 motivation connects two series of data. In *Ideen II* it connects the data to a freely moving, oriented body, and the analysis of the person later builds on that.

## §18 f, Hua IV 80–81: the solus ipse

Reads [§18 f.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §18 f.3, second and third paragraphs under § 18 f.

The solus ipse of the thought experiment is not a human being left alone. It is a subject that knows nothing of others at all, not even that others are possible. Such a subject lacks what a human being alone in the world would still have, an "objective body", a body that it apprehends as visible to others. Its "abstraction" is therefore not a subtraction of others from a world that remains the same. It removes a whole apperceptive layer, and with it the sense of objectivity. The apprehension of the body plays a "special role" for intersubjectivity, the role on which all "objective" apprehension depends.

> Strictly considered, the solus ipse knows no *objective body* in the full and proper sense, even if it had the *phenomenon* of its body and the systems of belonging manifolds of experience, and had them just as perfectly as the social human being. In other words, the solus ipse does not truly deserve its name. The abstraction that we carried out as one justified by insight does not yield the *isolated human being*, or the isolated human personality. Nor did this abstraction consist in our arranging a mass murder of the human beings and animals of our surroundings, sparing only our own human subject. The subject that would then remain as the only one would still be a human subject, that is, still the intersubjective object, still apprehending and positing itself as such.

German:

> Genau besehen kennt der Solus-ipse keinen *objektiven* *Leib* im vollen und eigentlichen Sinn, selbst wenn er das *Phänomen* seines Leibes und die Systeme zugehöriger Erfahrungsmannigfaltigkeiten hätte und sie genau so vollkommen hätte wie der soziale Mensch. M.a.W. der Solus-ipse verdient seinen Namen in Wahrheit nicht. Die Abstraktion, die wir als einsichtig berechtigte vollzogen haben, liefert nicht den *isolierten* *Menschen*, bzw. die isolierte menschliche Persönlichkeit. Diese Abstraktion bestand ja auch nicht darin, daß wir einen Massenmord der Menschen und Tiere unserer Umwelt veranstalteten, das eigene menschliche Subjekt allein verschonend. Das dann als einziges verbleibende Subjekt wäre noch immer Menschensubjekt, d.i. noch immer der intersubjektive Gegenstand, sich selbst immer noch als solches auffassend und setzend.

*Note on the text.* A footnote marker after "eigentlichen Sinn" is omitted. Shortly after the passage, p. 81 breaks off in mid-word ("Das Ding, das sich in gerege", :1663), and p. 82 begins with the stray running head "Husserl Ideen II" and "sinnen-anschaulich" (:1664–:1665). Some words of the sentence are lost. The main commentary (§18 f.4) gives it as "The thing that constitutes itself in regulated manifolds of concordant experiences for the individual subject".

The name is the argument. *Solus ipse* means "he himself alone", and Husserl says the figure "does not truly deserve its name" (*verdient seinen Namen in Wahrheit nicht*). To be alone is to be without others whom one could have, and this subject has no concept of others to be without. The opposition between "*phenomenon*" and "*objective*" makes the same point for the body. The solus ipse has "the phenomenon of its body" with all its manifolds, "just as perfectly as the social human being", and lacks only the body as something seen from elsewhere. Nothing in its experience is missing. What is missing is a sense of the body.

The mass murder is the image of what the abstraction is not, and it is drastic on purpose. The verb is theatrical, "arranged" (*veranstalteten*), and the participle is morally coloured: the own subject is "spared" (*verschonend*). A survivor of such a murder would still be a human being, "the intersubjective object", "apprehending and positing itself as such". The last of the human beings would still know itself as one among others, and its solitude would be a loss. The abstraction of § 18 is something else, and the next sentences say what it removes. The constructed subject "knows nothing of a human surrounding, knows nothing of an actuality or even the real possibility of 'others'", and so "nothing of its own body as one understandable by others" (Hua IV 81). In spaced type the conclusion follows: "the apprehension of the body plays a special role for intersubjectivity, in which all objects are apprehended 'objectively', as things in the one objective time, in one objective space" (Hua IV 81).

The paragraph before the passage contains the experiment proper, and its weak point. The solipsist's world suddenly acquires others, and their consistent experience contradicts long series of his earlier, perfectly concordant experiences. Then "my so beautifully verified actuality is called by them the hallucination of someone who until this moment was mentally ill" (Hua IV 80). The "so beautifully" (*so schön*) is ironic. The claim that "nothing changes" in this as an a priori state of affairs, however perfect the solipsistic verification, is strong, and it rests on a stipulation: the others experience "as we assume, necessarily concordantly" (*wie wir voraussetzen: notwendig einstimmig*). Why the many should be right against the one is not argued. It is built into the case. A footnote limits the case further: "This conflict may not, however, be a complete one. For a basic stock of common experiences is presupposed if communication is to take place at all" (Hua IV 80 n., :1897). Husserl's later margin to that note goes to the heart of it: "But much belongs to that, already identical nature" (*Aber dahin gehört doch sehr viel, schon identische Natur*, apparatus to 80, note). The experiment, meant to show how others make nature objective, presupposes a shared nature as the condition of their correcting the solipsist. At the head of the subsection, the apparatus records only a question mark in Husserl's hand (apparatus to 80,1).

Husserl sees the circle and names it. Right after the experiment he asks: "do we not entangle ourselves in a circle, since the apprehension of human beings presupposes the apprehension of the body and thus the apprehension of things?" (Hua IV 80). The answer is a method, not a solution: "question the thing-apprehension itself". The passage read here is the first result of that questioning. The self-forgetfulness of the analysing ego must be "lifted in a decisive point", and the decisive point is the body as something for others. The understanding reached is that objectivity is a sense, carried by the body-apprehension, and not a count of subjects. A world with one human in it is still intersubjective. A subject without the apprehension of others is not even alone.

The book follows the understanding in both directions. § 42 shows what the solipsistically constituted body lacks: "in solipsistic experience we do not attain the givenness of ourselves as a spatial thing like all others", nor the natural object "human being", to which the higher strata are "'introjected'". "To get there we must go beyond our own subject" (Hua IV 161, :3020). § 46 shows how: "only with empathy … does the closed unity 'human being' constitute itself, and this I then transfer to myself" (Hua IV 167, :3083). The circle Husserl names in § 18 f returns as the "vicious circle" of § 53, between a nature that contains persons and persons who constitute nature: "We get here, it seems, into a vicious circle" (*Wir geraten hier, scheint es, in einen bösen Zirkel*, Hua IV 210, :3435). And § 64 runs the experiment the other way. "If we strike out all spirits from the world, there is no more nature. But if we strike out nature … something still remains: the spirit as individual spirit; only the possibility of sociality is lost" (Hua IV 297, :4597). § 18 removes the others and keeps nature, and objectivity is lost. § 64 removes nature and keeps the subject, and sociality is lost. The two experiments mirror each other.

The image recurs in the corpus in a later work. Husserl's Paris lectures of 1929 say that the phenomenological epoché makes me "in a certain sense *solus ipse*: not in the ordinary sense, say that of a human being left over in the still-existing world after a collapse of all the stars" (*nicht im gewöhnlichen, etwa in dem <eines> bei einem Zusammensturz aller Gestirne übrig gebliebenen Menschen in der noch immer seienden Welt*, Hua I 12). The catastrophe has become cosmic, but the logic is that of the mass murder. The survivor of a catastrophe is a human in a world, and so not the solitude philosophy means. The difference is in what replaces him. In *Ideen II* it is an abstraction within the natural attitude, a subject without the apperception of others. In the Paris lectures it is the transcendental ego. The image recurs because the same misunderstanding has to be blocked in each case, the reading of an abstraction as a subtraction.

## §18 g, Hua IV 85–86: God's distorting glasses and the thing as rule

Reads [§18 g.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §18 g.4, third and fourth paragraphs under § 18 g.

The non-objectivity of secondary qualities means only that they are not exempt from the relativity of appearances to subjects. It does not mean that a mind with better access sees the qualities things really have. The proof is a reductio. A God who saw things "as they are in themselves" could share a world with us only through exchangeable appearances, and so only with a body and sense-organs of his own. Objectivity is therefore intersubjective, and the thing is "a rule of possible appearances", the identical something that answers to the regulated appearances of all who can communicate.

> Should we say that God sees things as they are in themselves, and we through sense-organs that are a kind of distorting glasses? Are things filled space with absolute quality that we just do not know? But if the things are to be the same that appear to us as they appear, then a unity of mutual understanding between God and us would have to be possible, just as between different human beings the possibility of recognizing that the things the one sees and those the other sees *are the same* exists only through mutual understanding. But how would the identification be conceivable other than through the supposed absolute spirit's seeing things, too, through sensuous appearances, which would have to be exchangeable in a mutual understanding (or at least one-sidedly) like our appearances between human beings? Then God would simply be colour-blind and so on, and we blind to his qualities. But does it make sense to dispute which are the right qualities? The new qualities would again be secondary, and would be excluded by physics in exactly the same way, physics that must be the same for all if the things are the same. Naturally the absolute spirit would also have to have a body for the purposes of mutual understanding, so the dependence on sense-organs would be there too.

German:

> Sollen wir sagen, Gott sieht die Dinge, wie sie an sich sind, und wir durch Sinnesorgane, die eine Art verzerrender Brillen sind? Die Dinge sind erfüllter Raum mit absoluter Qualität, die wir nur nicht kennen? Aber sollen die Dinge dieselben sein, die uns so erscheinen, wie sie erscheinen, dann müßte doch eine Einheit der Verständigung zwischen Gott und uns möglich sein, wie zwischen verschiedenen Menschen nur durch Verständigung die Möglichkeit besteht zu erkennen, daß die Dinge, die der eine sieht, und die, die der andere sieht, *dieselben sind*. Aber wie wäre die Identifizierung denkbar als so, daß der supponierte absolute Geist die Dinge eben auch durch sinnliche Erscheinungen sieht, die ähnlich austauschbar sein müßten in einer Wechselverständigung — oder mindestens einseitig — wie unsere Erscheinungen zwischen Menschen. Dann wäre Gott eben farbenblind usw., und wir blind für seine Qualitäten. Hat es aber einen Sinn zu streiten, welches die richtigen Qualitäten sind? Die neuen Qualitäten wären wieder sekundär und würden genau so ausgeschaltet durch die Physik, die für alle dieselbe sein muß, wenn die Dinge dieselben sind. Natürlich müßte der absolute Geist zu Zwecken der Wechselverständigung auch einen Leib haben, also wäre ja auch die Abhängigkeit von Sinnesorganen da.

*Note on the text.* According to the apparatus, the whole passage from "Sollen wir sagen" to "Sinnesorganen da" (Hua IV 85,10–30) was bracketed in Landgrebe's typescript (*in L. eingeklammert*, apparatus to 85,10–30). The apparatus does not say who set the brackets or what they meant. The edition prints the passage.

The passage is built as two questions and a reductio. The first question gives the picture to be refuted in its most familiar form: God sees things "as they are in themselves" (*wie sie an sich sind*), and our sense-organs are "a kind of distorting glasses" (*eine Art verzerrender Brillen*). The image places the distortion in an instrument between us and the thing, so that a mind without the instrument would see undistorted. The second question states the metaphysics behind the image: "filled space with absolute quality that we just do not know". The argument then goes through a conditional, "if the things are to be the same", and its premise is the one § 18 f established. The identity of the seen thing is recognized "only through mutual understanding" (*nur durch Verständigung*). If God and we see the same things, a "unity of mutual understanding" between God and us must be possible.

The reductio has two steps, and both are compressed. First: identification with God would require that God, too, sees through appearances "exchangeable" with ours. Then "God would simply be colour-blind and so on, and we blind to his qualities". The symmetry is the joke. If our qualities are distortions relative to his, his are distortions relative to ours, and the question "which are the right qualities" loses its sense. Second: physics would exclude his new qualities "in exactly the same way" as ours, because physics "must be the same for all if the things are the same". The final sentence draws the consequence with the word "Naturally" (*Natürlich*). The absolute spirit "would also have to have a body for the purposes of mutual understanding". The most important step of the argument is presented as the most obvious one. That communication needs a body was the result of § 18 f, and here it is applied to God without new argument. The inference holds only if knowing the same world requires being able to communicate about it. Husserl assumes this. A God who knew the world without ever sharing it would escape the argument, and the passage does not consider him.

What the reductio refutes is stated in the next paragraph. The non-objectivity of the secondary qualities may be understood only to mean "that they are in no way withdrawn from the relativism of appearances" (Hua IV 85). The conclusion is restrictive: no quality is privileged. It does not reverse the doctrine. Subjects may be blind to colour or tone, and their senses "may be wholly different", but they cannot be blind "with respect to *all* senses and so at the same time *space-blind, movement-blind, energy-blind*". Otherwise there would be no world of things for them (Hua IV 86). The paragraph read in the commentary's §18 g.4 states the result as a definition: "Nature is an intersubjective actuality", and "the thing is a rule of possible appearances", "a reality as unity of a manifold of regulated appearances that belong together. And this unity is an intersubjective one" (Hua IV 86, :1811). The word *Regel* [rule] is Kant's, and the hyphenation "ge-regelt" in the file marks the root. The parenthesis gives the content of the rule: "the properties express capacities, they are causal properties, referred to an 'if–so'". The "if–so" of § 18 a (row 9) and the "circumstances" of § 15 c (row 7) thus meet in the definition of the thing.

The argument's limits are visible in Husserl's own margins. Against the claim that the step to the physical thing is motivated by the plurality of differently sensing subjects, he wrote: "But I myself as an isolated one am exposed to sensuous anomaly and to variations of my originally constitutive normality; so physics does not first have a relation to intersubjectivity" (apparatus to 86,29). That reopens what § 18 e had granted and § 18 h will grant again (row 12). A solitary subject can already reach the physical thing, so intersubjectivity is not the only route to it. Read with this note, the reductio on God shows only that intersubjectivity is sufficient to deprive any quality of privilege, not that it is necessary.

The understanding runs on in two directions. The thing becomes ever emptier. Two pages later it is "a merely empty identical something", with "no sensuous-intuitive content that could be given intersubjectively as identical" (*nur ein leeres identisches Etwas*, Hua IV 88, :1825). In § 47 the natural-scientific object is "the x of 'mathematical' qualities" (§47.1, Hua IV 170). In the last paragraph of the book, "Nature is the X" (*Die Natur ist das X*, Hua IV 302, :4691). The empty something of § 18 g becomes the X that § 64 sets against the spirit, which "is no X but what is given itself". The rule, on the other hand, moves inward. In § 61 the objective thing-world, "as an intersubjectively valid rule of possible appearances, and already of possible sensation-complexes of the single spirits, points back to a lower level of all spiritual existence": "Every spirit has a 'nature-side'" (Hua IV 279, :4300). The rule of appearances that defined the thing in § 18 g becomes in § 61 an index of a level of the spirit itself.

*Ideas I* had used God against the same picture. "God, the subject of absolutely perfect knowledge and so also of all possible adequate perception", is supposed to have the "adequate perception of the thing itself" denied to us finite beings. "But this view is countersensical" (*widersinnig*). It would make the spatial thing "a really inherent constituent" of the divine stream of consciousness, an experience, and it is misled by the thought that "the transcendence of the thing is that of an image or sign" (*Ideen I* § 43, Hua III 89; the corpus file is unreviewed, and its OCR is poor at this point; the English translation, `husserl-cw2-…`, agrees). In *Ideas I* God fails as a perceiver because a thing given without appearances would no longer be transcendent. In *Ideen II* God fails as a fellow subject because a world shared without appearances and a body would not be the same world. The first argument concerns the essence of perception. The second concerns the essence of objectivity. Placed together, they show the work that *Ideen II* adds: objectivity comes to mean what can be communicated, and the "distorting glasses" become the sense-organs of a member of a community of mutual understanding.

## §18 h, Hua IV 89–90: a member of nature

Reads [§18 h.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 18 h.

The objective, physical thing can in principle be reached on either path: by a solitary subject that corrects its own anomalies, or by a community. The path through others is not necessary for physics. What only the intersubjective path gives is the subject's apprehension of itself as part of nature. A solipsistic subject could have an objective nature before it, but it could not apprehend itself as "a member of nature", a psychophysical animal. The self as natural object is constituted through others. This is the hinge on which Section I turns into Section II.

> At any rate we see: on the one hand, the *possibility* of advancing to the constitution of the "objective" (physical) *thing* exists already for the solipsistic stage. On the other hand, there is not for the intersubjective stage either the unconditional *necessity* of getting that far. But there is (apart from the fact that constitution factually takes place intersubjectively) a difference of principle between these two possible paths of building up an "objective nature". The solipsistic subject could indeed have an objective nature over against it, but it could not apprehend itself as a member of nature, could not apperceive itself as psychophysical subject, as *animal*, as happens at the intersubjective stage of experience. This can of course become evident only when one follows the constitution of animal nature. Just as, quite generally, the exhibition of the relations of dependence between experienced nature and experiencing subject has shown that for a full clarification of the sense and structure of physical nature the study of subjectivity is unconditionally required.

German:

> Immerhin sehen wir: einerseits besteht schon für die solipsistische Stufe die *Möglichkeit*, bis zur Konstitution des ,,objektiven'' (physikalischen) *Dinges* vorzudringen. Andererseits besteht auch für die intersubjektive Stufe nicht die unbedingte *Notwendigkeit*, so weit zu gelangen. Es besteht aber — abgesehen davon, daß faktisch die Konstitution sich intersubjektiv vollzieht -- ein prinzipieller Unterschied zwischen diesen beiden möglichen Wegen des Aufbaus einer „objektiven Natur“. Das solipsistische Subjekt könnte zwar eine objektive Natur sich gegenüber haben, aber es könnte nicht sich selbst als ein Glied der Natur auffassen, sich nicht als psychophysisches Subjekt, als animal, apperzipieren, so wie es auf intersubjektiver Erfahrungsstufe geschieht. Das kann natürlich erst einsichtig werden, wenn man die Konstitution der animalischen Natur verfolgt. Wie ja überhaupt der Aufweis der Abhängigkeitsbeziehungen zwischen erfahrener Natur und erfahrendem Subjekt gezeigt hat, daß zu einer vollen Klärung von Sinn und Aufbau der physischen Natur das Studium der Subjektivität unbedingt erforderlich ist.

*Note on the text.* A footnote marker after "geschieht" is omitted. Its note refers to "the sections on body-constitution, p. 158ff." (:1898). The main commentary cites the passage at Hua IV 90. The apparatus places the end of § 18 h at 89,31ff., and the passage runs across the page break.

The passage is built on a chiasm, and the emphasis in the file marks it: "*possibility*" for the solipsistic stage, "*necessity*" denied for the intersubjective one. Each modal word is the opposite of what the reader of § 18 f would expect. § 18 f had shown that the solus ipse lacks objectivity in the full sense. Here Husserl grants it the *possibility* of reaching the physical thing, and the preceding paragraph explains how. "We are free intelligent beings": even in a world without illness or illusion, "we can perform *voluntary* interventions in our own and others' bodies, and now 'anomalies' appear" (Hua IV 89). A solitary subject that experiments on itself can produce the anomalies that motivate physics. Conversely, a community of perfectly normal subjects living "passively, animal-like 'in the world'" need never reach the physical thing. The "physical world-view" is a product of freedom, not of plurality.

The concession clarifies the claim, and the parenthesis keeps fact and principle apart: "apart from the fact that constitution factually takes place intersubjectively". What remains of the difference is stated with a verb of self-relation: the solipsistic subject "could not apprehend itself as a member of nature" (*nicht sich selbst als ein Glied der Natur auffassen*). *Glied* means a member of a whole, and also a limb. The phrase is followed by two equivalents, "psychophysical subject" and "*animal*" in Latin. Of the three, the Latin word is the plainest: what the solitary subject cannot see itself as is an animal among animals.

The next sentence makes a promise: "This can of course become evident only when one follows the constitution of animal nature." The "of course" (*natürlich*) admits that the thesis has not been shown. The final sentence broadens the result into a program: the "study of subjectivity is unconditionally required" for "a full clarification of sense and structure of physical nature". The "unconditionally" (*unbedingt*) answers the "unconditional necessity" denied two sentences earlier. Physics does not need intersubjectivity unconditionally. The clarification of physics needs subjectivity unconditionally. Husserl's late margin on this paragraph is ambivalent: "That should have been said much earlier. But it would then be a mere determination of that subjectivity in which it is constituted" (apparatus to 89,31ff.). The note seems to fear that stating the program earlier would have reduced the analysis of nature to a description of its constituting subjectivity, which is the direction Section III takes.

The passage closes Section I by stating what the analysis of nature cannot do by itself. It looks back to the margin quoted in the previous reading: "I myself as an isolated one am exposed to sensuous anomaly … so physics does not first have a relation to intersubjectivity" (apparatus to 86,29). That note and this paragraph agree, and they take back the strong reading of § 18 f. Others are not required to make nature objective. They are required to make me an object in it. Section II begins from exactly this point. Its introduction asks for the "soul as natural object" (§ 19), and its first concept is the "ego-human-being", the whole human being "with body and soul" (§ 21).

The promise is kept in two steps in Section II. § 42 states the deficit in the language of § 18 h: "in solipsistic experience we do not attain the givenness of ourselves as a spatial thing like all others … and not the natural object 'human being' (animal being) … To get there we must go beyond our own subject and turn to the animalia that meet us in the external world" (Hua IV 161, :3020). § 46 supplies the mechanism: it would not occur to me in self-experience "to stuff all my psychic life into my body"; "only with empathy … does the closed unity 'human being' constitute itself, and this I then transfer to myself" (Hua IV 167, :3083). Being "a member of nature" is a status I receive from others and apply to myself afterwards. In Section III the same order governs the person. In § 56 h, "the comprehending representation that others have of me" serves me "to apprehend myself as a social 'human being'" (Hua IV 242, :3724), and only then, by a change of attitude and "more mediately than in the case of others", does "the spiritual ego" turn into "the psychic ego of natural-scientific psychology" (*wenn auch mittelbarer wie bei Anderen, das geistige Ich in das seelische der naturwissenschaftlichen Seelenlehre*, Hua IV 243, :3727). The naturalistic self-apprehension is reached last, and § 18 h already states the reason: the self appears as a member of nature only through others.

The Cartesian Meditations, the one later work in the corpus that reruns the experiment, draw the line elsewhere. There the abstraction to the sphere of ownness leaves "a sense *mere nature*" that has lost its "*for-everyone*", and within it "I find, uniquely distinguished, *my body*", "the only object within my abstractive world-stratum" to which I ascribe fields of sensation (§ 44, Hua I 128). § 45 then goes further than *Ideen II* allows: "I, the reduced *human ego* (psychophysical ego), am thus constituted as member of the *world*" (*Ich, das reduzierte Menschen-Ich (psychophysische Ich), bin also konstituiert als Glied der Welt*, Hua I 128–29), before any other has been constituted. The word *Glied* [member] is the word of § 18 h, and the claim is its denial. The meditations can make it because their "world" is in quotation marks: an "ownness-reduced nature" in a "reduced 'world'", whose "objects", "things" and "psychophysical ego" all carry the marks of the reduction (Hua I 128–29). *Ideen II* has no such quotation marks. Its solipsistic subject faces a nature in the ordinary sense, and in that nature it cannot find itself as an animal. Read together, the two texts show what § 18 h's thesis depends on. A subject without others can be a member of a nature that is its own. It cannot be a member of the nature that is "for everyone", and that is the only nature Section I knows.

## §21, Hua IV 94–96: having a body

Reads [§21.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), §21.3 and §21.4, first, third and fourth paragraphs under § 21.

The everyday "I" covers the whole human being, body and soul, and ordinary speech nonetheless says that I *have* a body and a soul and *am* neither. Husserl takes the two grammars together. The unity of the human being is close enough that states of either component count as mine. The relation is still one of having, because the psychic has a "preeminence": without a soul there is dead matter, but a soul without any body cannot even be thought. The ghost is the test case. It lacks a material body but not a body, and so the body turns out to be what spirit needs in order to exist objectively, for others.

> In normal I-speech (or in the normal use of personal pronouns generally), the "whole" human being with body and soul is thus comprised under the "I". It can therefore very well be said: I am not my body, but I have my body; I am not a soul, but I have a soul.
>
> On the other hand it is easy to see that the *psychic* has a *preeminence* and is what essentially *determines* the concept of the I. If the soul falls away, we have dead matter, a merely material thing that no longer has anything of the human I in it. The body, on the other hand, cannot fall away. Even the ghost necessarily has its ghost-body. Admittedly this is no actual material thing, the appearing materiality is an illusion, but with it so is the soul belonging to it, and so the whole ghost.

German:

> In der normalen Ichrede (bzw. im normalen Gebrauch der persönlichen Pronomina überhaupt) ist also unter dem Ich der „ganze“ Mensch mit Leib und Seele umspannt. Es kann daher sehr wohl heißen: ich bin nicht mein Leib, sondern ich habe meinen Leib, ich bin nicht eine Seele, sondern ich habe eine Seele.
>
> Andererseits ist leicht einzusehen, daß das *Seelische* einen *Vorzug* hat und das *Ichbegriff* wesentlich *Bestimmende* ist. Fällt die Seele fort, so haben wir tote Materie, ein bloß materielles Ding, das nichts mehr von dem Menschen-Ich an sich hat. Der Leib andererseits kann nicht fortfallen. Selbst das Gespenst hat notwendig seinen Gespensterleib. Freilich ist dieser kein wirkliches materielles Ding, die erscheinende Materialität eine Täuschung, aber damit zugleich die zugehörige Seele und so das ganze Gespenst.

*Note on the text.* "das *Ichbegriff* wesentlich *Bestimmende*" should read *das den Ichbegriff wesentlich Bestimmende* [what essentially determines the concept of the I]. The file has also run "Ich-Rede von seinen" together ("I c h - *Redevonseinen*", :1947). Neither touches the reading.

The formula rests on a "therefore" (*daher*). Because the everyday I comprises the "whole" human being, it "can very well be said" that I have my body and am not it. The inference looks odd: one would expect the whole human being to *be* body and soul. The scare quotes on "whole" (*„ganze“*) show where it turns. The whole that the pronoun covers is not the sum of its components. It is the I that owns them, and so the paragraph before the formula lists what is predicated of that I. "I perceive, I judge", "I am so constituted", but also "he dances", "he is beaten, stung, touched" when his body is, "he is dirty when his finger is" (Hua IV 94). Pronouns attach bodily states to the I without identifying the I with the body. The double negation then states what the grammar shows: "ich bin nicht mein Leib, sondern ich habe meinen Leib". *Haben* [to have] is the verb of ownership, and the possessive *mein* [my] is the sign of it.

The second paragraph explains the asymmetry, and its key word is *Vorzug* [preeminence], in italics. The thought experiments come in pairs. If the soul falls away, "dead matter" remains, with "nothing of the human I in it". If the body falls away, nothing remains, because "the body cannot fall away" at all. The ghost confirms this: even a ghost "necessarily has its ghost-body" (*Gespensterleib*). The compound is coined for the occasion and grammatically sober: a ghost's body, not a body that is a ghost. The following sentences of § 21 give the analysis. A ghost is a subject whose body "is a pure 'spatial phantom', without any material properties" (Hua IV 95). The phantom of § 15 b (row 6) returns as the body of a ghost, and Husserl appeals to its "one-sided detachability" (*einseitig ablösbar*) from materiality. A soul without a *material* body is therefore thinkable, "as a ghost instead of a natural animal being", "but in no way without a body at all" (Hua IV 95).

The argument for the necessity of the body is intersubjective, and its turning point is in spaced type. If a psychic being is to have "*objective* existence", "the *conditions of the possibility of intersubjective givenness* must be fulfilled". That is possible only through "'empathy', which for its part presupposes an intersubjectively experienceable body" (Hua IV 95). The body is necessary because spirit must be experienceable by others, not because spirit is matter. The preeminence of the psychic lies in the same ground: "The spirit must, in order to be objectively experienceable, be the ensoulment of an objective body … whereas conversely the objective experienceability of a spatial phantom or a material thing demands no ensoulment" (Hua IV 96). The relation is founded one way and required the other way. The section closes with the formula that Section III unpacks: "The body is not only a thing at all, but expression of the spirit, *and it is at the same time organ of the spirit*" (Hua IV 96).

Husserl's later footnote to the ghost passage is a supplementary sheet in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 95, note). It weakens the argument and points beyond it: "For distinguishing a ghost from the real embodiment of a subjectivity with its I, the reference back to phantoms is not quite correct. And no account is taken of the fundamentally essential role of *vocalization*, in one's own self-produced voice, belonging to one's own, originally given kinaestheses of the vocal muscles." The child's "self-produced and then analogically heard voice" seems to be "the bridge for the objectivation of the I, or for the formation of the 'alter'", before any visual analogy between bodies is possible (Hua IV 95 n.). The late Husserl thus doubts the very example that carries the necessity of the body and supplies a better one. The bridge to the other is the voice, which I produce and hear as others hear it.

In the book the being/having formula is carried furthest in Section III, where the personalistic attitude sharpens it. In the *inspectio sui* of § 54, "the body is *my* body and it is mine first of all as my counterpart, *my object*, as the house is my object … 'mine', but not a component of the I" (*„mein“, aber nicht Bestandstück des Ich*, Hua IV 212, :3471). The having of § 21 becomes a having of something over against me. In § 60 a a footnote credits Lipps with the remark that "the most original concept of possession, of 'I have', arises here: I have my limbs, I have power over them" (Hua IV 258 n., :4150). There the having is a power, the "I can" of the moving body. Between § 21 and § 60 the verb changes its object. In § 21 I have a body as a grammatical owner. In § 54 I have it as a counterpart. In § 60 I have it as the field of my power.

The formula returns almost verbatim in the 1925 lectures on phenomenological psychology. A person "stands there bodily, and I myself, when I take myself as a person, as when I simply say I and call the other you, have my body and bodily existence for myself. But in the frame of the personal attitude I am not posited for myself as a natural object" (*habe meinen Leib … Aber ich bin im Rahmen personaler Einstellung nicht für mich als Naturobjekt gesetzt*, Hua IX 228). In 1925 the having belongs explicitly to the personal attitude and is opposed to being a natural object. § 21, written inside the naturalistic analysis of the "ego-human-being", already contains it, but there the having is still a fact of ordinary speech. The understanding the passage reaches is that the I is the owner of its body, and the body is necessary to it because it is through the body that the I can be there for others.

## §22, Hua IV 97–99: the ego-ray

Reads [§22.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 22.

The pure ego is found as the point from which each performed act is directed at its object. Husserl describes this with an image, a "ray" (*Strahl*) that goes out from the ego through its acts to their objects. The ego stays "undivided and numerically identical" across all of them. The image is at once said to be "imperfectly usable". Directedness has modes: in desire, love and hate the object sends "counter-rays" back. The pure ego is thus a pole, identical and without content of its own. Its relation to objects has a direction in each act, and that direction is not always the ego's.

> … lies a ray of directedness, which I cannot describe otherwise than as taking its point of departure in the "I", which thereby evidently remains undivided and numerically identical while it lives in these manifold acts, is spontaneously active in them, and goes in ever new rays through them to the objectual element of their sense. More exactly, by the way, the pure I is related to objects in very different modes according to the kind of act performed. In a certain sense it is always free in its direction toward the object; on the other hand the image of "directing oneself toward" is only imperfectly usable for the phenomenon. In a certain general sense the I does indeed direct itself everywhere toward the object, but in a particular sense an ego-ray pushing out from the pure I sometimes goes toward the object, and counter-rays, as it were, come from it to meet it.

German:

> liegt ein Strahl des Gerichtetseins, den ich nicht anders beschreiben kann als seinen Ausgangspunkt nehmend im „Ich“, das dabei evidenterweise ungeteilt und numerisch identisch bleibt, während es in diesen mannigfaltigen Akten lebt, in ihnen sich spontan betätigt und in immer neuen Strahlen durch sie hindurch auf das Gegenständliche ihres Sinnes geht. Genauer gesprochen, ist übrigens das reine Ich je nach der Art des vollzogenen Aktes in sehr verschiedenen Modi auf Objekte bezogen. In gewissem Sinne ist es in seiner Richtung auf dasselbe allzeit ein freies, andererseits ist das Bild vom „sich richten auf“ die Erscheinung unvollkommen brauchbar. In gewissem allgemeinen Sinn richtet sich zwar überall das Ich auf das Objekt, aber im besondern Sinn geht mitunter ein vom reinen Ich vorschiebender Ichstrahl auf das Objekt hin, und kommen von diesem gleichsam Gegenstrahlen entgegen.

*Note on the text.* The passage continues the sentence "in jedem Aktvollzuge" [in every performance of an act] across stacked running heads for pp. 98 and 99 (:2013–:2015). "die Erscheinung" is probably *für die Erscheinung* [for the phenomenon].

The image is introduced as the only available description: a ray "which I cannot describe otherwise than as taking its point of departure in the 'I'" (*den ich nicht anders beschreiben kann als*). Husserl does not claim that the ego is a source of rays. He claims that he has no other words for the directedness of an act. The quotation marks around "I" (*„Ich“*) mark the ego as a point of origin, the place where the ray starts. What is said of it is said with the image: it remains "undivided and numerically identical", it "lives" in the acts and "is spontaneously active" in them, and it goes "in ever new rays" through them. An identity that is only the starting point of all rays has no content beyond that position. § 24 draws the conclusion: the pure ego "has no hidden inner riches", it is "absolutely simple, absolutely manifest" (§24.1, Hua IV 105, :2115).

The self-correction is part of the passage. "More exactly, by the way", the ego is related to objects "in very different modes", and "the image of 'directing oneself toward' is only imperfectly usable" (*unvollkommen brauchbar*). Husserl takes back the generality of his own metaphor. In "a certain general sense" every act directs itself toward its object. In "a particular sense" only some acts do: "sometimes" (*mitunter*) an ego-ray "pushing out" (*vorschiebend*) goes toward the object, and "as it were" (*gleichsam*) counter-rays come back. The examples follow at once: "in desiring I find myself attracted by the desired object", "in loving I feel myself inclined to the beloved, attracted by it", "in hating I am directed toward the hated, but repelled by it" (Hua IV 99). The image of the ray has to be doubled to describe feeling, because in feeling the object acts on the ego as well. The counter-ray is the first form of what Section III calls "stimulus" (*Reiz*) and "motivation": the object's pull on the person.

The apparatus shows that the vocabulary of the pole is late. "*Ichpol*" in the title of § 22, "The pure ego as ego-pole", is an "addition by H. in L.", and so is "(des Ichpols)" in the title of § 23 (apparatus to 97 and 101, §-titles). The text of §§ 22–23 speaks of the ego, the ray and the centre. The word "pole", which makes the image geometrical, was added after 1924. The parenthesis on absolute individuation in the first paragraph of § 22 ("This subject has absolute individuation, the I of the respective *cogitatio*, which is itself an absolutely individual thing", Hua IV 97) repeats almost word for word a sentence of § 64: "Absolute individuation belongs already to the pure I of the respective *cogitatio*, which is itself an absolutely individual thing in itself" (Hua IV 300, :4656). The apparatus records no insertion here, so the dating cannot be settled. The parallel shows, though, that § 22 already names the property that § 64 makes the ground of the priority of spirit.

In the chapter the passage is the positive account that *Ideas I* postponed. There the pure ego was "a transcendence of a peculiar kind, not constituted — a transcendence in immanence", and Husserl promised that the difficult questions of the pure ego would be given "a chapter of their own in the second book of this work" (*Ideen I* § 57, Hua III 124). The first chapter of Section II is that chapter, and the ego-ray is its first description. The image already occurs in *Ideas I* — "the ray of regard of the pure ego, dividing itself into a multiplicity of rays" in a synthetic act (Hua III/1 file, :5977) — but there it serves the analysis of acts. In § 22 the ray becomes the description of the ego itself: an origin that is nothing but the origin of rays.

The book does not leave the pole empty. The habitualities of § 29 (row 16) give the pure ego a "possession". § 64 then corrects the picture of § 22 explicitly: "But the I is not an empty pole, but bearer of its habituality, and in that lies that it has its individual history" (*Aber das Ich ist nicht leerer Pol sondern Träger seiner Habitualität*, Hua IV 300, :4656). The *Cartesian Meditations* make the same correction in the same words: "this centring I is not an empty pole of identity (any more than any object is), but by virtue of a lawfulness of *transcendental genesis* gains with each of the acts radiating from it … a new abiding property" (Hua I 100–101). The ray survives in *ausstrahlenden* [radiating]. What § 22 introduces is an image that cannot carry the ego's history, and the book's last section and the later Husserl both set it right.

## §26, Hua IV 107–108: Kant's sentence in a good sense

Reads [§26.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 26.

Consciousness can be wholly dull, without any actual cogito, as in dreamless sleep. The pure ego is then not present as a performing ego, but it is not absent in the sense of having to be added from outside when consciousness wakes. It belongs to every consciousness as the possibility of waking, of a cogito "welling up" at any point. Husserl expresses this by taking over Kant's sentence that the "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations, and giving it a sense of his own: "representations" are to include all dark consciousness.

> The I that becomes actual there is nothing put in or added from outside, nothing that first comes to be at the moment of its actual appearing in order then to disappear again into nothing. The **pure I** must be able to accompany all my representations. This Kantian sentence has a good sense if we understand by representations here all dark consciousness.

German:

> Das Ich, das da aktuell wird, ist nichts von außen Hineingesetztes oder Hinzugesetztes, nichts, was im Moment des aktuellen Auftretens allererst wird, um dann wieder ins Nichts zu verschwinden. Das **reine Ich** muß alle meine Vorstellungen begleiten können*. Dieser Kantische Satz hat einen guten Sinn, wenn wir unter Vorstellungen hier alles dunkle Bewußtsein verstehen.

*Note on the text.* The stray asterisk after *können* closes emphasis that the file has lost. The print probably sets the whole sentence off, as Husserl does with quotations.

The sentence is Kant's with one word exchanged. Kant writes: "The: I think, must be able to accompany all my representations" (*Das: Ich denke, muß alle meine Vorstellungen begleiten können*, *Kritik der reinen Vernunft*, B, § 16). Husserl writes *Das reine Ich* [the pure I] where Kant has *Das: Ich denke* [the: I think]. In Kant the subject of the sentence is a representation, the "I think", which is the act of the understanding by which a manifold becomes mine. In Husserl it is the ego. The exchange turns a claim about an act into a claim about its subject. The "must be able to" (*muß … können*) is kept, and it now carries all the weight. The ego does not accompany all representations. It is able to.

The second exchange is the gloss. "This Kantian sentence has a good sense if we understand by representations here all dark consciousness" (*alles dunkle Bewußtsein*). Kant had argued the other way: without the possibility of the "I think", something would be represented in me "which could not be thought at all", which "is as much as to say the representation would be either impossible or at least nothing for me" (B § 16). For Kant, a representation that the "I think" could not accompany would be nothing for me. Husserl takes the dark, unperformed, sleeping consciousness that is nothing for the waking ego and says that it is still able to be accompanied. The possibility does not legitimate representations. It is the possibility of waking.

The apparatus shows that Husserl first wrote something sharper. At 108,23 the manuscript reads "einen guten Sinn (aber nicht den Kants)" [a good sense (but not Kant's)] (apparatus to 108,23). The parenthesis was dropped in the later copies and is not in the edition. The manuscript states openly that Husserl's sense is not Kant's. The printed text lets "a good sense" suggest that Husserl is merely spelling out Kant. The difference between the two versions is the difference between a quotation turned against its author and a quotation reinterpreted, and the edition prints the milder one.

The surrounding paragraph prepares the reinterpretation with Leibniz. That consciousness can become wakeful means "to speak with *Leibniz*, that the monad passes from the stage of evolution into that of involution and becomes in higher acts a self-conscious 'spirit'" (Hua IV 108). The order of the stages reads oddly, since becoming a self-conscious spirit is an unfolding, and the passage may have exchanged the two words. The corpus has no Leibniz text to check them against. What matters for the reading is the claim the image supports: the I that becomes actual "is nothing put in or added from outside" (*nichts von außen Hineingesetztes oder Hinzugesetztes*). Waking is the ego's own entry into a stream that was already its stream. § 26 also sets limits on what is claimed. Whether a "dull I" is present during sleep "as another mode of the I" "could be doubted", "for it is hard to send reflective glances into the realm of darkness" (Hua IV 107). Husserl claims only the possibility of waking, and the Kantian sentence is shaped to say exactly that.

*Ideas I* had used the same sentence without the gloss. In § 57 the pure ego "lives itself out in a special sense in every actual cogito, but all background experiences belong to it too, and it to them". "In Kantian language: 'The "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations'" (Hua III 123). *Ideas I* keeps Kant's wording and declines to rewrite it. In *Ideen II* the "I think" has become the pure I, and the representations have become "all dark consciousness". In five years Husserl has moved from citing Kant to appropriating him, and the manuscript note "but not Kant's" shows that he was aware of the move.

The understanding returns at the end of the book in the form of a self that develops. § 58 asks whether "the sleeping I, as against the waking, is complete immersion in the I-material, in the hyle" (§58.5, Hua IV 253). § 64 makes waking the point at which the ego's individuality begins to be its own history. The sentence of § 26 gives the minimum on which both rest. Every consciousness, however dull, is a consciousness in which an ego can wake, and in that sense every consciousness is mine before I am awake in it.

## §29, Hua IV 112: positions taken as foundations

Reads [§29.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §29.2, first and second paragraphs under § 29.

Each new position the ego takes, a judgment, a joy, a resolve, "institutes" a lasting opinion or theme. From then on the ego holds to it as its own. It can change the position only if the motives change, because the same ego with the same motives cannot take a different position. The identity of the pure ego therefore includes a consistency of its positions. The pure ego is not only the identical pole of § 22. It has possessions, and these are what the later Husserl calls habitualities.

> … I am also in this, and a priori, the same I, insofar as I necessarily exercise consistency in my position-takings in a certain sense; every "new" position-taking institutes a lasting "opinion", or a theme (a theme of experience, of judgment, of joy, of will), so that from now on, as often as I grasp myself as the same as I was before, or as the same who is now and was before, I also hold fast to my themes, take them over as actual themes just as I posited them before. … If I am the same as I am, the position-taking cannot but "remain", and I remain with it; I can carry out a change only by the motives becoming different.

German:

> ich bin auch darin und a priori das selbe Ich, sofern ich in meinen Stellungnahmen notwendig Konsequenz übe in einem bestimmten Sinn; jede „neue“ Stellungnahme stiftet eine bleibende „Meinung“, bzw. ein Thema (ein Erfahrungsthema, ein Urtheilsthema, ein Freudenthema, ein Willensthema), so daß ich von nun ab, so oft ich mich als denselben erfasse, der ich früher war, oder als denselben, der jetzt ist und früher war, auch meine Themata festhalte, sie als aktuelle Thematag übernehme, so wie ich sie früher gesetzt habe.
>
> Bin ich derselbe, der ich bin, so kann die Stellungnahme nicht anders als „bleiben“ und ich bei ihr bleiben, ich kann eine Änderung nur dadurch vollziehen, daß die Motive andere werden.

*Note on the text.* The passage begins in mid-sentence because the file has lost p. 111. At :2197 the end of § 28 breaks off at "bloße intentionale Ein" and the text resumes in § 29 with "fassen kann, vielmehr: ich bin auch darin". The heading of § 29, its opening, and the sentence in which the commentary (§29.1) finds that "the habitus here belongs not to the empirical but to the pure ego" are in the lost text. This reading does not rely on them. *Thematag* is a misprint for *Themata*.

The decisive verb is *stiftet* [institutes, founds], and the apparatus shows it is Husserl's late correction. Landgrebe's typescript read *fixiert* [fixes]. Husserl replaced it with *stiftet* (apparatus to 112,4). The difference is the difference between a record and an act of founding. To fix an opinion is to hold a result in place. To institute one is to found something that then lasts and has its own life. The same family of words recurs a few pages later: convictions "can cease and possibly be newly instituted" (*neu gestiftet*, Hua IV 117), and on that page Husserl added "als Urstiftung" [as primal institution] in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 117,35). The word *Urstiftung* [primal institution] returns in § 60 c for thoughts taken over from others: a thought adopted from another person "keeps the character of the taking-over of what came from another I, of something that has its primal institution in him" (Hua IV 269, :4169). The vocabulary of founding comes into the account of the ego's position-takings in the late revision, and it holds the book together from § 29 to § 60.

The scare quotes mark what is new. "Every 'new' position-taking" (*jede „neue“ Stellungnahme*) puts "new" in quotation marks because what matters is the first taking, not a repetition. "A lasting 'opinion'" (*eine bleibende „Meinung“*) puts the noun in quotation marks because *Meinung* here is not a passing view but a held thesis. The list in parentheses extends the opinion to every kind of act: themes "of experience, of judgment, of joy, of will". The result is temporal: "from now on" (*von nun ab*), "as often as I grasp myself as the same as I was before". Identity across time is identity of what I hold.

The second paragraph states a necessity, and it is weaker than it sounds. "If I am the same as I am, the position-taking cannot but 'remain'." The conditional is tautologous if "the same" already includes the same positions, and Husserl's next clause shows that it does not: "I can carry out a change only by the motives becoming different." The sameness of the ego is tied to the sameness of its motives. A few lines later he grants that the ego can become "'unfaithful'" to a position only by "becoming another", insofar as it is subject to other motivations, and concludes: "In truth I am not unfaithful to myself; I am always the same, but in the changing stream of experiences in which ever new motives constitute themselves" (§29.2). Consistency here is not a moral demand. It is a law of motivation. The word *Motiv* enters the pure ego's chapter here, and it will become the law of the spiritual world in § 56.

The late Husserl judged the section inadequate. At 111,20ff. he noted in Landgrebe's typescript: "For this a new elaboration according to the better later manuscripts is necessary" (*Dafür eine neue Ausarbeitung nach den besseren späteren Manuskripten nötig*, apparatus to 111,20ff.). The better manuscripts are not in *Ideen II*, but the corpus has the doctrine in its developed form. The *Cartesian Meditations* describe the ego as "substrate of habitualities" in the vocabulary of § 29. "If I decide for the first time in an act of judgment for a being and being-so, this fleeting act passes, but now I am and remain the I thus decided, I am of the relevant conviction." "As long as it is valid for me I can come back to it repeatedly and find it again and again as mine, habitually my own." "*I stand by my deed*" (Hua I 100–101). The theme "held fast" in § 29 is the "abiding habitus" of the Meditations. What the Meditations add is the explicit name and the claim that this substrate is what "transcendental genesis" builds.

In *Ideen II* the understanding is completed in § 64. There the pure ego of § 22, "not an empty pole but bearer of its habituality", "has its individual history" (Hua IV 300, :4656), and "the same spirit cannot be twice, cannot even return to the same total state" (Hua IV 301, :4673). The positions instituted in § 29 are what give a pure ego a history. A thing returns to the same state under the same circumstances. An ego that has taken a position cannot return to the state before it took it.

## §30, Hua IV 123: the soul as a unity of capacities

Reads [§30.5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), fifth paragraph under § 30.

The soul, taken as a reality in the naturalistic attitude, is the unity of its properties in the same sense as the thing is. In its states it "behaves", in its properties it "is", and each property is "a mere ray of its being". Those properties are capacities: the soul is "the unity of the 'spiritual capacities'", built on the sensuous ones, "and nothing more". From this Husserl draws a defence of the old faculty psychology. A science of the soul has to be a doctrine of capacities. The old psychology's failure was one of method: it neglected the states in which capacities attest themselves.

> In this, like the thing itself, the soul itself is nothing further than the unity of its properties; in its states it "behaves" thus and so, in its properties it "is", and each of its properties is a mere ray of its being. We can also express this as follows: the soul is the unity of the "spiritual capacities" built on the lower, sensuous ones (and themselves again stratified in their own way), and it is nothing further.
>
> If the old psychology was a doctrine of capacities, it was what it alone could and had to be as a doctrine of the soul. If it erred, this was not due to the allegedly "wrong faculty psychology" but to its failing in method, that is, not developing the method that was prescribed to it as a doctrine of the soul, as a rightly understood doctrine of capacities. It erred, generally speaking, in particular in neglecting the systematic study of the psychic states, and so at the lowest level of the "states of consciousness", or taking it too lightly, whereas these, as the material of attestation of everything psychic, would have needed the most thorough study.

German:

> Dabei ist wie das Ding selbst so die Seele selbst nichts weiter als die Einheit ihrer Eigenschaften; in ihren Zuständen „verhält“ sie sich so und so, in ihren Eigenschaften „ist“ sie, und jede ihrer Eigenschaften ist ein bloßer Strahl ihres Seins. Wir können dies auch so ausdrücken: die Seele ist die Einheit der auf den niederen sinnlichen aufgebauten (und selbst wieder in ihrer Art sich aufstufenden) „geistigen Vermögen“, und sie ist nichts weiter.
>
> Wenn die alte Psychologie Vermögenslehre war, so war sie das, was sie eben als Seelenlehre einzig und allein sein konnte und sein mußte. Wenn sie fehlte, so lag das nicht an der angeblich „verkehrten Vermögenspsychologie“, sondern daran, daß sie methodisch versagte, d.i. nicht die Methode ausbildete, die ihr als Seelenlehre, als recht verstandene Vermögenslehre vorgezeichnet war. Sie fehlte, allgemein gesprochen, insbesondere darin, daß sie das systematische Studium der seelischen Zustände, also zu unterst der „Bewußtseinszustände“ unterließ oder gar zu leicht nahm, während diese doch das das Material der Beurkundung alles Seelischen des allergründlichsten Studiums bedurft hätten

*Note on the text.* The last sentence has a doubled "das das", and the footnote marker that ends it is omitted.

The first paragraph builds the soul from the vocabulary of the thing. "Like the thing itself, so the soul itself" (*wie das Ding selbst so die Seele selbst*) states the analogy as an equation, and the two verbs in quotation marks divide it between state and property: in its states the soul "behaves" (*„verhält“*), in its properties it "is" (*„ist“*). The image of the "ray" is taken from § 15 e, where real properties are "the mere rays of its unitary being" (§15 e.1). In § 22 the ray went out from the ego to its objects. Here it goes out from a being to its properties. The same image now serves the reality of the soul, and the pure ego of the previous chapter, which had no properties, is left behind. "Nothing further" (*nichts weiter*) is said twice. The soul has no core behind its capacities, as the thing has none behind its properties.

The second paragraph turns the definition into a historical judgment, and its tone is provocative. The old psychology "was what it alone could and had to be" (*einzig und allein sein konnte und sein mußte*). The doubled modal verb is meant to provoke. The slogan of its critics is put in quotation marks with a distancing adverb: "the allegedly 'wrong faculty psychology'" (*der angeblich „verkehrten Vermögenspsychologie“*). Husserl does not name the critics. The phrase marks the received verdict as received. The diagnosis then moves the error from doctrine to method. The old psychology lacked "the method … prescribed to it as a … rightly understood doctrine of capacities". It neglected "the psychic states", "at the lowest level the 'states of consciousness'", which are "the material of attestation (*Beurkundung*) of everything psychic". *Beurkundung* is the documentary word of § 15 d (row 7). Capacities are attested in states as material properties are attested in schemata. A science of capacities therefore has to be a science of the states that attest them, and only phenomenology studies those.

A footnote extends the criticism in a direction the main text does not take: the old psychology "erred in another respect too", since "it did not yet know how to separate a natural-scientific and a human-scientific psychology", and was "more human-scientifically interested", whereas "of modern psychology the reverse might hold" (Hua IV 123 n., :2427). This is the first appearance of the division of attitudes in the book, as a remark about the history of psychology. The faculty psychology Husserl defends was, on this note, a psychology of persons that had not yet learned to say so.

In the chapter the passage is the high point of the analogy between soul and thing, and § 32 marks its limit at once. "The soul, by contrast, does not schematize itself" (row 18), so that its capacities are not attested in the way a thing's properties are, and "of causality … in the case of the soul one cannot speak at all" (Hua IV 132). The analogy of § 30 is then explicitly an analogy of "ontological form" (§ 31). It does not hold of the mode of givenness. The doctrine of capacities survives the correction because it does not depend on schemata.

It returns in Section III under a different description. "The ego as unity is a system of 'I *can*'" (Hua IV 253, :3871). The person is "an organism of capacities" (§59.4), whose capacities are "positive potentialities" that the person's own activity "transforms, enriches, strengthens or weakens" (§59.4). The difference from § 30 is the difference of attitudes. In § 30 capacities are properties of a reality attested in its states. In § 59 they are powers that the person exercises and knows from within. The defence of faculty psychology in § 30 thus anticipates the personalistic account, and its footnote names the reason: a doctrine of capacities was always a doctrine of persons. What § 30 contributes is the thesis that the soul is nothing but its capacities. The book keeps the thesis and changes what it means to have a capacity.

## §32, Hua IV 127–132: the soul does not schematize itself

Reads [§32.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §32.4, third and fourth paragraphs under § 32.

The material thing constitutes itself in schemata: sensuously filled extension, given in adumbrations, whose changes depend on circumstances. The soul has no such schema. Its states are not appearances of it in the way a schema is an appearance of the thing. The consequences follow from the definitions of §§ 15–17. If substance is taken from the mathematical thing, there is no soul-substance. If causality is the dependence that constitutes persisting properties of a reality of the type "nature", there is no causality of the soul. A regulated dependence is not yet causality.

> The soul, by contrast, does not schematize itself. More exactly: for the material, the manifold that functions as state is of the type schema (sensuously filled bodily extension).
>
> If we hold the soul up against this, and take (as Kant did) the idea of *substance* from the mathematical thing, then we must undoubtedly say there is no soul-substance: the soul has no "in itself" like "nature", neither a mathematical nature like the thing of physics nor a nature like the thing of intuition (since it is no schematized unity). And as regards causality, we must say: if we call *causality* that functional or lawful relation of dependence which is the correlate of the constitution of persisting properties of a persisting real of the type nature, then of *causality* in the case of the *soul* we cannot *speak at all*. Not every lawfully regulated functionality in the sphere of facts is causality.

German:

> Die Seele dagegen schematisiert sich nicht. Genauer gesprochen: für das Materielle ist das Mannigfaltige, das als Zustand fungiert, vom Typus Schema (sinnlich erfüllte körperliche Ausdehnung).
>
> Halten wir dem gegenüber die Seele, und entnehmen wir (wie Kant es getan hat) die Idee der *Substanz* dem mathematischen Ding, so müssen wir zweifellos sagen, es gibt keine Seelensubstanz: die Seele hat kein „An sich“ wie die „Natur“, weder eine mathematische Natur wie das Ding der Physik noch eine Natur wie das Ding der Anschauung (da sie keine schematisierte Einheit ist). Und was die Kausalität anlangt, so ist zu sagen: nennen wir *Kausalität* dasjenige funktionelle oder gesetzliche Abhängigkeitsverhältnis, das Korrelat der Konstitution verharrender Eigenschaften eines verharrenden Realen vom Typus Natur ist, so ist bei der *Seele von Kausalität* *überhaupt nicht* zu reden. Nicht jede gesetzlich geregelte Funktionalität in der Sphäre der Tatsachen ist Kausalität.

The thesis is a reflexive verb. *Die Seele … schematisiert sich nicht* does not say that the soul has no schema. It says that the soul does not present itself in schemata. The reflexive is the grammar of constitution. A thing "constitutes itself" as a unity of schemata (Hua IV 127), and the soul does not constitute itself in that way. The next sentence makes the claim exact. For the material, "the manifold that functions as state is of the type schema". The soul has states, but its states are not of that type: they are not sensuously filled extension, and they are not given in adumbrations. The analogy of § 30 (row 17) breaks at the level of givenness.

The second paragraph draws two conditional consequences, and the conditionals carry the argument. "If … we take (as Kant did) the idea of substance from the mathematical thing", then "there is no soul-substance". "If we call causality" the dependence that constitutes persisting properties of a real "of the type nature", then "of causality in the case of the soul we cannot speak at all". Both negations are relative to definitions. The soul is not denied substance or causality as such. It is denied substance in the sense of the thing and causality in the sense of nature. The "in itself" in quotation marks (*kein „An sich“ wie die „Natur“*) and "nature" in quotation marks mark the borrowed concept that the soul fails to meet. The last sentence states the residue in general terms: "Not every lawfully regulated functionality in the sphere of facts is causality." The soul is regulated, but its regularity is not causal.

The name in parentheses is the polemical target, and Kant is named as an ally of a thesis Husserl will put to his own use. In the *Critique*'s Paralogisms, the inference to the persistence of the soul fails because "in inner intuition we have nothing persistent at all, for the I is only the consciousness of my thinking" (*Nun haben wir aber in der inneren Anschauung gar nichts Beharrliches, denn das Ich ist nur das Bewußtsein meines Denkens*, *Kritik der reinen Vernunft*, B, Paralogisms). Husserl agrees with the result: there is no soul-substance on the model of the persisting thing. He gives a different reason. Kant lacks a persisting intuition of the soul, while Husserl finds that the soul's states are not schemata. And the condition "if we take the idea of substance from the mathematical thing" leaves open another idea of substance, which § 31 has just supplied: "substantial reality" in a "formal-general" sense, under which material and psychic reality both fall (Hua IV 126). Kant's conclusion is accepted for one concept of substance and refused for another.

The surrounding argument then gives the soul its own kind of regularity. "The flow of psychic life has its unity in itself" (Hua IV 132). The soul "cannot persist in an unchanged psychic state", since "psychic life is by essential necessity a flux" (§32.5), and "every experience leaves dispositions behind". § 33 states the conclusion that follows: psychic realities "have a history" (row 19). Where the thing's regularity is that of circumstances and consequences, the soul's is that of a past that determines its present. That is what "not every regulated functionality is causality" leaves room for.

The soul's lack of a schema governs the book from here on. In Section III the regularity that is not causality gets its name, motivation, and the sentence of § 32 is echoed in § 56 f: "The 'because–so' of motivation has an entirely different sense from causation in the sense of nature" (Hua IV 229, :3626). And § 62 gives the general rule: "Causality is the relation of a reality to its correlative circumstance-realities", and the reality of spirit relates to circumstances "that consist in the 'surrounding world' and in other spirits" (Hua IV 283, :4367). The soul that "does not schematize itself" in § 32 is the first appearance of a reality whose dependencies are not those of nature. Section III shows that the dependencies are those of a person on its world.

## §33, Hua IV 136–37: realities without history

Reads [§33.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 33.

Material things are conditioned only from outside, never by their own past. Under the same external circumstances a thing would always be in the same total state, and in that sense it is "historyless". A psychic reality cannot in principle return to the same total state, because its earlier states determine its later ones. Psychic realities "have a history". The difference does not lie in the external circumstances, which could recur identically. It lies in the role of the past, which belongs to the soul's own states.

> Here the remarkable thing shows itself, that material things are conditioned exclusively from outside and are not conditioned by their own past; they are *historyless realities*. This follows from the fact (and this at the same time clearly determines the sense of the discussion) that to the essence of material reality belongs the possibility of material identity in all change of alterations, in such a way that no increase or detachment of materiality takes place (both of which likewise belong to the essence of such reality as ideal possibility). Likewise there belongs to it the ideal possibility that in cyclical processes it returns to identically the same external circumstances under which it has already been, however improbable this may be in a given case. But material reality is so constituted that in such a cyclical return it would have to have identically the same total state. By contrast, it belongs to the essence of *psychic reality* that it can in principle not return to the same total state: psychic realities simply have a *history*. Two adjacent cycles of external circumstances would act in the same way on the same soul, but in the soul itself the psychic courses of states could not be the same, because the earlier state functionally determines the later.

German:

> Da zeigt sich nun das Merkwürdige, daß materielle Dinge ausschließlich von außen her bedingt sind und nicht bedingt sind durch ihre eigene Vergangenheit; sie sind *geschichtslose Realitäten*. Das geht daraus hervor (und das bestimmt zugleich deutlich den Sinn der Ausführung), daß zum Wesen der materiellen Realität die Möglichkeit materieller Identität bei allem Wechsel von Veränderungen gehört, und zwar derart, daß dabei kein Zuwachs oder keine Abstückung von Materialität statthat (was ja beides ebenfalls zum Wesen solcher Realität als ideale Möglichkeit gehört). Desgleichen gehört zu ihr die ideale Möglichkeit, daß sie in zyklischen Prozessen in identisch dieselben äußeren Umstände zurückkehrt, unter denen sie schon gewesen ist, mag dergleichen auch gegebenenfalls höchst unwahrscheinlich sein. So geartet ist aber materielle Realität, daß sie bei solcher zyklischen Rückkehr identisch denselben Gesamtzustand haben müßte. Demgegenüber gehört es zum Wesen *seelischer* *Realität*, daß sie prinzipiell in denselben Gesamtzustand nicht zurückkehren kann: seelische Realitäten haben eben eine *Geschichte*. Zwei aneinander grenzende Zyklen äußerer Umstände würden in gleicher Weise auf dieselbe Seele wirken, aber in der Seele selbst könnten die seelischen Abläufe der Zustände nicht dieselben sein, weil der frühere Zustand den späteren funktionell bestimmt.

The two emphasized formulas frame the paragraph: *geschichtslose Realitäten* [historyless realities] and *Geschichte* [history]. "Historyless" is a privative coinage. It defines the thing by what the soul has and it lacks. The adverbs make the contrast exact: things are conditioned "exclusively from outside" (*ausschließlich von außen her*) and "not … by their own past". The proof is a thought experiment about recurrence, introduced with a parenthetical warning that it "at the same time clearly determines the sense of the discussion". A thing "returns" in cyclical processes "to identically the same external circumstances", and it "would have to have identically the same total state". The modal is strong: *müßte* [would have to]. It follows from the definition of materiality in § 15 c (row 7) as a function of circumstances. The soul "can in principle not return" (*prinzipiell … nicht zurückkehren kann*). The particle *eben* in the next clause, "psychic realities simply have a history", presents the conclusion as the plain statement of what was just shown.

The last sentence contains the passage's weak point, and Husserl seems to have noticed it. "Two adjacent cycles of external circumstances would act in the same way on the same soul" (*würden in gleicher Weise auf dieselbe Seele wirken*). *Wirken* [to act, to effect] is causal vocabulary, five pages after § 32 denied that "causality" applies to the soul (row 18). The circumstances act on the soul "in the same way", and the result differs "because the earlier state functionally determines the later". Husserl relies on the distinction he has just drawn between causality and "lawfully regulated functionality". The dependence of the later state on the earlier is functional, not causal. But the action of the circumstances is described with the causal verb, and the sentence does not say what it is for the same external causes to act "in the same way" on what is not the same. The apparatus records a deletion in the neighbouring line: Husserl put "…. zurückkehren kann bei Identität derselben äußeren Umstände" [can return … with identity of the same external circumstances] into deletion brackets in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 137,22). He struck the clause that tied the return to identical external circumstances.

The section draws the consequence for the concept of reality in the next paragraphs. Realities seem to divide into "mere nature-realities", "supra-natural realities" with no nature-side at all, and "mixed realities" like the soul, which has a nature-side and an "idiopsychic" side (§33.2). The soul is then "nature" only through its "attachment" to the body: it has "a quasi-nature and a quasi-causality" (§33.3). The paragraph ends by moving the opposition: what stands against material nature "is not the 'soul' but the concrete unity of body and soul, the human … subject" (§33.3). History is what makes the soul resist naturalization. The body is what still ties it to nature.

The understanding is taken up three times. In § 60 b the person says: "I am not a thing that under the same circumstances…" (*Aber ich bin eben nicht ein Ding, das unter gleichen Umständen…*, Hua IV 266, :4126). The commentary continues: "earlier I was motivated thus, now otherwise, precisely because in the meantime I have become another". The historylessness of the thing is now the person's own contrast term. In § 64 the thought experiment of recurrence returns in its strictest form: "The same spirit cannot be twice, cannot even return to the same total state and show its individuation merely in standing with the same content in different contexts" (Hua IV 301, :4673). The words of § 33, "return to the same total state" (*in denselben Gesamtzustand zurückkehren*), are repeated. The claim that in § 33 distinguished psychic from material reality becomes in § 64 the ground of the absolute individuation of spirit. And § 64 turns the thing's recurrence against it: "No thing has its individuality in itself" (Hua IV 299, :4647). A reality that can return to the same state has nothing in itself to individuate it. A reality that cannot return has its individuation in its own history.

## §34, Hua IV 139–43: the naming of the second attitude

Reads [§34.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 34.

The person whom we experience together with a human body seems to contain "a plus" that the naturalistic analysis of the soul does not capture. The person finds itself dependent on things, persons and institutions, and this dependence is not psychophysical. Husserl concludes that two essentially different modes of apprehension must be recognized, and he names them: the "psychological" and the "human-scientific (personal)". The ego apprehended in the first is the psychic ego. The ego apprehended in the second is the personal ego, the "spiritual individual". This is the book's first naming of the distinction on which Section III is built, and the section's title in the file names it differently.

> Terminologically we distinguish *psychological apprehension* and *experience* from *human-scientific* (personal) *apprehension*, or experience. The I apprehended as "psychic" is the psychic one; the I apprehended human-scientifically is the personal I or the spiritual individual.

German:

> Terminologisch scheiden wir *psychologische Auffassung* und *Erfahrung* von der *geisteswissenschaftlichen* (personalen) *Auffassung*, bzw. Erfahrung. Das Ich als „psychisch“ aufgefaßtes ist das seelische, das geisteswissenschaftlich aufgefaßte das personale Ich oder das geistige Individuum.

*Note on the text.* The section title in the file reads "Notwendigkeit der Unterscheidung von naturalistischer und humanistischer Einstellung" [necessity of distinguishing naturalistic and humanistic attitude] (:2709). The file's own editorial note says that the printed column title and heading read "naturalistischer und *ologistischer*", a misprint for *personalistischer*, and that the paragraph title is "naturalistischer und personalistischer" (:2715). The contents page also has *personalistischer* (:175). Nothing in the file supports "humanistischer", which appears to be the converter's attempt to mend the misprint. The commentary's heading ("Personalistic") is right. The backfill's correction on this point is withdrawn (see the list). Elsewhere in the section, "Auffassungserkenners" (:2774) is garbled. It seems to stand for something like *zwei Auffassungs[weisen] anerkennen* [recognize two modes of apprehension], and the reading does not quote it.

The title promises "attitudes" (*Einstellung*), and the sentence that names the distinction speaks of "apprehension" (*Auffassung*) and "experience" (*Erfahrung*). The word *Einstellung* does not occur in the definition at all. The section's argument is conducted in terms of apprehension: a "plus" in "the apprehension in which the human being is given to us in the human body" (Hua IV 140), "two modes of apprehension" that must be recognized "as essentially different" (Hua IV 142), "one and the same state of consciousness" that "stands under a totally different apperception" (Hua IV 142). The terminology of attitudes, which Section III uses throughout, is not yet the section's own. In the text of § 34 the difference is a difference between two ways of apprehending one subject.

The pair of names is asymmetrical, and the parenthesis shows it. The first term is a discipline, "psychological". The second is also a discipline, "human-scientific" (*geisteswissenschaftlich*), but it is glossed in parentheses with a different kind of word: "(personal)". The gloss names the object rather than the science, and it is the gloss that survives. In Section III's title the attitude is "personalistic", and the parenthesis of § 34 is where the word for the person displaces the word for the science. The correlates are named in the second sentence: the psychic ego (*das seelische*) and "the personal I or the spiritual individual" (*das personale Ich oder das geistige Individuum*). The quotation marks around "psychic" (*„psychisch“*) and the word "apprehended" (*aufgefaßtes*) put the difference on the side of the apprehension. It is one I, apprehended twice.

The paragraph that the commentary summarizes before the definition (§34.2) makes the case in examples, and they are the first examples of motivation in the book. "This thing there directs my gaze to itself", "I choose the cloth for its beautiful colour, for the soft material", "the noise of the street 'irritates' me, I am determined to close the window", "in short … *I feel myself conditioned by things*; but that evidently does not mean psychophysically conditioned" (Hua IV 141). The emphasis in the file falls on the formula of the conditioned person. The dependence is "on the things themselves, their beautiful colour", and "not on my body or my history". A footnote to the section's first paragraph draws the theoretical consequence: "the physio-psychic dependencies do not suffice for the constitution of the psychic subject … Rather, the concept of 'external circumstances' must be extended to the objects that *motivate* the subject in its behaviour" (Hua IV 139 n.). The circumstances of § 15 c (row 7) become motives. This footnote is where the transfer happens.

The section ends without a decision, and it says so. The natural scientist will answer that "in 'objective truth' individuality dissolves" into bodies and psychic correlates, and "it will be the question whether this answer suffices" (Hua IV 142). Husserl declares only that he does not adopt it, and for now remains "in the attitude in which we think the psychic ego as constituted solely by its dependencies" (Hua IV 142). The distinction is named, and its treatment is put off: "Later we will investigate in detail the two attitudes, or apprehensions, in their relation to one another" (Hua IV 143). The pairing "attitudes, or apprehensions" (*Einstellungen, bzw. Auffassungen*) in that announcement is the bridge to Section III's vocabulary.

Section III takes up the promise in its first chapter title: "The *personalistic attitude* in contrast to the naturalistic" (§ 49, :3178, emphasis in the file). There the second attitude is named by its object, and the naming is followed by the reversal the commentary calls decisive: the personalistic attitude is "a thoroughly natural and not an artificial attitude" (Hua IV 183, :3244), and the naturalistic attitude "subordinates itself" to it (Hua IV 184, :3248). In § 34 the two apprehensions stand side by side, and the human-scientific one is still described from the naturalistic side, as "a plus" over the soul. By § 49 the person is no longer a plus. It is the subject in whose surrounding world the naturalistic attitude is one possible stance. The terminological history traced here, from "human-scientific (personal) apprehension" to "personalistic attitude", is the history of that reversal. The disputed title is a symptom of it. Even in print, the section that first names the second attitude does not have a settled name for it.

## §36, Hua IV 144–46: sensings

Reads [§36.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §36.4, second and fourth paragraphs under § 36.

The body is the one thing on which I feel sensations as its own occurrences: warmth on the back of the hand, pressure in the fingertip, heaviness in the lifting arm. Husserl coins a word for these, *Empfindnisse* [sensings]. They are not properties of the body as a physical thing, since a lead ball or a table has nothing like them. They are properties of the thing "body", and properties of a particular kind: effects. They arise where and when the body is touched. The same pressure-sensation can be taken as the table's smoothness or as a sensing in the finger, depending on the direction of attention. Sensings are what make a physical thing a body.

> And so my body in general, entering into physical relation with other material things (blow, pressure, impact and so on), offers not merely the experience of physical occurrences, related to the body and the things, but also specific occurrences of the body of the kind we call *sensings*. Such occurrences are lacking in "merely" material things.
>
> The localized sensings are not properties of the body as a physical thing, but on the other hand they are properties of the thing body, namely effect-properties. They arise when the body is touched, pressed, pricked and so on, and they arise where it is, and at the time *when* it is; only under certain circumstances do they last long after the touching.

German:

> Und so bietet überhaupt mein Leib, mit anderen materiellen Dingem in physische Beziehung tretend (Schlag, Druck, Stoß usw.) nicht bloß die Erfahrung physischer Vorkommnisse dar, auf den Leib und die Dinge bezogen, sondern auch spezifische Leibesvorkommnisse der Art, die wir *Empfindnisse* nennen. Solche Vorkommnisse fehlen den ,,bloß" materiellen Dingem.
>
> Die lokalisierten Empfindnungen sind nicht Eigenschaften des Leibes als physischen Dinges, aber andererseits sind sie Eigenschaften des Dinges Leib, und zwar Wirkungskeigenschaften. Sie treten auf, wenn der Leib berührt, gedrückt, gestochen etc. wird, und treten da auf, wo er es wird und in der Zeit, *wann* er es wird; sie dauern nur unter Umständen noch lange nach der Berührung fort.

*Note on the text.* The file is damaged at the heart of § 36. At :2801 the sentence "Die anzeigenden Bewegungsempfindungen und die repräsen-" breaks off in mid-word and the text goes on with "sation hat. Die Hand liegt auf dem Tisch". The running head for p. 145 is missing. The analysis of one hand touching the other, in which the touched hand "senses" (commentary §36.2), is lost with it. This reading uses only what the file has. *Dingem* (twice) is a misprint for *Dingen*, *Empfindnungen* for *Empfindnisse*, and *Wirkungskeigenschaften* for *Wirkungseigenschaften*.

The word is formed by suffix. *Empfindung* [sensation] names the sensing and also what is sensed. *Empfindnis* is built with the suffix *-nis*, which forms nouns for results and states: *Ereignis* [event], *Erlebnis* [lived experience], *Gedächtnis* [memory]. The new noun names something that happens in the body and stays there, an occurrence "of the body" (*Leibesvorkommnis*). The verb *nennen* [to call] marks the coinage, and the italics in the file mark the term. The file itself is unsteady between the old word and the new. The second paragraph's *Empfindnungen* is a misprint that falls between the two forms, and the passage's argument depends on keeping them apart. A sensation in the old sense can present a property of the table. A sensing does not present anything. It is a state of the body.

The definition is built from the vocabulary of § 15 c (row 7). Sensings are "effect-properties" (*Wirkungseigenschaften*): they "arise" when the body is "touched, pressed, pricked", "where" and "when" it is. That is the vocabulary of the real thing, whose properties are unities of dependence on circumstances. The thing "body" thus has a stratum of properties that depend on circumstances as the elasticity of a spring does. They are not physical properties, because "a lead ball" has nothing like them (§36.2). The scare quotes on "'merely' material things" (*den ,,bloß" materiellen*) mark the contrast. Merely material things are those that lack this stratum. The body is not less material. It is material and something more.

The paragraph after the passage turns the definition into a description of one experience, and the description shows that the stratum is a matter of apprehension. Touching a paperweight, "I then experience actually the smooth glass surface". If I attend to the finger, "it has touch-sensations, which still linger when the hand is removed". "The very same sensations that function as indicating or presenting with respect to the thing paperweight function as touch-effects of the paperweight on the hand and as sensings produced in it." The pressure of the hand on the table is apprehended "once as perception of the table surface", and "with 'another direction of attention', in actualization of another stratum of apprehension", as "finger-pressure sensations" (Hua IV 147). One sensation, two apprehensions. A sensing is a sensation apprehended as a state of the body instead of as a feature of the thing. Where hand touches hand, "we have two sensations, and each can be apprehended, or experienced, in a double way" (Hua IV 147). The commentary's reading of the lost passage is the same structure seen from the touching hand.

The understanding is carried through §§ 37–40. § 37 measures vision against touch (row 22) and states the main contrast of the chapter: "*Localization of sensings* is in fact something *principally different* from *extension of all material thing-determinations*". Sensings "spread out" (*Ausbreitung*, *Hinbreitung*), but "for the sensing it makes no sense at all" to speak of schema and adumbration (Hua IV 149, :2825). § 40 draws the consequence for the reality of the body: "The sensitivity of the body thus constitutes itself throughout as a 'conditional' or psychophysical property", and in perception the body stands there as "*a physical-aesthesiological unity*", whose two strata can be separated "only abstractively" (Hua IV 155–56). The coinage of § 36 is the base of the "aesthesiological" stratum, which Section III treats as the lower stratum of the will-body (§ 62, row 48).

*Ideen III* uses the word in the plan of a science. There somatology, the science of the body, "is better a doctrine of the sensings of the body" (*die besser Lehre von dem Empfindnissen des Leibes ist*), and what it investigates is "the stratum of real properties of sensitivities belonging to the body as such, announcing itself in the original sensings and generally in the sense-fields as states of the soma" (*sich bekundend in den ursprünglichen Empfindnissen*, Hua V 9). The coinage of *Ideen II* has become the definition of a discipline. A science of the body as body, and not as physical thing, would be a science of sensings.

## §37, Hua IV 148: seen seeing, touched touching

Reads [§37.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 37.

Touch and vision differ in how they constitute the body. When one hand touches the other, the touched hand is felt as touching. The touch-sensation is apprehended both as a feature of the external object and as a sensing in the body. Nothing like this happens in vision. I do not see my eye seeing, and colours are not localized as sensings in a seen eye. The body therefore constitutes itself as body originally only through touch and what is localized with it. The eye belongs to the body because it can be touched, not because it sees.

> I do not see myself, my body, as I touch myself. What I call the seen body is not something seen that sees, as my body as touched body is something touched that touches.

German:

> Ich sehe mich selbst, meinen Leib, nicht, wie ich mich selbst taste. Das, was ich gesehenen Leib nenne, ist nicht gesehenes Sehendes, wie mein Leib als getasteter Leib getastetes Tastendes ist

*Note on the text.* A footnote marker ends the sentence in the file and is omitted. The note it refers to deals with sounds in the ear (§37.2–3). In the same paragraph, "Im aktuellen Gebiet" is probably *Im taktuellen Gebiet* [in the tactual domain], and "‚ußeren'" is a misprint for "äußeren".

The sentence is built as a chiasm. On one side, *gesehenes Sehendes* [something seen that sees]. On the other, *getastetes Tastendes* [something touched that touches]. Each phrase pairs a passive participle with an active one from the same verb. The touched hand is touched and touching at once. The seen body is seen and not seeing. The construction states the argument in one grammatical form. Only for touch can the same verb be both passive and active on the same object at the same time. The first sentence prepares this with a reflexive comparison: "I do not see myself … as I touch myself" (*wie ich mich selbst taste*). The reflexive *mich selbst* is used for both verbs, and the comparison denies that it means the same with both.

The sentences before the passage give the description that the chiasm sums up. In the tactual domain "we have the tactually constituting external object and the second object, body", and "we have, moreover, finger touching finger". "Here, then, there is that double apprehension: the same touch-sensation, apprehended as a feature of the 'external' object and apprehended as a sensation of the body-object" (Hua IV 148). Then the contrast: "We have nothing similar with the purely visually constituting object." People say "the eye, glancing over the object, as it were touches it", "but we notice the difference at once" (Hua IV 148). The common figure of speech for vision is tactile, and Husserl refuses its literal sense. "We have no spread-out eye-ness" (*keine ausgebreitete Augenhaftigkeit*), so that "eye could pass along eye" and "the phenomenon of double sensation could arise". The coinage *Augenhaftigkeit* names the missing surface by analogy with the skin.

The conclusion follows in the next sentences, and it is guarded. "A visual appearance of an object that sees, i.e. in which the light-sensation is intuited as being in it, is lacking. Lacking, then, is the analogue of the touch-sensation, which is really grasped with the touching hand." "The role of visual sensations in the correlative constitution of body and external things is therefore a different one". "If in the end the eye as organ, and with it the visual sensations, are assigned to the body, this happens by an indirect route, by means of the properly localized sensations" (Hua IV 148). On the next page: "The eye is a touch-object for the hand; it belongs originally to the merely touched and not seen objects", and "originally" here "means nothing temporal-causal" but "a primal group of objects that constitute themselves directly intuitively" (Hua IV 149). The eye is part of my body as a touched thing.

The apparatus shows that the late Husserl found the result too narrow. At § 39 he noted in Landgrebe's typescript: "But that is too much oriented by the touch-sensations. The visual data are also not directly localized, and still less the visual appearances of the things. Shape this more carefully here, rework it" (apparatus to 153,22ff.). The asymmetry of § 37 stands, but the late Husserl doubts the conclusion that touch-sensations alone provide the model of the body's relation to its sensations. The chapter as a whole was placed in question too. At its heading Husserl directed in Landgrebe's typescript that pp. 143–162, this chapter, should come before pp. 55–142, the analysis of the aesthetic body in Section I (apparatus to 143, chapter heading). The body that § 37 constitutes through touch was meant to come before the analysis of the thing.

The understanding is used in two later places. § 46 needs the asymmetry for the other's body. I see another's body, but I do not see it as touching, any more than I see my own eye as seeing, so the other's sensings can only be appresented. "Only in the manner of appresence can I have his appearances, and his 'Here' to which they are related, co-given with his body" (Hua IV 169, row 24 and 25). In Section III the solipsistically constituted body, "the body that I see", becomes "my counterpart" in the *inspectio sui*, "mine", but "not a component of the I" (Hua IV 212, :3471). What the chiasm of § 37 shows is that vision alone would give me a body only as an object among objects. Touch gives me a body that is mine because it feels, and the late notes ask only whether touch is the whole of that.

## §42, Hua IV 160–61: the conversion point

Reads [§42.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 42.

Seen from outside, the solipsistically constituted body is a material thing in causal relations with other things. It is also the "conversion point" at which those causal relations turn into conditional ones, between the external world and the bodily-psychic subject. A blow is a physical event. The pain it causes is a conditional consequence for a subject. The body is the point where the first becomes the second. Husserl puts the word in quotation marks, and it names the body's place between the two orders.

> 2.) Seen from outside — in the "outer attitude" — it stands there as a reality of its own kind: namely, first, as a material thing of special modes of appearance, "switched in" between the rest of the material world and the "subjective" sphere (the subject together with what was mentioned under 1.); as centre around which the rest of the spatial world groups itself; as standing in causal relations to the real external world, but at the same time as "conversion point", in which the causal relations are converted into conditional ones between external world and bodily-psychic subject, and by virtue of this as belonging to this subject and its specifically bodily properties and the psychic properties connected with them. What is constituted in the outer attitude and what is constituted in the inner attitude are there together: compresent.

German:

> 2.) Von außen betrachtet — in der „Außeneinstellung“ — steht er da als eine Realität eigener Art; nämlich einmal als ein materielles Ding von besonderen Erscheinungsweisen, das „eingeschaltet“ ist zwischen die übrige materielle Welt und die „subjektive“ Sphäre (das Subjekt samt dem unter 1. Erwähnten): als Zentrum, um das sich die übrige Raumwelt gruppiert; als in kausalen Beziehungen zur realen Außenwelt stehend, zugleich aber als „Umschlagspunkt“, in dem die kausalen Beziehungen sich in konditionale zwischen Außenwelt und leiblich-seelischem Subjekt umsetzen, und vermöge dessen als zugehörig zu diesem Subjekt und seinen spezifisch leiblichen und den damit verbundenen seelischen Eigenschaften. Das in Außeneinstellung und das in Inneneinstellung Konstituierte ist miteinander da: kompräsent.

The section's vocabulary is spatial and electrical, and the quotation marks set it apart. The body is "'switched in'" (*„eingeschaltet“*) between the material world and the "subjective" sphere, the word for inserting an element into a circuit. It is the "centre" around which space groups itself. It is the "'conversion point'" (*„Umschlagspunkt“*). *Umschlag* is a sudden change of state, the turning over of one thing into another. The verb in the relative clause says what is converted and into what: causal relations "convert themselves into conditional ones" (*sich in konditionale … umsetzen*). The conversion has a direction. It runs from the external world toward the subject, from cause to condition. Husserl does not say that a cause becomes a sensation. He says that a causal relation becomes a conditional one: a dependence between external world and subject.

The term first appears one page earlier, at the end of § 41 c, in a negative example. When red light falls on my hand, "the physical process to which the colour-sensation is attached — that red light rays strike my eyes — is not given to me at all. The 'conversion point' located in the body between causal and conditional process is lacking" (Hua IV 160, :3008). The warming of my hand by a warm thing is followed by a warmth-sensation in the hand. The reddening of my hand is not followed by a red-sensation in the hand. In the first case the hand is the conversion point. In the second it is only a physical surface. The conversion point is located where sensings are, so the concept depends on § 36 (row 21). The body converts causality into conditionality only where it senses.

The last sentence adds a term of its own. What is constituted in the outer attitude and what is constituted in the inner attitude "are there together: compresent" (*kompräsent*). The coinage stands alone after the colon. Outer and inner apprehension of my body are not two bodies but two strata given at once. The prefix *kom-* [together] is one of three that § 44 uses at once: *Urpräsenz* [original presence], *Appräsenz* [appresence], and in § 45 *Kompräsenz* for the regular coexistence that "point[s] to one another" (Hua IV 166, :3073). The solipsistic body is the first place where Husserl needs a word for presence that is not original presence. Its inner and outer strata are present together without either being given through the other.

The understanding returns in § 62, with its direction reversed. The body "as thing in the surrounding world" is "an appearance of the physical body", and "because the body of the surrounding world is at the same time an appearance of nature in the physical sense, the body has a second face: it is the conversion point (*Umschlagstelle*) of spiritual causality into nature-causality" (Hua IV 286, :4400). In § 42 causality turns into conditionality on the way from the world to the subject. In § 62 spiritual causality turns into natural causality on the way from the subject to the world. The fiat moves the hand, and the hand moves the stone. The word changes from *-punkt* [point] to *-stelle* [place], and the converted terms change, but the body remains the single place where the two orders meet. The quotation marks of § 42 have disappeared by § 62. The term has become Husserl's own.

The two passages together give the body the position that the book's last chapter needs. Spirit and nature never act on one another directly, since their circumstances are of different kinds (§ 62, row 48). They meet only at a conversion point, and the conversion point is the living body. § 42 introduces it from the side of nature, where it converts causes into conditions for a subject. Section III adds the other side.

## §44, Hua IV 162–63: original presence and appresence

Reads [§44.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 44.

An object is given in "original presence" (*Urpräsenz*) when it stands there "in the original", and in "appresence" (*Appräsenz*) when it is only "co-presented" with something originally present. Appresence points back to original presence. The distinction sorts objects into two classes. Some can be originally present to every subject in a community: their totality is nature in the first sense. Others can be originally present to one subject only: every subject's own acts, states and inner bodily life. Other animals and human beings are realities of a new kind. Their bodies are originally present and their inner life is appresent, and it can never be anything else.

> If we call original perception the experience in which a subject has the perceived object given in original presence, then this says that the object stands there "actually", "in the original", and is not merely "co-presented". We thus have a fundamental difference between original presence and appresence. The latter points back to original presence. … The totality of possibly originally present objects, which form a domain of common original presence for all communicating subjects, is *nature in the first and original sense*. It is the spatio-temporal-material nature. The one space, the one time, the one world of things for all: the one that can be given as originally present for all.

German:

> Nennen wir ursprüngliche Wahrnehmung das Erlebnis, in dem ein Subjekt den wahrgenommenen Gegenstand in ursprünglicher Präsenz gegeben hat, dann sagt das, daß der Gegenstand ,,wirklich'', ,,im Original'' dasteht und nicht bloß ,,mitpräsentiert'' ist. Wir haben also einen fundamentalen Unterschied zwischen Urpräsenz und Appräsenz. Die letztere weist auf Urpräsenz zurück. … Die Gesamtheit der möglicherweise urpräsenten Gegenstände, die für alle kommunizierenden Subjekte einen Bereich gemeinsamer Urpräsenz bilden, ist die *Natur im ersten und ursprünglichen Sinn*. Es ist die raum-zeitlich-materielle Natur. Der eine Raum, die eine Zeit, die eine Dingwelt für alle: die eine, die für alle urpräsent gegeben sein kann.

The two terms are a pair of prefixes on one Latinate stem. *Präsenz* [presence] takes *Ur-* [original, primal] in one case and *Ap-* (from Latin *ad*, toward, in addition to) in the other. The definition proceeds from a gloss. Original presence means that the object stands there "'actually', 'in the original'", and not merely "'co-presented'" (*mitpräsentiert*). The German *mit-* [co-, with] is the plain word that *Appräsenz* Latinizes. The quotation marks around all three glosses show that Husserl is borrowing ordinary expressions for a technical distinction. The next sentence fixes the order of dependence: "The latter points back to original presence" (*Die letztere weist auf Urpräsenz zurück*). There is no appresence without an original presence that carries it.

The definition is drawn with a concession that keeps it from proving too much. "Original presence of an object does not mean original presence of all its inner or property-determinations; some suffice, as with all physical things" (Hua IV 162). A thing whose back is unseen is still originally present. Appresence is not partial givenness. It is the givenness of something that cannot in principle become originally present for the subject to whom it is appresented. That is why the classification follows. Objects that can be originally present "identically for every other subject (as soon as such are constituted)" make up nature in the first sense. Objects that can be originally present for one subject only make up "the subjective": every subject "with all its acts, states, noematic correlates", "with the bodiliness and properties, or capacities, that constitute themselves for it in the inner attitude" (Hua IV 163). The emphasized phrase *Natur im ersten und ursprünglichen Sinn* defines nature by a mode of givenness. Nature is what can be originally present to all.

The last sentence of the passage is built as a list of "the one": "The one space, the one time, the one world of things for all". The colon then reduces the list to a single phrase: "the one that can be given as originally present for all" (*die eine, die für alle urpräsent gegeben sein kann*). The repetition of *eine* [one] carries the argument. Nature is one because it is the common domain of original presence, and it is common because original presence of a thing is not tied to a single subject. The understanding is completed in § 45. Animals and human beings "are realities that cannot be given in original presence for several subjects": "they contain subjectivities". They are "peculiar objectities whose original givenness is such that they presuppose original presences, while they themselves cannot be given in original presence" (Hua IV 164, :3061). An animal's body belongs to nature in the first sense, and its inner life belongs to another subject's "subjective" sphere. For me it is appresent.

The concept returns in the personalistic analysis, now as the boundary of empathy. In § 51 (restored pages) empathy grounds "a bodily being-there-with", "so far we have experience, perception". "But this being-there-with ('appresence' in the sense given earlier) can in principle not be converted into immediate original existence (original presence)". "What is peculiar to empathy is that it points to an original body-spirit consciousness, but as one that I myself cannot originally perform" (Hua IV 198, :3341). The pair of § 44, introduced for the naturalistic constitution of animals, marks in § 51 the limit of personal understanding. The other's experiences are given to me, but never as mine.

The *Cartesian Meditations* make appresence the name for the whole experience of the other, and they explain the word as § 44 does. In "analogizing apprehension", the other is given by "appresentation", "as the very word appresentation (making-co-present-to-consciousness) indicates" (*wie es schon das Wort Appräsentation (Als-mitgegenwärtig-bewußt-machen) andeutet*, Hua I 139). The meditations also draw the distinction that § 44 draws with "some suffice". The appresentation that co-constitutes the primordial thing can be confirmed by "corresponding fulfilling presentation (the back becomes the front)", "while for the appresentation that is to lead into another original sphere this must be excluded a priori" (Hua I 139). The difference *Ideen II* draws between partial original presence and appresence becomes in 1929 a distinction between two kinds of appresentation, and the term stays.

## §46, Hua IV 167–68: legitimate naturalization

Reads [§46.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §46.2, first and second paragraphs under § 46.

In self-experience it would never occur to me to "introject" my psychic life into my body. The closed unity "human being", a body with a soul localized in it, is first constituted in the experience of others: I see their bodies move and take their souls as moving with them. Only then do I transfer this unity to myself. The soul, however, "is nowhere". It is in space only by being regularly coordinated with a body. That coordination gives it a place and a time, and that, in quotation marks, is the "legitimate 'naturalization' of consciousness".

> Moved as it were: "If something is connected with a movable thing, it is moved along with its motion, and likewise the whole formed from both is moved." But this holds only if the connection is that of a physical whole. But the soul is nowhere, and its connection with the body is grounded only by functional connections: the body is "organ" of the subject, and all appearances are related to bodiliness through the connections of sensation, etc. The "being-somewhere" of the human being has its good sense, but "being regularly coordinated and thereby localized" and "being in space oneself" are two different things.
>
> That body and soul form an experiential unity of their own, and that the psychic receives its place in space and time by virtue of this unity, is what the legitimate "naturalization" of consciousness consists in.

German:

> Gleichsam bewegt: „Wenn etwas mit einem Beweglichen verbunden ist, so ist es mit seiner Bewegung mitbewegt, und ebenso ist das aus beiden gebildete Ganze bewegt“. Das gilt aber nur, wenn die Verbindung die eines physischen Ganzen ist. Aber die Seele ist nirgends, und ihre Verbindung mit dem Leibe ist nur begründet durch funktionelle Zusammenhänge: der Leib ist „Organ“ des Subjekts und alle Erscheinungen sind durch die Empfindungszusammenhänge mit der Leiblichkeit bezogen auf diese etc. Das „Irgendwo-sein“ des Menschen hat wohl seinen guten Sinn, aber „regelmäßig zugeordnet und damit lokalisiert sein“ und „selbst im Raum sein“ ist zweierlei.
>
> Daß Leib und Seide eine eigene Erfahrungseinheit bilden und das Seelische vermöge dieser Einheit seine Stelle in Raum und Zeit erhält, darin besteht die rechtmäßige „Naturalisierung“ des Bewußtseins.

*Note on the text.* *Seide* [silk] is an OCR error for *Seele* [soul], and a few lines earlier *Sehle* stands for the same word (:3087). The quotation from Aristotle is footnoted "Aristoteles, De anima A 3" in the file. The corpus has no text of *De anima* to check it against.

The paragraph opens with a hedge and a quotation. The soul moves with the body "as it were" (*gleichsam*), and Aristotle supplies the rule that would make the "as it were" literal: what is connected with a movable thing is moved with it, and so is the whole. Husserl accepts the rule and restricts its scope: "this holds only if the connection is that of a physical whole". Then comes the sentence on which the passage turns: "But the soul is nowhere" (*Aber die Seele ist nirgends*). The adverb is absolute. The soul is not somewhere unknown, or everywhere in the body. It has no place. Its connection with the body is "grounded only by functional connections", and the word that names the body in that connection is in quotation marks: the body is "'organ'" of the subject.

The apparatus shows that Husserl first answered Aristotle differently. Where the edition has "Das gilt aber nur, wenn die Verbindung die eines physischen Ganzen ist" [But this holds only if the connection is that of a physical whole], the manuscript reads: "Ja. Wenn es selbst Physisches ist, wenn die Verbindung eine Verbindung zu einem physischen Ganzen ist" [Yes. If it is itself something physical, if the connection is a connection to a physical whole] (apparatus to 167,31f.). The manuscript begins by agreeing ("Yes") and adds a condition. The printed text turns the condition into a restriction ("only"). The negation that follows, "the soul is nowhere", is the same in both versions, but in the print it no longer reads as a concession to Aristotle.

The last sentence of the first paragraph sets two phrases in quotation marks against a third. The "'being-somewhere' of the human being" (*Das „Irgendwo-sein“ des Menschen*) "has its good sense". It is the sense of ordinary speech, in which a person is in the next room. But "'being regularly coordinated and thereby localized'" and "'being in space oneself'" "are two different things" (*ist zweierlei*). The verb is singular, as if the two were one subject. The thesis is that a regular coordination with something in space yields a place, and that this place is borrowed. The soul is localized by coordination. The body is in space itself.

The second paragraph states the result as a definition with a demonstrative, *darin besteht* [in this consists]. Body and soul "form an experiential unity of their own", and by this unity "the psychic receives its place in space and time". That is "the legitimate 'naturalization' of consciousness" (*die rechtmäßige „Naturalisierung“ des Bewußtseins*). The adjective "legitimate" grants a right, and the quotation marks around "naturalization" keep the term at a distance. Naturalism, the doctrine, is not what is meant. What is meant is the operation by which consciousness gets a place in nature by attachment. The next sentences say where it happens first: "Thus localized and temporalized, the alien subjects stand there for us" (Hua IV 168). And § 46 opens by placing the operation in empathy. In self-experience "it would not occur to me" to "'introject'" my psychic life into my body, and "only with empathy … does the closed unity 'human being' constitute itself, and this I then transfer to myself" (Hua IV 167, row 12). Naturalization is legitimate because it is the right way to apprehend others as natural beings, and it reaches me only by transfer.

Section III takes up the term on both sides of its quotation marks. § 49 a names the operation with the word § 46 placed in quotation marks: "One could use the admittedly misleading expression *introjection*" (Hua IV 176, :3205). § 52 contrasts the person's own spatial and temporal position with it: "this temporalization and localization of spirit is something essentially other than that of 'introjection' (cf. p. 190f.)" (Hua IV 206, :3411). And § 64 sets the limit: "the spirit can be apprehended as dependent on nature and itself naturalized, but only up to a certain degree" (*die Grenze möglicher Naturalisierung*, Hua IV 297, :4595, row 50). The "legitimate naturalization" of § 46 is what remains when the limit of § 64 is respected: localization by coordination, as an apprehension of others, with no claim that the soul is in space itself. The soul that "is nowhere" in § 46 is the spirit that is "absolute" in § 64.

## §47, Hua IV 169–72: nature points beyond itself

Reads [§47.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 47.

Section II ends on a methodological result. The analysis of nature, carried out in the naturalistic attitude, has shown that this attitude "needs supplementing". It contains presuppositions and therefore "points beyond itself" to another domain of being and research, the field of subjectivity "that is no longer nature". The human being as an object of natural science is an "x" of mathematical qualities with a soul localized in it. That object is constituted by subjects who are not themselves objects of that kind, and the analysis of nature cannot reach them.

> The analysis of nature and of the consideration of nature thus shows that it needs supplementing, that it harbours presuppositions in itself and thus points beyond itself to another domain of being and research: that is the field of subjectivity that is no longer nature.

German:

> Die Analyse der Natur und Naturbetrachtung zeigt also, daß sie ergänzungsbedürftig ist, daß sie in sich Voraussetzungen birgt und somit über sich hinausweist auf ein anderes Gebiet des Seins und der Forschung: das ist das Feld der Subjektivität, die nicht mehr Natur ist.

The sentence has three predicates, and each says more than the one before. Nature "needs supplementing" (*ergänzungsbedürftig*), a word for something incomplete. It "harbours presuppositions in itself" (*in sich Voraussetzungen birgt*), a word for something that depends on what it does not contain. And it "points beyond itself" (*über sich hinausweist*). That last verb is the language of intentionality, used of a domain of being: nature refers beyond itself as an act refers to its object. The colon names the other domain and its definition is negative: "the field of subjectivity that is no longer nature". The phrase "no longer" (*nicht mehr*) implies a sequence. Up to a point subjectivity is nature: localized, temporalized, naturalized in the legitimate sense of § 46. Beyond that point it is not.

The paragraphs before the sentence show what the presuppositions are, and they turn the analysis of nature against itself. "The natural-scientific object is the x of 'mathematical' qualities, to whose substruction the causal analysis and the thinking founded on it compel us" (Hua IV 170, :3104). Natural science therefore relates the "merely subjective" appearances to subjects. "All the 'phenomenological' things are what they are only as noematic correlates of the perceptual experiences of the human being concerned. They are merely 'subjective', they have a 'merely subjective truth'" (Hua IV 170). In the naturalistic attitude, "physical nature is posited absolutely, and the aesthesiological is posited absolutely, and likewise all experiences" (Hua IV 170). The scare quotes on "merely subjective" show the tension. Nature reduces the intuited world to subjective appearances and then has to posit the subjects for whom they are appearances. It cannot posit them as what they are, the subjects to whom nature appears, because its only category for them is the natural object, the human being as an x with a localized soul.

The section also sums up the order of constitution that §§ 18–46 have traced, and the summary repeats the word of § 18 h. First a subject "that has body and soul, but is not yet the real unity 'human being', not yet a natural object"; then "the alien subjects, which are apperceived as analogues of the own and at the same time as natural objects"; then "nature as intersubjectively common and objectively (exactly) determinable, and the own subject as member of this 'objective nature'" (*das eigene Subjekt als Glied dieser „objektiven Natur“*, Hua IV 171). The *Glied der Natur* [member of nature] that the solipsistic subject could not apprehend itself as in § 18 h (row 12) is reached here, at the end of Section II. But "objective nature" now has quotation marks. The member of nature is a correlate of a constitution, and the constitution points to subjects.

The sentence is the hinge to Section III, and the next section picks it up in its opening. § 48 traces the distinctions between nature and spirit back to "the distinction between soul and spirit" (§48.1, Hua IV 172), and § 49 explains how the analyses of the first two sections become "purely phenomenological": "While one lives in an attitude, the attitude itself is not in one's field" (§49.1, Hua IV 174). What § 47 calls the field of subjectivity "that is no longer nature" § 49 calls the personal world, and the reversal is stated at once. The naturalistic attitude "subordinates itself to the personalistic" and gains its independence "through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal ego" (Hua IV 184, :3248). The presuppositions that § 47 found in the analysis of nature are the person and its world.

In the book's last section the "x" of § 47 is carried to the end. "Nature is the X and in principle nothing other than an X that determines itself through general determinations. Spirit, however, is no X, but what is given itself in spiritual experience" (Hua IV 302, :4691). What § 47 says from within the analysis of nature, that nature points beyond itself, § 64 says from the other side: nature is relative to spirit, "an absolute" that "carries all relativities" (Hua IV 297, :4595). Section II ends by finding that its object has presuppositions. Section III identifies them and reverses the order of founding.

## §48, Hua IV 172–73: Dilthey's intuition

Reads [§48.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 48.

Section III opens by placing itself in the dispute over the human sciences, and it judges its predecessor in one antithesis. Dilthey saw what had to be done: that a natural science of the soul cannot ground the human sciences, and that a "psychology" of a new kind is needed. He did not reach the formulations or the methodically secured solutions. The section will supply them by a "radical" inquiry into the constitution of the ideas of nature, body, soul, ego and person. The understanding the passage carries is a judgment on method. Intuition of the problems is not yet science of them, and only an inquiry into constitution can turn the one into the other.

> Dilthey, a man of genial intuition but not of rigorous scientific theorizing, did indeed glimpse the goal-giving problems, the directions of the work to be done; but he did not yet break through to the decisive formulations of the problems and to methodically secure solutions, however great the progress he made in precisely the years of the wisdom of old age.
>
> Only a *radical* investigation, directed at the phenomenological sources of the constitution of the ideas nature, body, soul and the various ideas of I and person, can give the decisive answers here and at the same time do justice to the valuable motives of all such investigations.

German:

> Dilthey, ein Mann genialer Intuition, aber nicht streng wissenschaftlicher Theoretisierung, erschaute zwar die zielgebenden Probleme, die Richtungen der zu leistenden Arbeit: aber zu den entscheidenden Problemformulierungen und methodisch sicheren Lösungen drang er noch nicht durch, so große Fortschritte er gerade in den Jahren der Altersweisheit darin machte.
>
> Nur eine *radikale*, auf die phänomenologischen Quellen der Konstitution der Ideen Natur, Leib, Seele und der verschiedenen Ideen von Ich und Person gerichtete Untersuchung kann hier die entscheidenden Aufschlüsse geben und zugleich den wertvollen Motiven aller solcher Untersuchungen ihr Recht angedeihen lassen.

The judgment is built from a contrast of two nouns, "intuition" (*Intuition*) and "theorizing" (*Theoretisierung*), and two verbs. Dilthey "glimpsed" (*erschaute*) the problems. The verb is a seer's verb, with the prefix *er-* of achievement. He "did not yet break through" (*drang … noch nicht durch*) to formulations. The concession is in *zwar* [indeed] and its counterpart *aber* [but]. Husserl concedes the first half and withholds the second. "Not yet" (*noch nicht*) puts Dilthey on a road whose end the phenomenologist claims to reach. The last clause adds praise that also places him: "however great the progress he made in precisely the years of the wisdom of old age" (*in den Jahren der Altersweisheit*). Dilthey died in 1911. The word "old-age wisdom" honours the late work and ends the account of it.

The word *Intuition* is set against the method that Husserl calls by another name. Section III will distinguish the two carefully. In § 60 d the sudden grasp of a person's character is called "'intuition', a word that very often means precisely the opposite of intuition (*Anschauung*), namely a *presentiment*, a foreseeing without seeing", and "this 'intuition' must not be confused with actual intuition" (Hua IV 273–74, :4249, row 46). In § 48 the word is not yet in quotation marks, but the pairing with "not of rigorous scientific theorizing" gives it the same sense. Dilthey's intuition is the kind of seeing that anticipates and has to be fulfilled. Phenomenology claims the other kind, the *Anschauung* that gives its object.

The paragraph's first sentences show what is at stake, and they say it in the vocabulary of worldviews. A group of "metaphysical and science-theoretical distinctions", between nature and the world of spirit, natural and human sciences, natural-scientific psychology and the theory of personality and community, all "have their source in the difficult distinction between *soul and spirit*". "Our whole worldview is fundamentally determined by the clarification of these distinctions" (Hua IV 172). For "decades", reactions have been mounting against "the naturalistic interpretation of the human sciences as mere descriptive natural sciences, self-evident to the natural-scientific age". Dilthey's merit was to see that "modern psychology, a natural science of the psychic, is incapable" of grounding the concrete human sciences, and that what is needed is "a new and essentially different 'psychology', a general science of spirit that is not 'psychophysical', not natural-scientific" (Hua IV 173). The quotation marks on "psychology" show that the word is kept for a discipline that does not yet exist.

The list of names that follows assigns everyone else a place. "Hermann Ebbinghaus's elegant critique, which however only dissolved the insufficient scientific elaboration of Dilthey's ideas", could not stop the development "despite the general applause of the naturalists". "Windelband, Rickert, Simmel, Münsterberg and others" tried to do justice to the oppositions "from new sides", but "we have not been brought through them to really conclusive clarifications" (Hua IV 173). The verb *durchdringen* [break through] returns. Dilthey did not break through, and the neo-Kantians did not bring us through. The second quoted sentence then names the inquiry that will. It is "radical" (in italics), and its object is "the phenomenological sources of the constitution of the ideas nature, body, soul and the various ideas of I and person". The plural "ideas of I and person" announces §§ 57–61, where pure ego, personal ego and naturalized soul are distinguished. The phrase "do justice to the valuable motives" (*ihr Recht angedeihen lassen*) is generous and also final. The others had motives, and phenomenology will give them their right.

The section reaches its answer to Dilthey in § 56 f. There "the 'because–so' of motivation has an entirely different sense from causation in the sense of nature", and when the historian wants to "explain", this "means he wants to lay motivations bare" (Hua IV 229, :3626, row 39). Dilthey's distinction between explanation and understanding returns as a distinction between two kinds of "because". The § 48 promise of "methodically secure solutions" is redeemed by a phenomenology of motivation, not by a methodology of the human sciences.

The corpus has the other side of the story. In his 1925 course, Heidegger says that Dilthey's work aimed at "a new psychology, a *personalistic* one", and that after the *Logical Investigations* "Dilthey gained particular influence" on Husserl, "precisely in the direction of gaining a new psychology" (GA 20 161). Husserl took up the tendency "in 1914/15" together with *Ideas I*, in a first elaboration that "was never published" (GA 20 168). Heidegger thus tells the history that Husserl's § 48 leaves out: Husserl took the direction from Dilthey. Husserl's § 48 assigns Dilthey intuition and phenomenology rigour. Heidegger reverses the debt. The problem was Dilthey's, and the question is whether Husserl's rigour reached it. His answer, in the same pages, is that it did not: "ontologically everything stays as it was" (GA 20 171; see rows 50 and 51), and "Husserl does not get beyond Dilthey, however superior his analyses certainly are in particulars" (*Husserl kommt über Dilthey nicht hinaus*, GA 20 173). § 48 claims to take up Dilthey's problem. Heidegger's verdict is that Husserl's rigour replaced the problem rather than reaching it.

## §49 a, Hua IV 176: introjection

Reads [§49 a.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 49 a.

In the naturalistic attitude the psychic is experienced as a stratum "at" the body, even though it is not localized in the body the way sensings are. It has no distinguishable place, and yet it is "really one" with the body and moves with it. Husserl proposes a name for this relation and warns against it in the same breath: "introjection". The soul is "laid into" the body. The term names the way naturalism gives consciousness a place, and the hedge shows that Husserl expects it to be misread as a theory of how souls get into bodies.

> Notwithstanding this, the psychic is in experience one, or really one, with the body; to that extent it is something at or in it, only without a distinguishable particular locality. One could use the admittedly misleading expression *introjection*; it would then express precisely this state of affairs.
>
> The surplus of reality over the mere physical thing is nothing separable for itself, nothing beside but at it; it therefore moves "with" it, and it gains its spatial determinacy through its being at what is spatial.

German:

> Dessen ungeachtet ist das Seelische erfahrungsmäßig eins, bzw. realiter eins mit dem Leibe, insofern ist es etwas an oder in ihm, nur ohne unterscheidbare besondere Lokalität. Man könnte den freilich mißdeutbaren Ausdruck *Introjektion* gebrauchen; er würde dann eben diese Sachlage ausdrücken.
>
> Der Realitätsüberschuß über das bloße physische Ding ist nichts für sich Abtrennbares, nichts neben sondern an diesem, bewegt sich also 'mit' ihm, es gewinnt durch das Sein an dem Räumlichen selbst seine Raumbestimmtheit.

The proposal is made in the conditional, twice. "One could use" (*Man könnte … gebrauchen*), and "it would then express" (*er würde dann … ausdrücken*). Husserl does not adopt the word. He offers it for a single use: to name "precisely this state of affairs" and no more. The adjective that qualifies it is *mißdeutbar* [open to misinterpretation], with *freilich* [admittedly]. The word is italicized in the file as a term on trial. Its misleading sense is in its morphology: *intro-* [inward] and *-jektion* [throwing]. The soul thrown into the body. What Husserl wants it to name is the opposite of a movement. It is the soul's being "at or in" (*an oder in*) the body "without a distinguishable particular locality".

The text does not say where the word comes from. The main commentary attributes it to Avenarius, and that attribution is logged under the list's corrections because the file names no author. The corpus shows where Husserl himself had used it. In *Ding und Raum* (1907) the sensations that receive a "double apprehension", once presenting the thing and once making "the body appear as sensing", become "subjective determinations laid into the body, localized in it". Beyond the sphere of appearance proper, "there finally arises the introjection of all sensations and all appearances, of all phenomenological occurrences into the I and the body of the I" (Hua XVI 163). In 1907 introjection extends localization from sensings to all of consciousness. In *Ideen II* it is limited to the psychic stratum of the naturalistic apprehension and hedged. A note from the intersubjectivity manuscripts of these years gives the reason for the hedge: "The introjection of sensations and appearances into a subject, or their apprehension as merely subjective, stems from intersubjectivity" (Hua XIII 389). This is the thesis of § 46 (row 25): introjection is learned from others and transferred to oneself.

The second paragraph specifies the relation with three prepositions: "nothing beside but at it" (*nichts neben sondern an diesem*), so that it "moves 'with' it" (*'mit' ihm*). The quotation marks on "with" mark the borrowed sense. The soul moves with the body as a quality moves with its thing, not as a passenger with a carriage. The final clause gives the result: the psychic "gains its spatial determinacy through its being at what is spatial". The surplus is spatial at second hand. That is the phenomenological content that the word "introjection" is meant to carry and that its etymology contradicts.

The apparatus records how Husserl later judged the pages that follow. At 177,21ff. he wrote in Landgrebe's typescript: "Null [worthless] up to p. 180. This is incorrect. Am I speaking of my, the philosophizing subject's, consciousness? But here intersubjectivity and empathy come into question", and he set a deletion sign as far as subsection e) (apparatus to 177,21ff.). The objection is the one the Hua XIII note states. A description of the soul as "laid into" the body is incomplete if it does not say whose apprehension it is, and the answer is that it is the apprehension of others, first. The edition prints the pages despite the sign.

Section III uses introjection as the foil of the personalistic attitude. In § 51, to take the other as a thing is to let him function "only as psychic being in the sense of the consideration of nature, as causally dependent on the body, onto which he appears grafted" (*dem er aufgepfropft erscheint*, Hua IV 190, :3288). In § 52 the temporalization and localization of spirit is "something essentially other than that of 'introjection' (cf. p. 190f.)" (Hua IV 205, :3408). In § 56 e, in the naturalistic apprehension of another, "the alien I … is posited introjectively", "built on the fundamental apprehension and positing of material nature" (§56 e.1, Hua IV 228). The understanding of § 49 a is thus the understanding of what the personalistic attitude does not do. The word Husserl offered with a warning becomes the name for the one operation the second attitude refuses.

## §49 d, Hua IV 179–80: natural, not naturalistic

Reads [§49 d.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §49 d.2, first and second paragraphs under § 49 d.

The phenomenological reduction frees us from the limits of the natural attitude. It shows that the natural scientist's results carry "an index of their merely relative sense". It also makes us receptive to other attitudes "of equal birth" with that one, which constitute equally relative correlates. The new attitude the section aims at is "in a certain sense very natural", and yet "not natural" in the sense of nature-directed: what is experienced in it is "a counterpart of nature". The distinction turns on a word, and Husserl changed the word after 1924.

> To such a *new attitude*, one in a certain sense very natural but *not nature-directed* (*natural*), we now turn. "*Not nature-directed*" says that what is experienced in it is *not nature in the sense of all the natural sciences*, but, so to speak, a *counterpart of nature*.

German:

> Auf eine solche *neue Einstellung*, die in gewissem Sinn sehr natürlich, aber *nicht natural* ist, haben wir es jetzt abgesehen. „*Nicht natural*“, das sagt, daß das in ihr Erfahrene *nicht Natur ist im Sinne aller Naturwissenschaften*, sondern sozusagen ein *Widerspiel der Natur*.

The passage turns on two adjectives that share a root and differ by a suffix. *Natürlich* is the ordinary word: natural, as opposed to artificial. *Natural* is a coinage: directed at nature, as the natural sciences understand it. The new attitude is "very natural" (*sehr natürlich*) and "not natural" (*nicht natural*). The sentence that follows defines the coinage by quoting it, "'*Not natural*' says…". What is experienced in this attitude "is *not nature in the sense of all the natural sciences*", but "so to speak a *counterpart of nature*" (*Widerspiel der Natur*). *Widerspiel* is a counter-play, the reflection or reverse of something. "So to speak" (*sozusagen*) marks it as an image. The personal world is not a second nature beside the first. It mirrors it.

The previous paragraph prepares the coinage with a self-correction. The reduction makes us receptive to attitudes "of equal birth (*ebenbürtig*) with the natural or, as we now say more distinctly, the nature-directed (*naturalen*) attitude" (Hua IV 180, :3224). The words "as we now say more distinctly" (*wie wir jetzt deutlicher sagen*) present the new adjective as a sharpening of the old one. The apparatus shows that the sharpening came late and replaced something else. At 179,32 the manuscript read *naturalistischen*, changed to *naturalen* by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript, and the manuscript has a marginal question: "is then the natural attitude the same as the naturalistic, and is not the whole page to be struck?" At 180,7, the "nicht natural" of the passage read "nicht naturalistisch" in both manuscript and typescript, and Husserl changed it (apparatus to 179,32 and 180,7). In the first draft, the new attitude was "very natural, but not naturalistic". In the late revision, it is "very natural, but not nature-directed".

The change matters for what the attitude is contrasted with. "Naturalistic" names a stance and a doctrine, the habit of seeing everything as nature, which § 49 e will call "habitual blinkers" (row 30). "Natural" in the new sense names only the direction of the attitude: at nature, as the natural sciences determine it. The first version opposed the personalistic attitude to an ideology. The second opposes it to a legitimate attitude of equal birth, which becomes illegitimate only when it absolutizes its correlate (row 31). The late Husserl's question in the margin shows why he made the change. If the natural attitude of *Ideas I* were the same as the naturalistic, the personalistic attitude, which is "a thoroughly natural attitude" (Hua IV 183), would be naturalistic too. The word *natural* separates the natural from the nature-directed so that the personal can be natural without being nature.

The commentary renders the self-correction as "the natural — or, as Husserl now says more precisely, the naturalistic — attitude" (§49 d.1). That reading would be right for the manuscript and is wrong for the edition, as logged under the list's corrections. The corpus has a witness to the earlier reading. In his 1925 course, Heidegger paraphrases this very passage: "we now aim at a new attitude, which is in a certain sense *very natural*, but *not naturalistic*. What we experience in it is not nature, but so to speak the physical counter-play of nature, the counter-throw" (*sehr natürlich*, aber *nicht naturalistisch* … *das physische Widerspiel der Natur, der Gegenwurf*, GA 20 169). Heidegger read a text with the earlier adjective, before Husserl's change in Landgrebe's typescript or without it. His paraphrase also differs from the edition in two words. *Gegenwurf* [counter-throw] is not in § 49 d, but it is Husserl's: a few pages earlier Heidegger quotes the *Logos* essay of 1911, where the psychic is "the counter-throw of nature" (*der Gegenwurf von Natur*, GA 20 165). He reads § 49 d through the earlier text. The adjective "physical" (*physische*) has no counterpart in the edition, and the corpus cannot say whether it comes from Heidegger's copy, his paraphrase or the transcript. Either way, the personal world, so described, is still defined by its relation to nature.

The apparatus also shows how Husserl judged the surrounding subsections. At 177,21ff. he marked the pages up to p. 180 "Null" and "incorrect" and set a deletion sign as far as e), and at 180,2ff. he noted: "Everywhere the doctrine of intersubjective reduction is lacking" (apparatus to 177,21ff. and 180,2ff.). The "methodical reflection" of § 49 d claims that the reduction to "absolute consciousness" allows the attitudes to be "evaluated absolutely" (Hua IV 180). The late Husserl's objection is that the absolute consciousness invoked here is a single one, while the attitudes in question are those of communicating persons. § 49 d thus states the method the section needs in the form that its author later thought insufficient.

The understanding the passage carries is the distinction between the natural and the nature-directed. The book's last chapter makes the distinction its ground. The naturalistic attitude "subordinates itself to the personalistic" (row 31), and § 62 sets the natural scientist inside "his life-world" (*seiner Lebenswelt*, Hua IV 288, :4461, row 49). The personal world is natural in the first sense, the world one lives in. Nature in the second sense is the correlate of a particular, legitimate abstraction within it. The single coined adjective *natural* is what lets Husserl say both things in one sentence.

## §49 e, Hua IV 183: physio-psychic, and the blinkers

Reads [§49 e.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), fourth paragraph under § 49 e.

In the naturalistic attitude human beings and animals are "zoological objects", and Husserl proposes to call them "physio-psychic": the reversal of the usual "psychophysical" shows the order of founding, the psychic resting on the physical. The personalistic attitude, by contrast, is the one "in which we always are" when we live with one another. The naturalist does not live otherwise. He has only acquired "habitual blinkers", which make him take every scientific attitude as a naturalistic one. As researcher he sees only "nature". As a person he lives like everyone else.

> All human beings and animals that we consider in this attitude are, if we pursue theoretical interests, anthropological, more generally zoological objects; we can also say physio-psychic ones, where the reversal of the usual expression "psychophysical" quite fittingly indicates the order of founding. What has been said concerns ourselves as much as all our fellow human beings — insofar as we consider ourselves theoretically in this attitude: we are ensouled bodies, natural objects, themes of the relevant natural sciences.
>
> Even for the zoologist and naturalistic psychologist this is not always the case; only that he has adopted the fixed habit, whose limits as a rule he can no longer break through, that as soon as he adopts a *scientific* attitude at all, he does so inevitably in the form of the naturalistic attitude (or, what is equivalent, the attitude directed at "objective" actuality). He has habitual blinkers. As researcher he sees only "nature". But as person he lives like everyone else and constantly "knows" himself as subject of his *surrounding world*. To live as a person is to posit oneself as a person, to find oneself in conscious relations to a "*surrounding world*" and to bring oneself into such relations.

German:

> Alle Menschen und Tiere, die wir in dieser Einstellung betrachten, sind, wenn wir theoretischen Interessen nachgehen, anthropologische, allgemeiner: zoologische Objekte; wir können auch sagen: physio-psychische, wobei die Umkehrung des üblichen Ausdruckes „psychophysisch” recht passend die Ordnung der Fundierung andeutet. Das Gesagte betrifft wie alle Nebenmenschen so uns selbst — wofern wir uns eben in dieser Einstellung theoretisch betrachten: wir sind beseelte Leiber, Naturobjekte, Themen der betreffenden Naturwissenschaften.
>
> Selbst für den Zoologen und naturalistischen Psychologen ist das nicht immer der Fall; nur daß er die feste Gewohnheit angenommen hat, deren Schranken er in der Regel nicht mehr zu durchbrechen vermag, daß er, sobald er überhaupt *wissenschaftliche* Einstellung annimmt, dies unweigerlich in der Form naturalistischer (oder, was damit äquivalent ist, auf „objektive” Wirklichkeit gerichteter) Einstellung tut. Er hat habituelle Scheuklappen. Als Forscher sieht er nur „Natur”. Aber als Person lebt er wie jeder andere und „weiß” sich beständig als Subjekt seiner *Umwelt*. Als Person leben ist sich selbst als Person setzen, sich zu einer „*Umwelt*” in bewußtseinsmäßigen Verhältnissen finden und in Verhältnisse bringen.

The first paragraph makes an argument from word order. *Psychophysisch* puts the psychic first. *Physio-psychisch* puts the physical first, and the reversal "fittingly indicates the order of founding" (*die Ordnung der Fundierung andeutet*). In the naturalistic attitude the physical founds, and the psychic is founded. The coinage is not an innovation in doctrine. It is a correction of a habit of speech that hides the doctrine. The book has already used the word: a footnote to § 34 warns that "the physiopsychic dependencies do not suffice for the constitution of the psychic subject" (Hua IV 139 n., :2716). § 49 e explains it. The consequence is drawn for "ourselves": "we are ensouled bodies, natural objects, themes of the relevant natural sciences". The restriction makes it a statement about an attitude: "insofar as we consider ourselves theoretically in this attitude" (*wofern*).

The second paragraph explains the attitude by a habit, and the metaphor is chosen with care. The naturalist "has adopted the fixed habit" (*die feste Gewohnheit*), "whose limits as a rule he can no longer break through". The result: "He has habitual blinkers" (*habituelle Scheuklappen*). Blinkers are put on a horse so that it sees only straight ahead. The adjective "habitual" makes them part of the wearer. The scope of the habit is exact. It is the identification of "*scientific*" (italic) attitude with naturalistic attitude, "or, what is equivalent, directed at 'objective' actuality". The scare quotes on "objective" mark the borrowing. The naturalist does not see only nature. He sees only "nature" (in quotation marks) as soon as he does science.

The paragraph ends with a definition of the person, and its quotation marks mark the person's own terms. "But as person he lives like everyone else and constantly 'knows' (*„weiß“*) himself as subject of his *surrounding world*." The verb *weiß* is in quotation marks because the knowing is not theoretical knowledge. It is the self-awareness of a life. The final sentence is a definition by infinitives: "To live as a person is to posit oneself as a person, to find oneself in conscious relations to a '*surrounding world*' and to bring oneself into such relations." Positing oneself, finding oneself, bringing oneself: the reflexive verbs make the person its own act. That is the definition on which § 50 builds (row 32).

The passage sits between the account of naturalism in its strongest form (§49 e.1–3) and the reversal of the attitudes (row 31). In the strongest form, even the cogito is "a natural fact": the "I think" "drops out" in dreamless sleep "on physical grounds" (Hua IV 181). The turn comes in the paragraph read here, in the description of the personalistic attitude "in which we always are when we live with one another, speak to one another, shake hands in greeting" (Hua IV 183). It is "a thoroughly natural and not an artificial attitude, which would first have to be won and secured by special means" (Hua IV 183). The coinage *physio-psychisch* and the blinkers are the two halves of this turn. The first gives the naturalistic attitude its correct name, and the second shows how it comes to seem the only scientific one.

Heidegger, who quotes this passage in his 1925 course, keeps both halves. "When we live with one another, are related to one another in disposition and deed, we experience ourselves as persons. This natural experience is not an artificial one that would first have to be won by special means" (GA 20 169). He adds the sentence that follows in § 49 e (row 31): "Indeed, the attitudes are not even co-ordinate, but the naturalistic is subordinated to the personalistic" (GA 20 169). He grants the reversal, and his objection comes in the next sentence: "if we look more closely at how the determination of the person proceeds", "we come back to what we already know", the "*inspectio sui*", "the ego of intentionality, the ego as subject of the *cogitationes*" (GA 20 169). The Latin phrase is Husserl's own: § 54 is titled "The I in the *inspectio sui*" (Hua IV 211). The blinkers, on this reading, are removed from the naturalist and not from the phenomenologist. The understanding that § 49 e gives the naturalistic attitude as a habit is what Heidegger turns against Husserl: the phenomenologist's habit is the cogito.

## §49 e, Hua IV 184: self-forgetfulness

Reads [§49 e.5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), fifth paragraph under § 49 e.

The two attitudes are not of equal rank. The naturalistic attitude subordinates itself to the personalistic, and gains a certain independence only "through an abstraction or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal ego". In doing so it "illegitimately absolutizes its world, nature". The thesis of the whole section is stated here in advance. The abstraction itself is legitimate. What is illegitimate is taking its correlate for the whole world. The mechanism is forgetting: the person who abstracts forgets that he is the one abstracting.

> On closer consideration it will even turn out that we do not have here two attitudes of equal right and equal order, or two apperceptions of fully equal right that at the same time interpenetrate, but that the naturalistic attitude subordinates itself to the personalistic and gains a certain independence through an abstraction, or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal I, thereby at the same time illegitimately absolutizing its world, nature.

German:

> Bei genauer Betrachtung wird sich sogar herausstellen, daß hier nicht einmal zwei gleichberechtigte und gleichgeordnete Einstellungen vorliegen, bzw. zwei völlig gleichberechtigte und sich zugleich durchdringende Apperzeptionen, sondern daß die naturalistische Einstellung sich der personalistischen unterordnet und durch eine Abstraktion oder vielmehr durch eine Art Selbstvergessenheit des personalen Ich eine gewisse Selbständigkeit gewinnt, dadurch zugleich ihre Welt, die Natur, unrechtmäßig verabsolutierend.

The sentence is built on negation and correction. "Not even" (*nicht einmal*) denies equal rank, which § 49 d had seemed to grant when it spoke of attitudes "of equal birth" (row 29). The two attributes denied are *gleichberechtigt* [of equal right] and *gleichgeordnet* [of equal order], and the alternative formulation repeats the first and adds "at the same time interpenetrating". The positive statement uses a reflexive verb: the naturalistic attitude "subordinates itself" (*sich … unterordnet*). The attitude is not subordinated by a verdict from outside. Its subordination is a fact about how it arises. The future tense, "it will turn out" (*wird sich … herausstellen*), marks the claim as an announcement that §§ 53–64 will argue.

The mechanism is stated with a self-correction: "through an abstraction or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness" (*durch eine Abstraktion oder vielmehr durch eine Art Selbstvergessenheit*). "Abstraction" names a legitimate operation. "Or rather" (*oder vielmehr*) replaces it with a name for a lapse. The phrase "a kind of" (*eine Art*) is the same hedge that § 11 put on "reduction" (row 4). There the abstraction from the heart was "a kind of 'reduction'". Here the same abstraction is "a kind of self-forgetfulness". The difference between the two descriptions is the argument of the book so far. The participle clause at the end states the consequence and its fault: "thereby at the same time illegitimately absolutizing its world, nature" (*unrechtmäßig verabsolutierend*). "Illegitimately" qualifies the absolutizing, not the abstraction. The naturalistic attitude has a right to its world, but not as the whole world.

The word *Selbstvergessenheit* [self-forgetfulness] recalls Section I's opening of § 18. The thing analyzed in §§ 15–17 was the thing for a subject "that remains, as it were, self-forgetful (*selbstvergessen*)", and "this self-forgetfulness also remains unnoticed by the analyst" (Hua IV 55, :1357). In § 18 the self-forgetfulness was a methodical restriction that had to be "lifted at a decisive point" (Hua IV 81). In § 49 e it is the naturalist's condition, and lifting it is the aim of Section III. The analysis of Section I was itself conducted in the naturalistic attitude. The forgetting that § 49 e diagnoses was practised, knowingly, in the book's own first section.

The sentence comes from the first draft of the section, and Husserl's later revision of the paragraphs around it shows that he kept it. The apparatus reports a change "by H. in L." a few lines later, at 184,9f. Seen naturalistically, all consciousness is bodily founded, and the edition goes on: "hence also the total stock of what intentionally constitutes, in the persons, the world with all its properties" (Hua IV 185, :3254). The manuscript and Landgrebe's copy read "the total stock of what intentionally constitutes the person with all its personal properties" (apparatus to 184,9f.). The late revision makes the naturalistic claim wider. What is bodily founded is not only the constitution of the person but the constitution of the world. The thesis sentence itself is untouched. Heidegger quotes it in 1925 almost word for word: "the attitudes are not even co-ordinate, but the naturalistic is subordinated to the personalistic" (GA 20 169). He drops "self-forgetfulness", the mechanism.

The book carries the thesis out in three steps. § 53 states the "vicious circle" (row 35): nature contains persons, and persons constitute nature. § 62 places the naturalistic apprehension inside the personal one: the natural scientist, "even where he investigates nature", finds himself "constantly living as a person in the personal world, his life-world", and "the apprehension as nature thus subordinates itself to the personal apprehension" (Hua IV 288, :4461, row 49). The verb *unterordnen* returns. § 64 states the result as an ontological priority: "Nature is a field of thoroughgoing relativities", relative to "an absolute", "the spirit" (Hua IV 297, :4595, row 50). The self-forgetfulness of § 49 e is what lets the naturalist absolutize nature. The remembering of § 62 is what lets the phenomenologist see nature as relative.

## §50, Hua IV 185–86: the person as subject of a surrounding world

Reads [§50.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 50.

A person is what it is as "subject of a surrounding world" (*Umwelt*). The two concepts are inseparable. Every person has its surrounding world, and persons who communicate have a common one. The surrounding world is not the physical environment. It is the world as the person perceives, remembers, thinks of, surmises, values and shapes it, the world "of which this personal I is conscious". It is a world "for me", not "in itself", and so it changes with the person's knowledge and is "constantly in becoming".

> As person I am what I am (and every other person is what it is) as *subject of a surrounding world*. The concepts I and surrounding world are inseparably related to one another. To each person belongs its surrounding world, while at the same time several persons communicating with one another have a common surrounding world. The surrounding world is the world perceived, remembered, grasped in thought, surmised or inferred in this or that respect *by the person* in its acts, the world of which this personal I is conscious, which is there for it, to which it relates in one way or another, for example thematically experiencing and theorizing with respect to the things appearing to it, or feeling, valuing, acting, shaping technically, and so on.

German:

> Als Person bin ich, was ich bin (und ist jede andere Person, was sie ist) als *Subjekt einer Umwelt*. Die Begriffe Ich und Umwelt sind untrennbar aufeinander bezogen. Dabei gehört zu jeder Person ihre Umwelt, während zugleich mehrere miteinander kommunizierende Personen eine gemeinsame Umwelt haben. Die Umwelt ist die *von der Person* in ihren Akten wahrgenommene, erinnerte, denkmäßig gefaßte, nach dem und jenem vermutete oder erschlossene Welt, die Welt, deren dieses personale Ich bewußt ist, die für es da ist, zu der es sich so oder so verhält, z.B. thematisch erfahrend und theoretisierend in Beziehung auf die ihm erscheinenden Dinge oder fühlend, wertschätzend, handelnd, technisch gestaltend usw.

The first sentence is a tautology broken by a qualification. "As person I am what I am" is empty, and "as *subject of a surrounding world*" (italic) fills it. The parenthesis extends the claim to "every other person" before the definition is complete, so that the definition is not solipsistic. The second sentence states the correlation without priority: "The concepts I and surrounding world are inseparably related to one another" (*untrennbar aufeinander bezogen*). *Aufeinander* [to one another] is reciprocal. Neither concept is defined first.

The third sentence defines the surrounding world by the acts it correlates with, and the list is long on purpose. It is the world "perceived, remembered, grasped in thought, surmised or inferred". The emphasis falls on the agent, "*by the person*" (*von der Person*). The world is defined by participles whose subject is the person. The relative clauses then widen the relation from knowing to living: "of which this personal I is conscious", "which is there for it", "to which it relates in one way or another", "feeling, valuing, acting, shaping technically". The *Umwelt* is the correlate of the whole person, not of its perception. The value-characters of § 4 (row 1) and the practical characters of § 7 belong to it as constitutively as colours.

The paragraphs after the passage draw two consequences that the definition makes possible. "Physical actuality is not simply and in general the actual surrounding world of a person, but only insofar as it 'knows' of it". "If it knows nothing of the discoveries of physics, the world does not belong to its actual surrounding world with the sense-content of physics". "Quite generally, the surrounding world is no world 'in itself' but a world 'for me'", and "as such it is in a certain way constantly in becoming" (*immerfort im Werden*, Hua IV 186). The quotation marks on "in itself" and "for me" mark the contrast as one between two concepts of world. The first consequence makes the surrounding world relative to knowledge. The second makes it historical.

*Ideas I* had described the same world from the other side. In § 27, "the world of the natural attitude: I and my surrounding world", the world is "constantly 'present' for me, and I myself am its member. And this world is there for me not as a mere world of things but, in the same immediacy, as a world of values, a world of goods, a practical world." "Things stand there immediately as objects of use, the 'table' with its 'books', the 'drinking glass', the 'vase', the 'piano'" (Hua III 58). The description is almost the same. The status differs. In *Ideas I* the surrounding world is the starting point that the epoché will bracket. In *Ideen II* § 50 it is the correlate of the person, the object of a constitutive analysis in its own right, and the natural sciences have their place inside it.

The rest of the section depends on the definition. § 51 distinguishes "egoistic" and "communicative" surrounding worlds (Hua IV 193). It also distinguishes "surrounding world in the full sense", which includes the subject as "subject-object", from "outer world" (Hua IV 195–96). § 55 calls the surrounding world "the thematic world of my and our intentional life" (§55.5, Hua IV 218). § 62 places nature inside it as "secondary surrounding world", "a secondary object of the surrounding world, whose primary object is precisely the appearance" (Hua IV 285–86). The definition of § 50 is the ground on which Section III rests: the world is the person's world, and every other world is a level within it.

## §50, Hua IV 189–90: stimulus and motivation

Reads [§50.9](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), ninth paragraph under § 50.

The word "stimulus" (*Reiz*) has two senses. In the natural-scientific sense, a physical excitation stimulates a nerve, and the final excitation in the brain stimulates the soul to react in sensation. In the personal sense, the things of the surrounding world, as they are meant and experienced, "stimulate" the person: they attract attention, invite enjoyment, demand use. In place of the causal relation between natural realities, a "motivation relation" holds between persons and things. Its things are not things in themselves but intentional objects. This is the first appearance of the concept on which the whole spiritual world is built.

> In place of the causal relation between things and human beings as natural realities there steps the *motivation relation* between persons and things, and these things are not the things of nature existing in themselves — of exact natural science with the determinations that it alone accepts as objectively true — but experienced, thought or otherwise positingly meant things as such, intentional objectivities of personal consciousness.

German:

> Statt des Kausalverhältnisses zwischen Dingen und Menschen als Naturrealitäten tritt die *Motivationsbeziehung* zwischen Personen und Dingen, und diese Dinge sind nicht die an sich seienden Dinge der Natur — der exakten Naturwissenschaft mit den Bestimmtheiten, die sie als allein objektiv wahre gelten läßt —, sondern erfahrene, gedachte oder sonstwie setzend vermeinte Dinge als solche, intentionale Gegenständlichkeiten des personalen Bewußtseins.

The sentence substitutes one relation for another, and both terms change. "In place of" (*Statt*) the "causal relation" (*Kausalverhältnis*) "steps" (*tritt*) the "*motivation relation*" (*Motivationsbeziehung*, italic). The relata change with the relation: "things and human beings as natural realities" become "persons and things". The things themselves are redefined: "not the things of nature existing in themselves", but "experienced, thought or otherwise positingly meant things as such". The phrase "as such" (*als solche*) is the noematic qualifier of *Ideas I*. What motivates is the object as meant. The dashes enclose an insertion, and the apparatus confirms that it is late: "der exakten Naturwissenschaft … gelten läßt" is an "insertion by H. in L." (apparatus to 189,16f.). The phrase "with the determinations that it alone accepts as objectively true" is the late Husserl's sharpening of "in themselves". The things in themselves are the things as one science determines them.

The paragraph surrounds this sentence with the two uses of *Reiz*, and quotation marks separate them. "From the physical natural object, one says, 'stimuli' proceed". "The sense-nerves are called stimulated by physical excitations", and "the soul too is then called stimulated", by "a final excitation in the central nervous system" that "functions as 'stimulus' for the soul" (Hua IV 190). The repeated "one says", "are called" (*heißen*) keep this sense at a distance. "But if we place ourselves on the ground of the intentional subject–object relation", "the concept of stimulus gains a fundamentally new sense" (Hua IV 190). After the substitution comes the new vocabulary, still in quotation marks: "'stimuli' proceed from things as such that 'lie in the sense' for the personal I", "noematic unities" are "starting points of more or less 'strong' tendencies", and "with sufficient 'stimulus-strength' the I 'follows' the stimulus, 'gives way' and turns toward it" (Hua IV 190). The quotation marks keep the force vocabulary of physiology, now used of an ego that can "give way" or "resist".

The last sentence of the paragraph gives the attitude a new name: "In a widest sense we can also call the personal or motivational attitude the *practical*: it is always a matter of the active or suffering I, and that in the proper inner sense" (Hua IV 190). The practical is defined by activity and passivity of the ego. The motivation relation is a relation of doing and suffering, and its things are things that solicit. The *Gegenstrahlen* [counter-rays] of § 22 (row 14), by which the desired object attracts the ego, become in § 50 the general form of the person's relation to its world.

The concept has a prehistory in the book. In § 18 a the kinaesthetic courses "motivate" the courses of appearance, and the apprehension of a thing as oriented is "not thinkable without such relations of motivation" (Hua IV 58, row 9). In § 34 a footnote demands that "the concept of 'external circumstances' must be extended to the objects that motivate the subject in its behaviour" (Hua IV 139 n., row 20). § 50 states the extension as a substitution. The relation to circumstances that made the thing real in § 15 c (row 7) is replaced, for the person, by a relation to motives.

Section III carries the concept furthest in § 56, whose title calls motivation "the fundamental lawfulness of spiritual life". There motivation is "subjective-objective 'causality'", "a causality that is not real causality but has a wholly peculiar sense: that of *motivational causality*" (Hua IV 216, :3515, row 36). And there "the 'because–so' of motivation has an entirely different sense from causation in the sense of nature" (Hua IV 229, :3626, row 39). The sentence read here is the first statement of the substitution, and its terms recur in § 62's account of spirit and nature. Spirit "acts into" nature without causality in nature's sense, because the circumstances of spirit are "the 'surrounding world' and other spirits" (Hua IV 283, :4367). What § 50 sets in place of causality is the relation that, by § 62, defines the reality of spirit.

## §51, Hua IV 191: comprehension

Reads [§51.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 51.

In the personalistic attitude the other is not first a body to which a soul is attached. The other is understood "without further ado" as a personal subject, related to the same objects we are related to: earth and sky, the room we share, a picture we look at. Husserl calls this understanding *Komprehension* [comprehension]. Persons and a common surrounding world constitute themselves together. An I becomes a person in the normal sense only when comprehension establishes the relation to a common world. Personhood is social by essence.

> In the *comprehending experience of the existence of the other* we thus understand him without further ado as personal subject and thereby as related to objectities to which we too are related: to earth and sky, to field and forest, to the room in which "we" are together, to a picture that we see, and so on. We are in relation to a common surrounding world — we are in a personal association: that belongs *together*. We could not be persons for others if a common surrounding world did not stand over against us in a communality, an intentional connectedness of our life; correlatively speaking, the one constitutes itself essentially with the other. Each I can become a person in the normal sense for itself and others, a person in the personal association, only when comprehension establishes the relation to a common surrounding world.

German:

> In der *komprehensiven Erfahrung vom Dasein des Anderen* verstehen wir ihn also ohne weiteres als personales Subjekt und dabei auf Objektitäten bezogen, auf die auch wir bezogen sind: auf Erde und Himmel, auf Feld und Wald, auf das Zimmer, in dem „wir“ gemeinsam weilen, auf ein Bild, das wir sehen usw. Wir sind in Beziehung auf eine gemeinsame Umwelt — wir sind in einem personalen Verband: das gehört *zusammen*. Wir könnten für Andere nicht Personen sein, wenn uns nicht in einer Gemeinsamkeit, einer intentionalen Verbundenheit unseres Lebens eine gemeinsame Umwelt gegenüber stünde; korrelativ gesprochen: eins konstituiert sich wesensmäßig mit dem anderen. Jedes Ich kann für sich und andere erst zur Person im normalen Sinn, zur Person im personalen Verband werden, wenn Komprehension die Beziehung auf eine gemeinsame Umwelt herstellt.

The term is Latin and differs from the German word it competes with. *Einfühlung* [empathy, literally "feeling-into"], the word of Section II, names an act directed at another's inner life. *Komprehension* (from *comprehendere*, to grasp together) names a grasp that takes in the other and the world the other is related to. The first sentence makes this exact. "In the *comprehending experience of the existence of the other*" (italic), we understand him "without further ado" (*ohne weiteres*) "as personal subject and thereby as related to objectities to which we too are related". The list gives the shared objects in pairs, "earth and sky, field and forest", and then in places of shared life, "the room in which 'we' are together". The quotation marks on "we" (*„wir“*) show that the first-person plural is the product of the comprehension.

The second sentence states the correlation in a formula: "We are in relation to a common surrounding world — we are in a personal association: that belongs *together*" (*das gehört zusammen*). The dash equates the two clauses, and the italic *zusammen* [together] says that neither comes first. The third sentence argues it in the counterfactual: "We could not be persons for others if a common surrounding world did not stand over against us". The fourth states the condition: an I "can become a person in the normal sense … only (*erst*) when comprehension establishes the relation to a common surrounding world". The particle *erst* [only then] puts comprehension before personhood in the order of constitution. "In the normal sense" leaves room for the abnormal case: a person without a common world would be a limit case, not a person in the full sense.

The paragraph is followed by an expansion that the restored pages make available. "The *common surrounding world* receives commonalities of a new and higher sense through the acts of personal mutual determination that take place on the ground of reciprocal comprehensions" (Hua IV 191). Persons "apprehend one another comprehensively", first in that "the one understands the bodiliness of the other and its spiritual sense as body", "interpreting facial expression, gestures, spoken words as manifestation of personal life". They also "'determine one another'", acting "jointly and not only singly" (Hua IV 191). There is "another form of the effect of persons on persons": they "direct themselves toward one another" and perform acts "with the intention of being understood". So arise "relations of *mutual agreement* (*Einverständnis*): to speech follows answer", and to the demand (*Zumutung*) "that the one makes on the other follows the as it were answering turn, agreement or refusal". The surrounding world constituted in such agreement is called "*communicative*" (Hua IV 192–93, :3298–:3303). Comprehension is the first level. Agreement is the second. Only the second makes a community.

The section's apparatus shows how unfinished Husserl found it. At 193,1ff.: "that does not suffice". At 193,14ff.: "not quite clear and to be continued in improved form". At 194,12f. "one-sided understanding into" (*einseitigen Hineinverstehens*) is his addition, where Landgrebe's copy had *Einverstehens* [understanding-with]. At 195,34: "concept of the outer world still to be separated off!" (apparatus to 193–195). The late Husserl is refining the steps between comprehension and community. He leaves the paragraph read here unrevised.

The concept runs through the rest of the book in both directions. In § 56 e "the givenness of the fellow human being through comprehension" is "common to both" attitudes, "but comprehension functions differently in the two cases", "in the one, nature, in the other, spirit, is what is comprehended" (Hua IV 228). In § 56 h comprehension is the apprehension of the "comprehensive unity" of body and spirit, and empathy is redefined as "the apprehension that understands the sense" (Hua IV 244, :3743, row 40). And in § 64 the loss of nature would take with it "the possibility of a comprehension that presupposes a certain intersubjectivity of the body" (Hua IV 297, :4597). The word introduced in § 51 for the grasp of the other with his world becomes the word for every grasp of a "unity of body and sense".

## §53, Hua IV 208–10: the vicious circle

Reads [§53.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 53.

The naturalistic analysis began with nature and found persons in it as natural objects with a surplus over their bodies. The personalistic analysis began with persons and found nature as a correlate constituted in their community. Each analysis treats as subordinate what the other treats as presupposed. Husserl calls this "a vicious circle", and the rest of the section and book is devoted to dissolving it. The circle is not resolved by choosing a side. It is resolved by showing which of the two relations founds the other.

> We get here, it seems, into a vicious circle. For if at the beginning we posited nature simply, in the way every natural scientist and everyone naturalistically attuned otherwise does, and took human beings as realities that have a *plus* over their physical bodiliness, then persons were subordinate natural objects, components of nature. But if we pursued the essence of personality, then nature presented itself as something constituting itself in the intersubjective association of persons, and thus presupposing it.

German:

> Wir geraten hier, scheint es, in einen bösen Zirkel. Denn setzten wir zu Anfang die Natur schlechthin, in der Weise, wie es jeder Naturforscher und jeder naturalistisch Eingestellte sonst tut, und faßten wir die Menschen als Realitäten, die über ihre physische Leiblichkeit ein *plus* haben, so waren die Personen untergeordnete Naturobjekte, Bestandstücke der Natur. Gingen wir aber dem Wesen der Personalität nach, so stellte sich Natur als ein im intersubjektiven Verband der Personen sich Konstituierendes, also ihn Voraussetzendes dar.

The circle is stated as two conditionals in the past tense, each one a stage of the book. "If at the beginning we posited nature simply" describes Sections I and II, and "if we pursued the essence of personality" describes §§ 49–52. Each conditional yields a relation of subordination. In the first, "persons were subordinate natural objects, components of nature" (*untergeordnete Naturobjekte, Bestandstücke der Natur*). In the second, nature presented itself "as something constituting itself in the intersubjective association of persons, and thus presupposing it". The two predicates are opposed: *untergeordnet* [subordinate] and *voraussetzend* [presupposing]. The verb of § 49 e (row 31), "subordinates itself", is used here of persons. The circle is that each attitude subordinates the other's object to its own.

Three words limit the claim. "It seems" (*scheint es*) makes the circle provisional. "Simply" (*schlechthin*) marks the naturalist's positing of nature as unqualified, without the index of § 49 d (row 29). And the italic Latin "*plus*" quotes the naturalistic description of the person: a reality with a surplus over its body. The word comes from § 34, where the apprehension of a person "seems to contain a *plus*" (Hua IV 140, row 20). In § 53 the *plus* is what the naturalistic attitude sees of the person, and the personalistic attitude has shown that the person is not a surplus at all.

The apparatus shows that the page before the circle was heavily rewritten, in a way that sharpens it. The sentences "This naturalistically considered world is not the world. Rather, the world is pregiven as everyday world, and within it the theoretical interest arises for man", ending "But that is not all" (Hua IV 208–209), are an insertion "by H. in L." (apparatus to 208,25–209,2). The everyday world, *Alltagswelt*, enters § 53 in the late revision, as the ground on which natural science grows. Husserl then put the next sentences in deletion brackets, with the marginal note "that is quite wrong!" (*das ist ganz verkehrt!*). They said that to reflect in this attitude means "performing in advance the theoretical positing of 'nature'", so that "the person is eo ipso apprehended as naturally existing, as bodily annex" (apparatus to 209,7–10). The edition prints them. The late Husserl thus deletes, as wrong, a description of the naturalistic attitude's power to absorb the person, and adds, as right, a pregiven everyday world in which both attitudes arise. The circle is rewritten from within. In the revised text the first term, "nature simply", is no longer the beginning. The everyday world is.

The paragraphs after the circle begin the resolution, and the apparatus marks them too. "A change of attitude means nothing other than a thematic transition from one direction of apprehension into another". The question is "whether it is really a matter of two worlds", "separated by cardinal differences of being", which need not mean that "the two worlds have nothing to do with one another" (Hua IV 210). The model is given in examples: "the world of ideas and the world of experience", pure consciousness and "the transcendent unities constituted in it", "things as appearances" and "the things of physics" (Hua IV 210–11). Husserl put most of this in deletion brackets, from "Uns handelt es sich hier" to "jene konstituierten Einheiten usw." (apparatus to 210,21–211,12). The model of two worlds mediated by relations of sense is the one the late Husserl struck.

The resolution comes in §§ 62–64, and it does not choose between the two conditionals. It shows that the second founds the first. § 62 places the natural scientist inside his "life-world" (Hua IV 288, row 49). § 64 states the asymmetry by a thought experiment that reverses § 18 f: "If we strike out all spirits from the world, there is no more nature. But if we strike out nature … something still remains: the spirit as individual spirit" (Hua IV 297, :4597). The relation of subordination in the first conditional is real. It holds within the naturalistic attitude, and the naturalistic attitude holds within the personal world. The circle is broken by an order of founding, and § 53's late insertion of the *Alltagswelt* is the first statement of that order.

## §55, Hua IV 216–17: motivational causality and the ghost

Reads [§55.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 55.

The spiritual ego relates to its surrounding world through relations that are not real relations but subject–object relations. Husserl names them with a paradox: a "causality" that is not real causality, "motivational causality". The surrounding world to which they hold "can contain not only realities but also ghosts", and the test of the concept is the object that does not exist. If the thing turns out to be nothing, it no longer motivates me as a thing. But the illusory object as such, the noema struck through, can still act on me. Motivation needs a meant object, not an existing one.

> We find a wealth of relations between the posited objects and the *spiritual* subject, as we now call the subject of intentionality, relations which in the sense indicated are relations between what is posited as reality and the positing I, but not real relations, rather *subject–object relations*. To these belong relations of *subjective-objective* "causality", a causality that is not real causality but has an entirely peculiar sense: that of *motivational causality*.
>
> In the contrary case they are nothing, they no longer determine or motivate me in this respect; but possibly the illusory objects as such act on me, the noemata in their being struck through, in their way, as in another case before the noemata in their primal-doxic character of being.

German:

> … dann finden wir eine Fülle von Beziehungen zwischen den gesetzten Objekten und dem *geistigen* Subjekt, wie wir das Subjekt der Intentionalität jetzt nennen, die im bezeichneten Sinne Beziehungen zwischen dem als Realität Gesetzten und dem setzenden Ich sind, aber nicht reale Beziehungen, sondern *Subjekt-Objekt-Beziehungen*. Dahin gehören Beziehungen *subjektiv-objektiver* „Kausalität“, einer Kausalität, die nicht reale Kausalität ist, sondern einen völlig eigenen Sinn hat: den der *Motivationskausalität*.
>
> Im gegenteiligen Fall sind sie nichts, bestimmen, motivieren mich in dieser Beziehung nicht mehr; ev. aber wirken auf mich die illusionären Objekte als solche, die Noemata in ihrer Durchstreichung, in ihrer Art, wie in anderer vorher die Noemata in ihrem urdoxischen Seinscharakter.

*Note on the text.* The file prints Husserl's spaced type as split italics ("*g e i -* *stigen*", "*Subjekt-Objekt-* *Beziehungen*", "*subjektiv-* *objektiver*", :3515). They are given here as single italic words.

The first sentence is built on a correction inside a definition. The relations are "relations between what is posited as reality and the positing I". The words "as reality" (*als Realität*) carry the weight: the relata are a positing and a posited, not two realities. Hence "not real relations, rather *subject–object relations*". The subject gets a new name in passing, "as we now call the subject of intentionality": the *spiritual* subject. "Spirit", in Section III, is the name for the subject of intentionality taken with its world.

The second sentence names the relation with a word Husserl puts in quotation marks and then denies. "'Causality'" (*„Kausalität“*), "a causality that is not real causality". The paradox is resolved by a compound: *Motivationskausalität* [motivational causality]. The adjective that precedes it is double, "*subjective-objective*", the two terms of the relation joined by a hyphen. The pair returns in § 56 f as the "because–so" (row 39). It is a relation of grounds, and Husserl keeps the word "causality" because it is a relation of determination.

The paragraph after the definition gives the examples, and they are deliberately humble. The object exercises "a greater or lesser 'stimulus'", "'awakens' an interest", and "the tendency flows off freely in the turning-toward, or only after counter-tendencies have been weakened, overcome". "All this takes place between *I and intentional object*" (Hua IV 216). The object stimulates me "by virtue of its *experienced* properties: not the physical ones, of which I need know nothing" (Hua IV 216). It stimulates me "to eat", as "a good of the class foodstuffs" (Hua IV 217). Then come value and will: "the object has value-qualities and is 'experienced' with them", and "I can also transform these things creatively" (Hua IV 217). The stimulus vocabulary of § 50 (row 33) returns, now under its proper title.

The quoted second paragraph is the test case, and the paragraph that contains the definition opens by announcing it. The surrounding world is "the world posited by it, to which not only realities but also, for example, ghosts can belong" (*z. B. auch Gespenster*, Hua IV 216, :3515). A ghost is the limit of the motivation relation, because it is an object that motivates and does not exist. In the case where the things turn out "to be nothing", they "no longer determine or motivate me in this respect". The words "in this respect" (*in dieser Beziehung*) limit the loss. They no longer motivate me as things. "But possibly (*ev.*) the illusory objects as such act on me". The noema struck through (*in ihrer Durchstreichung*) is still an object for me, now with the character of nullity, and it can still act on me, as a ghost can frighten one who no longer believes in it. The parallel with the noema "in its primal-doxic character of being" (*urdoxisch*) says that crossing-out is a modification of the same structure, not its absence.

The apparatus shows that this test case is the most insecure sentence of the paragraph. Husserl put the clause "the noemata in … character of being" in deletion brackets in Landgrebe's typescript, and he added the word *urdoxischen* [primal-doxic] (apparatus to 217,19–21). The edition prints both. The deletion and the addition point in different directions. Husserl sharpened the term for the positive case and then struck the parallel that used it. What survives his revision without question is the first half: "but possibly the illusory objects as such act on me". The noema as the bearer of motivation is the late Husserl's thesis. The exact correlation between crossing-out and primal doxa is what he left in doubt.

The section's previous paragraphs prepare this by separating two relations. "1. this *intentional relation*: I have the object given … 2. the *real relation*: the object O stands in a real-causal relation to me, to the I-man, thus first to the body". "The real relation falls away if the thing does not exist: the intentional relation remains". That the two run "parallel" whenever the object exists "is a psychophysical fact. It does not lie in the intentional relation itself, which suffers nothing through the non-actuality of the object" (Hua IV 215–16). Motivational causality is the name for the relation that survives the thing's non-existence. Ghosts belong to the surrounding world because the surrounding world is defined by this relation.

The commentary calls this "the first full statement" of motivational causality. Its scope reaches the book's close. In § 64 the "absolute" spirit is what remains if nature is struck out (Hua IV 297, :4597, row 50). § 55 shows the first form of that independence. The spirit's relations to its world do not depend on the existence of the world's objects. They depend on its sense.

## §56 b, Hua IV 222–23: hidden motives

Reads [§56 b.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 56 b.

Motivation is not limited to position-takings that follow from position-takings. It covers "the whole realm of associations and habits", the sedimentations of past acts and the "drives" of passivity. Each element in this dark ground is motivated, and it has its "psychic grounds" that one can ask about: "how do I come to this?" That one can ask this "characterizes all motivation whatsoever". The grounds are often deeply hidden, but they can be brought to light by "psychoanalysis". The understanding is that motivation is defined by the possibility of a question, and the question reaches into what is unnoticed.

> The individual element therein is motivated in the dark ground, has its "psychic grounds", after which one can ask: how do I come to this, what has brought me to it? That one can ask so characterizes all motivation whatsoever. The "motives" are often deeply hidden, but can be brought to light by "psychoanalysis". A thought "reminds" me of other thoughts, calls a past experience back into memory, and so on. In some cases this can be perceived. In most cases, however, the motivation is indeed actually present in consciousness, but it does not come into relief, it is unnoticed or unnoticeable ("unconscious").

German:

> Das einzelne darin ist im dunklen Untergrunde motiviert, hat seine „seelischen Gründe“, nach denen man fragen kann: wie komme ich darauf, was hat mich dazu gebracht? Daß man so fragen kann, charakterisiert alle Motivation überhaupt. Die „Motive“ sind oft tief verborgen, aber durch „Psychoanalyse“ zutage zu fördern. Ein Gedanke „erinnert“ mich an andere Gedanken, ruft ein vergangenes Erlebnis in die Erinnerung zurück usw. In manchen Fällen kann das wahrgenommen werden. In den meisten Fällen aber ist die Motivation zwar im Bewußtsein wirklich vorhanden, aber sie kommt nicht zur Abhebung, sie ist unbemerkt oder unmerklich („unbewußt“).

*Note on the text.* The question "wie komme ich darauf" is in spaced type and bold in the file (:3567), with margin line numbers inside the sentence. They are dropped here. A footnote marker after "seelischen Gründe" is dropped too; the note is quoted below.

The passage defines motivation by a question, and the question is in the first person. "How do I come to this, what has brought me to it?" (*wie komme ich darauf, was hat mich dazu gebracht?*). The next sentence makes the possibility of asking it the criterion: "That one can ask so characterizes all motivation whatsoever". The criterion is not that the subject knows the answer. It is that the question has a sense. A natural cause is found by investigation from outside. A motive is something the subject can ask after in its own life, even when the answer is hidden.

The quotation marks are thick, and each one marks a borrowed word. "'Psychic grounds'" (*„seelischen Gründe“*), "the 'motives'", "'psychoanalysis'", "a thought 'reminds' me", and at the end "('unconscious')". Husserl uses the vocabulary of the psychology of his day and holds it at a distance. "Psychoanalysis" is not endorsed as a theory. It is named as a technique for bringing hidden motives "to light" (*zutage zu fördern*), the miner's phrase. "Unconscious" is given only in parentheses, as the popular name for something Husserl describes otherwise: the motivation is "actually present in consciousness", but it "does not come into relief" (*kommt nicht zur Abhebung*), it is "unnoticed or unnoticeable". The unconscious, so described, is the background of consciousness, not something outside it.

The footnote to "psychic grounds" asks the question the passage raises: "There one speaks, in the 'egoless' sphere, of the I that has been motivated. Is the I there the stream itself?" (Hua IV 222 n., :3569). The motivations of association are passive. No act of the ego is in play, and yet one says "I" was motivated. The quotation marks on "egoless" (*„ichlosen“*) show that the description is not settled. Section III will answer by the ego's "dark underground", which § 61 calls a "root-soil" (row 47). § 56 b leaves it as a question.

The paragraph that follows sharpens the contrast between the two kinds of motivation. "Associative motivations" and "motivations in the pregnant sense of ego-motivation (reason-motivation)" differ as habit differs from reason. Once a connection has occurred in a stream of consciousness, "there exists in the same stream the tendency" that a similar new beginning continue "in the sense of the similarity". When I reflect on this, "the existence of the similar piece demands the existence of the similar complementary piece. That is a *law of motivation*", a "'primal' demand, a demand of reason" (Hua IV 223). Habit is the "first law", and every position-taking is "subject to it" (Hua IV 223). The two causalities "intertwine": "the 'causality' in the underground of association and apperception and the 'causality' of reason, the passive and the active or free" (Hua IV 224). The word "causality" in quotation marks returns from § 55 (row 36), now for the passive and the active alike.

The limit of the passage comes in § 56 f. "The physiological processes in the sense-organs, in nerve and ganglion cells do not motivate me". "What I do not 'know', what does not stand over against me in my experiencing … as represented, perceived, remembered, thought etc., does not 'determine' me spiritually. And what is not in my experiences, be it unnoticed or implicitly intentionally contained, does not motivate me, not even in an unconscious way" (Hua IV 231, :3637). The "not even in an unconscious way" (*auch nicht in unbewußter Weise*) fixes the boundary that § 56 b's "unconscious" left open. The hidden motive can be deeply hidden, but it must be in my experiences. What psychoanalysis can bring to light is motivation. What physiology can find is cause. Husserl's openness to the first is bought by excluding the second from the spirit.

## §56 d, Hua IV 226–27: nature dissolved into motivations

Reads [§56 d.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 56 d.

Motivation is not only the lawfulness of personal life. It also constitutes nature. When we look at the structure of thing-constituting consciousness, "all nature with space, time, causality and so on" is "completely dissolved into a web of immanent motivations". The functional dependencies found there are neither the natural-scientific dependencies of a real thing on a real body, nor dependencies of appearances on realities. They are relations of motivation, and they can be studied from the side of the noeses or from the side of the noemata. The commentary calls the sentence "the strongest formulation in the section" of the thesis that nature is constituted.

> If we fix our eye on the structure of thing-constituting consciousness, we see that all nature with space, time, causality etc. is completely dissolved into a web of immanent motivations. In the unity of the total experience that is consciousness of a thing being there and an I being here with its body, we find various objectivities distinguishable and we find functional dependencies that are not dependencies of the actual thing on the actual body and actual I in the world, in short, not natural-scientific psychic and psychophysical dependencies, but also not dependencies of subjective appearances (as had by the experiencing subject) on real objectities that are posited or accepted as real.

German:

> Fassen wir die Struktur des dingkonstituierenden Bewußtseins ins Auge, so sehen wir, daß alle Natur mit Raum, Zeit, Kausalität usw. völlig aufgelöst ist in ein Gewebe von immanenten Motivationen. In der Einheit des Gesamterlebnisses, das Bewußtsein ist von einem dort seienden Ding und einem hier seienden Ich mit seinem Leib, finden wir mancherlei Gegenständlichkeiten unterscheidbar und finden wir funktionelle Abhängigkeiten, die nicht Abhängigkeiten des wirklichen Dinges vom wirklichen Leib und wirklichen Ich in der Welt sind, kurzum, nicht naturwissenschaftliche psychische und psychophysische Abhängigkeiten, aber auch nicht Abhängigkeiten von subjektiven Erscheinungen (als vom erlebenden Subjekt gehabten) zu realen Objektitäten, die als reale gesetzt oder hingenommen sind.

The first sentence states a result in the perfect passive: nature "is completely dissolved" (*völlig aufgelöst ist*). The verb *auflösen* means to dissolve, as a solid in a liquid, and to resolve, as a problem or a chord. Both senses are in play. Nature "with space, time, causality etc." is not denied. It is analyzed into its constituents. And the constituents are "a web" (*Gewebe*) of "immanent motivations". The image of a woven fabric says that nothing is left over: no thread of nature outside the motivations. The phrase "with space, time, causality etc." lists exactly the forms that the naturalistic attitude treats as given. § 56 d claims that each of them is a product of motivation.

The second sentence states what kind of dependency is found, by two denials. The functional dependencies in thing-constituting consciousness are "not dependencies of the actual thing on the actual body and actual I in the world". The repeated "actual" (*wirklich*) names the naturalistic relata, and "in short" (*kurzum*) names the sciences that study them: "not natural-scientific psychic and psychophysical dependencies". The second denial is subtler. They are "also not dependencies of subjective appearances … on real objectities that are posited or accepted as real". That rules out the representationalist picture, in which the appearance depends on the real thing it represents. The appearance does not depend on the thing. Both are terms in a web of motivations, and the thing is their unity.

The paragraph goes on to name the two directions of study. "We can now consider the noetic experiences according to their relations of motivation", "whereby progress from positing to positing, i.e. positing 'in consequence', occurs". "Or we consider the thetic correlates, the themata, in their noematic belonging-together, whereby again on this side the correlative 'in consequence' occurs" (Hua IV 227). The apparatus records that the late Husserl wanted this distinction made explicit. At 220,15ff. he wrote in the margin: "to be clearly separated: noetic and noematic concept of motivation" (apparatus to 220,15ff.). The subsection's title, "Motivation on the noetic and noematic side", is the answer to that demand.

The next paragraph draws the difficulty, and it limits the dissolution. "It is not said that in the unity of my stream of experience every experience is necessary, necessarily conditioned by the preceding". When I see "a shooting star flare up in the night sky" or "quite unexpectedly hear the crack of a whip", the thesis is not a thesis "in consequence". "Yet here too a kind of motivation is to be shown, which is enclosed in the *form of inner time-consciousness*. This form is something absolutely fixed: the subjective form of the now, before etc. I can change nothing in it" (Hua IV 227). The web has a frame. The form of time is not one more motivation among others. It is the fixed form in which even the unmotivated is placed, so that "now this is" conditions "something will be" (Hua IV 227).

The thesis has a precedent and an echo in the corpus. *Ideas I* § 49 bears the title "Absolute consciousness as residue of world-annihilation" (Hua III 103, :2350): if the world is thought away, consciousness remains. § 56 d says the same in the opposite direction. Nature is not thought away. It is dissolved into the consciousness that constitutes it. Heidegger, in 1925, read the end of *Ideen II* as "the same consideration regarding pure consciousness as residue of world-annihilation", and he concluded that "ontologically everything stays as it was" (GA 20 171). The § 56 d sentence is the strongest support for his reading. Nature is dissolved, and what it dissolves into is never asked about for its own manner of being.

The same paragraph also contains Husserl's own resistance to the dissolution. Motivation in the noematic sense has the thing as its theme. The "in consequence" is found "on this side" too, in the belonging-together of the themata. The dissolution of nature into motivations does not reduce the thing to a sequence of experiences. It shows that the thing's unity is a unity of sense, correlated with a lawful course of positings. The next pages state the consequence for the natural sciences in their own terms: "the true nature of the physicist is a methodically necessary substruction of thought" (Hua IV 230–31, :3637). The nature that the web of motivations constitutes is intuitive. The nature of physics is constructed from it.

## §56 f, Hua IV 229: the "because–so"

Reads [§56 f.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 56 f.

The "because" of motivation has a different sense from the "because" of nature. No amount of causal research can improve the understanding we have when we have understood a person's motivation. The unity of a motivation is founded in the acts themselves, and asking "why" means wanting to know this connection. Natural causality has its correlate in laws that determine unambiguously what must follow. When the historian or sociologist wants to "explain", he wants to lay motivations bare, to make intelligible how people "came" to act as they did. This is Husserl's answer to the human-sciences dispute of § 48 (row 27).

> The "because–so" of motivation has an entirely different sense from causation in the sense of nature. No causal research, however far it goes, can improve the understanding that we have when we have understood the motivation of a person. The unity of motivation is a connection founded in the relevant acts themselves, and when we ask about the "because", about the ground of a personal behaviour, we want nothing other than to come to know this connection.
>
> When, on the other hand, it is said in the sphere of the human sciences that the historian, sociologist, researcher of culture wants to "explain" facts of the human sciences, this means that he wants to lay motivations bare, he wants to make intelligible how the people in question "came" to behave in this or that way, what influences they underwent and exercised, what determined them in and to the commonality of their activity, and so on.

German:

> Das „Weil — So“ der Motivation hat einen ganz anderen Sinn als Kausation im Sinne der Natur. Keine noch so weitgehende Kausalforschung kann das Verständnis verbessern, das wir haben, wenn wir die Motivation einer Person verstanden haben. Die Einheit der Motivation ist ein in den betreffenden Akten selbst fundierter Zusammenhang, und wenn wir nach dem „Weil“, nach dem Grunde eines persönlichen Verhaltens fragen, so wollen wir nichts anderes als diesen Zusammenhang kennen lernen.
>
> Wenn es dagegen in der geisteswissenschaftlichen Sphäre heißt, es wolle der Historiker, Soziologe, Kulturforscher geisteswissenschaftliche Fakta „erklären“, so heißt das, er vill Motivationen klarlegen, er will verständlich machen, wie die betreffenden Menschen „dazu kamen“, sich so und so zu verhalten, welche Beeinflussungen sie erfahren und ausgeübt haben, was sie in der und zu der Gemeinsamkeit des Wirkens bestimmt hat usw.

*Note on the text.* "er vill" is an OCR slip for *er will* [he wants]; it is quoted as the file has it (:3626).

The first sentence names the relation by its grammar. "The 'because–so'" (*Das „Weil — So“*) is the connective of a motivated sentence: because I was angry, so I left. The dash joins two words, as the hyphen joined "subjective-objective" in § 55 (row 36). The quotation marks set the connective off as a word under analysis. The claim is about its sense: "entirely different" (*ganz anderen Sinn*) from "causation in the sense of nature". The word *Kausation* is used once and not again. Natural causation has a sense, and motivation has another.

The second sentence states the consequence as a limit on research. "No causal research, however far it goes, can improve the understanding". The verb *verbessern* [improve] is chosen with care. Causal research does not refute the understanding of a motive, and it does not compete with it. It cannot add to it. The understanding is complete in its kind "when we have understood the motivation of a person". The third sentence gives the reason. "The unity of motivation is a connection founded in the relevant acts themselves". A motive is not a third thing between two acts. It is their connection, and it is founded in them. Asking "why" (*nach dem „Weil“*) is asking for that connection, "and nothing other".

Between the two quoted paragraphs Husserl states the contrast with natural causality. "The causality of nature in the natural sciences has its correlate in natural laws, according to which it is to be determined unambiguously (at least in the realm of physical nature) what must follow under unambiguously determining circumstances" (Hua IV 229). The parenthesis "at least in the realm of physical nature" (*mindestens im Gebiet der physischen Natur*) concedes that psychophysical laws may not meet the ideal. The contrast is between the unambiguous determination of a law and the understanding of a connection.

The second paragraph applies the contrast to the human sciences, and it does so by redefining a word. The historian, sociologist and "researcher of culture" want to "explain" (*„erklären“*, in quotation marks), and "this means" (*so heißt das*) that they want to lay motivations bare. The quotation marks take the word from the dispute of § 48. Dilthey had opposed explanation to understanding. Husserl keeps the historian's word "explain" and gives it the sense of understanding. What the historian explains is "how the people in question 'came' (*„dazu kamen“*) to behave". The phrase is the § 56 b question "how do I come to this?" (*wie komme ich darauf*) in the third person (row 37). The historian asks of others the question that defines motivation in the first person.

The list at the end widens the field from individual to social: "what influences they underwent and exercised, what determined them in and to the commonality of their activity". The motivations of the human sciences include the "personal effects" of § 51 (row 34): one person determining another through agreement and demand. The historian's object is a web of motivations among persons.

The text that follows draws the line between the two "because"s once more, with the physicist's nature. "The true nature of the physicist is a methodically necessary substruction of thought", and "it makes no sense" to underlay motivation, "which is to be grasped originarily-intuitively", with "substructions of thought" (Hua IV 230–31). Spirit taken "in one with the body as natural object" is also caught up in natural causality, which is then "natural-inductive" (*natural-induktiv*, Hua IV 231). The apparatus shows that this adjective is the late Husserl's (apparatus to 231,13). The revision distinguishes, again, the nature-directed from the natural, as in § 49 d (row 29).

In § 48 Husserl promised "methodically secure solutions" where Dilthey had only intuition (row 27). § 56 f is the solution. It is not a methodology of the human sciences but a phenomenology of the "because". Heidegger's verdict on the outcome, that "Husserl does not get beyond Dilthey" (GA 20 173), can be tested on this page. The distinction between explaining and understanding is Dilthey's. What Husserl adds is the account of motivation in the acts themselves. Whether that is a step beyond Dilthey depends on whether one asks, with Heidegger, about the being of the one who is motivated.

## §56 h, Hua IV 244: empathy as understanding sense

Reads [§56 h.16](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), sixteenth paragraph under § 56 h.

The unity of body and spirit is a "comprehensive" unity, like the unity of a written page and its sense. Empathy into persons is redefined accordingly. It is "nothing other than the apprehension that understands the sense", that grasps the body in its sense and in the unity of sense it is meant to carry. To carry out empathy is to grasp "an objective spirit", to see a human being. There is no apprehension of the body first as a physical object, to which something psychic is then added. The unity is an objectivation of a higher level, in which the lower and higher layers are distinguished only afterwards.

> *Empathy* into *persons* is nothing other than the apprehension that *understands* the sense, that is, grasps the body in its sense and in the unity of the sense that it is to carry. To carry out empathy means to grasp an *objective spirit*, to see a human being, a crowd of people and so on. Here we have no apprehending of the body as bearer of something psychic in the sense that the body is posited (experienced) as a physical object and something else is now added to it, as if it were apprehended as something in relation to or in connection with something else.

German:

> Einfühlung in Personen ist nichts anderes als diejenige Auffassung, die eben den Sinn versteht, d. i. den Leib in seinem Sinn erfaßt und in der Einheit des Sinnes, die er tragen soll. Einfühlung vollziehen, das heißt einen *objektiven Geist* erfassen, einen Menschen sehen, eine Menschenmenge usw. Hier haben wir kein Auffassen des Leibes als Trägers eines Psychischen in dem Sinne, daß der Leib als physisches Objekt gesetzt (erfahren) und ihm nun etwas anderes hinzugefügt, as ob er aufgefaßt würde als etwas in Beziehung zu oder in Verbindung mit etwas anderem.

*Note on the text.* The file prints no emphasis in the first sentence. Pp. 243–44 were re-converted, and the italics that the earlier conversion had on *Einfühlung*, *Personen* and *versteht* are lost (see the list's caveats); the English follows the earlier conversion. Only the spaced "*o b-* *jektiven Geist*" survives, given here as one italic phrase. "as ob" is an OCR slip for *als ob* [as if], quoted as the file has it (:3743).

The first sentence is a definition by identity, and its form is a demotion. "Empathy into persons is *nothing other than* (*nichts anderes als*) the apprehension that understands the sense". The word *Einfühlung* [empathy, literally "feeling-into"] promises a feeling one's way into another's inner life. The definition gives it a different object. It is not a feeling but an apprehension (*Auffassung*), and what it apprehends is not an inner life but "the sense" (*den Sinn*). The explicative clause says what that means: "that is, grasps the body in its sense and in the unity of the sense that it is to carry". The body is the bearer, and "is to carry" (*tragen soll*) gives the relation a normative note. The body is meant to carry a sense, as a page is meant to carry a text.

The second sentence equates the act with three objects. "To carry out empathy means to grasp an *objective spirit*, to see a human being, a crowd of people and so on." The equation is bold. "Objective spirit" names spirit that has become an object, there for others in a body. Husserl applies the phrase to the single human being seen. To see a human being is to grasp objective spirit, spirit made visible in a body. The verb "see" (*sehen*) matters. Empathy, so understood, is a kind of seeing, not an inference.

The third sentence excludes the model it replaces. "Here we have no apprehending of the body as bearer of something psychic in the sense that the body is posited (experienced) as a physical object and something else is now added to it." That is the model of § 49 a, introjection (row 28), and of the naturalistic attitude, which takes the other as body with a psychic annex. The next sentence states the positive model: "It is a matter of an objectivation of a higher level, which so overlays that of the other layer of apprehension that unity of one object is constituted". That object "implies (without any connection, which would presuppose separation) an object-layer of lower and higher level, which are only distinguished afterwards" (Hua IV 244). The words "without any connection, which would presuppose separation" make the point. Body and spirit are not connected, because they were never separate.

The model comes from the subsection's opening, which the passage closes. § 56 h begins with the book. "When I read the 'pages and lines' of this book", physical things stand there: "the book is a body, the pages are pages of paper sheets, the lines are blackenings". But "I 'live comprehending in the sense'" (*ich „lebe komprehendierend im Sinn“*, Hua IV 236, :3678). The page after the passage returns to the image: "so little do we, in reading, posit the written sign on the paper in an actual experiential thesis", "the written sign 'appears', but we 'live' in the performance of the sense. Likewise the body appears, but we carry out the acts of comprehension" (Hua IV 244). The body of the other is read as a page is read. The analogy has a limit that the passage marks by its word "persons". A book carries a sense that a spirit gave it. A body carries the sense of the spirit that lives in it.

The apparatus shows that Husserl found these pages repetitive. At 243,12ff. he wrote "repeated p. 244", at 244,3ff. "following page repeated", and at 244,19ff. "repetition of p. 243 with some good turns of phrase" (apparatus to 243–44). At 245,10ff.: "deepenings in the repetition". The edition prints the repetition. The definition of empathy read here falls in the stretch that Husserl judged a repetition with "good turns of phrase". The judgment fits it. The definition is a restatement of § 51's comprehension (row 34) in the vocabulary of sense.

The redefinition of empathy has a consequence the book draws in § 64. Comprehension "presupposes a certain intersubjectivity of the body" (Hua IV 297, :4597, row 50). If empathy is the understanding of a sense carried by a body, then without a body that can appear to others there is no empathy. The spirit can survive the loss of nature, but not as a person among persons. § 56 h states the positive side of that limit. The other is given in the unity of body and sense, and the unity is primary.

## §57, Hua IV 249–51: is "habit" the good title?

Reads [§57.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 57.

§ 57 describes how the personal ego becomes known to itself. In reflection I experience how I "behave" under various circumstances, how I am "accustomed" to be motivated, and so what sort of personal subject I am. The person, so known, is a unity of regularities, given as a thing is given after the thing-apperception has developed. Husserl's own notes, printed as footnotes, attack this account. Is "habit" the good title? Do I not have my position-takings "not from mere habits but from freedom and capacities"? An active capacity is not a habit. The understanding the passage carries is a self-correction: the inductive account of the person is incomplete because a person is a subject of capacities.

> But is "habit" the good title here? Do I as I not have my position-takings and my manner of taking positions, and not from mere habits but from freedom and capacities of various kinds?
>
> But here the egoic capacities and the somatological ones of the lower stratum always and from the outset come into consideration. An active capacity is surely not a habit, not an inductively constituted peculiarity, a merely associative formation, if we take association in the ordinary sense. The analysis of personality is thus very imperfect here.

German:

> Aber ist „Gewohnheit“ hier der gute Titel? Habe ich als Ich nicht meine Stellungnahmen und meine Art Stellung zu nehmen und nicht aus bloßen Gewohnheiten sondern aus Freiheit und Vermögen verschiedener Art?
>
> Es kommen dabei aber immer und von vornherein die ichlichen Vermögen und die somatologischen der Unterschicht in Betracht. Ein aktives Vermögen ist doch nicht eine Gewohnheit, nicht eine induktiv konstituierte Eigenheit, ein bloß assoziatives Gebilde, wenn wir Assoziation im gewöhnlichen Sinne nehmen. Die Analyse der Personalität ist hier also sehr unvollkommen.

*Note on the text.* Both paragraphs are notes, not main text. The first is the end of a footnote to p. 250 (:3835–:3839). The second closes a long footnote that the file splices into the body between pp. 250 and 251 (:3824–:3827); the apparatus identifies that note's text as a supplementary sheet (*Beiblatt*) by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 250).

The main text of § 57 is confident. "I, the personal I, am, after the development of the empirical ego-apperception, a pregiven for myself, just as the thing is pregiven to me after the thing-apperception has developed". As experience teaches me the thing, "so also for the empirical I. I go deliberately 'into experience' and come to know myself 'better'" (Hua IV 250). Reflection teaches me "that my pure ego-acts unfold in a regular way under their subjective circumstances". I can see "eidetically" that the "'representation' I-person" must necessarily develop from these regular courses (Hua IV 251). The person is the apperceptive unity of regularities, and the model is the thing.

The first note questions the model by questioning one word. "But is 'habit' the good title here?" (*der gute Titel*). The quotation marks set off *Gewohnheit* as the word under suspicion. The note had begun with a distinction: "habits that I have, which I did not have at various times in the past", and "the habit-style" (Hua IV 250 n.). Then the question. The answer is put as a second question, and it names what habit leaves out: "Do I as I not have my position-takings and my manner of taking positions, and not from mere habits but from freedom and capacities of various kinds?" The pair "freedom and capacities" (*Freiheit und Vermögen*) is the counter-title. A position-taking is something I do, not something that happens in me regularly. A capacity is the power to do it.

The second note, the late sheet, says the same thing in the vocabulary of constitution. It begins with a précis of the inductive account: "The personal I is the human I". From repeated reflection on others' behaviour "an inductive apperception arises", and I come to know myself "as personal 'reality'" (Hua IV 250 n.). The scare quotes on "reality" (*„Realität“*) already mark the doubt. "But there are still many questions left." The first is about origins. A piece of inductive self-apperception arises "before the experience of others, as somatological", and "how a fixed ability is constituted (I can move my hand there, I can feel along etc.)" must be "seriously considered" (Hua IV 250 n.). The quoted paragraph follows. "An active capacity is surely not a habit, not an inductively constituted peculiarity". The particle *doch* [surely] is an objection to the main text. The verdict is short: "The analysis of personality is thus very imperfect here".

The apparatus confirms that the late Husserl judged these pages harshly. At 248,29ff. he wrote in Landgrebe's typescript: "from here on unusable, after all I have much better manuscripts", and at 249,1ff. the single word "Null" [worthless] (apparatus to 248,29ff. and 249,1ff.). At 250,1ff.: "unclear" (apparatus to 250,1ff.). The main text is the part he called unusable. The notes are where he says why. A third note asks, in the same spirit, whether the personal I arises "merely through repeated reflection on the motivations of my affections and actions", and answers: "But I as personal I am the human being among human beings". It ends: "There is much still to clarify" (*Da ist viel noch zu klären*, Hua IV 249 n., :3840–:3848).

The commentary reports the notes and treats § 59 as the correction. The text bears it out. § 58 asks whether the personal ego is constituted only through reflection (row 42), and § 59 begins, "The I as unity is a system of 'I *can*'" (row 43). The "freedom and capacities" of the first note become the subject of a section. The inductive account is not abandoned. § 60 b still says "I know myself from experience, I know what sort of character I am" (Hua IV 266). But the late margin there reads "so only inductively?" (apparatus to 266,7ff.). The question of § 57's note follows the account wherever it goes.

The passage is a rare case where the book's correction of itself is printed beside the text it corrects. The thing-model of the person was the result of § 57. The notes show that, for the person, the model leaves out the one who takes positions. § 58 answers with the person "out of life".

## §58, Hua IV 252: out of life, not out of experience

Reads [§58.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 58.

The ego constituted in reflection points back to another. Originally I am not a unity out of associative and active experience, in the sense in which a thing is a unity of experience. I am the subject of my life, and the subject develops in living. Primarily it experiences not itself but natural objects, values, tools. Primarily it shapes not itself but things into works. The ego is "originally not out of experience but out of life": it is what it is not for the ego, but as the ego itself. The commentary calls this "the deepest correction the section makes to its own inductive account".

> But even apart from the associative connections, the I constituted in reflection points back to another: originally I am really not a unity out of associative and active experience (if experience means the same as with the thing). I am the subject of my life, and in living the subject develops; primarily it experiences not itself, but it constitutes natural objects, value-things, tools etc. It forms, shapes as active primarily not itself, but things into works. The I is originally not out of experience — in the sense of associative apperception, in which unities of manifolds of connection are constituted — but out of life (it is what it is, not for the I, but itself the I).

German:

> Aber auch abgesehen von den assoziativen Zusammenhängen weist das in Reflexion konstituierte Ich auf ein anderes zurück: Ursprünglich bin ich eigentlich nicht eine Einheit aus assoziativer und aktiver Erfahrung (wenn Erfahrung dasselbe besagt wie beim Ding). Ich bin das Subjekt meines Lebens, und lebend entwickelt sich das Subjekt; es erfährt primär nicht sich, sondern es konstituiert Naturgegenstände, Wertsachen, Werkzeuge etc. Es bildet, gestaltet als aktives primär nicht sich, sondern Sachen zu Werken. Das Ich ist ursprünglich nicht aus Erfahrung — im Sinne von assoziativer Apperzeption, in der sich Einheiten von Mannigfaltigkeiten des Zusammenhanges konstituieren, sondern aus Leben (es ist, was es ist, nicht für das Ich, sondern selbst das Ich).

The passage is built on two prepositions, "out of experience" (*aus Erfahrung*) and "out of life" (*aus Leben*). The first sentence states the denial with a qualification. Originally I am "not a unity out of associative and active experience", and the parenthesis limits the sense of experience: "if experience means the same as with the thing" (*wenn Erfahrung dasselbe besagt wie beim Ding*). That is the model of § 57 (row 41), where the person was "a pregiven, just as the thing". The denial is aimed at it. The adverb *eigentlich* [really, properly] gives the sentence the tone of a correction.

The apparatus shows that the correction was sharpened late. "Associative and active" (*assoziativer und aktiver*) is an addition by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 252,16f.). So are "as active" (*als aktives*) in the next sentence and "associative" (*assoziativer*) in the last (apparatus to 252,21 and 252,23). In the earlier text the denial was simpler: originally I am not a unity "out of experience". The late Husserl specified which experience he meant. It is experience as the association that constitutes the thing, and also the active experience that learns about it. Neither makes the person originally.

The positive statement is in the first person and then in the third. "I am the subject of my life, and in living the subject develops" (*lebend entwickelt sich das Subjekt*). The participle *lebend* [living] makes life the medium of development. The subject's primary direction is outward: "primarily it experiences not itself, but it constitutes natural objects, value-things, tools". And the same holds for action: "It forms, shapes … primarily not itself, but things into works". The person is formed in forming things. Its self is a by-product of its world.

The last sentence states the result and adds a parenthesis that defines it. "The I is originally not out of experience … but out of life (it is what it is, not for the I, but itself the I)." The parenthesis separates two ways of being an ego. "For the I" (*für das Ich*) is the ego as object of its own reflection, the "Me" that the next page will name. "Itself the I" (*selbst das Ich*) is the ego that lives. On p. 253 Husserl draws the distinction explicitly: "One must therefore distinguish: 'I, who I am' on the subject-side and 'I, who I am' as object for me … the Me" (*das Mich*, Hua IV 253). The apparatus reports that this sentence, from "das Mich" to "bewußt ist", is "an insertion by H. in the Ms., not taken into account in L." (apparatus to 253,15f.).

The next paragraph draws the consequence for self-knowledge. "The I can be more and other than the I as apperceptive unity. It can have hidden capacities (dispositions), which have not yet come forth". "Someone does not 'know' himself, does not 'know' what he is; he learns to know himself". "'Learning to know oneself' is one with the development of the self-apperception, the constitution of the 'self', and this takes place in one with the development of the subject itself" (Hua IV 252). The ego of life is ahead of the ego of reflection. Self-knowledge catches up with it and never coincides with it.

The last page of § 58 states the ego's development as a kind of self-preservation. The waking ego "constantly constitutes its counterpart, and in this process it is motivated and ever newly motivated, and not arbitrarily but practising '*self-preservation*'". "The I practises itself, accustoms itself", "it 'acquires' capacities, sets itself goals and in reaching the goals acquires practical capacities", for example, "I want to be able to play a piano piece smoothly" (Hua IV 253). The habit that § 57's note suspected returns, now as something the ego does to itself. That leads directly to § 59's "I can" (row 43), whose first example is the same piano.

The passage gives the personal ego a priority that Section II gave only to the pure ego (row 14). The person is not first an object that I learn about. It is first the life that does the learning. That is the sense in which the commentary calls this the deepest correction: the inductive account is placed inside the life it describes.

## §59, Hua IV 253–54: a system of "I can"

Reads [§59.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 59.

The ego as unity is a system of "I can". Husserl distinguishes the physical "I can", bodily and bodily mediated, from the spiritual. I have power over my body: I move this hand, I can play the piano. But the ability can lapse. I fall out of practice, I must learn again to walk after a long illness, I can become ill in the nerves and lose the mastery of my limbs: "I cannot". In that respect I have become another. The person is defined by what it can do, and its identity changes with its capacities.

> The I as unity is a system of "I *can*". Here one has to distinguish between the physical "I can", the bodily and bodily mediated, and the spiritual.
>
> I have power over my body, I am the one who moves this hand and can move it etc. I can play the piano. But it does not always work. I have unlearned it again, I have got out of practice. I practise my body. In the commonest activities I generally do not get out of practice. But if I lie ill for a long time, I must then learn to walk again; I quickly get back into it. But I can also become ill in the nerves, I lose the mastery over my limbs, "I cannot". In this respect I have become another.

German:

> Das Ich als Einheit ist ein System des ,,Ich *kann*". Dabei ist zu unterscheiden zwischen dem physischen ,,Ich kann", dem leiblichen und leiblich vermittelten, und dem geistigen.
>
> Ich habe Macht über meinen Leib, ich bin es, der diese Hand bewegt und bewegen kann etc. Ich kann Klavier spielen. Aber es geht nicht immer. Ich habe es wieder verlernt, ich bin aus der Übung gekommen. Ich übe meinen Leib. In den gemeinsten Betätigungen komme ich im allgemeinen nicht aus der Übung. Ich muß aber, wenn ich lange krank liege, dann wieder das Gehen lernen, schnell komme ich wieder hinein. Ich kann aber auch nervenkrank werden, ich verliere die Herrschaft über meine Glieder, ,,ich kann nicht". In der Hinsicht bin ich ein anderer geworden.

*Note on the text.* The file has the formula as ",,I c h  *kann*"" (:3871): *Ich* in spaced type, *kann* in italics. It is given here with *kann* alone in italics. The file's ",," for the opening quotation mark is kept.

The formula defines the unity of the ego by a modal verb. "The I as unity" (*als Einheit*) is not a substance or a bundle. It is "a system of 'I can'" (*ein System des „Ich kann“*). The quotation marks make the phrase a noun, and the emphasis falls on the verb: "I *can*". The spaced type on *Ich* in the file shows that both words were emphasized in the print. The distinction that follows divides the system in two. There is "the physical 'I can', the bodily and bodily mediated, and the spiritual". The physical one includes what I can do with my body and through it. The spiritual one includes what I can think, decide, value. § 60 a will analyze its senses.

The examples are ordinary on purpose, and they are all about failure. "I have power over my body (*Macht über meinen Leib*), I am the one who moves this hand and can move it." The second clause doubles the verb, "moves and can move" (*bewegt und bewegen kann*), so that the act and the ability are given together. "I can play the piano. But it does not always work." The ability can lapse through lack of use: "I have unlearned it again, I have got out of practice". It can be restored: after a long illness "I must then learn to walk again; I quickly get back into it". And it can be lost: "I can also become ill in the nerves, I lose the mastery over my limbs, 'I cannot'". The quotation marks on "I cannot" give it the status of the formula's negation.

The last sentence draws the consequence for identity: "In this respect I have become another" (*In der Hinsicht bin ich ein anderer geworden*). The phrase "in this respect" limits it. I do not become another person when I lose an ability. I become other in respect of the system of my "I can". The person whose unity is a system of abilities changes when the system changes. The § 58 ego that "acquires capacities" (row 42) is the same ego whose capacities can be lost.

The formula reaches back to Section I. § 18 a described the kinaesthetic freedom by which the courses of appearance are "motivated", with the "I move" behind each one (row 9). § 59 names it as an ability. In § 60 a Husserl states the order: "Originally the 'I move', 'I do' precedes the 'I can do'". The "I can" is "not merely a representation, but beyond it a thesis, which concerns … the 'doing', not the actual doing but the being-able-to-do" (Hua IV 261, :4030). The apparatus shows that the sentence about the order of "I do" and "I can" is a late insertion by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 261,1f.).

The section credits a source. A footnote to § 60 a says: "Lipps, *Psychologie*, 2nd ed., p. 24ff. gives the first fundamental discussion of this. He also points out that the most original concept of possession, of the 'I have', arises here: I have my limbs; I have power over them. Cf. also above p. 253" (Hua IV 268 n., :4150). The "above p. 253" is this passage. The phrase "I have power over my body" has a named precedent, and Husserl names it. The corpus has no Lipps file, so the comparison cannot be checked from the corpus. The note is enough to show that the formula of § 59 is not presented as a discovery. It is presented as a phenomenological account of a point Lipps made first.

The apparatus shows how § 59 came to be a section. In Landgrebe's typescript the § 58 title has a margin note by Husserl: "Therein also 'I as subject of capacities', p. 253". He made the same note in the contents and at p. 253, "so that at 253 a new § was begun" (apparatus to 251, §-title). The § is an editorial division prompted by Husserl's own late notes. The understanding of § 59 belongs to § 58's correction. The ego "out of life" (row 42) is the ego whose unity is what it can do.

## §60 a, Hua IV 265: the murder I cannot imagine committing

Reads [§60 a.20](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), twentieth paragraph under § 60 a.

I can imagine that I commit a murder or a theft, and yet I cannot imagine that I would do it. I could imagine that I judged the angles of a triangle to sum to three right angles, and yet I could not so judge. Husserl asks what kind of antinomy this is, and answers with a distinction between two consciousnesses of ability. "I could do it" is the neutrality modification of the doing. "I could not do it after all" is the lack of the original consciousness of power for this act, a consciousness that is original and not neutralized even in a fictive action. The act conflicts with the kind of person I am.

> We see, however, that with this consideration we do not yet get through: I can imagine that I committed a murder, a theft etc., and yet I cannot imagine that I would do it. I could imagine that I judged that the sum of the angles in a triangle was 3 right angles, and yet I could not judge so. What kind of antinomy is this?
>
> "I could do it" — that is the neutrality modification of the doing and the practical possibility taken from it. "I could not do it after all" — I lack the original consciousness of ability or power for this action (which even in a fictive action is an original, non-neutralized one); this action conflicts with the kind of my person, my kind of letting myself be motivated.

German:

> Wir sehen aber, mit dieser Betrachtung kommen wir noch nicht aus: ich kann mir vorstellen, ich vollzöge einen Mord, einen Diebstahl etc., und doch, ich kann mir nicht vorstellen, daß ich es täte. Ich könnte mir vorstellen, daß ich urteilte, die Winkelsumme in einem Dreieck wäre 3 Rechte, und doch könnte ich nicht so urteilen. Was ist das für eine Antinomie?
>
> ,,Ich könnte es tun'' — das ist die Neutralitätsmodifikation des Tuns und die ihr entnommene praktische Möglichkeit. ,,Ich könnte es doch nicht tun'' — es fehlt mir das ursprüngliche Könnens- oder Machtbewußtsein für diese Handlung (das auch in einer fiktiven Handlung ein originäres, nicht-neutralisiertes ist); diese Handlung widerstreitet der Art meiner Person, meiner Art, mich motivieren zu lassen.

*Note on the text.* A footnote marker after the last sentence is dropped. The file prints the quotation marks as ",,…''".

The antinomy is built from two constructions with the same verb. "I can imagine that I committed a murder" (*ich kann mir vorstellen, ich vollzöge einen Mord*): the subjunctive *vollzöge* presents the act in fiction. "And yet I cannot imagine that I would do it" (*daß ich es täte*): the subjunctive *täte* presents it as my possible deed. The first is a picture of me doing it. The second is a picture of me as one who would do it. The example from judgment repeats the structure with a theoretical act: I can imagine judging that the angles of a triangle sum to three right angles, "and yet I could not so judge". The two examples are chosen to cover practice and theory, will and belief. The question "What kind of antinomy is this?" names the contradiction between two senses of "can imagine".

The answer separates two modalities. "'I could do it' — that is the neutrality modification of the doing and the practical possibility taken from it." The neutrality modification is the concept of *Ideas I*: the act performed "as if", without its positing force. From the fictive doing, a practical possibility is "taken" (*entnommen*). In that sense I could do anything my body can do. "'I could not do it after all' (*doch nicht*) — I lack the original consciousness of ability or power for this action". The parenthesis is the key to the solution. That consciousness "even in a fictive action is an original, non-neutralized one". When I imagine myself committing the murder, I neutralize the act but not my consciousness of what I can do. That consciousness remains original, and it says no.

The final clause gives the ground. "This action conflicts with the kind of my person, my kind of letting myself be motivated" (*meiner Art, mich motivieren zu lassen*). The appositive defines a person's kind by its motivations. A person is not the sum of its possible acts. It is a style of being moved. The murder is not impossible for my body. It is impossible for my kind of motivation, and the original consciousness of ability registers that impossibility in fiction too.

The next subsection continues the thought into self-knowledge. "I know myself from experience, I know what sort of character I am: I have an ego-apperception, an empirical 'self-consciousness'". The I is "a unity constituted out of its own position-takings (actively) and out of its own habits and capacities, and thereafter externally apperceptive", "whose core is the pure I" (Hua IV 266). The apparatus shows that the clause "out of its own position-takings … thereafter externally" is a late insertion by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 265,23–25), and that he wrote beside the next lines, "so only inductively?" (apparatus to 266,7ff.). The late Husserl pushed the account of character away from induction, toward the person's own position-takings.

The Supplements have a parallel that states the conclusion more sharply. "Personality proper lies in the I as substrate of decisions and not in the I of mere capacities. The 'I cannot carry out a certain decision, e.g. the one for a murder', 'I cannot do such a thing', says how I am". "All the motives that belong to a murder as the ones possibly determining it are for me no effective motives". The possibility of murder is "a practical possibility, insofar as I, supposing I wanted it, could carry it out". "But as regards the position-taking", "its possibility does not belong within the frame of practical possibilities at all" (Beilage XI, Hua IV 331, :5404). The Beilage says outright what § 60 a implies. My body could do it. I could not want it.

The commentary says the antinomy turns § 60 a's four senses of "I can" into a doctrine of character, and the passage bears that out. The "I can" of § 59 (row 43) was bodily and spiritual ability. § 60 a adds the ability to will, and finds it limited by the kind of person I am. The limit is known in imagination. I learn what I cannot will by trying to imagine willing it.

## §60 c, Hua IV 268–69: demand, primal institution and "one"

Reads [§60 c.1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 60 c.

A person develops under the influence of others: their thoughts, suggested feelings, commands. What I take over from another can stand to me as a demand (*Zumutung*), a tendency that goes out from the other and addresses me, to which I give way passively or reluctantly. Or I can appropriate it, so that it becomes my position-taking, though it still has the character of something that has its primal institution (*Urstiftung*) in the other. Beside the demands of persons stand those of custom, usage and tradition, in the form of an indeterminate generality: "one" judges so, "one" holds the fork so. To these too one can yield passively or take a position freely.

> What is foreign, "taken over" by me, more or less external, can be characterized as going out from the foreign subject, at first as a tendency going out from him and addressed to me, as a demand to which I possibly give way passively, possibly reluctantly, yet compelled. But possibly I appropriate it actively, and it becomes my property. It no longer has the mere character of a demand to which I give way, which determines me from outside; it has become a position-taking that goes out from my I, not a mere stimulus that goes toward it, and yet has the character of a taking-over of what has come from another I, of something that has its primal institution in him.
>
> Beside the tendencies that go out from other persons stand the demands of custom, of usage, of tradition, of the spiritual milieu, which appear in the intentional form of indeterminate generality: "one" judges so, "one" holds the fork so, and so on, the demands of the social group, of the estate, etc. To these too one can passively give way or actively take a position, freely decide for them.

German:

> Das Fremde, von mir „Übernommene“, mehr oder minder Äußerliche kann charakterisiert sein als vom fremden Subjekt ausgehend, zunächst als von ihm ausgehende und an mich sich wendende Tendenz, als Zumutung, der ich ev. passiv nachgebe, ev. widerwillig, doch bezwungen. Ev. aber eigne ich es mir selbsttätig an, und es wird zu meinem Eigentum. Es hat nun nicht mehr den bloßen Charakter einer Zumutung, der ich nachgebe, die mich von außen bestimmt; es ist eine Stellungnahme geworden, die von meinem Ich ausgeht, nicht ein bloßer Reiz, der darauf hingeht und doch den Charakter hat als Übernahme des von einem anderen Ich Hergekommenen, von etwas, das in ihm seine Urstiftung hat.
>
> Neben den Tendenzen, die von anderen Personen ausgehen, stehen die in der intentionalen Gestalt unbestimmter Allgemeinheit auftretenden Zumutungen der Sitte, des Brauchs, der Tradition, des geistigen Milieus: „man“ urteilt so, „man“ hält so die Gabel u. dgl., die Forderungen der sozialen Gruppe, des Standes usw. Auch ihnen kann man passiv Folge leisten oder aktiv dazu Stellung nehmen, sich frei dafür entscheiden.

The passage sorts what is foreign by its manner of being had, and the key words are legal and economic. What is foreign is "'taken over'" (*„Übernommene“*). When I appropriate it "actively" (*selbsttätig*), "it becomes my property" (*Eigentum*). The intermediate state is a "demand" (*Zumutung*), a word that in ordinary German means an imposition, something one is expected to put up with. Husserl gives it a structural sense: a tendency "going out from him and addressed to me". The verbs that describe my response show its force: I give way "passively, possibly reluctantly, yet compelled" (*doch bezwungen*). A demand is a motivation that does not issue from me.

The second sentence describes the change that appropriation brings. The foreign thought "no longer has the mere character of a demand"; "it has become a position-taking that goes out from my I, not a mere stimulus that goes toward it". The contrast of direction, from me and toward me, is the contrast of § 50 between motivation and stimulus (row 33). Then the qualifier: "and yet has the character of a taking-over of what has come from another I, of something that has its primal institution in him". The word *Urstiftung* [primal institution, original founding] is the late Husserl's word for the first act that establishes a lasting sense. Ownership of a thought, so defined, has two sides. It is mine because I take a position on it. It is the other's because it was instituted in him.

The apparatus shows that the qualifier is late. The words from "and yet the character" to "own habitus" are "an insertion by H. in L." (apparatus to 269,12–16). The inserted sentence ends with a comparison the passage does not quote: "A similar case as in my egoistic sphere: primal institution and later reproduction as actualized own habitus" (Hua IV 269). So the term *Urstiftung* enters Section III in the late revision. The late Husserl reads appropriation of another's thought on the model of my own habits: an act of institution, and a later reproduction of what it instituted. The only difference is whose act did the instituting.

The second paragraph extends the demand from persons to the anonymous. The demands of "custom, usage, tradition, the spiritual milieu" appear "in the intentional form of indeterminate generality" (*unbestimmter Allgemeinheit*). The examples are given in the impersonal pronoun with quotation marks: "'one' judges so, 'one' holds the fork so". The quotation marks on *man* [one] mark it as a quoted voice. Custom speaks as "one". The examples range from the theoretical to the trivial, from judging to table manners. The last sentence restores the person's freedom. "To these too one can passively give way or actively take a position, freely decide for them." The section's title is "Foreign influences and freedom of the person", and the freedom is the possibility of a position-taking.

The corpus has a later analysis of the same pronoun, from the other side. In his 1925 course Heidegger names the "who" of everyday being-with-one-another "the One" (*das Man*, GA 20 § 26 b). For him "the first thing given is the common world — the One", "the world in which Dasein is absorbed", so that it "has not yet come to itself" (GA 20 339). And "publicness takes from Dasein its choice, its forming of judgments and its valuing", it "relieves Dasein" of being itself, "insofar as it lives in the One" (GA 20 340). The corpus shows no reference from one text to the other, and resemblance is not influence. The contrast is in where each puts the "one". For Husserl it is a demand that a person, already constituted, meets and can freely accept or refuse. For Heidegger it is where Dasein is first, before it comes to itself. Husserl's "one" judges as a voice outside me. Heidegger's "one" is who I am, most of the time. Heidegger also rejects the starting point that § 60 c presupposes: that there are "at first single subjects, each with its own world", whose worlds must then be "pushed together" (GA 20 339).

The apparatus judges the surrounding pages severely: "Hardly legible. Fleeting drafts badly put together" (apparatus to 270,1ff.). The passage read here stands just before them. It is the clearest statement in the section of how a person is made by others and remains free with respect to them.

## §60 d, Hua IV 273–74: "intuition" is not intuition

Reads [§60 d.13](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), thirteenth paragraph under § 60 d.

Understanding a person is often a sudden grasp, before any explication of the connections. Husserl calls it "intuition", in quotation marks, and immediately warns that the word very often means precisely the opposite of intuition (*Anschauung*): a presentiment, a foreseeing without seeing, an empty anticipating grasp. To see a human being is not yet to know him. A person has an individual type, constituted in a course of life, and familiarity with analogous lines in other people lets us grasp it "intuitively", with a lead for fulfilling the intentions in actual intuition. "Intuition" in this sense is the success of a more determinate apperception.

> We speak here of "intuition", a word that very often means precisely the opposite of intuition, namely a *presentiment*, a foreseeing without seeing, a dark, namely symbolic, often incomprehensibly empty anticipating grasp; the actual connection is an aim grasped in advance, an empty intention, but one so determinate that we can follow the determinately directed tendency and in the fulfilment gain a concatenation of actual intuitions (simple experiential intuitions or logical evidences etc.).
>
> One may therefore, in my opinion, not confuse this "intuition" with actual intuition. It is a matter here of the success of a more closely determining apperception, which like every apperception offers a lead for confirming often extremely complex intentional connections in the progress of experience.

German:

> Wir sprechen hier von ,,Intuition”, ein Wort, das sehr oft gerade das Gegenteil von Anschauung besagt, nämlich eine *Vorahnung*, ein Voraussehen ohne Sehen, ein dunkles, nämlich symbolisches, oft unfaßbar leeres Vorauserfassen; der wirkliche Zusammenhang ist ein voraus erfaßtes Ziel, eine leere Intention, die aber so bestimmt ist, daß wir der bestimmt gerichteten Tendenz nachgehen und in der Erfüllung eine Verkettung von wirklichen Anschauungen (schlichten Erfahrungsanschauungen oder logischen Evidenzen etc.) gewinnen können.
>
> Man darf also meines Erachtens diese ,,Intuition“ nicht mit der wirklichen Anschauung verwechseln. Es handelt sich hier um das Gelingen einer näher bestimmenden Apperzeption, die wie jede Apperzeption einen Leitfaden bietet, um oft äußerst komplexe intentionale Zusammenhänge im Fortgang der Erfahrung zu bestätigen.

*Note on the text.* The word *Intuition* is split across the p. 274 running head in the file, "Intui -" … "*tion*”" (:4244/:4249). It is given here as one word. The file's ",," for the opening quotation mark is kept.

The passage turns on two German words that English renders by one. *Intuition* is the borrowed Latin word, in quotation marks. *Anschauung* is the phenomenological term, the givenness of an object in person. "A word that very often means precisely the opposite of intuition" says, in German, "the opposite of *Anschauung*" (*das Gegenteil von Anschauung*). The passage cannot be read in English without the original. The English "intuition" is the word Husserl's translators use for *Anschauung*, and here Husserl sets it against its Latin double.

The definition of the vogue word is a sequence of appositions, each emptier than the last. "A *presentiment* (*Vorahnung*), a foreseeing without seeing (*ein Voraussehen ohne Sehen*), a dark, namely symbolic, often incomprehensibly empty anticipating grasp". The middle phrase is a paradox built on one root: *Voraus-sehen ohne Sehen*. The third names its status in the vocabulary of the *Logical Investigations*: "symbolic", that is, signitive, an intention without fulfilment. What it grasps is "an aim grasped in advance, an empty intention". The positive turn comes at once. The intention is "so determinate" that we can follow it and "in the fulfilment gain a concatenation of actual intuitions". The so-called intuition is the opposite of *Anschauung*, but it leads to it.

The second paragraph draws the conclusion "in my opinion" (*meines Erachtens*), a rare first-person hedge in the book. "This 'intuition'" (*diese*, with quotation marks) must not be confused "with actual intuition" (*wirkliche Anschauung*). What it is instead is named: "the success of a more closely determining apperception". The word "success" (*Gelingen*) is precise. The intuitive grasp of a person is not a different faculty. It is an apperception that happens to hit, and it is confirmed or refuted "in the progress of experience".

The sentences between the two paragraphs give the object of this apperception. "To see a human being does not yet mean to know him." "Every thing is of a kind. If one knows that, one is done. But man has an individual kind, each one another". His "characterological kind, his personality, is a unity constituted in his course of life as subject of position-takings". Familiarity with "analogous lines" in different people lets us grasp this unity "'intuitively'" and gives us "a lead" (*Leitfaden*) for fulfilling the intentions (Hua IV 274). The individual type of a person is grasped through the general types we already know. The subsection's title says this: "The general-typical and the individual-typical in the understanding of persons".

The passage before it states the first step, and it compares persons with nature. "It is already too much to say that empirical understanding is as much as gaining full intuition of experiential connections. Connections of external nature too light up suddenly, before one really lays out in intuition how matters stand. That only comes afterwards" (Hua IV 272–73). Historical and logical connections "light up like lightning" too, "all before the explication" (Hua IV 272–73). The sudden grasp is not peculiar to persons. It is a feature of all understanding. Husserl's point is that its name should not flatter it.

The apparatus shows how the late Husserl marked the page. At 273,35ff., in the margin: "Intuition, in particular intuition of the whole personality of a human being" (apparatus to 273,35ff.). The note is a heading. And at 272,25, a few lines before: "from here better" (apparatus to 272,25). He thought this stretch better than the preceding one, which he had partly bracketed as "unclear" (apparatus to 272,2–9).

The passage connects to § 48's judgment on Dilthey, "a man of genial intuition (*Intuition*), but not of rigorous scientific theorizing" (Hua IV 173, row 27). The *Intuition* granted to Dilthey is the one § 60 d defines: a foreseeing without seeing, an empty intention that may lead to fulfilment. Phenomenology claims the *Anschauung* that fulfils it. The demotion of "insight into character" to apperception is the same move made on the human sciences' favourite faculty. What the human sciences call intuition, phenomenology calls an apperception to be confirmed.

## §61, Hua IV 279–80: root-soil, nature-side, spirit-soul

Reads [§61.11](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) to §61.14, eleventh to fourteenth paragraphs under § 61.

The ego's habitual way of behaving is distinguished from its peculiarity in the weaving of its backgrounds: there is "as it were a root-soil in dark depths". Every spirit has a "nature-side", the underground of subjectivity: sensations, their reproductions, associations, the lowest apperceptions, the lower life of feeling and drive. In this lower stratum a sensuous "soul" announces itself, one with the subject of position-takings. This soul is not an objective, natural reality but a "spirit-soul", defined not by relations to circumstances in objective nature but by an immanent lawfulness of dispositions. The commentary calls the spirit-soul "the concept that makes § 62 possible".

> According to what has been said, the peculiarity of the ego-subject as a habitual peculiarity of behaving separates itself from the peculiarity in the weaving of the backgrounds. There is, as it were, a root-soil in dark depths.
>
> Every spirit has a "nature-side". This is precisely the underground of subjectivity, its having consciousness of sensations, its having of reproductions of sensations, its associations, its formation of apperceptions, namely the lowest ones, which constitute unities of experience. To the nature-side belongs immediately the lower life of feeling, the life of drive, and probably also the function of attention, which is a specific ego-function, as is the general function of turning-toward. It forms the bridge to the specific being and living of the ego.
>
> This soul is here not an objective (nature-directed) reality, but "spirit-soul", i.e. soul in this sense is not defined as real unity in relation to circumstances of objective nature, thus not psychophysically, or at least need not be so defined.

German:

> Es scheidet sich nach dem Gesagten die Eigenart des Ichsubjekts als habituelle Eigenart des Sich-verhaltens von der Eigenart im Sichweben der Hintergründe. Es ist gewissermaßen ein Wurzelboden da in dunklen Tiefen.
>
> Jeder Geist hat eine „Naturseite“. Das ist eben der Untergrund der Subjektivität, ihr Bewußthaben von Empfindungen, ihr Haben von Empfindungsreproduktionen, ihre Assoziationen, ihre Bildung von Apperzeptionen, und zwar der untersten, Erfahrungseinheiten konstituierenden. Zur Naturseite gehört unmittelbar das niedere Gefühlsleben, das Triebleben und wohl auch die Funktion der Aufmerksamkeit, die eine spezifische Ichfunktion ist ebenso wie die allgemeine Funktion der Zuwendung. Sie bildet die Brücke zum spezifischen Ichsein und Ichleben.
>
> Diese Seele ist hierbei nicht objektive (naturale) Realität, sondern ,,Geistesseele“, d.h. Seele in diesem Sinn ist nicht definiert als reale Einheit mit Beziehung auf Umstände der objektiven Natur, also nicht psychophysisch, oder braucht doch nicht so definiert zu werden.

The three terms are three images, and each one is hedged. "Root-soil" (*Wurzelboden*) is introduced with "as it were" (*gewissermaßen*): "There is, as it were, a root-soil in dark depths". "Nature-side" (*„Naturseite“*) is in quotation marks. "Spirit-soul" (*„Geistesseele“*) is in quotation marks too. Husserl coins a vocabulary for something the attitudes so far have no word for: what in the spirit is like nature without being nature. The images are botanical and spatial. The ego's habits grow out of a soil, in "dark depths", and the depth is below the level of acts.

The first sentence states the distinction from which the image grows. The ego's peculiarity "as a habitual peculiarity of behaving" (*habituelle Eigenart des Sich-verhaltens*) separates from its peculiarity "in the weaving of the backgrounds" (*im Sichweben der Hintergründe*). The reflexive noun *Sichweben* is unusual: the backgrounds weave themselves. The habitual peculiarity is what § 57 described (row 41), the regularities of position-taking. The other peculiarity is passive, in the background, before all behaviour. The paragraph before says so: there is "ultimately a background that lies before all behaviour, rather is presupposed by all behaviour" (Hua IV 279).

The second paragraph lists the contents of the nature-side, and the list reaches upward. It begins with sensations and their reproductions, associations and "the lowest" apperceptions. Then "the lower life of feeling, the life of drive" (*das Triebleben*). Then, "probably" (*wohl*), "the function of attention, which is a specific ego-function". The hedge "probably" marks the uncertain border. Attention belongs to the nature-side and is already an ego-function. "It forms the bridge to the specific being and living of the ego." The nature-side is not a separate realm. It is continuous with the ego, and attention is where the continuity is felt. The apparatus records a note in Stein's typescript at 279,13: "Nothing is said there of drives and tendencies" (apparatus to 279,13). The edition's list names the life of drive, so the note may concern an earlier state of the passage. The apparatus does not say.

The third paragraph defines the soul that announces itself in the lower stratum by its relation to nature. It is "not an objective (nature-directed) reality, but 'spirit-soul'". The parenthesis "(naturale)" is an addition by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 280,12). It brings the vocabulary of § 49 d (row 29) into § 61. The spirit-soul is "not defined as real unity in relation to circumstances of objective nature, thus not psychophysically". Its definition is negative and then hedged again: "or at least need not be so defined" (*oder braucht doch nicht so definiert zu werden*). The next sentence gives the positive side: "There is an immanent lawfulness of the formation of dispositions as foundation for the position-taking subject" (Hua IV 280). The soul of § 61 is the soul of §§ 30–33, the substrate of dispositions (rows 17–19), now taken without its relation to circumstances in nature.

The apparatus shows the late Husserl working at this definition. Between the two paragraphs, the sensuous soul is "one with the subject of position-taking", and it belongs "to the person as founding underground". The words "inseparably" (*untrennbar*) and "founding" (*fundierender*) are late additions (apparatus to 280,9 and 280,10). A margin note at 280,4f. objects from the other side: "Soul in the full sense is after all the surplus over the bodily corporeality in the natural objective, nature-directed attitude" (apparatus to 280,4f.). And at 280,28, where Landgrebe's copy read "This nature is the psychic in the sense of nature-directed psychology", Husserl changed the text and wrote: "In the sense of nature-directed psychology? I hold this conception of soul to be wrong" (apparatus to 280,28). The late notes pull both ways. One says the full soul is the naturalistic surplus. The other rejects the identification of this nature-side with the soul of natural psychology. Read together, they say that the word "soul" has two senses, and that § 61 means the spiritual one.

That is why the commentary says the spirit-soul makes § 62 possible. § 62 asks how spirit and nature are related, and answers by "two cuts" through one absolute consciousness (row 48). The spirit-soul is what lies on the spirit's side of the cut and still has a lawfulness like nature's. Heidegger, in 1925, paraphrases these pages: "Every such I has at the same time its nature-side as underground of subjectivity. Spirit is no abstract I, but the full personality" (GA 20 169). He cites them right after noting that the term *inspectio sui* recalls Descartes, and two pages later he concludes that the analysis is "ultimately oriented to Descartes" (GA 20 171). The hedges in § 61, "as it were", "probably", "need not be so defined", show Husserl meeting the same difficulty from inside.

## §62, Hua IV 281, 286: another cut, and the point of conversion

Reads [§62.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §62.12, second and twelfth paragraphs under § 62.

The flow of experiences stands under a law whose index is the reality "soul", dependent on the natural body. The body expresses one intersubjective regulation, the soul a second, related to the first. The spirit too is the index of a regulation, but "here, so to speak, another cut is made through absolute consciousness". The spirit depends on the soul, and the soul on the body, so the spirit is conditioned by nature, but it does not stand in a relation of causality to nature. The body has two faces. As thing of the surrounding world it is in causality with spirit, in a homogeneous sphere. As appearance of physical nature it is the point where spiritual causality converts into natural causality.

> But here, so to speak, another cut is made through absolute consciousness, and a peculiar regulation running in it is designated. The spirit in its spiritual acts is dependent on the soul, insofar as the stream of experience lets the acts well forth from itself (namely, the I performs them on the basis of the rest of the stream of experience); so the spiritual I depends on the soul and the soul on the body: thus it is conditioned by nature; but it does not on that account stand in a relation of causality to nature.
>
> On the other hand, because the body of the surrounding world is at the same time appearance of nature in the physical sense, the body has a second face, it is the point of conversion of spiritual causality into natural causality.

German:

> Aber es ist hier sozusagen ein anderer Schnitt durch das absolute Bewußtsein vollzogen und eine in diesem verlaufende eigenartige Regelung bezeichnet. Der Geist in seinen geistigen Akten ist abhängig von der Seele, sofern der Erlebnisstrom die Akte aus sich hervorquellen läßt (nämlich das Ich vollzieht sie auf Grund des übrigen Erlebnisstromes); so hängt das geistige Ich von der Seele und die Seele vom Leibe ab: also er ist naturbedingt; aber in einem Verhältnis der Kausalität steht er darum nicht zur Natur.
>
> Dagegen weil der umweltliche Leib zugleich Erscheinung ist der Natur im physikalischen Sinn, hat der Leib ein zweites Gesicht, er ist Umschlagstelle von geistiger Kausalität in Naturkausalität.

*Note on the text.* *Naturkausalität* is hyphenated across a line break in the file ("Natur-" / "kausalität", :4400–:4401). It is given here as one word.

The first sentence is an image of anatomy or geology, and "so to speak" (*sozusagen*) marks it as an image. A cut (*Schnitt*) is made "through absolute consciousness", and what the cut exposes is "a peculiar regulation running in it". The word "another" (*ein anderer*) refers to the sentences before. "The reality body-thing expresses an intersubjective regulation". "The reality soul expresses a second regulation, and one related to the first: the soul is dependent on the body" (Hua IV 281). Each reality is the "index" of a regulation in the one absolute consciousness. Spirit is a third index, and its regulation is a third cut. The image makes the realities of nature and spirit into sections of one consciousness. They differ as cuts differ, by where they are made.

The second sentence states the dependence and then denies that it is causal. The spirit "is dependent on the soul, insofar as the stream of experience lets the acts well forth from itself". The parenthesis says what that means for an ego: "the I performs them on the basis of the rest of the stream". So the spiritual I depends on the soul, and the soul on the body: "thus it is conditioned by nature" (*naturbedingt*). The concession is complete. Then the denial: "but it does not on that account stand in a relation of causality to nature". Conditioning is not causality. The sentences that follow give the reason in § 15's terms. The spirit "has an underground that is conditionally dependent". "As spirit it has a soul, a complex of nature-like dispositions" (Hua IV 281). And a page later: "Causality is the relation of a reality to its correlative circumstance-realities. But the reality of the spirit is not related to real circumstances lying within nature, but to real circumstances that consist in the 'surrounding world' and in other spirits" (Hua IV 283, :4367). The spirit is conditioned by nature through its underground. It is caused only by its world and by other spirits.

The second passage gives the body "two faces" and names the place where they meet. The sentences before it set out the first face. The body as thing in the surrounding world is "the experienced, intuitive body-thing", and it is "appearance of the physical body". Physical nature "does not concern the surrounding world, at least not the primary surrounding world. We can say: it is *secondary surrounding world*" (Hua IV 285). The body as thing of the surrounding world is the basis for the aesthesiological body and the will-body, "and so the body stands in causality to spirit: there we are in a homogeneous sphere" (Hua IV 285). Then the quoted sentence: "On the other hand (*Dagegen*), because the body of the surrounding world is at the same time appearance of nature in the physical sense, the body has a second face". The body is the point of conversion (*Umschlagstelle*) "of spiritual causality into natural causality". My free movement of my body is an act in my surrounding world, and by virtue of "the correspondence that prevails here" it is also a change in physical nature (Hua IV 285).

*Umschlagstelle* takes up a word from Section II. In § 42 the body was the "conversion point" (*Umschlagspunkt*), "in which causal relations convert themselves into conditional ones between outer world and bodily-psychic subject" (Hua IV 161, row 23). In § 42 the conversion runs from nature to the subject: physical causality becomes the conditionality of my sensations. In § 62 it runs from spirit to nature: my action becomes a physical event. The point is the same, the body. The direction is reversed. The body is where nature enters the subject and where the subject goes out into nature, and in both directions it changes the kind of relation, not just its terms.

The apparatus marks this part of § 62 lightly. At 281,10ff. Husserl wrote only a question mark in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 281,10ff.). At 285,33 he added the comparison of physical nature with "an objective value", the value-thing that becomes an object of the surrounding world (apparatus to 285,33). The two faces were present in the first draft. The late revision gave the secondary surrounding world its analogue in the world of values.

The understanding of the two passages is a thesis about the relation of spirit and nature that is neither parallelism nor interaction. Spirit is conditioned by nature through its underground, and acts on nature through its body. Neither relation is causality between two realities of the same kind. § 63 draws the consequence for the old debate (row 50).

## §62, Hua IV 288: the life-world, in a note

Reads [§62.18](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md), eighteenth paragraph under § 62.

The concepts I and we are relative. The I requires the you, the we, the "other", and the person requires a relation to a world of things. All science is an intersubjective activity in the attitude "we and actuality", and as objective science it speaks of the world without speaking of the we. Yet we always find ourselves, the natural scientist too, even where he investigates nature, living as persons in the personal world, our life-world. The apprehension as nature is subordinate to the personal apprehension. The commentary calls this "what may be the earliest use of 'life-world' in Husserl's published work".

> But here we constantly find ourselves, and the natural scientist too finds himself, even where he investigates nature, constantly as living as a person in the personal world, his life-world: only that he is theoretically directed exclusively at physical or zoological nature, etc. The apprehension as nature thus subordinates itself to the personal apprehension.

German:

> Dabei aber finden wir uns beständig, findet sich auch der Naturforscher, selbst wo er Natur erforscht, beständig als Person lebend in der personalen Welt, seiner Lebenswelt: nur daß er theoretisch gerichtet ist ausschließlich auf die physische oder zoologische Natur etc. Die Auffassung als Natur ordnet sich der personalen Auffassung also unter.

*Note on the text.* The sentence belongs to a footnote, not to the main text. The file prints the footnote after the § 63 heading, inside § 63's first sentence (between "eine interpersonale" at :4457 and "Kulturleistung" at :4467); it carries the marker "[^1]", and no marker in the body points to it. The apparatus places it on p. 288: "the text of the footnote is given in L. as a supplement" (apparatus to 288). § 63's sentence runs on across the note without a gap.

The sentence turns on a correction introduced by "but" (*Dabei aber*). The sentence before it describes science. Science is "an intersubjective activity, a research in the attitude 'we and actuality'". "It seeks objective validity; as objective science it makes statements in this attitude about the world simply, that is, statements in which nothing is said of the we, but only of the objective, first and lowest of physical nature" (Hua IV 288). The sentence read here adds what objective science leaves out of its statements. The we that is never mentioned is still there. "But here we constantly find ourselves (*finden wir uns beständig*)". The adverb *beständig* [constantly] is repeated, once for us and once for the natural scientist. The scientist is not a special case. He is the hard case, and the claim holds "even where he investigates nature" (*selbst wo er Natur erforscht*).

The word *Lebenswelt* [life-world] is introduced as an apposition, and the possessive matters. The scientist lives "in the personal world, his life-world" (*seiner Lebenswelt*). The life-world is the personal world taken as the world a person lives in. It is "his": each person's, and the scientist's too. The restriction comes after the colon: "only that he is theoretically directed exclusively at physical or zoological nature". The scientist's direction differs, not his world. He lives in the life-world and looks, as a theorist, at a part of it.

The last sentence states the consequence in the verb of § 49 e. "The apprehension as nature thus subordinates itself to the personal apprehension" (*ordnet sich … unter*). In § 49 e, "the naturalistic attitude subordinates itself to the personalistic" (Hua IV 184, row 31). The thesis announced there in the future tense ("it will turn out") is stated here as a result ("thus", *also*). The note goes on to divide research directed at persons: either at persons "as spiritually determined by the things, as motivated by the things", or at persons "with regard to their real dependence on the things as natures", where "the things are apprehended doubly" (Hua IV 288). The first is the human sciences, the second psychophysics. Both take place within the life-world.

The note's first sentences give the life-world its ground in intersubjectivity. "According to our presentations the concepts I–we are relative; the I demands the you, the we, the 'other'. And further the I (the I as person) demands the relation to a world of things. Thus I, we, the world belong together: the world as common surrounding world thereby bearing the stamp of subjectivity" (Hua IV 288). The life-world is the world that bears this stamp. It is the common surrounding world of § 51 (row 34) under a new name.

The note's status limits what can be said about the word. The apparatus says only that Landgrebe gave its text as a supplement (*Beilage*). It does not date it, and it does not say whether the word *Lebenswelt* was Husserl's in the manuscript or entered in a later revision. The commentary's "may be" is the right hedge. The word stands in a note that the typescript set apart from the text, and the corpus cannot place it more exactly. The Supplements in the file use the word too: "the life-world is the natural world", and "the basic relation in this life-world, which prescribes the point of view of method, is not that of causality but that of motivation" (:6542, :6546). Those passages belong to the supplementary texts and have their own dates. They show that the word belongs to the same circle of manuscripts. They do not date the note.

Whatever its date, the note states the result of Section III in one sentence. The § 53 "vicious circle" (row 35) is resolved here. Nature contains persons as natural objects only within an apprehension that persons perform, in a world they live in. The life-world is the name for that world.

## §63–§64, Hua IV 294, 297: refuting parallelism, and the limit of naturalization

Reads [§63.16](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §64.1, sixteenth paragraph under § 63 and first under § 64.

If consciousness has a priori laws of its own, such as the laws by which retentions follow impressions, then not everything in it can depend on the states of the body. Only what the essential laws leave open can be empirically conditioned. With such grounds one can radically refute psychophysical parallelism, and the refutation has a different style from the usual ones that operate with crooked concepts of causality and substance and head straight for interaction. Rejecting parallelism decides nothing for interaction. § 64 states the result as a limit. Spirit can be naturalized only to a certain degree. Nature is a field of thoroughgoing relativities, relative to an absolute that bears them all: the spirit.

> With such grounds one can, it seems to me, radically refute *parallelism*, and the refutation has a quite different style from the usual refutations, which operate with crooked concepts of causality and substance and with traditional prejudices of many kinds and steer straight for interaction: as if the question "parallelism or interaction" were the radical and complete one. With the rejection of parallelism nothing at all is yet decided for interaction.
>
> From the preceding considerations there results the limit of possible *naturalization*: the spirit can be grasped as dependent on nature and itself naturalized, but only to a certain degree. […] Subjects cannot be absorbed in being nature, since then what gives nature its sense would be missing. Nature is a field of thoroughgoing relativities and can be so because these are always relative to an absolute, which thus bears all relativities: the spirit.

German:

> Mit solchen Gründen kann man, scheint mir, den *Parallelismus* radikal widerlegen, und die Widerlegung hat dabei einen ganz anderen Stil als die gewöhnlichen mit schiefen Kausalitäts- und Substanzbegriffen und mit traditionellen Vorurteilen mancher Art operierenden Widerlegungen, die gleich auf Wechselwirkung lossteuern: als ob die Frage Parallelismus oder Wechselwirkung die radikale und vollständige wäre. Mit der Ablehnung des Parallelismus ist noch gar nichts für die Wechselwirkung entschieden.
>
> Aus den bisherigen Betrachtungen ergibt sich die Grenze möglicher *Naturalisierung*: der Geist kann als abhängig von Natur gefaßt und selbst naturalisiert werden, aber nur bis zu einem gewissen Grade. … Subjekte können nicht darin aufgehen, Natur zu sein, da dann das fehlen würde, was der Natur Sinn gibt. Natur ist ein Feld durchgängiger Relativitäten und kann es sein, weil dieselben immer doch relativ zu einem Absoluten sind, das somit alle Relativitäten trägt: der Geist.

The first passage ends an argument from essence, and the argument is in the sentences before it. "The decisive question is now how far what belongs to the essence of consciousness assigns *limits* to admissible possibilities. It is surely an absolutely indubitable truth that there are *essential laws* of *consciousness*. Is a thoroughgoing psychophysical parallelism *compatible* with them?" (Hua IV 293). The answer uses time-consciousness. If "as belonging to the a priori essence of consciousness, certain necessities of sequence exist — as for example the modes of retention belonging to the constitution of time are connected in necessary successions (a priori) to any impressions whatsoever — then these connections of sequence could not be conditioned by the C [the physical correlate of a consciousness, which the text writes as C with the index m] and the sequences of its objective states". "Only what the essential connections leave *open* can be empirically conditioned. Only the sensation could be conditioned, say, but not what attaches to it by way of retentions" (Hua IV 294). Parallelism claims that every state of consciousness has a physical correlate on which it depends. Phenomenology shows that some of consciousness's connections are necessary a priori. They cannot depend on anything that could be otherwise.

The quoted sentence draws the conclusion with a hedge and a claim. "It seems to me" (*scheint mir*) is the hedge. "Radically" (*radikal*) is the claim. The refutation goes to the root, because it uses the essence of consciousness rather than a theory of causality. The style is contrasted with the usual refutations, which are characterized by three faults: "crooked concepts of causality and substance" (*schiefen Kausalitäts- und Substanzbegriffen*), "traditional prejudices of many kinds", and haste: they "steer straight for interaction" (*gleich auf Wechselwirkung lossteuern*). The last fault is the logical one. They assume the alternative is complete, "as if the question 'parallelism or interaction' were the radical and complete one". The last sentence denies it: "With the rejection of parallelism nothing at all is yet decided for interaction". § 62 had already given the third possibility: spirit conditioned by nature, but not in causality with it (row 48).

A footnote to the argument tried a stronger thought and was struck. "It would be conceivable that there were no bodies at all and no dependencies of consciousness on material occurrences in constituted nature; thus also no empirical soul, while yet absolute consciousness would remain as something absolutely not to be struck out." It would have "a principle of factual unity, its own rule". The note ends: "But that is a questionable consideration" (*Aber das ist eine fragliche Überlegung*, Hua IV 294 n., :4590). The apparatus reports that in Landgrebe's typescript this text stood in the main text and that Husserl put it in deletion brackets; in Stein's typescript he noted that "in the manuscript it is called a questionable consideration" (apparatus to 294). The edition prints it as a footnote. The thought experiment that § 64 will state as a thesis appears first here, as a doubt.

The second passage states the thesis without the doubt, and it begins with a result: "the limit of possible *naturalization*" (italic). Spirit "can be grasped as dependent on nature and itself naturalized, but only to a certain degree". The sentences left out by the ellipsis say what lies beyond the degree: "An unambiguous determination of spirit through merely nature-like dependencies, a reduction to something like physical nature … is unthinkable" (Hua IV 297). Then the ground: "Subjects cannot be absorbed in being nature, since then what gives nature its sense would be missing". The verb *aufgehen in* [be absorbed in, be wholly contained in] is exact. Subjects have a nature-side (row 47). They cannot be wholly nature, because nature has its sense from them.

The last sentence states the relation of relative and absolute. "Nature is a field of thoroughgoing relativities" (*Feld durchgängiger Relativitäten*). Every natural thing is what it is in relation to circumstances, as § 15 showed (row 7). "And can be so because these are always relative to an absolute, which thus bears all relativities: the spirit." The colon puts the name last, after the function. The next paragraph gives the thought experiment, now without hesitation. "If we strike out all spirits from the world, there is no more nature. But if we strike out nature, the 'true', objective-intersubjective existence, something still remains: the spirit as individual spirit; only the possibility of sociality is lost, the possibility of a comprehension that presupposes a certain intersubjectivity of the body" (Hua IV 297, :4597). The apparatus shows that the hyphenated "-intersubjektive" in "objective-intersubjective" is a late addition (apparatus to 297,25). The late Husserl specified that the nature struck out is intersubjective nature.

Heidegger quotes the thought experiment in his 1925 course. His text has "the possibility of society" (*Sozietät*) where the edition has "sociality" (*Sozialität*), another sign that he read an earlier text (GA 20 170). His verdict follows. "That is the same consideration regarding pure consciousness as residue of world-annihilation. Husserl only comes back here to his primal division of being under another title. Ontologically everything stays as it was" (GA 20 171). The comparison with *Ideas I* § 49 is just (row 38). What the comparison leaves out is the footnote on p. 294. Husserl knew the thought experiment was "questionable" and printed it in § 64 anyway, with a qualification that *Ideas I* does not have. What survives the striking-out of nature is a spirit without sociality, an "individual spirit", not a person "in the narrower, social sense" (Hua IV 297). The absolute of § 64 is a poorer thing than the person of Section III. Husserl says so: "with all the enormous impoverishment of 'personal' life" (Hua IV 297).

## §64, Hua IV 300–302: thisness is a form; nature is the X

Reads [§64.13](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-4-ideas-ii-commentary.md) and §64.20, thirteenth and twentieth paragraphs under § 64.

The pure ego of each *cogitatio* already has absolute individuation, and the ego is not an empty pole but the bearer of its habituality and so of its individual history. Can two experiences be absolutely alike, differing only by their thisness? Is not *haecceitas* too a universal, since each experience has its own? Husserl answers that the question confuses quality with "this". Thisness is a form, not a whatness. The only originally individual thing is consciousness with its ego, and every other individual has its individuation through appearance to it. So the book ends: nature is the X, determined by universal determinations, and nothing else. The spirit is no X, but what is given itself in the experience of spirit.

> Is not *haecceitas* too a universal, insofar as each experience has its *haecceitas* in itself? But it is not to be asked at all what distinguishes one this and another this purely as this, and again what both have "in common". That already means confusing quality and this. So too when we speak of the "essence of thisness". Thisness is a form.
>
> Nature is the X and in principle nothing other than X, which determines itself through universal determinations. The spirit, however, is no X, but what is given itself in the experience of spirit.

German:

> Ist aber nicht auch die haecceitas ein Allgemeines, sofern jedes Erlebnis in sich seine haecceitas hat? Aber es ist gar nicht zu fragen, was ein Dies und ein anderes Dies rein als Dies unterscheidet, und wieder, was beide ,,gemeinsam'' haben. Das heißt schon Qualität und Dies verwechseln. So auch, wenn wir vom ,,Wesen der Diesheit'' sprechen. Diesheit ist eine Form.
>
> Die Natur ist das X und prinzipiell nichts anderes als X, das sich durch allgemeine Bestimmungen bestimmt. Der Geist aber ist kein X, sondern das in Geisteserfahrung Gegebene selbst.

*Note on the text.* The file does not italicize *haecceitas* (:4661); the English sets it in italics as a foreign term. The file prints the inner quotation marks as ",,…''".

The first passage takes a term from scholastic philosophy and turns it against a scholastic question. *Haecceitas* [thisness] is the Latin name for what makes an individual this one and not another. Husserl asks whether it is not itself "a universal" (*ein Allgemeines*), "insofar as each experience has its *haecceitas* in itself". If every experience has a thisness, thisness is what they have in common, and it is a universal. The answer rejects the question rather than answering it. "It is not to be asked at all what distinguishes one this and another this purely as this". To ask so "already means confusing quality and this". The quotation marks on "in common" (*„gemeinsam“*) and on "essence of thisness" (*„Wesen der Diesheit“*) mark the confused phrases. The last sentence gives the correct category: "Thisness is a form" (*Diesheit ist eine Form*). The next page explains: "The form of the this is no *whatness* and in this sense no essence. It is universal in the sense of form" (Hua IV 301). Thisness is universal as a form is, not as a quality is.

The argument begins from the ego, and the apparatus shows that its key sentence is late. "Absolute individuation already belongs to the pure I of each *cogitatio*, which is itself something absolutely individual. But the I is not an empty pole but bearer of its habituality, and therein lies that it has its individual history" (Hua IV 300, :4656). The second sentence, from "But the I" to "history", is an addition by Husserl in Landgrebe's typescript (apparatus to 300,2–4). In § 22 the pure ego was introduced with a parenthesis on absolute individuation and no history (row 14). The late Husserl adds the history here, at the end of the book. The pure ego of Section II was a pole. The pure ego of § 64, after Section III, carries its habits.

The sentences between the two quoted paragraphs state the thesis on which the ending rests. "The only originally individual thing is consciousness concretely with its I" (Hua IV 301). The words "concretely with its I" (*konkret mit seinem Ich*) are again a late addition (apparatus to 301,16). "Everything else individual is something appearing and has the principle of its individuation in actual and possible appearing, which for its part points back to an individual consciousness". "Absolute individuation enters into the *personal I*." The surrounding world has "the *secondary individuation* of the counterpart, while in the I itself lies the original, the absolute" (Hua IV 301). And: "The same spirit cannot be twice" (Hua IV 301).

The second passage is the book's last sentence, and it answers a question about the soul. "How does it stand now with the soul and the human being as nature? Is that too merely an exemplar of a universal?" The answer divides: "insofar as the soul is naturalized spirit and specific individuality belongs to the spirit, no". But "everything through which the soul determines itself as natural reality is exemplary and universal. Individuality does not lie in what is nature here" (Hua IV 302). Then the contrast. "Nature is the X and in principle nothing other than X, which determines itself through universal determinations." The letter X is the empty bearer of predicates, the "empty *something*" that physics determines through "intersubjectively constituted forms" (Hua IV 88, :1825). Nature is that and "in principle" (*prinzipiell*) nothing more. Its individuals are exemplars. "The spirit, however, is no X, but what is given itself in the experience of spirit" (*das in Geisteserfahrung Gegebene selbst*). The word "itself" (*selbst*) is the phenomenological word for originary givenness. Spirit is not determined through predicates of an X. It is given in person.

Heidegger quotes the last sentence in 1925, and he quotes another sentence of § 64 beside it, "Spirits are precisely not unities of appearances, but unities of absolute connections of consciousness", adding "— what is immanently given" (GA 20 170). The edition goes on, "more precisely spoken, ego-unities" (*genauer gesprochen Icheinheiten*, Hua IV 302, :4677). Heidegger stops before it. His verdict is that the contrast of X and given self is the old division of transcendent and immanent being "under another title" (GA 20 171). The late addition at 300,2–4 is the part of § 64 that his verdict does not reach. A pure ego that "has its individual history" is not only the residue of an annihilated world. It is a self whose individuation is its history. The book ends by contrasting nature's X with a spirit "given itself", and the late Husserl has given that spirit a past.

The commentary calls this the book's last word on individuation, and it is also the end of the line that began with § 22 (row 14). There the pure ego had absolute individuation as a pole. Here the person has it as a history, and the world has its individuation from the person. Nature is the X because it has no history of its own. It has only the histories of the spirits for whom it appears.

# Husserl, *Thing and Space*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md) works through the 1907 lectures section by section. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding goes in the rest of the course.

The main commentary is keyed to Richard Rojcewicz's English translation, *Thing and Space: Lectures of 1907*, which is not in the corpus. The readings quote the German original instead, Husserliana XVI, *Ding und Raum. Vorlesungen 1907*, edited by Ulrich Claesges (1973), from the converted file `sources/husserl/husserl-hua-16-ding-und-raum-vorlesungen-1907.md` in `faultynode/sources`. The § numbers are the same in both editions. Passages are cited by § and Hua XVI page, and unit references such as "§11.3" name the third paragraph of the commentary under §11. All English renderings of the German are my own working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows. They are not Rojcewicz's, and where a reading questions the commentary's English, it cannot tell whether the commentary or the translator is responsible.

The file has limits that bear on more than one reading. Its page numbers come from marker comments, which can fall in mid-paragraph, so a page can be one out; the marker for p. 64 reads "6". Several stretches are garbled or invented and are never quoted: a fragment in §1 (p. 5), the opening of §6 (p. 17), the opening of §14 (pp. 42–43), §16 pp. 51–52, most of §40 (pp. 139–141, where the file also lacks the § heading), §69 pp. 241–242, and a block of invented English mathematics after §84. Where one of these touches a passage, the reading says so. The file keeps Husserl's italics, so emphasis can be argued from with care. Beilage I prints the critical notes Husserl later wrote on his own manuscript, keyed to page and line ("ad 51, 10 ff."). The main commentary never uses them; several readings do, because they record what Husserl came to think of the passage at hand.

## §1, Hua XVI 4: the refusal of "theory"

Reads [§1.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-world-of-natural-experience-and-the-world-of-scientific-theory).

For Husserl in 1907, a "theory" explains and grounds: it derives what is given from something else, as mathematics and natural science do. The lectures do something else. They clarify (*aufklären*) the essence of what experience gives, starting from its lowest forms, and nothing in that field needs explaining in the theorist's sense. The passage declines the obvious name for the course, "theory of experience", and the refusal is a statement of method before any method has been shown.

> I can now designate the theme of the following lectures in a few words. It concerns the foundational parts of a future phenomenology of experience: a clarification of the essence of experiential givenness, starting from the nearest and first beginnings and carried from there as deep and as far as possible, at least in its lower forms and levels. If one insisted on seeking support in the ways of speaking now current, one would have to speak of a theory of experience. But on the one hand I have, as already with the word epistemology [theory of knowledge], my misgivings about talk of theory, which fits mathematical and natural-scientific explanation and grounding, but certainly fits nowhere where there is nothing in this sense to explain and to ground; it does not fit even the experiencing of the morphologizing or typifying sciences, let alone that of phenomenology. Besides, since Cohen and the Marburg School applied the title "theory of experience" to Kant's critique of experiential knowledge, it has taken on a breadth that spans just about all the problems of pure theoretical reason; and we are not so bold here as to want to face and treat them all.

German:

> Ich kann nun mit wenigen Worten das Thema der nachfolgenden Vorlesungen bezeichnen. Es handelt sich um die grundlegenden Partien einer künftigen Phänomenologie der Erfahrung, um eine von den nächstliegenden und ersten Anfängen ausgehende und von da aus möglichst tief und weit geführte Aufklärung des Wesens der Erfahrungsgegebenheit, mindestens in ihren niederen Formen und Stufen. Wollte man durchaus Anlehnung an die jetzt üblichen Redeweisen suchen, so wäre von Theorie der Erfahrung zu sprechen. Doch habe ich einerseits, wie schon bei dem Worte Erkenntnistheorie, meine Bedenken gegen die Rede von Theorie, die auf mathematische und naturwissenschaftliche Erklärung und Begründung paßt, aber sicher nirgends paßt, wo es in diesem Sinne nichts zu erklären und zu begründen gilt, schon nicht auf das Erfahren der morphologisierenden oder typisierenden Wissenschaften, geschweige denn auf das der Phänomenologie. Zudem hat der Titel ,,Theorie der Erfahrung“, seit Cohen und die Marburger Schule ihn auf Kants Kritik der Erfahrungserkenntnis angewandt haben, eine Weite angenommen, die so ziemlich alle Probleme der reinen theoretischen Vernunft umspannt; und so kühn sind wir hier nicht, uns allen stellen und alle behandeln zu wollen.

The positive name comes before the refused one. The course is "a phenomenology of experience" and, in apposition, "a clarification (*Aufklärung*) of the essence of experiential givenness". "Theory of experience" enters only in a counterfactual: "if one insisted on seeking support in the ways of speaking now current" (*Wollte man durchaus Anlehnung … suchen*). The subjunctive keeps the phrase at arm's length before the objection to it is even stated. The two objections that follow are not of the same kind, and the commentary runs them together. The first is about the word "theory" (*die Rede von Theorie*): it belongs where something is "to be explained and grounded" (*zu erklären und zu begründen*), and the paired infinitives define theory by its task. The second is about a title already occupied, "theory of experience" in quotation marks, which the Marburg School stretched over the whole of Kant's critique. Only the first is a principle. The second is a matter of scale, and Husserl answers it with irony, "we are not so bold".

The scope of the first objection is wider than phenomenology. Theory does not fit "even the experiencing of the morphologizing or typifying sciences" (*schon nicht … geschweige denn*): the sciences that describe shapes and types, botany or anatomy, already do without explanation in the mathematical sense, "let alone" phenomenology. The graded construction places phenomenology at the far end of a line of descriptive disciplines, not outside science altogether. The parenthetical "as already with the word epistemology" (*wie schon bei dem Worte Erkenntnistheorie*) shows that the misgiving is old and is about a word. Husserl objects to "theory" even inside the name of the discipline he had been practising.

The same week's lectures state the principle. In the fourth of the five introductory lectures, published as *Die Idee der Phänomenologie*, phenomenology "proceeds by seeing, clarifying, determining sense and distinguishing sense" and "does not theorize and mathematize; that is, it carries out no explanations in the sense of deductive theory" (*Sie theoretisiert und mathematisiert nicht; sie vollzieht nämlich keine Erklärungen im Sinne der deduktiven Theorie*, Hua II 58–59). The §1 passage applies that rule to the course's own title. Two editorial facts in Hua XVI make the refusal concrete. The 1904/05 course had been announced as "Main Parts of a Phenomenology and Theory of Knowledge" (*Hauptstücke aus einer Phänomenologie und Theorie der Erkenntnis*, editor's note, Hua XVI 123); the 1907 course was announced as "Main Parts of Phenomenology and Critique of Reason" (*Hauptstücke aus der Phänomenologie und Kritik der Vernunft*, editor's introduction). Between the two, "theory" left the title and "critique" took its place. And the heading of Section I, "The Foundations of a Phenomenological Theory of Perception", stands in the editor's angle brackets: the word Husserl refuses in §1 was put back over his first Section by Claesges.

The paragraph that follows sets the refusal against the "very high goals" (*sehr hohe Ziele*) it postpones: the constitution of scientific reality by deduction, induction and logically mediated knowledge. The course will cultivate "the field of experiential phenomena", and "we will be doing well" if later workers can attempt the higher forms. The modesty is a claim about order, made good at the end of §1, where Husserl argues that one may begin "from below" (*von unten*) without fear of a phenomenological game indifferent to science, because all mediate grounding rests on immediate givenness. "Theory" is refused at the bottom of the edifice, not banished from it.

The word returns rarely in the lectures, and each return confirms the rule. At §14 the "image-theory" (*Bildertheorie*) is a theory in the refused sense, a construction that explains perception by a copy inside it, and it "has its absurdities" (Hua XVI 45). At §35 Husserl calls his result "a very good argument against the field-theory of givenness" (Hua XVI 124). At the end of §82 he admits that the "full theory" of judgments about movement and rest is not exhausted, because "only of this", the intuition of movement and rest, "have we attempted a clarification" (*Nur von dieser haben wir ja eine Aufklärung versucht*, Hua XVI 278). The last return pairs the two words §1 set apart. Theory names what lies beyond the course; clarification names what the course did.

## §3, Hua XVI 13–14: "nothing existing and yet a being"

Reads [§3.6–9](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#essential-cognition-of-perception-on-the-basis-of-phantasized-perceptions).

The examples on which the course rests are singular essences. A perception taken as an example, whether actually lived or only phantasized, is neither a psychological fact nor an abstraction. It is "this singular perception there", given absolutely, and its existence does not count. The formula for it is a paradox: "nothing existing and yet a being". The paradox licenses the course's method, because it lets a phantasized perception do everything an actual one does, and it fixes what the course's evidence is about.

> But even this positing of existence that takes place here in reflection, the positing as *cogitatio*, as actual, now existing perception, stays out of play. It has nothing to say here. Phantasy-presentifications of perceptions do us the same service, insofar as they set perceptions before our eyes and we then in fact see, and can grasp with evidence as a givenness, what we want to grasp, namely what the essence of perception is, what something like "perception" means. In the dignity of givenness as actual lived experience over against mere presentification we are not interested here, any more than we are interested at all in the constitution of the formations of consciousness that make up the evidence we are now carrying out step by step. In evidence, in the sphere of pure self-givenness, we investigate the essential peculiarities of perception. But what we investigate are precisely these, and not the evidence that itself makes up the investigating here. The investigation of the phenomenological constitution of these evidences belongs, of course, to another stratum of problems.
>
> I have also already pointed out earlier that the givens at our disposal in the examples are singular essences. If the existence of the example perceptions is out of play, even their existence as *cogitatio*, if merely phantasized perceptions, which wholly lack the positing of existence, fully suffice, then what is given here in the absolute sense is nothing existing and yet a being, namely in each case a singular essence (this singular perception there, whether it exist or not). To these singular givens the evident essential generalizations of a higher level relate; for example, we draw from them the universal essence "perception in general", which is singularized in them in such and such a way.

German:

> Aber selbst diese Existentialsetzung, die in der Reflexion hier statthat, die Setzung als *cogitatio*, als aktuelle, jetzt seiende Wahrnehmung bleibt außer Spiel. Sie hat hier nichts zu sagen. Phantasievergegenwärtigungen von Wahrnehmungen tun uns dieselben Dienste, sofern sie uns Wahrnehmungen vor Augen stellen und wir nun in der Tat das sehen, mit Evidenz als Gegebenheit fassen können, was wir fassen wollen, nämlich das Wesen von Wahrnehmung ist, was dergleichen wie „Wahrnehmung“ meint. Für die Dignität der Gegebenheit als aktuelles Erlebnis gegenüber der bloßen Vergegenwärtigung interessieren wir uns hier nicht, ebensowenig, als wir uns überhaupt für Konstitution der Bewußtseinsgestaltungen interessieren, die die Evidenz ausmachen, welche wir jetzt schrittweise vollziehen. In der Evidenz, in der Sphäre reiner Selbstgegebenheit erforschen wir die wesentlichen Eigentümlichkeiten der Wahrnehmung. Aber was wir erforschen, sind eben diese und nicht die Evidenz, die das Forschen hier selbst ausmacht. Die Untersuchung der phänomenologischen Konstitution dieser Evidenzen gehört natürlich einer anderen Problemschicht an.
>
> Ich habe auch schon früher darauf hingewiesen, daß die Gegebenheiten, über die wir in den Beispielen verfügen, singuläre Essenzen sind. Ist die Existenz der Beispielswahrnehmungen außer Spiel, selbst die Existenz als *cogitatio*, genügen vollgültig auch bloß phantasierte Wahrnehmungen, denen die Existenzsetzung gänzlich fehlt, so ist das hier im absoluten Sinn Gegebene nichts Existierendes und doch ein Seiendes, nämlich jeweils eine singuläre Essenz (diese singuläre Wahrnehmung da, mag sie existieren oder nicht). Auf diese singulären Gegebenheiten beziehen sich die evidenten Wesensverallgemeinerungen höherer Stufe; z.B. entnehmen wir ihnen das allgemeine Wesen „Wahrnehmung überhaupt'', das sich in ihnen so und so singularisiert.

*Note on the text.* The file has an editor's footnote marker after "the same service" (*dieselben Dienste*); the footnote refers the reader to two critical notes of Husserl's in Beilage I, discussed below. The marker is left out of the quotation.

The paradox depends on splitting two German words that ordinary usage keeps together. "Existing" (*Existierendes*) here means posited as actually there, whether as a physical thing or as a *cogitatio* lived now. "Being" (*Seiendes*) means whatever can be given and judged about. The conditional sentence builds the split in two steps: if even the existence of the example "as *cogitatio*" is out of play, and if phantasized perceptions "fully suffice" (*genügen vollgültig*), then what remains is a being without existence. The adverb carries weight. A phantasized perception is not a second-best example that will do for want of a real one. It is "fully valid", because existence was never part of what the analysis looks at. The first paragraph had already said the same of actual perception in reflection: its positing of existence "has nothing to say here" (*hat hier nichts zu sagen*).

The parenthesis gives the singular essence its form of words: "this singular perception there, whether it exist or not" (*diese singuläre Wahrnehmung da, mag sie existieren oder nicht*). The demonstrative "this … there" keeps the individual; the concessive subjunctive cancels its existence. A singular essence is thus not yet a universal. The universal "perception in general" is drawn from such givens at "a higher level" (*höherer Stufe*) and is "singularized in them". The course works on two levels of essence at once, and the commentary's gloss, that the method is what "later phenomenology calls free variation in phantasy" (§3.8), skips the lower one. Variation compares examples to reach the universal; the singular essence is what each example already is before any comparison.

The first paragraph also draws a line that Husserl later rejected. The course is interested neither in the "dignity" of actual over phantasized givenness nor in "the constitution of the formations of consciousness that make up the evidence"; that belongs "to another stratum of problems". Beilage I prints two notes on this page. On line 10 Husserl wrote "*Nota bene*, so already in 1907, but probably much earlier" (*Nota bene also schon 1907, aber wohl schon viel früher*), dating the method of phantasized examples back before the course. On lines 15–25, where the exclusion stands, he wrote "No! The whole further procedure consists precisely in that" (*Nein! Das ganze weitere Verfahren besteht gerade darin*), and pointed to "the correction in the next lecture" at Hua XVI 19 (Hua XVI 337). That page is the opening of §7, where the method of strata turns on the question the exclusion set aside: "how are evident assertions possible about an objectivity that is not actually given in the phenomenon?" (Hua XVI 20). The singular-essence thesis survives the note. The refusal to study how evidence is constituted does not, and by Husserl's own account the correction came in the very next lecture.

The thesis was stated a few days before in the introductory lectures. There an evident grasp of essence "points back to singular intuition", "but not therefore to singular perception", and "for the consideration of essence, perception and phantasy-presentation rank quite equally" (*Für die Wesensbetrachtung rangiert Wahrnehmung und Phantasievorstellung ganz gleich*, Hua II 69). The phantasy-tone "does *not* exist", and that fact "belongs to another consideration". What §3 adds is the ontological formula. Hua II says that existence is irrelevant; §3 says what is left when it is, a being that does not exist.

The course then uses the singular essence as its working unit. In §10 the perceptions of a house from inside and outside, considered "in phenomenological reduction or, as we can now also say, in self-posings, and indeed as singular essences" (*als singuläre Wesen*), each look different, "and yet we say, with a certain evidence, that they present the same house" (Hua XVI 26). The identity-consciousness that §§10–11 analyse joins singular essences, not lived acts, and in §11 Husserl imagines having "all singular perception-essences before our eyes" (*alle singulären Wahrnehmungsessenzen*, Hua XVI 31) in order to sort them into immanent and transcendent. The paradox of §3 is what lets that thought-experiment count as evidence: an infinite collection of perceptions, none of which need exist, is still a collection of beings.

## §5, Hua XVI 15–16: *Perzeption*, perception without taking-for-true

Reads [§5.8](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#presence-in-the-flesh-and-belief-perception-perzeption-and-position-taking).

*Perzeption* is Husserl's name for perception with belief taken out: the phenomenon of an object standing there in the flesh, before any position is taken on whether it exists. The German word for perception, *Wahrnehmung*, contains the word for "true" (*wahr*), and Husserl gets the restricted concept by taking that word apart. What is left once the "true" is removed is the stratum that §§14–43 analyse as appearance.

> The concept of perception is often restricted in such a way that it excludes what is properly to be called taking-for-true (let alone actual true-taking), that is, excludes the character of belief, the character of standing there in a believed way. That has its advantages and disadvantages. In any case, the concept that is narrower in content (or wider in extension) needs a name that holds it fast. We will say *Perzeption*, and then speak, say, of perceptual belief (perception in the normal sense), perceptual disbelief, doubt and so on. Yet where these differences of the new characters, which we call differences of position-taking, are irrelevant, and where we can do without the distinction altogether, we will keep to speaking of perception, so that we leave it open whether we have mere *Perzeptionen* or *Perzeptionen* with position-takings and with other phenomenal characters indifferent to us. At bottom, then, it is *Perzeptionen* that are analysed here, but it is more agreeable to use the familiar German expression, provided only that care is taken that its ambiguities do not mislead.

German:

> Öfters wird der Begriff der Wahrnehmung so beschränkt, daß er das eigentlich so zu nennende Für-wahr-Nehmen (geschweige denn das wirkliche Wahr-Nehmen) ausschließt, nämlich ausschließt den Charakter des Glaubens, den Charakter des in glaubhafter Weise Dastehens. Das hat seine Vorteile und Nachteile. Jedenfalls bedarf es für den inhaltlich beschränkteren (bzw. den umfangsweiteren) Begriff einer festhaltenden Benennung. Wir werden sagen Perzeption und dann etwa sprechen von perzeptivem Glauben (Wahrnehmung im normalen Sinn), perzeptivem Unglauben, Zweifel usw. Doch werden wir, wo diese Unterschiede der neuen Charaktere, die wir als Unterschiede der Stellungnahme bezeichnen, irrelevant sind und es überhaupt ohne Scheiden geht, dabei bleiben, von Wahrnehmung zu sprechen, so daß wir es offen lassen, ob wir bloße Perzeptionen haben oder Perzeptionen mit Stellungnahmen und mit sonstigen für uns gleichgültigen phänomenalen Charakteren. Im Grunde genommen werden also hierbei die Perzeptionen analysiert, aber es ist angenehmer, den vertrauten deutschen Ausdruck zu gebrauchen, wofern nur Sorge getragen ist, daß seine Vieldeutigkeiten nicht beirren.

The paragraph before prepares the split with a rhyme. §5 opens by setting "in the flesh" (*leibhaft*) against "credible" (*glaubhaft*), two adjectives with one suffix. In ordinary perception the two are "fused" (*verschmolzen*); the unmasked hallucination and the case of doubt pull them apart, and reduction then separates "presence in the flesh, which is fundamentally essential to perception as such", from "credibility, which can be added and can be missing" (Hua XVI 16). The suffix makes the two look like coordinate features. The argument makes them unequal: one belongs to the essence, the other is an addition.

The hyphens then do to the noun what the rhyme did to the adjectives. "Taking-for-true" (*Für-wahr-Nehmen*) is belief: to take something as true. "True-taking" (*Wahr-Nehmen*), "actual" (*wirklich*), is the stronger reading of the word, a taking of what is in fact true, a perception that succeeds. The parenthesis "let alone" (*geschweige denn*) ranks them. If the restricted concept leaves out belief, it leaves out success all the more. Neither hyphenated form is a word of ordinary German; they are the word *Wahrnehmung* read as a compound, and English "perception" has no part that means "true". The split also shows why the restriction is coherent. A concept that excludes belief excludes nothing that the phenomenon of presence needs, because the hallucination still presents its object in the flesh.

Husserl does not claim the restriction as his own. "The concept of perception is often restricted" (*Öfters wird … beschränkt*) reports a usage, and he weighs it coolly: it "has its advantages and disadvantages". What he adds is the name, a "holding-fast" one (*festhaltende Benennung*), and the parenthesis "narrower in content (or wider in extension)" states the logic of the abstraction: a concept with fewer marks covers more cases, hallucinations and doubted appearances included. The commentary has Husserl "propose" the Latin term for a concept of his own (§5.8). The source has him name a concept already in use.

The last sentences undo the terminology as soon as it is set up. Where position-taking does not matter, the course will "keep to speaking of perception", because the familiar German word is "more agreeable" (*angenehmer*). "At bottom", it is *Perzeptionen* that are analysed. The rest of the course follows this rule. *Wahrnehmung* stays the working word, and *Perzeption* appears where the difference from belief is the point, as in §15's mannequin, where "two *Perzeptionen* stand in conflict" (Hua XVI 46), or where the analysis is "referred to mere *Perzeption*" because position-taking is irrelevant to it.

The restriction has a limit that §9 sets at once. In absolutely giving perception, "we cannot speak of a *Perzeption* that is separable by itself and combined now with belief or disbelief, now with doubt": the having and the positing of the self-posing perception "cannot be separated analytically" (Hua XVI 25). *Perzeption* is therefore a concept of outer, presenting perception only. Only where an object is presented and not itself given can presence in the flesh survive the loss of belief. Husserl's later note on §9 strikes at the other half of the rhyme: "No! In the flesh = impressional; that makes no sense here" (*Nein! leibhaft = impressional; das gibt hier keinen Sinn*, Beilage I, ad 23, 9–10, Hua XVI 338). The note narrows *leibhaft* to a mode of impression, and so takes from §5 the assumption that presence in the flesh is one character common to every perception.

§15 then ties *Perzeption* to the course's two governing concepts. *Perzeption* is perception "in its ordinary word-sense", "only that we do not take position-taking in with it"; it is "contents of apprehension taken in their apprehension"; and since a hallucination also has an appearance, "appearance is evidently nothing other than *Perzeption*" (*Also ist Erscheinung offenbar nichts anderes als Perzeption*, Hua XVI 49). The pun of §5 thus founds the course's subject matter. Everything Sections II to V say about appearances, sides and adumbrations is said of *Wahrnehmung* with its *wahr* set aside, and §43 has to bring belief-positing back explicitly (Hua XVI 151) before the analysis can speak of the thing's being.

## §6, Hua XVI 18–19: the unease of transcendence

Reads [§6.9–10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#assertions-about-perceptions-and-assertions-about-perceived-objects-real-reell-and-intentional-components-of-perception).

The problem of transcendence, as the course first states it, is not whether the thing exists. Existence is already bracketed. It is how evidence can reach an object that is not really (*reell*) contained in the perception. The reduced perception is given whole; the table it presents is not. Yet I judge evidently that the table is three-dimensional. The passage holds the two evidences side by side and does not reconcile them, and the course's first two Sections are built to answer it.

> That these evidences exist is certain; we need only actually carry them out on examples. On the other hand, we feel an unease here. With regard to perception in the givenness of pure intuition it is clear that we can state what it is according to its essence, what this essence really contains in itself, and accordingly what a singularly given perception really has and is. But the object of a perception is appearing, "intentional", and yet not given in the same sense, not actually, fully and properly given; and so, in the consideration of essence too, its individual essence is not actually and properly given. And yet we are to judge evidently about it, to find what really constitutes it, although in the strict sense it is not something to be found at all. The perception that stands before my eyes, and on which I practise phenomenological reduction, is an absolute givenness; I have it, as it were, itself, with everything that essentially makes it up. It is an "immanence". The intentional object, however, is precisely a transcendence. Yes, it appears in the flesh, and it is the essence of perception to present it in the flesh. But do I really have it itself given, with the moments that really constitute it? The table, for example, in its three-dimensional extension, which after all belongs to its essence? Do I really have its essence? And yet I have the evidence that it is three-dimensional in the sense of this presentation in the flesh. It appears as three-dimensional and as otherwise characterized in such and such a way.
>
> In any case the givenness that belongs to the perception, to the phenomenon itself, is different from the givenness that belongs to the "perceived as such"; so the two evidences are of different character.

German:

> Daß diese Evidenzen bestehen, das ist sicher; wir brauchen sie nur an Beispielen wirklich zu vollziehen. Andererseits empfinden wir hier ein Unbehagen. Auf die Wahrnehmung in der Gegebenheit reiner Intuition bezogen ist es klar, daß wir aussagen können, was sie ihrem Wesen nach ist, was dieses Wesen reell in sich enthält und demgemäß, was eine singulär gegebene Wahrnehmung reell hat und ist. Aber der Gegenstand einer Wahrnehmung ist erscheinender, „intentionaler“, aber doch nicht in demselben Sinn gegeben, nicht wirklich, voll und eigentlich gegeben; und so ist auch in der Wesensbetrachtung nicht wirklich und eigentlich gegeben sein individuelles Wesen. Und doch sollen wir über ihn evident urteilen, vorfinden, was ihn reell konstituiert, während es im strengen Sinn gar kein Vorfindliches ist. Die Wahrnehmung, die mir vor Augen steht und an der ich phänomenologische Reduktion übe, ist eine absolute Gegebenheit, ich habe sie gleichsam selbst mit allem sie essentiell Ausmachenden. Sie ist eine „Immanenz“. Der intentionale Gegenstand ist aber gerade eine Transzendenz. Ja, er erscheint leibhaft, und es ist das Wesen der Wahrnehmung, ihn leibhaft darzustellen. Aber habe ich wirklich ihn selbst mit den ihn reell konstituierenden Momenten gegeben? Den Tisch z.B. in seiner dreidimensionalen Ausbreitung, die doch zu seinem Wesen gehört? Habe ich wirklich sein Wesen? Und doch habe ich die Evidenz, er sei dreidimensional im Sinne dieser leibhaften Darstellung. Er erscheint als dreidimensional und sonst so und so charakterisierter.
>
> Jedenfalls ist die Gegebenheit, die der Wahrnehmung, dem Phänomen selbst eignet, eine andere als die Gegebenheit, die dem „Wahrgenommenen als solchen“ eignet; also sind die beiden Evidenzen von verschiedenem Charakter.

*Note on the text.* The opening of §6 (Hua XVI 17) is garbled in the file and is not used. The passage begins after the p. 18 marker, where the text is clean.

The passage opens by granting the evidences, "that these evidences exist is certain", and names the problem as a feeling, an "unease" (*Unbehagen*). The word is not an argument. It registers that two certainties sit badly together, and the rest of the paragraph spells out why without removing either. The key adverb is "really" (*reell*), set against "actually, fully and properly" (*wirklich, voll und eigentlich*). Of the perception I can say what its essence "really contains" and what it "really has and is". Of the object I am asked to find "what really constitutes it", although it "is not something to be found at all" (*gar kein Vorfindliches*). The adverb moves from the act to the object, and the paradox lies in that move: the language of real containment, which fits the perception, is being used for what the perception does not contain.

The quotation marks set the terms of the problem at a distance. "Immanence" (*„Immanenz“*) and "intentional" stand in quotation marks; "transcendence" does not, and it comes with "precisely" (*gerade*). Husserl is not yet entitled to the first two as terms of art. §9 will define the immanent as the self-posed. Here the words are provisional, and the bare noun "transcendence" names the difficulty, not a doctrine. The questions follow in a series that narrows from the object to its essence: "do I really have it itself … ? The table … in its three-dimensional extension? Do I really have its essence?" The last question extends the problem into eidetic reflection. It is not only the individual table that escapes, but "its individual essence", which is "not actually and properly given" even "in the consideration of essence". §3's singular essence of the perception is given; the singular essence of its object is not.

The reply begins with "and yet" (*und doch*), and its scope is restricted. I have the evidence that the table is three-dimensional "in the sense of this presentation in the flesh" (*im Sinne dieser leibhaften Darstellung*). The qualifier gives the second evidence its object: not the table outright but the table as this perception presents it. Hence the conclusion that the two givennesses differ, and the second, the "perceived as such" in quotation marks, belongs in a sense "within the framework of the first", because presenting an object is part of what the perception is. The commentary reports this conclusion (§6.10). It does not say that the conclusion is also a postponement. The paragraph ends: "So further research will be needed. For now we are not far enough to solve this difficulty" (Hua XVI 19).

§7 turns the unease into a programme. Its second step asks "how are evident assertions possible about an objectivity that is not actually given in the phenomenon?" and states the thesis in four words, "objectivity constitutes itself in the lived experiences" (*Die Gegenständlichkeit konstituiert sich in den Erlebnissen*, Hua XVI 20). §9 then supplies the terms the quotation marks withheld: the self-posed is immanent, the presented transcendent. §11 answers the passage's narrower question. The relation to an object is itself given in the mode of self-posing, as the fittingness of perceptions to found identity-consciousness (row 6). On that answer, the evidence "in the sense of this presentation" is evidence about something the reduced sphere does contain, the identity-synthesis, and the "perceived as such" becomes a correlate of that synthesis.

Husserl later doubted that the answer worked. Against p. 30, the start of the problems of evidence about perception and the perceived, he wrote: "Posed, but not really solved" (*Hingestellt, aber nicht wirklich gelöst*), and marked the stretch to p. 41 (Beilage I, ad 30, 24, Hua XVI 338). The note covers §§11–13, the whole first resolution. The passage's unease therefore outlasts Section I. The commentary has §§8–10 prepare a resolution and §11 deliver it (§11.1); Husserl's note says that what §11 delivers is the problem posed again, not solved.

The difficulty had been put a few days earlier in the introductory lectures, in almost the same terms: "We do not understand how perception can reach the transcendent; but we understand how perception can reach the immanent" (*Wir verstehen nicht, wie Wahrnehmung Transzendentes treffen kann; aber wir verstehen, wie Wahrnehmung Immanentes treffen kann*, Hua II 49). There the contrast is between understanding and not understanding. §6 moves it into evidence itself: I do not only fail to understand how perception reaches the table; I have, and must account for, an evidence that it does.

## §9, Hua XVI 23–24: self-posing and presenting

Reads [§9.1–3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#self-posing-perceptions-and-presentational-perceptions-inseparability-of-perception-and-belief-within-self-posing-perception).

A self-posing (*selbststellend*) perception holds its object itself; a presenting (*darstellend*) perception sets its object before consciousness by way of something that is not the object. §9 makes this pair define immanence and transcendence, so that both words acquire their sense from two kinds of perception and not from a distinction between inside and outside the mind. The definition is built into the morphology: one verb, "to place" (*stellen*), with two prefixes.

> It seems most fitting to distinguish terminologically between self-posing perceptions and presenting perceptions. I read the expression "self-posing" in Münsterberg, but with him it has a completely different sense, so that confusion would not be feared. Through the phenomenological characterization of self-posing perception we first define the sense of immanence and transcendence. The self-posed is called immanent; the presented (be it in the sense of a perception as presented in the flesh, or as presented itself) is called transcendent. Parts or moments of something self-posed are called immanent to it, and indeed, to avoid all ambiguity, really immanent, insofar as they can evidently come to self-posing, that is, insofar as the essence of the total self-posing grounds, according to its possibility, new self-posing whose objects are evidently partially identical with the object of the total self-posing.

German:

> Am passendsten erscheint es, terminologisch zu scheiden zwischen selbststellenden Wahrnehmungen und darstellenden Wahrnehmungen. Den Ausdruck ,,Selbststellung'' habe ich bei Münsterberg gelesen, doch hat er bei ihm einen völlig anderen Sinn, so daß eine Verwechslung nicht zu fürchten wäre. Durch die phänomenologische Charakterisierung der selbststellenden Wahrnehmung definieren wir erst den Sinn von Immanenz und Transzendenz. Das Selbstgestellte heißt immanent, das Dargestellte (und sei es im Sinn einer Wahrnehmung als leibhaft oder selbst dargestellte) heißt transzendent. Teile oder Momente eines Selbstgestellten heißen ihm immanent, und zwar, um jede Zweideutigkeit zu vermeiden, reell immanent, sofern sie evidenterweise zur Selbststellung kommen können, d.h. sofern das Wesen der gesamten Selbststellung neue Selbststellung ihrer Möglichkeit nach begründet, deren Gegenstände evidenterweise partial identisch sind mit dem Gegenstand der gesamten Selbststellung.

*Note on the text.* The file has two footnote markers in this passage: one after "Münsterberg", which cites his *Grundzüge der Psychologie* (Leipzig 1900), p. 50, and one after the last sentence, which records a marginal note discussed below. Both are left out of the quotation.

The two coinages share their verb and differ in their prefix. "To present" (*darstellen*) is ordinary German: to set something forth, to show it, also to represent or portray. Its prefix *dar-* points away from the act, to a "there" where something is shown. "Self-posing" (*Selbststellung*) is Husserl's construction: the act places the object itself, with nothing between. The pair is morphologically exact and philosophically loaded, because presenting is now defined by what it lacks. The paragraph before had already put the contrast in plain words: perception that is "merely presenting in the flesh" against perception whose essence is "not only to present but to grasp the objectivity in the flesh itself" (*die leibhafte Gegenständlichkeit selbst zu fassen*, Hua XVI 23). §9 condenses "grasp itself" into a single verb.

The parenthesis shows that word order decides the sense. What is presented is transcendent "be it in the sense of a perception as presented in the flesh, or as presented itself" (*oder selbst dargestellte*). "Presented itself" (*selbst dargestellt*) and "self-posed" (*selbstgestellt*) use the same two words. In the first, "itself" qualifies the presenting: the object is presented in person, as the thing itself. In the second, "self" is fused into the verb: the object is posed as itself, with no presenting. The first is still transcendence. Presence in person is no guarantee of immanence, and the hyphenless compound marks a different kind of givenness from the adverbial phrase. English "self-presented" and "self-posed" make the two look closer than the German does, where only one is a word.

The definition is a decision about words, and the passage says so. "It seems most fitting" (*Am passendsten erscheint es*) introduces a terminological choice, and "we first define" (*definieren wir erst*) claims that immanence and transcendence have had no fixed sense until now. That claim answers §6, where both words stood in quotation marks. The source's remark on Münsterberg, which the commentary omits, guards the choice from the other side. Husserl names the borrowing and disowns the borrowed sense in one sentence: the expression has "a completely different sense" with Münsterberg, "so that confusion would not be feared". He takes a word from experimental psychology and empties it, keeping only the morphology. The defence works only because the sense is now fixed by a phenomenological characterization, not by the source of the word.

The last sentence defines "real immanence" (*reelle Immanenz*) through possibility. Parts of the self-posed are really immanent "insofar as they can evidently come to self-posing", that is, insofar as the whole grounds further self-posings whose objects are "partially identical" with it. Husserl's marginal note to the sentence calls this "what properly defines self-posing perception. The object really immanent to it: partial identification" (Hua XVI 24, note 6). Real immanence is therefore not containment in a mental box but a structure of possible identifications within the self-posed sphere. That is the structure §§10–12 then find in transcendent perception as well, where partial and total identification relate perceptions of a part to perceptions of the whole.

Husserl changed the distinction at the lectern. His note on the lecture that begins at p. 21 reports that "immediately before the start of the lecture" he realized that the distinction "had to split into a double distinction": adequate against inadequate perception, "or, what is the same, absolutely giving and not giving perception"; and self-posing against presenting perception (Beilage I, ad 21, Hua XVI 337). "Every self-posing perception is absolutely giving, but not every absolutely giving one self-posing." The note then distinguishes the "real" (*reell*) from the absolute: real givenness is "*eo ipso* absolute, but to be distinguished from it conceptually. It makes up the character of self-posing as against presentation" (Hua XVI 338). The printed §9 fuses what the delivered lecture separated. In the note's wording, self-posing is a narrower concept than absolute givenness, and the delivered §9 left open whether some presenting perceptions give their objects absolutely. The file reads, in the note, that Husserl left open "that presenting perceptions are self-posing (and they are, e.g., in the case of phantoms)"; the preceding sentence leads one to expect "absolutely giving" there, and the file cannot settle whether the slip is Husserl's, the editor's or the conversion's.

The pair governs the course only to §15. There "it is apprehension that distinguishes self-posing and presenting perception": only in the latter does a really immanent content function "as presenting", taken "as something it is not itself" (Hua XVI 46). After that the word "self-posing" does not return in the lectures. "Presentation" (*Darstellung*) becomes the course's working term for outer perception, and Husserl's later note on p. 145 insists on its strictness: "the word presentation is always to be taken in the strict sense", so that only what properly appears is presented (Beilage I, ad 145, 9, Hua XVI 339). The coinage did its work in Section I by defining its partner; once presentation was fixed, self-posing was no longer needed.

## §11, Hua XVI 31: intentional relation as fittingness

Reads [§11.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#resolution-of-a-difficulty-even-intentional-components-of-perception-given-in-the-mode-of-self-posing).

A perception's relation to its object is, in §11, a property of its essence: its fittingness to enter, with other perceptions, into a consciousness of identity, and to exclude a consciousness of difference. The relation is not a reaching beyond consciousness. It is a set of ideal possibilities of synthesis that the perception's essence grounds and that self-posing reflection can survey. Husserl's word for the property, *Schicklichkeit* (fittingness), comes from a verb that §10 has been using for two pages, and the reconception of intentionality is carried by that word family.

> Here the thought naturally presses upon us: relation to an objectivity is, phenomenologically regarded, nothing other than the fittingness, grounded in the essence of the objectivating lived experience, here of the perception, to found identity-consciousness. Or better, it is the essential peculiarity of the perception that makes it fit to found identity-consciousness and precisely thereby to exclude consciousness of distinction, and again to found consciousness of distinction and thereby to exclude identity-consciousness. That is, in the essence of each perception there are grounded, as the respective self-posings show, ideal possibilities for identity-connections with other perceptions of such and such a kind, actual or possible. Subjectively speaking: if we had all singular perception-essences before our eyes, and compared them in self-posing with the given perception *A*, and were disposed to gain and fix all the essential insight grounded in these essences, then these essences would fall into two classes. Every perception-essence of the one class would found with *A*, the essence *A*, an identity-consciousness (this too taken as an essence), and every perception-essence of the other class a consciousness of non-identity. The first group would contain, if for example we were speaking of the perception of a house, the ideal total sum of "possible perceptions" of the same house.

German:

> Da drängt sich uns jetzt natürlich der Gedanke auf: Beziehung auf eine Gegenständlichkeit ist, phänomenologisch angesehen, gar nichts anderes als die im Wesen des objektivierenden Erlebnisses, hier der Wahrnehmung, gründende Schicklichkeit, Identitätsbewußtsein zu fundieren. Oder besser, sie ist die Wesenseigentümlichkeit der Wahrnehmung, die sie geschickt macht, Identitätsbewußtsein zu fundieren und eben damit Unterscheidungsbewußtsein auszuschließen, und wieder Unterscheidungsbewußtsein zu fundieren und damit Identitätsbewußtsein auszuschließen. Nämlich im Wesen der jeweiligen Wahrnehmung gründen, wie die jeweiligen Selbststellungen es ausweisen, ideale Möglichkeiten für Identitätsverknüpfungen mit so und so gearteten anderen Wahrnehmungen, wirklichen oder möglichen. Subjektiv gesprochen: Hätten wir alle singulären Wahrnehmungsessenzen vor Augen und verglichen wir sie in der Selbststellung mit der vorgegebenen Wahrnehmung *A* und wären wir disponiert, alle Wesenseinsicht zu gewinnen und zu fixieren, die in diesen Essenzen gründet, dann zerfielen diese Essenzen in zwei Klassen. Jede Wahrnehmungsessenz der einen Klasse fundierte mit *A*, dem Wesen *A*, ein Identitätsbewußtsein (dieses ebenfalls als Wesen genommen) und jede Wahrnehmungsessenz der anderen Klasse ein Bewußtsein von Nichtidentität. Die erste Gruppe enthielte, wenn z. B. die Rede wäre von der Wahrnehmung eines Hauses, den idealen Gesamtinbegriff ,,möglicher Wahrnehmungen'' von demselben Haus.

The noun has a history in the preceding pages. In §10 perceptions of an elephant and of a stone "do not fit themselves by their essence into an identification" (*schicken sich ihrem Wesen nach in eine Identifikation nicht*), and the identity of two perceptions' object "says nothing other than that by their essence they fit themselves into the unity of an identity-consciousness" (*sich in die Einheit eines Identitätsbewußtseins schicken*, Hua XVI 28). At the end of §10 Husserl puts the verb in quotation marks and draws the moral: "it says a great deal, this 'now fitting oneself into an identity-consciousness' and on the other hand 'fitting oneself into a non-identity-consciousness'" (Hua XVI 30). §11 turns the reflexive verb into a noun and then, in the self-correction "or better" (*oder besser*), into a causative: the essential peculiarity "that makes it fit" (*die sie geschickt macht*). The three forms say one thing with different subjects. In §10 the perceptions fit themselves; in §11 their essence makes them fit. The correction moves the ground of the relation from what perceptions do in a synthesis to what their essence allows, which is why the next sentence speaks of "ideal possibilities".

*Schicklichkeit* in ordinary German also means propriety, what is seemly. The everyday sense drops away here; what remains is the sense of the verb, to fit or suit. The commentary gives the German as *Geeignetheit* (§11.3), a word that occurs nowhere in the file. The substitution loses the evidence that the definition grows out of §10's verb (logged under Corrections in the key-passage list).

The definition is deflationary in its syntax. "Relation to an objectivity is … nothing other than" (*gar nichts anderes als*) a fittingness, and the phrase "phenomenologically regarded" restricts the identity to the reduced sphere. The relation has two sides, and both are founding and excluding at once: fittingness for identity excludes distinction, fittingness for distinction excludes identity. The commentary reads the result as a demystification (§11.13). The passage's opening sentence earns that word honestly: the evidences "contain no mystery at all" and need no "enigmatic reaching out of consciousness" beyond what is really immanent to it (Hua XVI 30). But the thought-experiment that follows shows the price. "Subjectively speaking", the relation to the house becomes a division of all singular perception-essences into two classes, and the object becomes "the ideal total sum of 'possible perceptions' of the same house". The conditionals pile up, "if we had … and compared … and were disposed", because no actual consciousness performs this classification. The relation is demystified by being made ideal, and the quotation marks on "possible perceptions" admit that the house is now defined by a sum no one surveys.

The next paragraph guards the definition against a confusion. The question is "not yet" about the reality or existence of what is presented, only about the "merely 'intentional relation'" (Hua XVI 31). Fittingness for identity holds of hallucinated and phantasized houses as much as of real ones. That keeps the definition inside §5's *Perzeption*. It also leaves for later what the course must still explain: how the classes of possible perceptions are ordered, so that one perception leads to the next and not merely belongs with it.

The word family returns where the course reformulates intentionality at higher levels, and its subject changes each time. In §41 the meaning (*Meinung*) of a perception implies its relation to the sides not presented, and "that means nothing other than that the meaning has an essential character that makes it fit for such identifications, in which the evidence lies" (*der sie zu solchen Identifikationen geschickt macht*, Hua XVI 144). There the fittingness belongs to the apprehension, not to the perception as a whole, and it explains co-meant sides. In §54 the apprehension has "the characterization that makes it fit to enter into other series of fulfilment and to found the consciousness 'if *K* were such and such, then the "appearance" would be such and such'" (Hua XVI 189). The same causative formula now carries kinaesthetic motivation. The ideal classes of §11 have become ordered series governed by circumstances, which is what §11 lacked.

Husserl's later notes judge the §11 definition insufficient on exactly this point. On p. 31 he wrote that either "we refer to a continuous synthesis, then we see the identity of the 'objective relation', or we keep to the identifying 'meaning'" (Beilage I, ad 31, 36 ff., Hua XVI 338). On p. 28, where §10 grounds identity in essence, he wrote: "But that is not enough. We must assume from the outset the synthesis of continuous unity-consciousness, to which alone evident identification is referred", and added, "I saw that later myself" (ad 28, 4–20, with a cross-reference to Hua XVI 154). Fittingness for discrete identifications is the first form of the thesis. The course's own development, from the kinetic synthesis of §30 to the motivated series of §54, replaces it with continuity.

## §13, Hua XVI 40–41: consciousness as bearer

Reads [§13.9–10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#rejection-of-a-misunderstanding-articulation-of-the-giving-consciousness-not-an-articulation-of-the-object).

Consciousness, at the end of Section I, is the primal ground and bearer (*Urgrund und Träger*) of every transcendent objectivity. It is itself an object in a wider sense, since it can be identified and judged about, but it is not constituted in further connections of consciousness. It is given simply, in self-posing. The thing, by contrast, "is what it is" only through connections of intention. The passage states this asymmetry as an image of bearing, and then refuses the reading that would make the bearer an ego.

> But in a wider sense "consciousness" too, everything that this title covers, perception, intuition in general, judgment and the like, is something objective and is subject to the same law that the sense of objectivity prescribes. But this objective something has a privilege that grounds a fundamental opposition between consciousness and object in the narrower sense, insofar as all transcendent objectivity has its primal ground and bearer in the objectivity in the wider sense that we call consciousness. The thing constitutes itself in consciousness; it is an intentionality that emerges according to essential laws in connections of consciousness of a determinate kind, or is essentially proper to them, which gives sense to it and to its "actual being". By its sense it is inseparable from such connections. Consciousness itself, however, is absolute being and for that very reason not thingly being. Simple self-posing brings it to givenness as absolute; it is what is given in pure seeing; it is something identifiable and therefore also an object, but it does not first constitute itself in connections of consciousness and in the sense that binds them synthetically; it is simple and is seen. The world is, as it were, borne by consciousness, but consciousness itself needs no bearer. Meaning can in turn be meant, but it is not, and does not first constitute itself, in connections of meaning, which in turn would be what they are only in further connections of meaning, and so *in infinitum*. The thing, however, is what it is only by virtue of the connections of intention of the kinds and forms still to be investigated.
>
> Is the thing, then, only the connection of my psychic acts, my presentations, perceptions, judgments, etc.? Whoever raises this question has of course missed all understanding. The phenomenological reduction is not the solipsistic reduction, and the ego is itself something thingly, constituting itself only in intentional connection and its essential forms and only thereby showing itself. What right there is to relate the formations of consciousness to an ego, to this or that person, has first to be grounded through objectivating thinking and its logic; and this right shows its sense in phenomenological analysis. But the thinking of which the analysis speaks is no one's thinking.

German:

> Aber im weiteren Sinn ist auch „Bewußtsein“, all das, was dieser Titel befaßt, Wahrnehmung, Anschauung überhaupt, Urteil u. dgl. ein Gegenständliches und untersteht demselben Gesetz, das der Sinn von Gegenständlichkeit vorschreibt. Aber dieses Gegenständliche hat einen Vorzug, der es begründet, in fundamentaler Weise Bewußtsein und Gegenstand im engeren Sinn gegenüberzustellen, sofern nämlich alle transzendente Gegenständlichkeit ihren Urgrund und Träger in der Gegenständlichkeit im weiteren Sinn hat, die wir Bewußtsein nennen. Das Ding konstituiert sich im Bewußtsein, es ist eine in Zusammenhängen des Bewußtseins von bestimmter Art sich wesensgesetzlich herausstellende oder ihnen wesentlich eigene Intentionalität, die ihm und seinem „wirklichen Sein“ Sinn gibt. Sinngemäß ist sie von solchen Zusammenhängen unabtrennbar. Bewußtsein selbst aber ist absolutes Sein und eben darum nicht dingliches Sein. Die schlichte Selbststellung bringt es als absolut zur Gegebenheit, es ist das im puren Schauen Gegebene; es ist ein Identifizierbares und darum auch Gegenstand, aber es konstituiert sich nicht erst in Zusammenhängen des Bewußtseins und dem sie synthetisch verbindenden Sinn, es ist einfach und wird geschaut. Die Welt wird gleichsam getragen vom Bewußtsein, aber das Bewußtsein selbst braucht keinen Träger. Das Meinen kann wieder gemeint werden, aber es ist und konstituiert sich nicht erst in Zusammenhängen des Meinens, und diese wieder ‹sind›, was sie sind, nur in weiteren Zusammenhängen des Meinens, und so *in infinitum*. Das Ding aber ist, was es ist, nur vermöge der Zusammenhänge der Intention der noch zu erforschenden Arten und Formen.
>
> Ist also das Ding nur der Zusammenhang meiner psychischen Akte, meiner Vorstellungen, Wahrnehmungen, Urteile etc.? Wer diese Frage einwirft, hat natürlich alles Verständnis verfehlt. Die phänomenologische Reduktion ist ja nicht die solipsistische Reduktion, und das Ich ist ja selbst ein Dingliches, nur im intentionalen Zusammenhang und seinen wesentlichen Formen sich Konstituierendes und nur dadurch sich Ausweisendes. Welches Recht es hat, die Bewußtseinsgestaltungen auf ein Ich zu beziehen, auf die oder jene Person, das ist durch objektivierendes Denken und durch seine Logik erst zu begründen; und dieses Recht weist seinen Sinn aus in der phänomenologischen Analyse. Das Denken aber, von dem sie spricht, ist niemandes Denken.

*Note on the text.* The angle brackets around ‹sind› [are] mark an editor's addition, kept as the file has it.

The passage starts by levelling before it ranks. Consciousness "too" is "something objective" (*ein Gegenständliches*) and "is subject to the same law that the sense of objectivity prescribes". Only then comes its "privilege" (*Vorzug*). The order matters for what "absolute" means. Consciousness is not exempt from objectivity; it is the one objectivity that does not need constituting. The two senses of "object", wider and narrower, carry the distinction, and the narrower sense is defined by dependence: transcendent objectivity "has its primal ground and bearer" in consciousness. The paired nouns are not synonyms. A ground (*Urgrund*) is what something follows from; a bearer (*Träger*) is what something rests on. The next sentence specifies the dependence as one of sense: intentionality "gives sense" to the thing and to its "actual being", set in quotation marks because the word is now to be understood through that intentionality.

The negations then define the absolute by what it does not need. Consciousness "does not first constitute itself" (*konstituiert sich nicht erst*) in connections of consciousness; it is "simple and is seen". The "first" (*erst*) recurs in the sentence about meaning, and there it shows the argument. If meaning were constituted only in connections of meaning, these would in turn be what they are only in further connections, "and so *in infinitum*". The absoluteness of consciousness is the stop to a regress. That is a stronger claim than the evidence of self-posing gives. Self-posing shows that the act is given whole; it does not show that no further synthesis underlies its being given, and Husserl's later note on p. 28, which demands "from the outset the synthesis of continuous unity-consciousness" beneath every evident identification (Beilage I, Hua XVI 338), points to such a synthesis.

The image of the bearer carries a hedge. "The world is, as it were, borne by consciousness" (*gleichsam getragen*). The "as it were" withholds the causal or metaphysical sense of bearing, and the second half, "but consciousness itself needs no bearer", turns the image against an infinite chain of substrates. The sentence reverses the old notion of substance: what needs no bearer is not the thing but the consciousness the thing depends on. Six years later *Ideas I* states the same reversal in scholastic words: immanent being is absolute being in the sense "that it in principle *nulla 're' indiget ad existendum*" [needs no "thing" in order to exist] (§49, Hua III/1 104, first edition 92). The 1907 image already holds the thesis; the 1913 formula adds the thought-experiment of the annihilation of the world, which in 1907 waits until §84.

The second paragraph meets the obvious objection with a rebuke, "whoever raises this question has of course missed all understanding". The reply rests on two claims. "The phenomenological reduction is not the solipsistic reduction", and "the ego is itself something thingly". The ego belongs on the side of the constituted, so the bearer of the world cannot be "my" acts. The final sentence is the sharpest in Section I: "the thinking of which the analysis speaks is no one's thinking" (*niemandes Denken*). The pronoun negates ownership, not existence. It makes consciousness anonymous in order to keep it absolute, and it defers to "objectivating thinking and its logic" the right to ascribe acts to persons. The commentary states both claims (§13.10). It does not mark the cost: the course has defined consciousness as what needs no bearer, and has then removed the subject that might have seemed to bear it.

The course does not return to the thesis in these words. Its constitutive work makes good the last sentence of the first paragraph, that the thing is what it is only through "connections of intention of the kinds and forms still to be investigated" (*noch zu erforschenden*): the participle is the programme of Sections II to VI. And the ego that §13 sets among the constituted comes back as the Body. In §47 the Body is "bearer of the ego" (Hua XVI 162), and in §83 its anomaly, that it cannot run away from me, sends constitution towards empathy with others (rows 21 and 32). The bearer-image passes from consciousness to the Body, and what §13 called "something thingly" turns out to be the one thing that cannot be constituted like the others.

## §14, Hua XVI 43: space as form of thinghood, not "form of intuition"

Reads [§14.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#contents-of-sensation-and-the-qualities-of-things).

Space, for the lectures, belongs to the side of the object. It is "the necessary form of thinghood", and sensations have spatial character only as material that a thing's spatiality will be constituted from, not because consciousness imposes a spatial form on them. The sentence that says so carries its argument in a correction of vocabulary: Kant's term "form of intuition" (*Anschauungsform*) is not a doctrine to be refined but "a fundamentally false expression".

> … a certain spatial modification. But space is the necessary form of thinghood and not the form of lived experiences, and more precisely of "sensuous" lived experiences. Form of intuition is a fundamentally false expression and implies, in Kant too, a fatal false opinion. That sensation and perception are not one and the same, that between the sensed total content and the perceived object, between single sensation-contents and the features "corresponding to them", there is a real distinction to be made, is evident from the outset.

German:

> … einer gewissen räumlichen Modifikation. Der Raum aber ist die notwendige Form der Dinglichkeit und nicht die Form der Erlebnisse, und näher der „sinnlichen“ Erlebnisse. Anschauungsform ist ein grundfalscher Ausdruck und impliziert auch bei Kant eine verhängnisvolle Irrmeinung. Daß Empfindung und Wahrnehmung nicht einerlei ist, daß zwischen empfundenem Gesamtinhalt und wahrgenommenem Gegenstand, zwischen einzelnen Empfindungsinhalten und den „ihnen entsprechenden“ Merkmalen wirklich zu scheiden ist, das ist von vornherein evident.

*Note on the text.* The opening of §14 (Hua XVI 42–43) is garbled in the file and sits inside a stray code fence. The clean text resumes in mid-sentence on the line that closes the fence, with "a certain spatial modification" (*einer gewissen räumlichen Modifikation*). What the sensations undergo that modification, and what the sentence that ends there claimed, cannot be read. The reading uses only what follows the break, and cannot say what the "But" (*aber*) answers.

The thesis is built as a pair of genitives: space is the form "of thinghood" (*der Dinglichkeit*) and not the form "of lived experiences". The second genitive is narrowed at once, "and more precisely of 'sensuous' lived experiences". The quotation marks on "sensuous" (*„sinnlichen“*) point to Kant's sensibility (*Sinnlichkeit*), the faculty whose form, in the Transcendental Aesthetic, space is said to be. Husserl distances the word because the lectures do not recognize a faculty of sensibility at all, only sensations as really immanent contents. The "necessary" (*notwendige*) is kept, and so is "form": Husserl does not deny that space is necessary and formal. He relocates the necessity, from the conditions under which a subject receives data to the essence of a thing.

The second sentence turns the relocation against a word. "Form of intuition" is "a fundamentally false expression" (*ein grundfalscher Ausdruck*), and it "implies, in Kant too, a fatal false opinion" (*impliziert auch bei Kant eine verhängnisvolle Irrmeinung*). The verb "implies" makes the error a consequence of the term: whoever speaks of a form of intuition is already committed to placing space in the act. The "too" (*auch*) is the sharpest word in the sentence. It treats Kant as one case among the users of the expression, not as its author whose system might justify it. *Irrmeinung* is stronger than "error": a false opinion, a conviction one holds. The commentary's "fatally erroneous position" keeps the force but loses the point that Husserl objects first to an expression and only through it to a position.

The last sentence shows why the thesis belongs at the head of §14. Sensation and perception "are not one and the same", and between sensed contents and the features "corresponding to them", again in quotation marks, "there is a real distinction to be made". If space were a form of sensations, the sensed extension of a colour and the thing's extension would coincide, and the correspondence would be identity. The distinction of sensation and perceived feature is the ground of the anti-Kantian sentence, and it is "evident from the outset". The next paragraph supplies the argument the word "evident" claims: if a sensation-complex and a thing were simply the same content twice, "we would have two things, an immanent and a transcendent one" (Hua XVI 44). The yellow sphere then shows that one objective colour corresponds to a continuous variety of sensed colours (row 9).

The lectures keep the thesis by building the form of thinghood from below. In §20 spatial form "by itself is nothing" (*Raumform für sich nichts ist*) and needs the materializing qualities as its *materia prima* (Hua XVI 67, row 13); space is a form of things only as filled. Sections IV and V then constitute it from the oculomotor field and kinaesthetic motivation, and §61 calls space "the ordered context of things" (*Ordnungszusammenhang der Dinge*, row 25). The anti-Kantian sentence is the promissory note those Sections redeem: they show how a form of things arises in a consciousness that has none.

Kant returns once more, and the return limits the quarrel. In §34 Husserl meets the difficulty "that space is infinite (as Kant says, it is represented as an infinite given magnitude. At any rate, we do not doubt the evidence of infinity.) But the field is finite" (Hua XVI 119). Kant's description of space is accepted where it states an evidence, and the infinity it attributes to space drives the argument for necessary inadequacy in §35 (row 17). What Husserl rejects is the location the word "form of intuition" gives that infinity. Space is infinite as the form of possible things, and the finitude belongs to the field of sensation, which is exactly the place Kant's expression would have put space.

## §14, Hua XVI 44–45: adumbration and the image-theory

Reads [§14.8–10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#contents-of-sensation-and-the-qualities-of-things).

Adumbration (*Abschattung*) names the relation of sensed content to objective feature: an unvarying quality of the thing is presented by a sensed content that varies continuously. The term enters the lectures here, with Locke's yellow sphere, and its first use carries the course's first negative thesis. Because one feature is presented by many different sensations, and even within one perception by a shaded sensation, the perception cannot contain an image of its object.

> If we have the perception of a uniformly yellow sphere (it is an example of Locke's), and indeed a perception unchanged during its duration, with an unchanged object, then we say on the one hand: the seen coloration, related of course to the "properly" appearing front side, is uniform.
>
> But if we attend to the immanent content of the perception, we find a continuous adumbration of the yellow, and it is clear that a connection of necessity holds here: only if such adumbration is sensed does a uniformly coloured sphere present itself. And again it must be pointed out that identity of objective determinations in the sphere of proper perception is not compatible with a change or a continuous alteration of sensation-contents, but is necessarily demanded with respect to many determinations. As the sphere approaches or recedes we have continually new and new perceptions. The moment of extension within the sensation demands a constant alteration, if the manifold perceptions are to gain unity in consciousness: it is the same sphere, unchanged in its extension and form.
>
> So identity of the objective feature in no way means identity of the corresponding sensation. The sensation offers no reduplication of the feature. The perception therefore does not contain in itself an image of the object, the word "image" taken in the common sense, as a second thing that represents an original through similarity. The perception contains neither a repetition of the whole thing nor one of single features. The image-theory, looked at from many sides, has its absurdities. Here we have the one side.

German:

> Haben wir die Wahrnehmung einer gleichmäßig gelben Kugel (es ist ein Beispiel Lockes), und zwar eine während ihrer Dauer unveränderte Wahrnehmung bei unverändertem Gegenstand, so sagen wir einerseits: Die gesehene Färbung, natürlich bezogen auf die „eigentlich“ erscheinende Vorderseite, ist gleichmäßig.
>
> Achten wir aber auf den immanenten Inhalt der Wahrnehmung, so finden wir eine stetige Abschattung des Gelb, und es ist klar, daß hierbei ein Notwendigkeitszusammenhang besteht: nur wenn solche Abschattung empfunden ist, stellt sich eine gleichmäßig gefärbte Kugel dar. Und wieder ist darauf hinzuweisen, daß Identität gegenständlicher Bestimmungen sich in der Sphäre eigentlicher Wahrnehmung nicht mit einem Wechsel oder einer kontinuierlichen Veränderung von Empfindungsinhalten verträgt, sondern hinsichtlich vieler Bestimmungen notwendig gefordert ist. Bei Annäherung oder Entfernung der Kugel haben wir stetig neue und neue Wahrnehmungen. Das Moment der Ausbreitung innerhalb der Empfindung verlangt eine ständige Änderung, wenn die mannigfaltigen Wahrnehmungen Einheit gewinnen sollen im Bewußtsein: es sei dieselbe Kugel, ihrer Ausdehnung und Form nach unverändert.
>
> Also Identität des objektiven Merkmals bedeutet in keiner Weise Identität der entsprechenden Empfindung. Die Empfindung bietet keine Reduplikation des Merkmals. Die Wahrnehmung enthält in sich also kein Bild des Gegenstandes, das Wort ,,Bild" im gemeinen Sinn genommen, als ein zweites Ding, das ein Original durch Ähnlichkeit repräsentiert. Die Wahrnehmung enthält weder eine Wiederholung des ganzen Dinges noch eine solche einzelner Merkmale. Die Bildertheorie hat, von vielen Seiten her angesehen, ihre Widersinnigkeiten. Hier haben wir die eine Seite.

*Note on the text.* The second paragraph's middle sentence, as the file has it, says that identity of objective determinations "is not compatible with" (*nicht … verträgt*) a change of sensation-contents, "but is necessarily demanded". The construction *nicht … sondern* [not … but] requires a contrast of two degrees, and the sense of the paragraph needs "not only compatible with … but demanded" (*nicht nur … sondern*). A word seems to have been lost. The rendering follows the file, and the reading relies only on the "necessarily demanded", which both versions share. The file also carries a conversion artifact after the paragraph (a footnote reading "Full text of footnote 1"), left out here.

The word arrives without a definition, as the name of something "we find" when we attend to the immanent content: "a continuous adumbration of the yellow" (*eine stetige Abschattung des Gelb*). It is the word's first occurrence in the file. *Schatten* is shadow, and *abschatten* is to shade off, to grade a colour by degrees. The genitive is precise: the adumbration is *of the yellow*, a shading of the colour itself across the sensed extent, not a shadow cast on the sphere. The term therefore starts as a description of sensation, and the objective uniformity on the other side is guarded by two signs of distance. The coloration is uniform "related of course to the 'properly' appearing front side", with "properly" in quotation marks: the concept of proper appearance, which §16 will define, is used before it is introduced.

The necessity is a biconditional in one direction. "Only if such adumbration is sensed does a uniformly coloured sphere present itself" (*nur wenn … stellt sich … dar*). Uniformity on the side of the object requires non-uniformity on the side of sensation. That is stronger than the image-theory's opponents usually claim. It is not that sensation may differ from the feature; it must, if this feature is to be presented. The second step extends the claim from a single perception to a series. As the sphere approaches or recedes, "the moment of extension within the sensation demands a constant alteration" if the perceptions are to be unified in the consciousness "it is the same sphere". The subjunctive *es sei* (it be) gives the content of that consciousness as a quoted judgment. Identity is what the alteration serves.

The conclusion falls into three negative sentences, each narrower than the last. Identity of the feature "in no way means" (*bedeutet in keiner Weise*) identity of sensation; the sensation offers "no reduplication" (*keine Reduplikation*); the perception contains no image "in the common sense" of a second thing similar to an original. "Reduplication" names what the image-theory needs, a second instance of the same. The paragraph then deflates its own result. "The image-theory, looked at from many sides, has its absurdities. Here we have the one side." The commentary renders this as "The image-theory is, looked at from any side, countersensical" (§14.10). The German claims less: many sides, not every side, and "its absurdities", a plural noun, not a predicate of the whole theory. "Here we have the one side" marks the argument as partial. Adumbration refutes the image-theory with respect to similarity; other sides, such as the regress of an inner image that would itself need perceiving, are left aside (logged under Corrections in the key-passage list).

The passage prepares §15. If sensation does not resemble the feature, something must make it present the feature, and §15 names that something apprehension, "the plus" beyond sensation (row 10). Adumbration is the datum that makes apprehension necessary: the shaded yellow is "taken as" a uniform yellow it is not.

The word then changes its subject across the course. Here it is a shading within one sensed content. In §21 Husserl speaks of the "adumbration-extension of the sensuous data" (*Abschattungsextension der sinnlichen Data*, Hua XVI 70), still on the side of sensation. In §30 it has become a series: to each moment of the object belongs "a continuity of constituting appearance-moments … for example the adumbration of the square", and "it is not one adumbration there" (Hua XVI 102). The thing is then "the identical in the systematic unity of these adumbrations or possibilities of adumbration" (row 15). Between §14 and §30 the term passes from the sensed colour of a static perception to the ordered manifold of appearances in motion. The yellow sphere already contains both, the shading within one perception and the "new and new perceptions" of approach, and the course develops the second out of the first.

## §15, Hua XVI 46: apprehension as "the plus"

Reads [§15.3–5](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#presentational-contents-and-apprehension-perception).

Apprehension (*Auffassung*) is what a perception has beyond its sensations: the character by which a sensed content is taken as presenting something it is not. §15 introduces it as a remainder. The mannequin shows that one sensational basis can bear two perceptions, so the difference between them must lie in something else, "the plus". The concept is defined by subtraction, and its first description is a metaphor.

> Further, we have already mentioned the evidence that the complexion of sensation-contents is very different and yet the corresponding perceptions present themselves by their essence as perceptions of the same object; conversely it also holds that the same complexion of sensation-contents can ground different perceptions, perceptions of different objects, as every doll proves, insofar as here, with the standpoint held fixed, two *Perzeptionen* stand in conflict, that of the doll-thing and that of the presented human being, both built on the same basis of sensation. This consideration directs our gaze to the plus that is to be found really in the perception over and above the sensation-complexion, and that, in the most intimate interpenetration with what is sensed, first makes up perception. The sensation-contents by themselves still contain nothing of the character of perception, nothing of its direction toward the one perceived object; they are not yet what makes a thingly objective something stand there in the flesh. We call this surplus the character of apprehension and say that the sensation-contents undergo apprehension. Through the apprehension they, which in themselves would be as it were a dead stuff, gain animating significance, such that with them an object comes to presentation. With respect to this we call the contents presenting contents, in contrast to the presented, namely the objective determinations.

German:

> Wir haben schon der Evidenz Erwähnung getan, daß die Komplexion der Empfindungsinhalte sehr verschieden *ist* und doch die zugehörigen Wahrnehmungen ihrem Wesen nach als Wahrnehmungen von demselben Gegenstand sich ausgeben; umgekehrt gilt auch, daß dieselbe Komplexion von Empfindungsinhalten verschiedene Wahrnehmungen begründen kann, Wahrnehmungen von verschiedenen Gegenständen, wie jede Puppe beweist, sofern hier bei festgehaltenem Standpunkt zwei Perzeptionen in Widerstreit stehen, die des Puppendinges und die des dargestellten Menschen, beide aufgebaut auf derselben Empfindungsgrundlage. Diese Erwägung lenkt unseren Blick auf das Plus, das über die Empfindungskomplexion hinaus in der Wahrnehmung reell zu finden ist und das in allerengster Durchdringung mit dem Empfundenen erst Wahrnehmung ausmacht. Die Empfindungsinhalte für sich enthalten noch nichts von dem Charakter der Wahrnehmung, nichts von ihrer Richtung auf den einen wahrgenommenen Gegenstand; sie sind noch nicht das, was es macht, daß ein Dinggegenständliches in Leibhaftigkeit dasteht. Wir nennen diesen Überschuß den Charakter der Auffassung und sagen, daß die Empfindungsinhalte Auffassung erfahren. Durch die Auffassung gewinnen sie, die an sich gleichsam ein toter Stoff wären, beseelende Bedeutung derart, daß mit ihnen ein Gegenstand zur Darstellung kommt. Mit Beziehung darauf nennen wir die Inhalte darstellende Inhalte im Gegensatz zu den dargestellten nämlich den gegenständlichen Bestimmtheiten.

The argument is a double variation, and the two halves are not equally strong. §14 gave the first: different sensations, one object. The doll gives the converse: one sensational basis, two objects. Only the converse forces the conclusion, because two perceptions that share every sensed content and still differ must differ in something that is not sensed. The phrase "with the standpoint held fixed" (*bei festgehaltenem Standpunkt*) secures this. Nothing in the visual data changes while perception flips between a doll and a person. "As every doll proves" (*wie jede Puppe beweist*) is a confident universal for an experience that occurs only with some dolls and some light; the argument needs only one case.

The new item is first named in arithmetic: "the plus" (*das Plus*), then "this surplus" (*diesen Überschuß*). Both nouns say only that there is more. The location is precise: the plus is "to be found really (*reell*) in the perception", so apprehension belongs to the act's real content and not to the object. And its relation to sensation is not addition. It "first makes up perception" (*erst Wahrnehmung ausmacht*) "in the most intimate interpenetration" with what is sensed. The arithmetic word says more; the qualifying phrase denies that the more is a separable part. §41 repeats the point: the unity of presenting content and apprehension is "not a sum or a twoness bound together", but "a most intimate unity, which we sought to characterize by the word animation" (Hua XVI 144).

The metaphor then supplies what subtraction cannot. In themselves the contents "would be as it were a dead stuff" (*gleichsam ein toter Stoff*), and through apprehension they gain "animating significance" (*beseelende Bedeutung*). The subjunctive "would be" and the hedge "as it were" mark that dead stuff never occurs: sensations are always met already animated, and their deadness is an abstraction. "Significance" (*Bedeutung*) links apprehension to meaning; the contents come to signify an object. The next paragraph gives the definition that the metaphor stands in for. In presenting perception a really immanent content functions "as presenting", as one "that is not simply taken but taken-as something it is not itself" (*nicht einfach gefaßt, sondern als etwas aufgefaßt wird, was er nicht selbst ist*, Hua XVI 46). The prefix *auf-* turns grasping into grasping-as. That is the whole difference between self-posing, which takes its content simply, and presenting, which takes it as another. The sentence after it is "At first this is a quite crude analysis" (*eine ganz rohe Analyse*).

The commentary calls this "the content-apprehension schema that will govern every subsequent constitutive analysis" (§15.16). Husserl's own word for it is "crude", and the course keeps qualifying the schema rather than applying it. In §41 the animation-metaphor is cut back to its phenomenal basis: "this animation says nothing other than the consciousness that is the changing element when the sensuous data are apprehended in different senses", as when "we waver conflictingly between two thing-apprehensions" (Hua XVI 146). The doll has become the definition: apprehension is what changes when sensation does not. And his later note on p. 160 splits the schema's central term: "presentation in the narrower sense (projection …) and presentation in the wider sense. Not all sensation-data are presenting in the first sense" (Beilage I, ad 160, 16, Hua XVI 340). The kinaesthetic sensations of §46 are just such data, functioning in apprehension without presenting (row 20). What governs the later analyses is the distinction of sensation and apprehension; the schema of an animated stuff is what they revise.

*Ideas I* keeps the metaphor and adds the distance §15 lacked. Over the sensuous moments, "which in themselves have nothing of intentionality", lies "a layer as it were 'animating', sense-giving" (*eine gleichsam „beseelende“, sinngebende … Schicht*), and whether all sensuous lived experiences "everywhere and necessarily carry some 'animating apprehension'" is "not to be decided here" (§85, Hua III/1 192). In 1913 the animation stands in quotation marks, and the universality §15 took for granted has become an open question.

## §16, Hua XVI 50, 52: proper and improper appearance, and the inadequacy Husserl retracted

Reads [§16.3–5](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-object-of-apprehension-as-appearance-appearance-in-the-proper-sense).

Proper appearance (*eigentliche Erscheinung*) is the part of a perception's total apprehension that has presenting contents; improper appearance is the rest, which makes the thing's other determinations conscious without presenting them. From this division §16 draws the thesis that every single outer perception is essentially inadequate. The passage states the thesis with care, restricts it in its last sentences, and was later marked by Husserl as doubtful. Its understanding is a thesis held under qualification.

> Accordingly, the total apprehension and total appearance of the perception divides into the proper appearance, whose correlate is the side of the object that is perceived in the proper sense, that actually comes to presentation, and into the improper appearance, the appendix of the proper appearance, which has its correlate in the rest of the object. It presents nothing, although it does in a certain way make its objective something conscious. If we attend to a moment of this "turned-away" side, we can no longer say of it, taken by itself, that we have it before our eyes, that we see it, that we perceive it. Only what is presented is seen, is "intuitively" given. But proper and improper appearance are nothing separate; they are one in the appearance in the wider sense.
>
> […]
>
> Here, then, there is an essential inadequation of every single outer perception. It is perception of spatial things and as such can only be one-sided. A three-dimensional intuition, we can also say, is impossible as a proper intuition that would bring to presentation at one stroke the full content of the thing according to all its constitutive parts and moments, outside and inside, front side and back side. There is only improper intuition of space. That holds for the moment of spatial shape as well as for the spatial filling founded in it. At any rate this holds with respect to the means of presentation and forms of presentation at our disposal phenomenologically, thus for the specific essences of physical contents and forms of apprehension that we find in phenomenological seeing and that delimit our idea of outer perception. That it lies in the essence of physical contents in general that, as the sensation-basis of presenting perceptions, they can necessarily found only one-sided thing-perceptions, one must not venture to assert.

German:

> Sonach teilt sich die Gesamtauffassung und Gesamterscheinung der Wahrnehmung in die eigentliche Erscheinung, deren Korrelat die im eigentlichen Sinn wahrgenommene, die wirklich zur Darstellung kommende Seite des Gegenstandes ist, und in die uneigentliche Erscheinung, den Appendix der eigentlichen Erscheinung, der sein Korrelat in dem Übrigen des Gegenstandes hat. Sie stellt nichts dar, obschon sie doch ihr Gegenständliches in gewisser Weise bewußt macht. Achten wir auf ein Moment dieser „abgewendeten“ Seite, so können wir von ihm, für sich genommen, nicht mehr sagen, wir hätten es vor Augen, wir schauten es, wir nähmen es wahr. Nur Dargestelltes ist geschaut, ist „intuitiv“ gegeben. Eigentliche und uneigentliche Erscheinung sind aber nichts Getrenntes, sondern einig in der Erscheinung im weiteren Sinn.
>
> […]
>
> Hier liegt also eine wesentliche Inadäquation jeder vereinzelten äußeren Wahrnehmung vor. Sie ist Wahrnehmung räumlicher Dinge und kann als solche nur einseitig sein. Eine dreidimensionale Anschauung, können wir auch sagen, ist als eigentliche Anschauung, die den vollen Gehalt des Dinges nach allen konstitutiven Teilen und Momenten, nach Außen und Innen, nach Vorderseite und Hinterseite in einem Schlage zur Darstellung brächte, unmöglich. Es gibt nur uneigentliche Raumanschauung. Das gilt für das Moment der Raumgestalt ebenso wie für die in ihr fundierte Raumfülle. Jedenfalls gilt dies mit Beziehung auf die Darstellungsmittel und Darstellungsformen, über die wir phänomenologisch verfügen, also für die spezifischen Wesen von physischen Inhalten und Auffassungsformen, die wir im phänomenologischen Schauen vorfinden und die unsere Idee von äußerer Wahrnehmung begrenzen. Daß es im Wesen physischer Inhalte überhaupt liegt, daß sie als Empfindungsunterlage darstellender Wahrnehmungen notwendig nur einseitige Dingwahrnehmungen zu fundieren vermögen, das darf man nicht wagen zu behaupten.

*Note on the text.* The first paragraph is on Hua XVI 50, the second on p. 52. The pages between (from p. 50 to the head of p. 52) are garbled in the file, with a bogus heading and invented words, and are not quoted. The second paragraph follows a clean stretch on p. 52 that contrasts side and piece. The editor's footnote marker after "Here" (*Hier liegt*) points to Husserl's critical note, discussed below; it is left out.

The definition rests on the verb "present" (*darstellen*), which §9 made the mark of transcendent perception and §15 tied to presenting contents. Proper appearance is "the side … that actually comes to presentation"; improper appearance "presents nothing, although it does in a certain way make its objective something conscious" (*Sie stellt nichts dar, obschon sie doch … bewußt macht*). The concessive "although" keeps two things apart that the course will need: presenting and making conscious. The noun "appendix" (*Appendix*) ranks the two at once. The improper is an attachment of the proper. But the last sentence of the paragraph withdraws the ranking: the two "are nothing separate", "one in the appearance in the wider sense". The improper appearance is dependent on the proper in being defined by it, and inseparable from it in being.

Quotation marks mark the words that §16 is redefining. The back is the "turned-away" side, and only what is presented is "intuitively" given. "Intuitive" is now restricted to the presented, so that the back of the house, which ordinary speech would call seen with the rest, is not intuited at all. This is the step that licenses the later thesis. If only presented determinations are intuited, and a three-dimensional thing has determinations that no single perception presents, then "there is only improper intuition of space" (*Es gibt nur uneigentliche Raumanschauung*). The restriction of "intuitive" carries the inference. Without it, the claim would only be that no perception presents every side, which no one disputes.

The thesis paragraph then qualifies itself in two steps that the commentary does not report. "At any rate" (*Jedenfalls*) restricts it to "the means of presentation and forms of presentation at our disposal phenomenologically", the physical contents and forms of apprehension "that delimit our idea of outer perception". And the last sentence refuses the stronger claim: that it lies "in the essence of physical contents in general" to found only one-sided perceptions, "one must not venture to assert" (*das darf man nicht wagen zu behaupten*). The "essential inadequation" is essential to the idea of outer perception that our presenting means define. It is not, in §16, a law about any possible sensuous basis. The commentary calls the one-sidedness something that "belongs to the very essence of thing-perception as such" (§16.5). §16 says less, and says that it says less (logged under Corrections in the key-passage list).

Husserl's later notes go further than the hedge. Against p. 51, 10 ff., where the doctrine begins in pages the file has lost, he wrote: "Not used. The following doctrine of the essential inadequacy of thing-perception, insofar as it could only be one-sided, is to be reconsidered on the basis of the later analysis of space, which probably speaks against it" (*Nicht benutzt. … ist neu zu erwägen aufgrund der späteren Raumanalyse, die wohl dagegen spricht*). Against p. 52, 20 ff., where the thesis paragraph stands: "Reconsider; doubtful!" (*Neu überlegen; zweifelhaft!*, Beilage I, Hua XVI 338). "Not used" seems to mean that the pages were not delivered. The objection is precise. It is not to inadequacy in general but to inadequacy "insofar as it could only be one-sided", and the ground is "the later analysis of space", the kinaesthetic constitution of Sections IV and V, in which a thing is given through motivated series and not as the sum of sides.

The course itself moves the thesis to new ground before Husserl's note does. §§32–35 rebuild necessary inadequacy without appeal to one-sidedness, from the finitude of the field against the infinity of space, and §33 states that the limit holds "neither for us nor for the dear Lord" (rows 16–17). That argument claims what §16 would "not venture to assert": a limit that does not depend on our presenting means. The two arguments do not stand or fall together. §16's one-sidedness is what the later note doubts. §35's economy of finite means is what the course keeps. Read with its hedge and its retraction, §16 is the first and weakest form of a thesis the course goes on to prove differently.

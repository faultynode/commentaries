# Husserl, *Thing and Space*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md) works through the 1907 lectures section by section. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding goes in the rest of the course.

The main commentary is keyed to Richard Rojcewicz's English translation, *Thing and Space: Lectures of 1907*, which is not in the corpus. The readings quote the German original instead, Husserliana XVI, *Ding und Raum. Vorlesungen 1907*, edited by Ulrich Claesges (1973), from the converted file `sources/husserl/husserl-hua-16-ding-und-raum-vorlesungen-1907.md` in `faultynode/sources`. The § numbers are the same in both editions. Passages are cited by § and Hua XVI page, and unit references such as "§11.3" name the third paragraph of the commentary under §11. All English renderings of the German are my own working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows. They are not Rojcewicz's, and where a reading questions the commentary's English, it cannot tell whether the commentary or the translator is responsible.

The file has limits that bear on more than one reading. Its page numbers come from marker comments, which can fall in mid-paragraph, so a page can be one out; the marker for p. 64 reads "6". Several stretches are garbled or invented and are never quoted: a fragment in §1 (p. 5), the opening of §6 (p. 17), the opening of §14 (pp. 42–43), §16 pp. 51–52, most of §40 (pp. 139–141, where the file also lacks the § heading), §69 pp. 241–242, and a block of invented English mathematics after §84. Where one of these touches a passage, the reading says so. The file keeps Husserl's italics, so emphasis can be argued from with care. Beilage I prints the critical notes Husserl later wrote on his own manuscript, keyed to page and line ("ad 51, 10 ff."). The main commentary never uses them; several readings do, because they record what Husserl came to think of the passage at hand.

## §1, Hua XVI 4: the refusal of "theory"

Reads [§1.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-world-of-natural-experience-and-the-world-of-scientific-theory).

For Husserl in 1907, a "theory" explains and grounds: it derives what is given from something else, as mathematics and natural science do. The lectures do something else. They clarify (*aufklären*) the essence of what experience gives, starting from its lowest forms, and nothing in that field needs explaining in the theorist's sense. The passage declines the obvious name for the course, "theory of experience", and the refusal is a statement of method before any method has been shown.

> I can now designate the theme of the following lectures in a few words. It concerns the foundational parts of a future phenomenology of experience: a clarification of the essence of experiential givenness, starting from the nearest and first beginnings and carried from there as deep and as far as possible, at least in its lower forms and levels. If one insisted on seeking support in the ways of speaking now current, one would have to speak of a theory of experience. But on the one hand I have, as already with the word epistemology [theory of knowledge], my misgivings about talk of theory, which fits mathematical and natural-scientific explanation and grounding, but certainly fits nowhere where there is nothing in this sense to explain and to ground; it does not fit even the experiencing of the morphologizing or typifying sciences, let alone that of phenomenology. Besides, since Cohen and the Marburg School applied the title "theory of experience" to Kant's critique of experiential knowledge, it has taken on a breadth that spans just about all the problems of pure theoretical reason; and we are not so bold here as to want to face and treat them all.

German:

> Ich kann nun mit wenigen Worten das Thema der nachfolgenden Vorlesungen bezeichnen. Es handelt sich um die grundlegenden Partien einer künftigen Phänomenologie der Erfahrung, um eine von den nächstliegenden und ersten Anfängen ausgehende und von da aus möglichst tief und weit geführte Aufklärung des Wesens der Erfahrungsgegebenheit, mindestens in ihren niederen Formen und Stufen. Wollte man durchaus Anlehnung an die jetzt üblichen Redeweisen suchen, so wäre von Theorie der Erfahrung zu sprechen. Doch habe ich einerseits, wie schon bei dem Worte Erkenntnistheorie, meine Bedenken gegen die Rede von Theorie, die auf mathematische und naturwissenschaftliche Erklärung und Begründung paßt, aber sicher nirgends paßt, wo es in diesem Sinne nichts zu erklären und zu begründen gilt, schon nicht auf das Erfahren der morphologisierenden oder typisierenden Wissenschaften, geschweige denn auf das der Phänomenologie. Zudem hat der Titel ,,Theorie der Erfahrung“, seit Cohen und die Marburger Schule ihn auf Kants Kritik der Erfahrungserkenntnis angewandt haben, eine Weite angenommen, die so ziemlich alle Probleme der reinen theoretischen Vernunft umspannt; und so kühn sind wir hier nicht, uns allen stellen und alle behandeln zu wollen.

The positive name comes before the refused one. The course is "a phenomenology of experience" and, in apposition, "a clarification (*Aufklärung*) of the essence of experiential givenness". "Theory of experience" enters only in a counterfactual: "if one insisted on seeking support in the ways of speaking now current" (*Wollte man durchaus Anlehnung … suchen*). The subjunctive keeps the phrase at arm's length before the objection to it is even stated. The two objections that follow are not of the same kind, and the commentary runs them together. The first is about the word "theory" (*die Rede von Theorie*): it belongs where something is "to be explained and grounded" (*zu erklären und zu begründen*), and the paired infinitives define theory by its task. The second is about a title already occupied, "theory of experience" in quotation marks, which the Marburg School stretched over the whole of Kant's critique. Only the first is a principle. The second is a matter of scale, and Husserl answers it with irony, "we are not so bold".

The scope of the first objection is wider than phenomenology. Theory does not fit "even the experiencing of the morphologizing or typifying sciences" (*schon nicht … geschweige denn*): the sciences that describe shapes and types, botany or anatomy, already do without explanation in the mathematical sense, "let alone" phenomenology. The graded construction places phenomenology at the far end of a line of descriptive disciplines, not outside science altogether. The parenthetical "as already with the word epistemology" (*wie schon bei dem Worte Erkenntnistheorie*) shows that the misgiving is old and is about a word. Husserl objects to "theory" even inside the name of the discipline he had been practising.

The same week's lectures state the principle. In the fourth of the five introductory lectures, published as *Die Idee der Phänomenologie*, phenomenology "proceeds by seeing, clarifying, determining sense and distinguishing sense" and "does not theorize and mathematize; that is, it carries out no explanations in the sense of deductive theory" (*Sie theoretisiert und mathematisiert nicht; sie vollzieht nämlich keine Erklärungen im Sinne der deduktiven Theorie*, Hua II 58–59). The §1 passage applies that rule to the course's own title. Two editorial facts in Hua XVI make the refusal concrete. The 1904/05 course had been announced as "Main Parts of a Phenomenology and Theory of Knowledge" (*Hauptstücke aus einer Phänomenologie und Theorie der Erkenntnis*, editor's note, Hua XVI 123); the 1907 course was announced as "Main Parts of Phenomenology and Critique of Reason" (*Hauptstücke aus der Phänomenologie und Kritik der Vernunft*, editor's introduction). Between the two, "theory" left the title and "critique" took its place. And the heading of Section I, "The Foundations of a Phenomenological Theory of Perception", stands in the editor's angle brackets: the word Husserl refuses in §1 was put back over his first Section by Claesges.

The paragraph that follows sets the refusal against the "very high goals" (*sehr hohe Ziele*) it postpones: the constitution of scientific reality by deduction, induction and logically mediated knowledge. The course will cultivate "the field of experiential phenomena", and "we will be doing well" if later workers can attempt the higher forms. The modesty is a claim about order, made good at the end of §1, where Husserl argues that one may begin "from below" (*von unten*) without fear of a phenomenological game indifferent to science, because all mediate grounding rests on immediate givenness. "Theory" is refused at the bottom of the edifice, not banished from it.

The word returns rarely in the lectures, and each return confirms the rule. At §14 the "image-theory" (*Bildertheorie*) is a theory in the refused sense, a construction that explains perception by a copy inside it, and it "has its absurdities" (Hua XVI 45). At §35 Husserl calls his result "a very good argument against the field-theory of givenness" (Hua XVI 124). At the end of §82 he admits that the "full theory" of judgments about movement and rest is not exhausted, because "only of this", the intuition of movement and rest, "have we attempted a clarification" (*Nur von dieser haben wir ja eine Aufklärung versucht*, Hua XVI 278). The last return pairs the two words §1 set apart. Theory names what lies beyond the course; clarification names what the course did.

## §3, Hua XVI 13–14: "nothing existing and yet a being"

Reads [§3.6–9](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#essential-cognition-of-perception-on-the-basis-of-phantasized-perceptions).

The examples on which the course rests are singular essences. A perception taken as an example, whether actually lived or only phantasized, is neither a psychological fact nor an abstraction. It is "this singular perception there", given absolutely, and its existence does not count. The formula for it is a paradox: "nothing existing and yet a being". The paradox licenses the course's method, because it lets a phantasized perception do everything an actual one does, and it fixes what the course's evidence is about.

> But even this positing of existence that takes place here in reflection, the positing as *cogitatio*, as actual, now existing perception, stays out of play. It has nothing to say here. Phantasy-presentifications of perceptions do us the same service, insofar as they set perceptions before our eyes and we then in fact see, and can grasp with evidence as a givenness, what we want to grasp, namely what the essence of perception is, what something like "perception" means. In the dignity of givenness as actual lived experience over against mere presentification we are not interested here, any more than we are interested at all in the constitution of the formations of consciousness that make up the evidence we are now carrying out step by step. In evidence, in the sphere of pure self-givenness, we investigate the essential peculiarities of perception. But what we investigate are precisely these, and not the evidence that itself makes up the investigating here. The investigation of the phenomenological constitution of these evidences belongs, of course, to another stratum of problems.
>
> I have also already pointed out earlier that the givens at our disposal in the examples are singular essences. If the existence of the example perceptions is out of play, even their existence as *cogitatio*, if merely phantasized perceptions, which wholly lack the positing of existence, fully suffice, then what is given here in the absolute sense is nothing existing and yet a being, namely in each case a singular essence (this singular perception there, whether it exist or not). To these singular givens the evident essential generalizations of a higher level relate; for example, we draw from them the universal essence "perception in general", which is singularized in them in such and such a way.

German:

> Aber selbst diese Existentialsetzung, die in der Reflexion hier statthat, die Setzung als *cogitatio*, als aktuelle, jetzt seiende Wahrnehmung bleibt außer Spiel. Sie hat hier nichts zu sagen. Phantasievergegenwärtigungen von Wahrnehmungen tun uns dieselben Dienste, sofern sie uns Wahrnehmungen vor Augen stellen und wir nun in der Tat das sehen, mit Evidenz als Gegebenheit fassen können, was wir fassen wollen, nämlich das Wesen von Wahrnehmung ist, was dergleichen wie „Wahrnehmung“ meint. Für die Dignität der Gegebenheit als aktuelles Erlebnis gegenüber der bloßen Vergegenwärtigung interessieren wir uns hier nicht, ebensowenig, als wir uns überhaupt für Konstitution der Bewußtseinsgestaltungen interessieren, die die Evidenz ausmachen, welche wir jetzt schrittweise vollziehen. In der Evidenz, in der Sphäre reiner Selbstgegebenheit erforschen wir die wesentlichen Eigentümlichkeiten der Wahrnehmung. Aber was wir erforschen, sind eben diese und nicht die Evidenz, die das Forschen hier selbst ausmacht. Die Untersuchung der phänomenologischen Konstitution dieser Evidenzen gehört natürlich einer anderen Problemschicht an.
>
> Ich habe auch schon früher darauf hingewiesen, daß die Gegebenheiten, über die wir in den Beispielen verfügen, singuläre Essenzen sind. Ist die Existenz der Beispielswahrnehmungen außer Spiel, selbst die Existenz als *cogitatio*, genügen vollgültig auch bloß phantasierte Wahrnehmungen, denen die Existenzsetzung gänzlich fehlt, so ist das hier im absoluten Sinn Gegebene nichts Existierendes und doch ein Seiendes, nämlich jeweils eine singuläre Essenz (diese singuläre Wahrnehmung da, mag sie existieren oder nicht). Auf diese singulären Gegebenheiten beziehen sich die evidenten Wesensverallgemeinerungen höherer Stufe; z.B. entnehmen wir ihnen das allgemeine Wesen „Wahrnehmung überhaupt'', das sich in ihnen so und so singularisiert.

*Note on the text.* The file has an editor's footnote marker after "the same service" (*dieselben Dienste*); the footnote refers the reader to two critical notes of Husserl's in Beilage I, discussed below. The marker is left out of the quotation.

The paradox depends on splitting two German words that ordinary usage keeps together. "Existing" (*Existierendes*) here means posited as actually there, whether as a physical thing or as a *cogitatio* lived now. "Being" (*Seiendes*) means whatever can be given and judged about. The conditional sentence builds the split in two steps: if even the existence of the example "as *cogitatio*" is out of play, and if phantasized perceptions "fully suffice" (*genügen vollgültig*), then what remains is a being without existence. The adverb carries weight. A phantasized perception is not a second-best example that will do for want of a real one. It is "fully valid", because existence was never part of what the analysis looks at. The first paragraph had already said the same of actual perception in reflection: its positing of existence "has nothing to say here" (*hat hier nichts zu sagen*).

The parenthesis gives the singular essence its form of words: "this singular perception there, whether it exist or not" (*diese singuläre Wahrnehmung da, mag sie existieren oder nicht*). The demonstrative "this … there" keeps the individual; the concessive subjunctive cancels its existence. A singular essence is thus not yet a universal. The universal "perception in general" is drawn from such givens at "a higher level" (*höherer Stufe*) and is "singularized in them". The course works on two levels of essence at once, and the commentary's gloss, that the method is what "later phenomenology calls free variation in phantasy" (§3.8), skips the lower one. Variation compares examples to reach the universal; the singular essence is what each example already is before any comparison.

The first paragraph also draws a line that Husserl later rejected. The course is interested neither in the "dignity" of actual over phantasized givenness nor in "the constitution of the formations of consciousness that make up the evidence"; that belongs "to another stratum of problems". Beilage I prints two notes on this page. On line 10 Husserl wrote "*Nota bene*, so already in 1907, but probably much earlier" (*Nota bene also schon 1907, aber wohl schon viel früher*), dating the method of phantasized examples back before the course. On lines 15–25, where the exclusion stands, he wrote "No! The whole further procedure consists precisely in that" (*Nein! Das ganze weitere Verfahren besteht gerade darin*), and pointed to "the correction in the next lecture" at Hua XVI 19 (Hua XVI 337). That page is the opening of §7, where the method of strata turns on the question the exclusion set aside: "how are evident assertions possible about an objectivity that is not actually given in the phenomenon?" (Hua XVI 20). The singular-essence thesis survives the note. The refusal to study how evidence is constituted does not, and by Husserl's own account the correction came in the very next lecture.

The thesis was stated a few days before in the introductory lectures. There an evident grasp of essence "points back to singular intuition", "but not therefore to singular perception", and "for the consideration of essence, perception and phantasy-presentation rank quite equally" (*Für die Wesensbetrachtung rangiert Wahrnehmung und Phantasievorstellung ganz gleich*, Hua II 69). The phantasy-tone "does *not* exist", and that fact "belongs to another consideration". What §3 adds is the ontological formula. Hua II says that existence is irrelevant; §3 says what is left when it is, a being that does not exist.

The course then uses the singular essence as its working unit. In §10 the perceptions of a house from inside and outside, considered "in phenomenological reduction or, as we can now also say, in self-posings, and indeed as singular essences" (*als singuläre Wesen*), each look different, "and yet we say, with a certain evidence, that they present the same house" (Hua XVI 26). The identity-consciousness that §§10–11 analyse joins singular essences, not lived acts, and in §11 Husserl imagines having "all singular perception-essences before our eyes" (*alle singulären Wahrnehmungsessenzen*, Hua XVI 31) in order to sort them into immanent and transcendent. The paradox of §3 is what lets that thought-experiment count as evidence: an infinite collection of perceptions, none of which need exist, is still a collection of beings.

## §5, Hua XVI 15–16: *Perzeption*, perception without taking-for-true

Reads [§5.8](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#presence-in-the-flesh-and-belief-perception-perzeption-and-position-taking).

*Perzeption* is Husserl's name for perception with belief taken out: the phenomenon of an object standing there in the flesh, before any position is taken on whether it exists. The German word for perception, *Wahrnehmung*, contains the word for "true" (*wahr*), and Husserl gets the restricted concept by taking that word apart. What is left once the "true" is removed is the stratum that §§14–43 analyse as appearance.

> The concept of perception is often restricted in such a way that it excludes what is properly to be called taking-for-true (let alone actual true-taking), that is, excludes the character of belief, the character of standing there in a believed way. That has its advantages and disadvantages. In any case, the concept that is narrower in content (or wider in extension) needs a name that holds it fast. We will say *Perzeption*, and then speak, say, of perceptual belief (perception in the normal sense), perceptual disbelief, doubt and so on. Yet where these differences of the new characters, which we call differences of position-taking, are irrelevant, and where we can do without the distinction altogether, we will keep to speaking of perception, so that we leave it open whether we have mere *Perzeptionen* or *Perzeptionen* with position-takings and with other phenomenal characters indifferent to us. At bottom, then, it is *Perzeptionen* that are analysed here, but it is more agreeable to use the familiar German expression, provided only that care is taken that its ambiguities do not mislead.

German:

> Öfters wird der Begriff der Wahrnehmung so beschränkt, daß er das eigentlich so zu nennende Für-wahr-Nehmen (geschweige denn das wirkliche Wahr-Nehmen) ausschließt, nämlich ausschließt den Charakter des Glaubens, den Charakter des in glaubhafter Weise Dastehens. Das hat seine Vorteile und Nachteile. Jedenfalls bedarf es für den inhaltlich beschränkteren (bzw. den umfangsweiteren) Begriff einer festhaltenden Benennung. Wir werden sagen Perzeption und dann etwa sprechen von perzeptivem Glauben (Wahrnehmung im normalen Sinn), perzeptivem Unglauben, Zweifel usw. Doch werden wir, wo diese Unterschiede der neuen Charaktere, die wir als Unterschiede der Stellungnahme bezeichnen, irrelevant sind und es überhaupt ohne Scheiden geht, dabei bleiben, von Wahrnehmung zu sprechen, so daß wir es offen lassen, ob wir bloße Perzeptionen haben oder Perzeptionen mit Stellungnahmen und mit sonstigen für uns gleichgültigen phänomenalen Charakteren. Im Grunde genommen werden also hierbei die Perzeptionen analysiert, aber es ist angenehmer, den vertrauten deutschen Ausdruck zu gebrauchen, wofern nur Sorge getragen ist, daß seine Vieldeutigkeiten nicht beirren.

The paragraph before prepares the split with a rhyme. §5 opens by setting "in the flesh" (*leibhaft*) against "credible" (*glaubhaft*), two adjectives with one suffix. In ordinary perception the two are "fused" (*verschmolzen*); the unmasked hallucination and the case of doubt pull them apart, and reduction then separates "presence in the flesh, which is fundamentally essential to perception as such", from "credibility, which can be added and can be missing" (Hua XVI 16). The suffix makes the two look like coordinate features. The argument makes them unequal: one belongs to the essence, the other is an addition.

The hyphens then do to the noun what the rhyme did to the adjectives. "Taking-for-true" (*Für-wahr-Nehmen*) is belief: to take something as true. "True-taking" (*Wahr-Nehmen*), "actual" (*wirklich*), is the stronger reading of the word, a taking of what is in fact true, a perception that succeeds. The parenthesis "let alone" (*geschweige denn*) ranks them. If the restricted concept leaves out belief, it leaves out success all the more. Neither hyphenated form is a word of ordinary German; they are the word *Wahrnehmung* read as a compound, and English "perception" has no part that means "true". The split also shows why the restriction is coherent. A concept that excludes belief excludes nothing that the phenomenon of presence needs, because the hallucination still presents its object in the flesh.

Husserl does not claim the restriction as his own. "The concept of perception is often restricted" (*Öfters wird … beschränkt*) reports a usage, and he weighs it coolly: it "has its advantages and disadvantages". What he adds is the name, a "holding-fast" one (*festhaltende Benennung*), and the parenthesis "narrower in content (or wider in extension)" states the logic of the abstraction: a concept with fewer marks covers more cases, hallucinations and doubted appearances included. The commentary has Husserl "propose" the Latin term for a concept of his own (§5.8). The source has him name a concept already in use.

The last sentences undo the terminology as soon as it is set up. Where position-taking does not matter, the course will "keep to speaking of perception", because the familiar German word is "more agreeable" (*angenehmer*). "At bottom", it is *Perzeptionen* that are analysed. The rest of the course follows this rule. *Wahrnehmung* stays the working word, and *Perzeption* appears where the difference from belief is the point, as in §15's mannequin, where "two *Perzeptionen* stand in conflict" (Hua XVI 46), or where the analysis is "referred to mere *Perzeption*" because position-taking is irrelevant to it.

The restriction has a limit that §9 sets at once. In absolutely giving perception, "we cannot speak of a *Perzeption* that is separable by itself and combined now with belief or disbelief, now with doubt": the having and the positing of the self-posing perception "cannot be separated analytically" (Hua XVI 25). *Perzeption* is therefore a concept of outer, presenting perception only. Only where an object is presented and not itself given can presence in the flesh survive the loss of belief. Husserl's later note on §9 strikes at the other half of the rhyme: "No! In the flesh = impressional; that makes no sense here" (*Nein! leibhaft = impressional; das gibt hier keinen Sinn*, Beilage I, ad 23, 9–10, Hua XVI 338). The note narrows *leibhaft* to a mode of impression, and so takes from §5 the assumption that presence in the flesh is one character common to every perception.

§15 then ties *Perzeption* to the course's two governing concepts. *Perzeption* is perception "in its ordinary word-sense", "only that we do not take position-taking in with it"; it is "contents of apprehension taken in their apprehension"; and since a hallucination also has an appearance, "appearance is evidently nothing other than *Perzeption*" (*Also ist Erscheinung offenbar nichts anderes als Perzeption*, Hua XVI 49). The pun of §5 thus founds the course's subject matter. Everything Sections II to V say about appearances, sides and adumbrations is said of *Wahrnehmung* with its *wahr* set aside, and §43 has to bring belief-positing back explicitly (Hua XVI 151) before the analysis can speak of the thing's being.

## §6, Hua XVI 18–19: the unease of transcendence

Reads [§6.9–10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#assertions-about-perceptions-and-assertions-about-perceived-objects-real-reell-and-intentional-components-of-perception).

The problem of transcendence, as the course first states it, is not whether the thing exists. Existence is already bracketed. It is how evidence can reach an object that is not really (*reell*) contained in the perception. The reduced perception is given whole; the table it presents is not. Yet I judge evidently that the table is three-dimensional. The passage holds the two evidences side by side and does not reconcile them, and the course's first two Sections are built to answer it.

> That these evidences exist is certain; we need only actually carry them out on examples. On the other hand, we feel an unease here. With regard to perception in the givenness of pure intuition it is clear that we can state what it is according to its essence, what this essence really contains in itself, and accordingly what a singularly given perception really has and is. But the object of a perception is appearing, "intentional", and yet not given in the same sense, not actually, fully and properly given; and so, in the consideration of essence too, its individual essence is not actually and properly given. And yet we are to judge evidently about it, to find what really constitutes it, although in the strict sense it is not something to be found at all. The perception that stands before my eyes, and on which I practise phenomenological reduction, is an absolute givenness; I have it, as it were, itself, with everything that essentially makes it up. It is an "immanence". The intentional object, however, is precisely a transcendence. Yes, it appears in the flesh, and it is the essence of perception to present it in the flesh. But do I really have it itself given, with the moments that really constitute it? The table, for example, in its three-dimensional extension, which after all belongs to its essence? Do I really have its essence? And yet I have the evidence that it is three-dimensional in the sense of this presentation in the flesh. It appears as three-dimensional and as otherwise characterized in such and such a way.
>
> In any case the givenness that belongs to the perception, to the phenomenon itself, is different from the givenness that belongs to the "perceived as such"; so the two evidences are of different character.

German:

> Daß diese Evidenzen bestehen, das ist sicher; wir brauchen sie nur an Beispielen wirklich zu vollziehen. Andererseits empfinden wir hier ein Unbehagen. Auf die Wahrnehmung in der Gegebenheit reiner Intuition bezogen ist es klar, daß wir aussagen können, was sie ihrem Wesen nach ist, was dieses Wesen reell in sich enthält und demgemäß, was eine singulär gegebene Wahrnehmung reell hat und ist. Aber der Gegenstand einer Wahrnehmung ist erscheinender, „intentionaler“, aber doch nicht in demselben Sinn gegeben, nicht wirklich, voll und eigentlich gegeben; und so ist auch in der Wesensbetrachtung nicht wirklich und eigentlich gegeben sein individuelles Wesen. Und doch sollen wir über ihn evident urteilen, vorfinden, was ihn reell konstituiert, während es im strengen Sinn gar kein Vorfindliches ist. Die Wahrnehmung, die mir vor Augen steht und an der ich phänomenologische Reduktion übe, ist eine absolute Gegebenheit, ich habe sie gleichsam selbst mit allem sie essentiell Ausmachenden. Sie ist eine „Immanenz“. Der intentionale Gegenstand ist aber gerade eine Transzendenz. Ja, er erscheint leibhaft, und es ist das Wesen der Wahrnehmung, ihn leibhaft darzustellen. Aber habe ich wirklich ihn selbst mit den ihn reell konstituierenden Momenten gegeben? Den Tisch z.B. in seiner dreidimensionalen Ausbreitung, die doch zu seinem Wesen gehört? Habe ich wirklich sein Wesen? Und doch habe ich die Evidenz, er sei dreidimensional im Sinne dieser leibhaften Darstellung. Er erscheint als dreidimensional und sonst so und so charakterisierter.
>
> Jedenfalls ist die Gegebenheit, die der Wahrnehmung, dem Phänomen selbst eignet, eine andere als die Gegebenheit, die dem „Wahrgenommenen als solchen“ eignet; also sind die beiden Evidenzen von verschiedenem Charakter.

*Note on the text.* The opening of §6 (Hua XVI 17) is garbled in the file and is not used. The passage begins after the p. 18 marker, where the text is clean.

The passage opens by granting the evidences, "that these evidences exist is certain", and names the problem as a feeling, an "unease" (*Unbehagen*). The word is not an argument. It registers that two certainties sit badly together, and the rest of the paragraph spells out why without removing either. The key adverb is "really" (*reell*), set against "actually, fully and properly" (*wirklich, voll und eigentlich*). Of the perception I can say what its essence "really contains" and what it "really has and is". Of the object I am asked to find "what really constitutes it", although it "is not something to be found at all" (*gar kein Vorfindliches*). The adverb moves from the act to the object, and the paradox lies in that move: the language of real containment, which fits the perception, is being used for what the perception does not contain.

The quotation marks set the terms of the problem at a distance. "Immanence" (*„Immanenz“*) and "intentional" stand in quotation marks; "transcendence" does not, and it comes with "precisely" (*gerade*). Husserl is not yet entitled to the first two as terms of art. §9 will define the immanent as the self-posed. Here the words are provisional, and the bare noun "transcendence" names the difficulty, not a doctrine. The questions follow in a series that narrows from the object to its essence: "do I really have it itself … ? The table … in its three-dimensional extension? Do I really have its essence?" The last question extends the problem into eidetic reflection. It is not only the individual table that escapes, but "its individual essence", which is "not actually and properly given" even "in the consideration of essence". §3's singular essence of the perception is given; the singular essence of its object is not.

The reply begins with "and yet" (*und doch*), and its scope is restricted. I have the evidence that the table is three-dimensional "in the sense of this presentation in the flesh" (*im Sinne dieser leibhaften Darstellung*). The qualifier gives the second evidence its object: not the table outright but the table as this perception presents it. Hence the conclusion that the two givennesses differ, and the second, the "perceived as such" in quotation marks, belongs in a sense "within the framework of the first", because presenting an object is part of what the perception is. The commentary reports this conclusion (§6.10). It does not say that the conclusion is also a postponement. The paragraph ends: "So further research will be needed. For now we are not far enough to solve this difficulty" (Hua XVI 19).

§7 turns the unease into a programme. Its second step asks "how are evident assertions possible about an objectivity that is not actually given in the phenomenon?" and states the thesis in four words, "objectivity constitutes itself in the lived experiences" (*Die Gegenständlichkeit konstituiert sich in den Erlebnissen*, Hua XVI 20). §9 then supplies the terms the quotation marks withheld: the self-posed is immanent, the presented transcendent. §11 answers the passage's narrower question. The relation to an object is itself given in the mode of self-posing, as the fittingness of perceptions to found identity-consciousness (row 6). On that answer, the evidence "in the sense of this presentation" is evidence about something the reduced sphere does contain, the identity-synthesis, and the "perceived as such" becomes a correlate of that synthesis.

Husserl later doubted that the answer worked. Against p. 30, the start of the problems of evidence about perception and the perceived, he wrote: "Posed, but not really solved" (*Hingestellt, aber nicht wirklich gelöst*), and marked the stretch to p. 41 (Beilage I, ad 30, 24, Hua XVI 338). The note covers §§11–13, the whole first resolution. The passage's unease therefore outlasts Section I. The commentary has §§8–10 prepare a resolution and §11 deliver it (§11.1); Husserl's note says that what §11 delivers is the problem posed again, not solved.

The difficulty had been put a few days earlier in the introductory lectures, in almost the same terms: "We do not understand how perception can reach the transcendent; but we understand how perception can reach the immanent" (*Wir verstehen nicht, wie Wahrnehmung Transzendentes treffen kann; aber wir verstehen, wie Wahrnehmung Immanentes treffen kann*, Hua II 49). There the contrast is between understanding and not understanding. §6 moves it into evidence itself: I do not only fail to understand how perception reaches the table; I have, and must account for, an evidence that it does.

## §9, Hua XVI 23–24: self-posing and presenting

Reads [§9.1–3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#self-posing-perceptions-and-presentational-perceptions-inseparability-of-perception-and-belief-within-self-posing-perception).

A self-posing (*selbststellend*) perception holds its object itself; a presenting (*darstellend*) perception sets its object before consciousness by way of something that is not the object. §9 makes this pair define immanence and transcendence, so that both words acquire their sense from two kinds of perception and not from a distinction between inside and outside the mind. The definition is built into the morphology: one verb, "to place" (*stellen*), with two prefixes.

> It seems most fitting to distinguish terminologically between self-posing perceptions and presenting perceptions. I read the expression "self-posing" in Münsterberg, but with him it has a completely different sense, so that confusion would not be feared. Through the phenomenological characterization of self-posing perception we first define the sense of immanence and transcendence. The self-posed is called immanent; the presented (be it in the sense of a perception as presented in the flesh, or as presented itself) is called transcendent. Parts or moments of something self-posed are called immanent to it, and indeed, to avoid all ambiguity, really immanent, insofar as they can evidently come to self-posing, that is, insofar as the essence of the total self-posing grounds, according to its possibility, new self-posing whose objects are evidently partially identical with the object of the total self-posing.

German:

> Am passendsten erscheint es, terminologisch zu scheiden zwischen selbststellenden Wahrnehmungen und darstellenden Wahrnehmungen. Den Ausdruck ,,Selbststellung'' habe ich bei Münsterberg gelesen, doch hat er bei ihm einen völlig anderen Sinn, so daß eine Verwechslung nicht zu fürchten wäre. Durch die phänomenologische Charakterisierung der selbststellenden Wahrnehmung definieren wir erst den Sinn von Immanenz und Transzendenz. Das Selbstgestellte heißt immanent, das Dargestellte (und sei es im Sinn einer Wahrnehmung als leibhaft oder selbst dargestellte) heißt transzendent. Teile oder Momente eines Selbstgestellten heißen ihm immanent, und zwar, um jede Zweideutigkeit zu vermeiden, reell immanent, sofern sie evidenterweise zur Selbststellung kommen können, d.h. sofern das Wesen der gesamten Selbststellung neue Selbststellung ihrer Möglichkeit nach begründet, deren Gegenstände evidenterweise partial identisch sind mit dem Gegenstand der gesamten Selbststellung.

*Note on the text.* The file has two footnote markers in this passage: one after "Münsterberg", which cites his *Grundzüge der Psychologie* (Leipzig 1900), p. 50, and one after the last sentence, which records a marginal note discussed below. Both are left out of the quotation.

The two coinages share their verb and differ in their prefix. "To present" (*darstellen*) is ordinary German: to set something forth, to show it, also to represent or portray. Its prefix *dar-* points away from the act, to a "there" where something is shown. "Self-posing" (*Selbststellung*) is Husserl's construction: the act places the object itself, with nothing between. The pair is morphologically exact and philosophically loaded, because presenting is now defined by what it lacks. The paragraph before had already put the contrast in plain words: perception that is "merely presenting in the flesh" against perception whose essence is "not only to present but to grasp the objectivity in the flesh itself" (*die leibhafte Gegenständlichkeit selbst zu fassen*, Hua XVI 23). §9 condenses "grasp itself" into a single verb.

The parenthesis shows that word order decides the sense. What is presented is transcendent "be it in the sense of a perception as presented in the flesh, or as presented itself" (*oder selbst dargestellte*). "Presented itself" (*selbst dargestellt*) and "self-posed" (*selbstgestellt*) use the same two words. In the first, "itself" qualifies the presenting: the object is presented in person, as the thing itself. In the second, "self" is fused into the verb: the object is posed as itself, with no presenting. The first is still transcendence. Presence in person is no guarantee of immanence, and the hyphenless compound marks a different kind of givenness from the adverbial phrase. English "self-presented" and "self-posed" make the two look closer than the German does, where only one is a word.

The definition is a decision about words, and the passage says so. "It seems most fitting" (*Am passendsten erscheint es*) introduces a terminological choice, and "we first define" (*definieren wir erst*) claims that immanence and transcendence have had no fixed sense until now. That claim answers §6, where both words stood in quotation marks. The source's remark on Münsterberg, which the commentary omits, guards the choice from the other side. Husserl names the borrowing and disowns the borrowed sense in one sentence: the expression has "a completely different sense" with Münsterberg, "so that confusion would not be feared". He takes a word from experimental psychology and empties it, keeping only the morphology. The defence works only because the sense is now fixed by a phenomenological characterization, not by the source of the word.

The last sentence defines "real immanence" (*reelle Immanenz*) through possibility. Parts of the self-posed are really immanent "insofar as they can evidently come to self-posing", that is, insofar as the whole grounds further self-posings whose objects are "partially identical" with it. Husserl's marginal note to the sentence calls this "what properly defines self-posing perception. The object really immanent to it: partial identification" (Hua XVI 24, note 6). Real immanence is therefore not containment in a mental box but a structure of possible identifications within the self-posed sphere. That is the structure §§10–12 then find in transcendent perception as well, where partial and total identification relate perceptions of a part to perceptions of the whole.

Husserl changed the distinction at the lectern. His note on the lecture that begins at p. 21 reports that "immediately before the start of the lecture" he realized that the distinction "had to split into a double distinction": adequate against inadequate perception, "or, what is the same, absolutely giving and not giving perception"; and self-posing against presenting perception (Beilage I, ad 21, Hua XVI 337). "Every self-posing perception is absolutely giving, but not every absolutely giving one self-posing." The note then distinguishes the "real" (*reell*) from the absolute: real givenness is "*eo ipso* absolute, but to be distinguished from it conceptually. It makes up the character of self-posing as against presentation" (Hua XVI 338). The printed §9 fuses what the delivered lecture separated. In the note's wording, self-posing is a narrower concept than absolute givenness, and the delivered §9 left open whether some presenting perceptions give their objects absolutely. The file reads, in the note, that Husserl left open "that presenting perceptions are self-posing (and they are, e.g., in the case of phantoms)"; the preceding sentence leads one to expect "absolutely giving" there, and the file cannot settle whether the slip is Husserl's, the editor's or the conversion's.

The pair governs the course only to §15. There "it is apprehension that distinguishes self-posing and presenting perception": only in the latter does a really immanent content function "as presenting", taken "as something it is not itself" (Hua XVI 46). After that the word "self-posing" does not return in the lectures. "Presentation" (*Darstellung*) becomes the course's working term for outer perception, and Husserl's later note on p. 145 insists on its strictness: "the word presentation is always to be taken in the strict sense", so that only what properly appears is presented (Beilage I, ad 145, 9, Hua XVI 339). The coinage did its work in Section I by defining its partner; once presentation was fixed, self-posing was no longer needed.

## §11, Hua XVI 31: intentional relation as fittingness

Reads [§11.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#resolution-of-a-difficulty-even-intentional-components-of-perception-given-in-the-mode-of-self-posing).

A perception's relation to its object is, in §11, a property of its essence: its fittingness to enter, with other perceptions, into a consciousness of identity, and to exclude a consciousness of difference. The relation is not a reaching beyond consciousness. It is a set of ideal possibilities of synthesis that the perception's essence grounds and that self-posing reflection can survey. Husserl's word for the property, *Schicklichkeit* (fittingness), comes from a verb that §10 has been using for two pages, and the reconception of intentionality is carried by that word family.

> Here the thought naturally presses upon us: relation to an objectivity is, phenomenologically regarded, nothing other than the fittingness, grounded in the essence of the objectivating lived experience, here of the perception, to found identity-consciousness. Or better, it is the essential peculiarity of the perception that makes it fit to found identity-consciousness and precisely thereby to exclude consciousness of distinction, and again to found consciousness of distinction and thereby to exclude identity-consciousness. That is, in the essence of each perception there are grounded, as the respective self-posings show, ideal possibilities for identity-connections with other perceptions of such and such a kind, actual or possible. Subjectively speaking: if we had all singular perception-essences before our eyes, and compared them in self-posing with the given perception *A*, and were disposed to gain and fix all the essential insight grounded in these essences, then these essences would fall into two classes. Every perception-essence of the one class would found with *A*, the essence *A*, an identity-consciousness (this too taken as an essence), and every perception-essence of the other class a consciousness of non-identity. The first group would contain, if for example we were speaking of the perception of a house, the ideal total sum of "possible perceptions" of the same house.

German:

> Da drängt sich uns jetzt natürlich der Gedanke auf: Beziehung auf eine Gegenständlichkeit ist, phänomenologisch angesehen, gar nichts anderes als die im Wesen des objektivierenden Erlebnisses, hier der Wahrnehmung, gründende Schicklichkeit, Identitätsbewußtsein zu fundieren. Oder besser, sie ist die Wesenseigentümlichkeit der Wahrnehmung, die sie geschickt macht, Identitätsbewußtsein zu fundieren und eben damit Unterscheidungsbewußtsein auszuschließen, und wieder Unterscheidungsbewußtsein zu fundieren und damit Identitätsbewußtsein auszuschließen. Nämlich im Wesen der jeweiligen Wahrnehmung gründen, wie die jeweiligen Selbststellungen es ausweisen, ideale Möglichkeiten für Identitätsverknüpfungen mit so und so gearteten anderen Wahrnehmungen, wirklichen oder möglichen. Subjektiv gesprochen: Hätten wir alle singulären Wahrnehmungsessenzen vor Augen und verglichen wir sie in der Selbststellung mit der vorgegebenen Wahrnehmung *A* und wären wir disponiert, alle Wesenseinsicht zu gewinnen und zu fixieren, die in diesen Essenzen gründet, dann zerfielen diese Essenzen in zwei Klassen. Jede Wahrnehmungsessenz der einen Klasse fundierte mit *A*, dem Wesen *A*, ein Identitätsbewußtsein (dieses ebenfalls als Wesen genommen) und jede Wahrnehmungsessenz der anderen Klasse ein Bewußtsein von Nichtidentität. Die erste Gruppe enthielte, wenn z. B. die Rede wäre von der Wahrnehmung eines Hauses, den idealen Gesamtinbegriff ,,möglicher Wahrnehmungen'' von demselben Haus.

The noun has a history in the preceding pages. In §10 perceptions of an elephant and of a stone "do not fit themselves by their essence into an identification" (*schicken sich ihrem Wesen nach in eine Identifikation nicht*), and the identity of two perceptions' object "says nothing other than that by their essence they fit themselves into the unity of an identity-consciousness" (*sich in die Einheit eines Identitätsbewußtseins schicken*, Hua XVI 28). At the end of §10 Husserl puts the verb in quotation marks and draws the moral: "it says a great deal, this 'now fitting oneself into an identity-consciousness' and on the other hand 'fitting oneself into a non-identity-consciousness'" (Hua XVI 30). §11 turns the reflexive verb into a noun and then, in the self-correction "or better" (*oder besser*), into a causative: the essential peculiarity "that makes it fit" (*die sie geschickt macht*). The three forms say one thing with different subjects. In §10 the perceptions fit themselves; in §11 their essence makes them fit. The correction moves the ground of the relation from what perceptions do in a synthesis to what their essence allows, which is why the next sentence speaks of "ideal possibilities".

*Schicklichkeit* in ordinary German also means propriety, what is seemly. The everyday sense drops away here; what remains is the sense of the verb, to fit or suit. The commentary gives the German as *Geeignetheit* (§11.3), a word that occurs nowhere in the file. The substitution loses the evidence that the definition grows out of §10's verb (logged under Corrections in the key-passage list).

The definition is deflationary in its syntax. "Relation to an objectivity is … nothing other than" (*gar nichts anderes als*) a fittingness, and the phrase "phenomenologically regarded" restricts the identity to the reduced sphere. The relation has two sides, and both are founding and excluding at once: fittingness for identity excludes distinction, fittingness for distinction excludes identity. The commentary reads the result as a demystification (§11.13). The passage's opening sentence earns that word honestly: the evidences "contain no mystery at all" and need no "enigmatic reaching out of consciousness" beyond what is really immanent to it (Hua XVI 30). But the thought-experiment that follows shows the price. "Subjectively speaking", the relation to the house becomes a division of all singular perception-essences into two classes, and the object becomes "the ideal total sum of 'possible perceptions' of the same house". The conditionals pile up, "if we had … and compared … and were disposed", because no actual consciousness performs this classification. The relation is demystified by being made ideal, and the quotation marks on "possible perceptions" admit that the house is now defined by a sum no one surveys.

The next paragraph guards the definition against a confusion. The question is "not yet" about the reality or existence of what is presented, only about the "merely 'intentional relation'" (Hua XVI 31). Fittingness for identity holds of hallucinated and phantasized houses as much as of real ones. That keeps the definition inside §5's *Perzeption*. It also leaves for later what the course must still explain: how the classes of possible perceptions are ordered, so that one perception leads to the next and not merely belongs with it.

The word family returns where the course reformulates intentionality at higher levels, and its subject changes each time. In §41 the meaning (*Meinung*) of a perception implies its relation to the sides not presented, and "that means nothing other than that the meaning has an essential character that makes it fit for such identifications, in which the evidence lies" (*der sie zu solchen Identifikationen geschickt macht*, Hua XVI 144). There the fittingness belongs to the apprehension, not to the perception as a whole, and it explains co-meant sides. In §54 the apprehension has "the characterization that makes it fit to enter into other series of fulfilment and to found the consciousness 'if *K* were such and such, then the "appearance" would be such and such'" (Hua XVI 189). The same causative formula now carries kinaesthetic motivation. The ideal classes of §11 have become ordered series governed by circumstances, which is what §11 lacked.

Husserl's later notes judge the §11 definition insufficient on exactly this point. On p. 31 he wrote that either "we refer to a continuous synthesis, then we see the identity of the 'objective relation', or we keep to the identifying 'meaning'" (Beilage I, ad 31, 36 ff., Hua XVI 338). On p. 28, where §10 grounds identity in essence, he wrote: "But that is not enough. We must assume from the outset the synthesis of continuous unity-consciousness, to which alone evident identification is referred", and added, "I saw that later myself" (ad 28, 4–20, with a cross-reference to Hua XVI 154). Fittingness for discrete identifications is the first form of the thesis. The course's own development, from the kinetic synthesis of §30 to the motivated series of §54, replaces it with continuity.

## §13, Hua XVI 40–41: consciousness as bearer

Reads [§13.9–10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#rejection-of-a-misunderstanding-articulation-of-the-giving-consciousness-not-an-articulation-of-the-object).

Consciousness, at the end of Section I, is the primal ground and bearer (*Urgrund und Träger*) of every transcendent objectivity. It is itself an object in a wider sense, since it can be identified and judged about, but it is not constituted in further connections of consciousness. It is given simply, in self-posing. The thing, by contrast, "is what it is" only through connections of intention. The passage states this asymmetry as an image of bearing, and then refuses the reading that would make the bearer an ego.

> But in a wider sense "consciousness" too, everything that this title covers, perception, intuition in general, judgment and the like, is something objective and is subject to the same law that the sense of objectivity prescribes. But this objective something has a privilege that grounds a fundamental opposition between consciousness and object in the narrower sense, insofar as all transcendent objectivity has its primal ground and bearer in the objectivity in the wider sense that we call consciousness. The thing constitutes itself in consciousness; it is an intentionality that emerges according to essential laws in connections of consciousness of a determinate kind, or is essentially proper to them, which gives sense to it and to its "actual being". By its sense it is inseparable from such connections. Consciousness itself, however, is absolute being and for that very reason not thingly being. Simple self-posing brings it to givenness as absolute; it is what is given in pure seeing; it is something identifiable and therefore also an object, but it does not first constitute itself in connections of consciousness and in the sense that binds them synthetically; it is simple and is seen. The world is, as it were, borne by consciousness, but consciousness itself needs no bearer. Meaning can in turn be meant, but it is not, and does not first constitute itself, in connections of meaning, which in turn would be what they are only in further connections of meaning, and so *in infinitum*. The thing, however, is what it is only by virtue of the connections of intention of the kinds and forms still to be investigated.
>
> Is the thing, then, only the connection of my psychic acts, my presentations, perceptions, judgments, etc.? Whoever raises this question has of course missed all understanding. The phenomenological reduction is not the solipsistic reduction, and the ego is itself something thingly, constituting itself only in intentional connection and its essential forms and only thereby showing itself. What right there is to relate the formations of consciousness to an ego, to this or that person, has first to be grounded through objectivating thinking and its logic; and this right shows its sense in phenomenological analysis. But the thinking of which the analysis speaks is no one's thinking.

German:

> Aber im weiteren Sinn ist auch „Bewußtsein“, all das, was dieser Titel befaßt, Wahrnehmung, Anschauung überhaupt, Urteil u. dgl. ein Gegenständliches und untersteht demselben Gesetz, das der Sinn von Gegenständlichkeit vorschreibt. Aber dieses Gegenständliche hat einen Vorzug, der es begründet, in fundamentaler Weise Bewußtsein und Gegenstand im engeren Sinn gegenüberzustellen, sofern nämlich alle transzendente Gegenständlichkeit ihren Urgrund und Träger in der Gegenständlichkeit im weiteren Sinn hat, die wir Bewußtsein nennen. Das Ding konstituiert sich im Bewußtsein, es ist eine in Zusammenhängen des Bewußtseins von bestimmter Art sich wesensgesetzlich herausstellende oder ihnen wesentlich eigene Intentionalität, die ihm und seinem „wirklichen Sein“ Sinn gibt. Sinngemäß ist sie von solchen Zusammenhängen unabtrennbar. Bewußtsein selbst aber ist absolutes Sein und eben darum nicht dingliches Sein. Die schlichte Selbststellung bringt es als absolut zur Gegebenheit, es ist das im puren Schauen Gegebene; es ist ein Identifizierbares und darum auch Gegenstand, aber es konstituiert sich nicht erst in Zusammenhängen des Bewußtseins und dem sie synthetisch verbindenden Sinn, es ist einfach und wird geschaut. Die Welt wird gleichsam getragen vom Bewußtsein, aber das Bewußtsein selbst braucht keinen Träger. Das Meinen kann wieder gemeint werden, aber es ist und konstituiert sich nicht erst in Zusammenhängen des Meinens, und diese wieder ‹sind›, was sie sind, nur in weiteren Zusammenhängen des Meinens, und so *in infinitum*. Das Ding aber ist, was es ist, nur vermöge der Zusammenhänge der Intention der noch zu erforschenden Arten und Formen.
>
> Ist also das Ding nur der Zusammenhang meiner psychischen Akte, meiner Vorstellungen, Wahrnehmungen, Urteile etc.? Wer diese Frage einwirft, hat natürlich alles Verständnis verfehlt. Die phänomenologische Reduktion ist ja nicht die solipsistische Reduktion, und das Ich ist ja selbst ein Dingliches, nur im intentionalen Zusammenhang und seinen wesentlichen Formen sich Konstituierendes und nur dadurch sich Ausweisendes. Welches Recht es hat, die Bewußtseinsgestaltungen auf ein Ich zu beziehen, auf die oder jene Person, das ist durch objektivierendes Denken und durch seine Logik erst zu begründen; und dieses Recht weist seinen Sinn aus in der phänomenologischen Analyse. Das Denken aber, von dem sie spricht, ist niemandes Denken.

*Note on the text.* The angle brackets around ‹sind› [are] mark an editor's addition, kept as the file has it.

The passage starts by levelling before it ranks. Consciousness "too" is "something objective" (*ein Gegenständliches*) and "is subject to the same law that the sense of objectivity prescribes". Only then comes its "privilege" (*Vorzug*). The order matters for what "absolute" means. Consciousness is not exempt from objectivity; it is the one objectivity that does not need constituting. The two senses of "object", wider and narrower, carry the distinction, and the narrower sense is defined by dependence: transcendent objectivity "has its primal ground and bearer" in consciousness. The paired nouns are not synonyms. A ground (*Urgrund*) is what something follows from; a bearer (*Träger*) is what something rests on. The next sentence specifies the dependence as one of sense: intentionality "gives sense" to the thing and to its "actual being", set in quotation marks because the word is now to be understood through that intentionality.

The negations then define the absolute by what it does not need. Consciousness "does not first constitute itself" (*konstituiert sich nicht erst*) in connections of consciousness; it is "simple and is seen". The "first" (*erst*) recurs in the sentence about meaning, and there it shows the argument. If meaning were constituted only in connections of meaning, these would in turn be what they are only in further connections, "and so *in infinitum*". The absoluteness of consciousness is the stop to a regress. That is a stronger claim than the evidence of self-posing gives. Self-posing shows that the act is given whole; it does not show that no further synthesis underlies its being given, and Husserl's later note on p. 28, which demands "from the outset the synthesis of continuous unity-consciousness" beneath every evident identification (Beilage I, Hua XVI 338), points to such a synthesis.

The image of the bearer carries a hedge. "The world is, as it were, borne by consciousness" (*gleichsam getragen*). The "as it were" withholds the causal or metaphysical sense of bearing, and the second half, "but consciousness itself needs no bearer", turns the image against an infinite chain of substrates. The sentence reverses the old notion of substance: what needs no bearer is not the thing but the consciousness the thing depends on. Six years later *Ideas I* states the same reversal in scholastic words: immanent being is absolute being in the sense "that it in principle *nulla 're' indiget ad existendum*" [needs no "thing" in order to exist] (§49, Hua III/1 104, first edition 92). The 1907 image already holds the thesis; the 1913 formula adds the thought-experiment of the annihilation of the world, which in 1907 waits until §84.

The second paragraph meets the obvious objection with a rebuke, "whoever raises this question has of course missed all understanding". The reply rests on two claims. "The phenomenological reduction is not the solipsistic reduction", and "the ego is itself something thingly". The ego belongs on the side of the constituted, so the bearer of the world cannot be "my" acts. The final sentence is the sharpest in Section I: "the thinking of which the analysis speaks is no one's thinking" (*niemandes Denken*). The pronoun negates ownership, not existence. It makes consciousness anonymous in order to keep it absolute, and it defers to "objectivating thinking and its logic" the right to ascribe acts to persons. The commentary states both claims (§13.10). It does not mark the cost: the course has defined consciousness as what needs no bearer, and has then removed the subject that might have seemed to bear it.

The course does not return to the thesis in these words. Its constitutive work makes good the last sentence of the first paragraph, that the thing is what it is only through "connections of intention of the kinds and forms still to be investigated" (*noch zu erforschenden*): the participle is the programme of Sections II to VI. And the ego that §13 sets among the constituted comes back as the Body. In §47 the Body is "bearer of the ego" (Hua XVI 162), and in §83 its anomaly, that it cannot run away from me, sends constitution towards empathy with others (rows 21 and 32). The bearer-image passes from consciousness to the Body, and what §13 called "something thingly" turns out to be the one thing that cannot be constituted like the others.

## §14, Hua XVI 43: space as form of thinghood, not "form of intuition"

Reads [§14.4](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#contents-of-sensation-and-the-qualities-of-things).

Space, for the lectures, belongs to the side of the object. It is "the necessary form of thinghood", and sensations have spatial character only as material that a thing's spatiality will be constituted from, not because consciousness imposes a spatial form on them. The sentence that says so carries its argument in a correction of vocabulary: Kant's term "form of intuition" (*Anschauungsform*) is not a doctrine to be refined but "a fundamentally false expression".

> … a certain spatial modification. But space is the necessary form of thinghood and not the form of lived experiences, and more precisely of "sensuous" lived experiences. Form of intuition is a fundamentally false expression and implies, in Kant too, a fatal false opinion. That sensation and perception are not one and the same, that between the sensed total content and the perceived object, between single sensation-contents and the features "corresponding to them", there is a real distinction to be made, is evident from the outset.

German:

> … einer gewissen räumlichen Modifikation. Der Raum aber ist die notwendige Form der Dinglichkeit und nicht die Form der Erlebnisse, und näher der „sinnlichen“ Erlebnisse. Anschauungsform ist ein grundfalscher Ausdruck und impliziert auch bei Kant eine verhängnisvolle Irrmeinung. Daß Empfindung und Wahrnehmung nicht einerlei ist, daß zwischen empfundenem Gesamtinhalt und wahrgenommenem Gegenstand, zwischen einzelnen Empfindungsinhalten und den „ihnen entsprechenden“ Merkmalen wirklich zu scheiden ist, das ist von vornherein evident.

*Note on the text.* The opening of §14 (Hua XVI 42–43) is garbled in the file and sits inside a stray code fence. The clean text resumes in mid-sentence on the line that closes the fence, with "a certain spatial modification" (*einer gewissen räumlichen Modifikation*). What the sensations undergo that modification, and what the sentence that ends there claimed, cannot be read. The reading uses only what follows the break, and cannot say what the "But" (*aber*) answers.

The thesis is built as a pair of genitives: space is the form "of thinghood" (*der Dinglichkeit*) and not the form "of lived experiences". The second genitive is narrowed at once, "and more precisely of 'sensuous' lived experiences". The quotation marks on "sensuous" (*„sinnlichen“*) point to Kant's sensibility (*Sinnlichkeit*), the faculty whose form, in the Transcendental Aesthetic, space is said to be. Husserl distances the word because the lectures do not recognize a faculty of sensibility at all, only sensations as really immanent contents. The "necessary" (*notwendige*) is kept, and so is "form": Husserl does not deny that space is necessary and formal. He relocates the necessity, from the conditions under which a subject receives data to the essence of a thing.

The second sentence turns the relocation against a word. "Form of intuition" is "a fundamentally false expression" (*ein grundfalscher Ausdruck*), and it "implies, in Kant too, a fatal false opinion" (*impliziert auch bei Kant eine verhängnisvolle Irrmeinung*). The verb "implies" makes the error a consequence of the term: whoever speaks of a form of intuition is already committed to placing space in the act. The "too" (*auch*) is the sharpest word in the sentence. It treats Kant as one case among the users of the expression, not as its author whose system might justify it. *Irrmeinung* is stronger than "error": a false opinion, a conviction one holds. The commentary's "fatally erroneous position" keeps the force but loses the point that Husserl objects first to an expression and only through it to a position.

The last sentence shows why the thesis belongs at the head of §14. Sensation and perception "are not one and the same", and between sensed contents and the features "corresponding to them", again in quotation marks, "there is a real distinction to be made". If space were a form of sensations, the sensed extension of a colour and the thing's extension would coincide, and the correspondence would be identity. The distinction of sensation and perceived feature is the ground of the anti-Kantian sentence, and it is "evident from the outset". The next paragraph supplies the argument the word "evident" claims: if a sensation-complex and a thing were simply the same content twice, "we would have two things, an immanent and a transcendent one" (Hua XVI 44). The yellow sphere then shows that one objective colour corresponds to a continuous variety of sensed colours (row 9).

The lectures keep the thesis by building the form of thinghood from below. In §20 spatial form "by itself is nothing" (*Raumform für sich nichts ist*) and needs the materializing qualities as its *materia prima* (Hua XVI 67, row 13); space is a form of things only as filled. Sections IV and V then constitute it from the oculomotor field and kinaesthetic motivation, and §61 calls space "the ordered context of things" (*Ordnungszusammenhang der Dinge*, row 25). The anti-Kantian sentence is the promissory note those Sections redeem: they show how a form of things arises in a consciousness that has none.

Kant returns once more, and the return limits the quarrel. In §34 Husserl meets the difficulty "that space is infinite (as Kant says, it is represented as an infinite given magnitude. At any rate, we do not doubt the evidence of infinity.) But the field is finite" (Hua XVI 119). Kant's description of space is accepted where it states an evidence, and the infinity it attributes to space drives the argument for necessary inadequacy in §35 (row 17). What Husserl rejects is the location the word "form of intuition" gives that infinity. Space is infinite as the form of possible things, and the finitude belongs to the field of sensation, which is exactly the place Kant's expression would have put space.

## §14, Hua XVI 44–45: adumbration and the image-theory

Reads [§14.8–10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#contents-of-sensation-and-the-qualities-of-things).

Adumbration (*Abschattung*) names the relation of sensed content to objective feature: an unvarying quality of the thing is presented by a sensed content that varies continuously. The term enters the lectures here, with Locke's yellow sphere, and its first use carries the course's first negative thesis. Because one feature is presented by many different sensations, and even within one perception by a shaded sensation, the perception cannot contain an image of its object.

> If we have the perception of a uniformly yellow sphere (it is an example of Locke's), and indeed a perception unchanged during its duration, with an unchanged object, then we say on the one hand: the seen coloration, related of course to the "properly" appearing front side, is uniform.
>
> But if we attend to the immanent content of the perception, we find a continuous adumbration of the yellow, and it is clear that a connection of necessity holds here: only if such adumbration is sensed does a uniformly coloured sphere present itself. And again it must be pointed out that identity of objective determinations in the sphere of proper perception is not compatible with a change or a continuous alteration of sensation-contents, but is necessarily demanded with respect to many determinations. As the sphere approaches or recedes we have continually new and new perceptions. The moment of extension within the sensation demands a constant alteration, if the manifold perceptions are to gain unity in consciousness: it is the same sphere, unchanged in its extension and form.
>
> So identity of the objective feature in no way means identity of the corresponding sensation. The sensation offers no reduplication of the feature. The perception therefore does not contain in itself an image of the object, the word "image" taken in the common sense, as a second thing that represents an original through similarity. The perception contains neither a repetition of the whole thing nor one of single features. The image-theory, looked at from many sides, has its absurdities. Here we have the one side.

German:

> Haben wir die Wahrnehmung einer gleichmäßig gelben Kugel (es ist ein Beispiel Lockes), und zwar eine während ihrer Dauer unveränderte Wahrnehmung bei unverändertem Gegenstand, so sagen wir einerseits: Die gesehene Färbung, natürlich bezogen auf die „eigentlich“ erscheinende Vorderseite, ist gleichmäßig.
>
> Achten wir aber auf den immanenten Inhalt der Wahrnehmung, so finden wir eine stetige Abschattung des Gelb, und es ist klar, daß hierbei ein Notwendigkeitszusammenhang besteht: nur wenn solche Abschattung empfunden ist, stellt sich eine gleichmäßig gefärbte Kugel dar. Und wieder ist darauf hinzuweisen, daß Identität gegenständlicher Bestimmungen sich in der Sphäre eigentlicher Wahrnehmung nicht mit einem Wechsel oder einer kontinuierlichen Veränderung von Empfindungsinhalten verträgt, sondern hinsichtlich vieler Bestimmungen notwendig gefordert ist. Bei Annäherung oder Entfernung der Kugel haben wir stetig neue und neue Wahrnehmungen. Das Moment der Ausbreitung innerhalb der Empfindung verlangt eine ständige Änderung, wenn die mannigfaltigen Wahrnehmungen Einheit gewinnen sollen im Bewußtsein: es sei dieselbe Kugel, ihrer Ausdehnung und Form nach unverändert.
>
> Also Identität des objektiven Merkmals bedeutet in keiner Weise Identität der entsprechenden Empfindung. Die Empfindung bietet keine Reduplikation des Merkmals. Die Wahrnehmung enthält in sich also kein Bild des Gegenstandes, das Wort ,,Bild" im gemeinen Sinn genommen, als ein zweites Ding, das ein Original durch Ähnlichkeit repräsentiert. Die Wahrnehmung enthält weder eine Wiederholung des ganzen Dinges noch eine solche einzelner Merkmale. Die Bildertheorie hat, von vielen Seiten her angesehen, ihre Widersinnigkeiten. Hier haben wir die eine Seite.

*Note on the text.* The second paragraph's middle sentence, as the file has it, says that identity of objective determinations "is not compatible with" (*nicht … verträgt*) a change of sensation-contents, "but is necessarily demanded". The construction *nicht … sondern* [not … but] requires a contrast of two degrees, and the sense of the paragraph needs "not only compatible with … but demanded" (*nicht nur … sondern*). A word seems to have been lost. The rendering follows the file, and the reading relies only on the "necessarily demanded", which both versions share. The file also carries a conversion artifact after the paragraph (a footnote reading "Full text of footnote 1"), left out here.

The word arrives without a definition, as the name of something "we find" when we attend to the immanent content: "a continuous adumbration of the yellow" (*eine stetige Abschattung des Gelb*). It is the word's first occurrence in the file. *Schatten* is shadow, and *abschatten* is to shade off, to grade a colour by degrees. The genitive is precise: the adumbration is *of the yellow*, a shading of the colour itself across the sensed extent, not a shadow cast on the sphere. The term therefore starts as a description of sensation, and the objective uniformity on the other side is guarded by two signs of distance. The coloration is uniform "related of course to the 'properly' appearing front side", with "properly" in quotation marks: the concept of proper appearance, which §16 will define, is used before it is introduced.

The necessity is a biconditional in one direction. "Only if such adumbration is sensed does a uniformly coloured sphere present itself" (*nur wenn … stellt sich … dar*). Uniformity on the side of the object requires non-uniformity on the side of sensation. That is stronger than the image-theory's opponents usually claim. It is not that sensation may differ from the feature; it must, if this feature is to be presented. The second step extends the claim from a single perception to a series. As the sphere approaches or recedes, "the moment of extension within the sensation demands a constant alteration" if the perceptions are to be unified in the consciousness "it is the same sphere". The subjunctive *es sei* (it be) gives the content of that consciousness as a quoted judgment. Identity is what the alteration serves.

The conclusion falls into three negative sentences, each narrower than the last. Identity of the feature "in no way means" (*bedeutet in keiner Weise*) identity of sensation; the sensation offers "no reduplication" (*keine Reduplikation*); the perception contains no image "in the common sense" of a second thing similar to an original. "Reduplication" names what the image-theory needs, a second instance of the same. The paragraph then deflates its own result. "The image-theory, looked at from many sides, has its absurdities. Here we have the one side." The commentary renders this as "The image-theory is, looked at from any side, countersensical" (§14.10). The German claims less: many sides, not every side, and "its absurdities", a plural noun, not a predicate of the whole theory. "Here we have the one side" marks the argument as partial. Adumbration refutes the image-theory with respect to similarity; other sides, such as the regress of an inner image that would itself need perceiving, are left aside (logged under Corrections in the key-passage list).

The passage prepares §15. If sensation does not resemble the feature, something must make it present the feature, and §15 names that something apprehension, "the plus" beyond sensation (row 10). Adumbration is the datum that makes apprehension necessary: the shaded yellow is "taken as" a uniform yellow it is not.

The word then changes its subject across the course. Here it is a shading within one sensed content. In §21 Husserl speaks of the "adumbration-extension of the sensuous data" (*Abschattungsextension der sinnlichen Data*, Hua XVI 70), still on the side of sensation. In §30 it has become a series: to each moment of the object belongs "a continuity of constituting appearance-moments … for example the adumbration of the square", and "it is not one adumbration there" (Hua XVI 102). The thing is then "the identical in the systematic unity of these adumbrations or possibilities of adumbration" (row 15). Between §14 and §30 the term passes from the sensed colour of a static perception to the ordered manifold of appearances in motion. The yellow sphere already contains both, the shading within one perception and the "new and new perceptions" of approach, and the course develops the second out of the first.

## §15, Hua XVI 46: apprehension as "the plus"

Reads [§15.3–5](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#presentational-contents-and-apprehension-perception).

Apprehension (*Auffassung*) is what a perception has beyond its sensations: the character by which a sensed content is taken as presenting something it is not. §15 introduces it as a remainder. The mannequin shows that one sensational basis can bear two perceptions, so the difference between them must lie in something else, "the plus". The concept is defined by subtraction, and its first description is a metaphor.

> Further, we have already mentioned the evidence that the complexion of sensation-contents is very different and yet the corresponding perceptions present themselves by their essence as perceptions of the same object; conversely it also holds that the same complexion of sensation-contents can ground different perceptions, perceptions of different objects, as every doll proves, insofar as here, with the standpoint held fixed, two *Perzeptionen* stand in conflict, that of the doll-thing and that of the presented human being, both built on the same basis of sensation. This consideration directs our gaze to the plus that is to be found really in the perception over and above the sensation-complexion, and that, in the most intimate interpenetration with what is sensed, first makes up perception. The sensation-contents by themselves still contain nothing of the character of perception, nothing of its direction toward the one perceived object; they are not yet what makes a thingly objective something stand there in the flesh. We call this surplus the character of apprehension and say that the sensation-contents undergo apprehension. Through the apprehension they, which in themselves would be as it were a dead stuff, gain animating significance, such that with them an object comes to presentation. With respect to this we call the contents presenting contents, in contrast to the presented, namely the objective determinations.

German:

> Wir haben schon der Evidenz Erwähnung getan, daß die Komplexion der Empfindungsinhalte sehr verschieden *ist* und doch die zugehörigen Wahrnehmungen ihrem Wesen nach als Wahrnehmungen von demselben Gegenstand sich ausgeben; umgekehrt gilt auch, daß dieselbe Komplexion von Empfindungsinhalten verschiedene Wahrnehmungen begründen kann, Wahrnehmungen von verschiedenen Gegenständen, wie jede Puppe beweist, sofern hier bei festgehaltenem Standpunkt zwei Perzeptionen in Widerstreit stehen, die des Puppendinges und die des dargestellten Menschen, beide aufgebaut auf derselben Empfindungsgrundlage. Diese Erwägung lenkt unseren Blick auf das Plus, das über die Empfindungskomplexion hinaus in der Wahrnehmung reell zu finden ist und das in allerengster Durchdringung mit dem Empfundenen erst Wahrnehmung ausmacht. Die Empfindungsinhalte für sich enthalten noch nichts von dem Charakter der Wahrnehmung, nichts von ihrer Richtung auf den einen wahrgenommenen Gegenstand; sie sind noch nicht das, was es macht, daß ein Dinggegenständliches in Leibhaftigkeit dasteht. Wir nennen diesen Überschuß den Charakter der Auffassung und sagen, daß die Empfindungsinhalte Auffassung erfahren. Durch die Auffassung gewinnen sie, die an sich gleichsam ein toter Stoff wären, beseelende Bedeutung derart, daß mit ihnen ein Gegenstand zur Darstellung kommt. Mit Beziehung darauf nennen wir die Inhalte darstellende Inhalte im Gegensatz zu den dargestellten nämlich den gegenständlichen Bestimmtheiten.

The argument is a double variation, and the two halves are not equally strong. §14 gave the first: different sensations, one object. The doll gives the converse: one sensational basis, two objects. Only the converse forces the conclusion, because two perceptions that share every sensed content and still differ must differ in something that is not sensed. The phrase "with the standpoint held fixed" (*bei festgehaltenem Standpunkt*) secures this. Nothing in the visual data changes while perception flips between a doll and a person. "As every doll proves" (*wie jede Puppe beweist*) is a confident universal for an experience that occurs only with some dolls and some light; the argument needs only one case.

The new item is first named in arithmetic: "the plus" (*das Plus*), then "this surplus" (*diesen Überschuß*). Both nouns say only that there is more. The location is precise: the plus is "to be found really (*reell*) in the perception", so apprehension belongs to the act's real content and not to the object. And its relation to sensation is not addition. It "first makes up perception" (*erst Wahrnehmung ausmacht*) "in the most intimate interpenetration" with what is sensed. The arithmetic word says more; the qualifying phrase denies that the more is a separable part. §41 repeats the point: the unity of presenting content and apprehension is "not a sum or a twoness bound together", but "a most intimate unity, which we sought to characterize by the word animation" (Hua XVI 144).

The metaphor then supplies what subtraction cannot. In themselves the contents "would be as it were a dead stuff" (*gleichsam ein toter Stoff*), and through apprehension they gain "animating significance" (*beseelende Bedeutung*). The subjunctive "would be" and the hedge "as it were" mark that dead stuff never occurs: sensations are always met already animated, and their deadness is an abstraction. "Significance" (*Bedeutung*) links apprehension to meaning; the contents come to signify an object. The next paragraph gives the definition that the metaphor stands in for. In presenting perception a really immanent content functions "as presenting", as one "that is not simply taken but taken-as something it is not itself" (*nicht einfach gefaßt, sondern als etwas aufgefaßt wird, was er nicht selbst ist*, Hua XVI 46). The prefix *auf-* turns grasping into grasping-as. That is the whole difference between self-posing, which takes its content simply, and presenting, which takes it as another. The sentence after it is "At first this is a quite crude analysis" (*eine ganz rohe Analyse*).

The commentary calls this "the content-apprehension schema that will govern every subsequent constitutive analysis" (§15.16). Husserl's own word for it is "crude", and the course keeps qualifying the schema rather than applying it. In §41 the animation-metaphor is cut back to its phenomenal basis: "this animation says nothing other than the consciousness that is the changing element when the sensuous data are apprehended in different senses", as when "we waver conflictingly between two thing-apprehensions" (Hua XVI 146). The doll has become the definition: apprehension is what changes when sensation does not. And his later note on p. 160 splits the schema's central term: "presentation in the narrower sense (projection …) and presentation in the wider sense. Not all sensation-data are presenting in the first sense" (Beilage I, ad 160, 16, Hua XVI 340). The kinaesthetic sensations of §46 are just such data, functioning in apprehension without presenting (row 20). What governs the later analyses is the distinction of sensation and apprehension; the schema of an animated stuff is what they revise.

*Ideas I* keeps the metaphor and adds the distance §15 lacked. Over the sensuous moments, "which in themselves have nothing of intentionality", lies "a layer as it were 'animating', sense-giving" (*eine gleichsam „beseelende“, sinngebende … Schicht*), and whether all sensuous lived experiences "everywhere and necessarily carry some 'animating apprehension'" is "not to be decided here" (§85, Hua III/1 192). In 1913 the animation stands in quotation marks, and the universality §15 took for granted has become an open question.

## §16, Hua XVI 50, 52: proper and improper appearance, and the inadequacy Husserl retracted

Reads [§16.3–5](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-object-of-apprehension-as-appearance-appearance-in-the-proper-sense).

Proper appearance (*eigentliche Erscheinung*) is the part of a perception's total apprehension that has presenting contents; improper appearance is the rest, which makes the thing's other determinations conscious without presenting them. From this division §16 draws the thesis that every single outer perception is essentially inadequate. The passage states the thesis with care, restricts it in its last sentences, and was later marked by Husserl as doubtful. Its understanding is a thesis held under qualification.

> Accordingly, the total apprehension and total appearance of the perception divides into the proper appearance, whose correlate is the side of the object that is perceived in the proper sense, that actually comes to presentation, and into the improper appearance, the appendix of the proper appearance, which has its correlate in the rest of the object. It presents nothing, although it does in a certain way make its objective something conscious. If we attend to a moment of this "turned-away" side, we can no longer say of it, taken by itself, that we have it before our eyes, that we see it, that we perceive it. Only what is presented is seen, is "intuitively" given. But proper and improper appearance are nothing separate; they are one in the appearance in the wider sense.
>
> […]
>
> Here, then, there is an essential inadequation of every single outer perception. It is perception of spatial things and as such can only be one-sided. A three-dimensional intuition, we can also say, is impossible as a proper intuition that would bring to presentation at one stroke the full content of the thing according to all its constitutive parts and moments, outside and inside, front side and back side. There is only improper intuition of space. That holds for the moment of spatial shape as well as for the spatial filling founded in it. At any rate this holds with respect to the means of presentation and forms of presentation at our disposal phenomenologically, thus for the specific essences of physical contents and forms of apprehension that we find in phenomenological seeing and that delimit our idea of outer perception. That it lies in the essence of physical contents in general that, as the sensation-basis of presenting perceptions, they can necessarily found only one-sided thing-perceptions, one must not venture to assert.

German:

> Sonach teilt sich die Gesamtauffassung und Gesamterscheinung der Wahrnehmung in die eigentliche Erscheinung, deren Korrelat die im eigentlichen Sinn wahrgenommene, die wirklich zur Darstellung kommende Seite des Gegenstandes ist, und in die uneigentliche Erscheinung, den Appendix der eigentlichen Erscheinung, der sein Korrelat in dem Übrigen des Gegenstandes hat. Sie stellt nichts dar, obschon sie doch ihr Gegenständliches in gewisser Weise bewußt macht. Achten wir auf ein Moment dieser „abgewendeten“ Seite, so können wir von ihm, für sich genommen, nicht mehr sagen, wir hätten es vor Augen, wir schauten es, wir nähmen es wahr. Nur Dargestelltes ist geschaut, ist „intuitiv“ gegeben. Eigentliche und uneigentliche Erscheinung sind aber nichts Getrenntes, sondern einig in der Erscheinung im weiteren Sinn.
>
> […]
>
> Hier liegt also eine wesentliche Inadäquation jeder vereinzelten äußeren Wahrnehmung vor. Sie ist Wahrnehmung räumlicher Dinge und kann als solche nur einseitig sein. Eine dreidimensionale Anschauung, können wir auch sagen, ist als eigentliche Anschauung, die den vollen Gehalt des Dinges nach allen konstitutiven Teilen und Momenten, nach Außen und Innen, nach Vorderseite und Hinterseite in einem Schlage zur Darstellung brächte, unmöglich. Es gibt nur uneigentliche Raumanschauung. Das gilt für das Moment der Raumgestalt ebenso wie für die in ihr fundierte Raumfülle. Jedenfalls gilt dies mit Beziehung auf die Darstellungsmittel und Darstellungsformen, über die wir phänomenologisch verfügen, also für die spezifischen Wesen von physischen Inhalten und Auffassungsformen, die wir im phänomenologischen Schauen vorfinden und die unsere Idee von äußerer Wahrnehmung begrenzen. Daß es im Wesen physischer Inhalte überhaupt liegt, daß sie als Empfindungsunterlage darstellender Wahrnehmungen notwendig nur einseitige Dingwahrnehmungen zu fundieren vermögen, das darf man nicht wagen zu behaupten.

*Note on the text.* The first paragraph is on Hua XVI 50, the second on p. 52. The pages between (from p. 50 to the head of p. 52) are garbled in the file, with a bogus heading and invented words, and are not quoted. The second paragraph follows a clean stretch on p. 52 that contrasts side and piece. The editor's footnote marker after "Here" (*Hier liegt*) points to Husserl's critical note, discussed below; it is left out.

The definition rests on the verb "present" (*darstellen*), which §9 made the mark of transcendent perception and §15 tied to presenting contents. Proper appearance is "the side … that actually comes to presentation"; improper appearance "presents nothing, although it does in a certain way make its objective something conscious" (*Sie stellt nichts dar, obschon sie doch … bewußt macht*). The concessive "although" keeps two things apart that the course will need: presenting and making conscious. The noun "appendix" (*Appendix*) ranks the two at once. The improper is an attachment of the proper. But the last sentence of the paragraph withdraws the ranking: the two "are nothing separate", "one in the appearance in the wider sense". The improper appearance is dependent on the proper in being defined by it, and inseparable from it in being.

Quotation marks mark the words that §16 is redefining. The back is the "turned-away" side, and only what is presented is "intuitively" given. "Intuitive" is now restricted to the presented, so that the back of the house, which ordinary speech would call seen with the rest, is not intuited at all. This is the step that licenses the later thesis. If only presented determinations are intuited, and a three-dimensional thing has determinations that no single perception presents, then "there is only improper intuition of space" (*Es gibt nur uneigentliche Raumanschauung*). The restriction of "intuitive" carries the inference. Without it, the claim would only be that no perception presents every side, which no one disputes.

The thesis paragraph then qualifies itself in two steps that the commentary does not report. "At any rate" (*Jedenfalls*) restricts it to "the means of presentation and forms of presentation at our disposal phenomenologically", the physical contents and forms of apprehension "that delimit our idea of outer perception". And the last sentence refuses the stronger claim: that it lies "in the essence of physical contents in general" to found only one-sided perceptions, "one must not venture to assert" (*das darf man nicht wagen zu behaupten*). The "essential inadequation" is essential to the idea of outer perception that our presenting means define. It is not, in §16, a law about any possible sensuous basis. The commentary calls the one-sidedness something that "belongs to the very essence of thing-perception as such" (§16.5). §16 says less, and says that it says less (logged under Corrections in the key-passage list).

Husserl's later notes go further than the hedge. Against p. 51, 10 ff., where the doctrine begins in pages the file has lost, he wrote: "Not used. The following doctrine of the essential inadequacy of thing-perception, insofar as it could only be one-sided, is to be reconsidered on the basis of the later analysis of space, which probably speaks against it" (*Nicht benutzt. … ist neu zu erwägen aufgrund der späteren Raumanalyse, die wohl dagegen spricht*). Against p. 52, 20 ff., where the thesis paragraph stands: "Reconsider; doubtful!" (*Neu überlegen; zweifelhaft!*, Beilage I, Hua XVI 338). "Not used" seems to mean that the pages were not delivered. The objection is precise. It is not to inadequacy in general but to inadequacy "insofar as it could only be one-sided", and the ground is "the later analysis of space", the kinaesthetic constitution of Sections IV and V, in which a thing is given through motivated series and not as the sum of sides.

The course itself moves the thesis to new ground before Husserl's note does. §§32–35 rebuild necessary inadequacy without appeal to one-sidedness, from the finitude of the field against the infinity of space, and §33 states that the limit holds "neither for us nor for the dear Lord" (rows 16–17). That argument claims what §16 would "not venture to assert": a limit that does not depend on our presenting means. The two arguments do not stand or fall together. §16's one-sidedness is what the later note doubts. §35's economy of finite means is what the course keeps. Read with its hedge and its retraction, §16 is the first and weakest form of a thesis the course goes on to prove differently.

## §17, Hua XVI 55: no presentation, "not even a mediate one"

Reads [§17.5–6](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#essential-appurtenance-of-determinate-genuses-of-sensible-data-to-objective-determinations).

The improperly appearing determinations of a thing, its back and interior, are co-apprehended but not presented in any way. §17 denies them every kind of presentation, including a mediate one, and it does so by retracting Husserl's own earlier formula, which had them represented by the sensations "indirectly", "by contiguity", "symbolically". What links the back of the house to its front is not a relation between sensations and the back. It is a relation among whole appearances in the synthesis of perception.

> As regards, on the other hand, the improper appearance that makes the total appearance of the perception full, it contains nothing of presentation. The improperly appearing objective determinations are co-apprehended, but not "sensualized", not presented through something sensuous, that is, through sensation-material. That they are co-apprehended is evident, for otherwise we would have no objects at all before our eyes, not even a side, since this can be a side only through the object.
>
> In earlier lectures I used to express myself thus: what appears improperly is represented by the given sensations not directly but indirectly, not through similarity but through contiguity, not intuitively but symbolically. This way of speaking has some ground in the synthesis of the perceptual connection, in which the essence of perceptual apprehension in a certain way further discloses or unfolds itself. But I now hold this mode of expression to be more than questionable, insofar as nothing of presentation, not even of a presentation to be called mediate, attaches to the sensation-contents, even when we look at the synthetic connection. The pointings ahead and pointings back that, in the course of a manifold of perceptions belonging together, help constitute objective givenness concern not merely the sensations but the whole appearances in unitary consciousness.

German:

> Was andererseits die uneigentliche Erscheinung anlangt, die die Gesamterscheinung der Wahrnehmung voll macht, so enthält sie nichts von Darstellung. Die uneigentlich erscheinenden gegenständlichen Bestimmtheiten sind mit aufgefaßt, aber nicht ,,versinnlicht'', nicht durch Sinnliches, d.i. Empfindungsmaterial dargestellt. Daß sie mit aufgefaßt sind, ist evident, denn sonst hätten wir gar keine Gegenstände vor Augen, nicht einmal eine Seite, da diese ja nur durch den Gegenstand Seite sein kann.
>
> In früheren Vorlesungen pflegte ich mich so auszudrücken: Das uneigentlich Erscheinende wird durch die gegebenen Empfindungen nicht direkt, sondern indirekt, nicht durch Ähnlichkeit, sondern durch Kontiguität, nicht intuitiv, sondern symbolisch repräsentiert. Diese Redeweise hat einigen Grund in der Synthesis des Wahrnehmungszusammenhanges, in dem sich das Wesen der Wahrnehmungsauffassung in gewisser Weise weiter enthüllt oder entfaltet. Doch halte ich diese Ausdrucksweise jetzt für mehr als bedenklich, sofern an den Empfindungsinhalten, selbst wenn wir auf den synthetischen Zusammenhang hinblicken, nichts von Darstellung, auch nicht von einer als mittelbar anzusprechenden Darstellung hängt. Die Hinweise und Rückweise, die im Ablauf einer Mannigfaltigkeit zusammengehöriger Wahrnehmungen die gegenständliche Gegebenheit konstituieren helfen, gehen nicht bloß die Empfindungen, sondern die ganzen Erscheinungen im einheitlichen Bewußtsein an.

The first paragraph sets the terms. The improper determinations are "co-apprehended" (*mit aufgefaßt*) and not "sensualized" (*„versinnlicht“*). The prefix *mit-* (with, co-) puts them inside the apprehension; the quotation marks on "sensualized" put a word at a distance that would mean "made sensuous", given a sensuous embodiment. Husserl glosses it at once, "not presented through something sensuous, that is, through sensation-material", as if the word were too figurative to stand alone. The evidence that they are co-apprehended is an argument by absence: without them "we would have no objects at all before our eyes, not even a side". The side depends on the object, as §16 had argued, so the dependence of the proper appearance is the proof that the improper is there.

The retracted formula is built of three antitheses in a row, "not directly but indirectly, not through similarity but through contiguity, not intuitively but symbolically". The rhythm makes them look like three descriptions of one relation. They are not. The first is about mediacy, the second about the associative ground (similarity against contiguity, the two classical laws of association), the third about the kind of intention (intuitive against symbolic). The formula fuses an epistemic, an associative and a semantic distinction, and the "represented" (*repräsentiert*) that governs all three gives the sensations a function in each. Husserl grants the formula "some ground" (*einigen Grund*) in the synthesis of perception, where the apprehension "discloses or unfolds itself", and then withdraws it as "more than questionable" (*mehr als bedenklich*). The comparative is a hedge stronger than "questionable" and weaker than "false".

The ground of the withdrawal lies in the scope of a negation. "Nothing of presentation, not even of a presentation to be called mediate, attaches to the sensation-contents" (*nichts von Darstellung, auch nicht von einer als mittelbar anzusprechenden Darstellung*). The phrase "to be called mediate" concedes that one might call it so, and denies that the name fits. The words "even when we look at the synthetic connection" block the obvious defence: in a series of perceptions the front leads to the back, so perhaps the front's sensations present the back at one remove. Husserl answers that the pointings ahead and back (*Hinweise und Rückweise*) "concern not merely the sensations but the whole appearances". The subject of the relation changes from data to appearances, and that change is the point of the passage.

The earlier formula is the *Logical Investigations*'. In the Sixth Investigation the intuitive content of a thing-perception gives the presenting content "its direct relation to corresponding objective moments, and only through contiguity are the new, in so far mediate, intentions of signitive kind attached to it" (*erst durch Kontiguität knüpfen sich an ihn die neuen, insofern also mittelbaren Intentionen signitiver Art*, LU VI §23, Hua XIX/2 611). The words "contiguity" and "mediate" are both there, and §17 denies them both. Hua XIX/2 also prints what Husserl did to that sentence in his own copy: "contiguity" is struck, and the margin offers "and only in the manner of interpenetration and interweaving" (*und erst in der Weise der Durchdringung und Verflechtung*, Hua XIX/2, notes to p. 611). The commentary's claim that §17 pulls away from the Sixth Investigation's frame (§17.6) is confirmed, and more precisely than it says. Husserl corrected the word "contiguity" in the book as well as in the lecture. What he kept in 1901 was the signitive component; what he dropped in 1907, and later struck, was the associative mechanism that tied it to the sensations.

The passage leaves the improper appearance without a positive account, and the course supplies one twice. In §30 the pointings return as anticipatory intentions: "each phase points to the next", and the series is "pervaded by a certain teleology" (row 15). Husserl's later note on that page judges it "unsatisfactory" and asks that the "intentions" taken as pointers (*Hinweise*) be set aside in favour of the structure of the unity of apprehensions (Beilage I, ad 102, 36 ff., Hua XVI 339). The word that §17 used to replace sensuous representation was itself replaced. In §54 the relation becomes motivation: the apprehension is "fit" to enter other series of fulfilment and to found the consciousness "if *K* were such and such, then the 'appearance' would be such and such" (Hua XVI 189). The co-apprehended back is then neither represented by a sensation nor pointed to by an appearance. It is what would appear under other kinaesthetic circumstances, and §17's "whole appearances in unitary consciousness" are redeemed as ordered possibilities.

## §20, Hua XVI 67: *materia prima* and the "worn-out terminology"

Reads [§20.3–6](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#spatial-extension-of-the-appearance-materia-prima-and-materia-secunda).

Materializing determinations, colour and the tactile qualities, fill a thing's spatial form in the first and proper sense and so make up its *materia prima*: with them a full thingly object is already constituted. Appended determinations, sound, smell, weight, presuppose that object and attach to it as a *materia secunda*. The distinction is one of constitutive order, and Husserl chooses Latin for it because the English-born pair of primary and secondary qualities is, in his phrase, "historically worn out".

> Bodily extension is materialized in the primary and proper sense by certain determinations whose complex makes up the *materia prima*. With that a full thingly object is already constituted; we already have a space-filled unity. To it, however, further determinations now come, which attach to the object and in a certain way make up a *materia secunda*.
>
> Here there would be a genuine and very significant sense for a distinction between primary and secondary qualities. But we cannot use this historically worn-out terminology. We must, however, distinguish, and will say, say, materializing and merely attaching determinations. Materializing determinations fill out spatial form as its *materia prima* and so create, since spatial form by itself is nothing and can be nothing, the thing-concretum in the fundamental sense. Once this is constituted, it can take on attaching determinations such as sound and noise, smell, or also weight and other empirical properties that do not lead back to peculiar primitive sense-contents.

German:

> Die körperliche Ausdehnung wird im primären und eigentlichen Sinn materialisiert durch gewisse Bestimmtheiten, deren Komplex die *materia prima* ausmacht. Damit konstituiert sich schon ein volles dingliches Objekt; wir haben schon raumerfüllte Einheit. Dazu treten nun aber weitere Bestimmtheiten, die dem Objekt anhängen und gewissermaßen eine *materia secunda* ausmachen.
>
> Hier wäre ein echter und sehr bedeutsamer Sinn für eine Unterscheidung zwischen primären und sekundären Qualitäten. Doch können wir diese historisch vergriffene Terminologie nicht brauchen. Wir müssen aber unterscheiden und werden etwa sagen: materialisierende und bloß anhängende Bestimmtheiten. Materialisierende Bestimmtheiten füllen die Raumform als ihre *materia prima* aus und schaffen so, da Raumform für sich nichts ist und nichts sein kann, das Dingkonkretum im fundamentalen Sinn. Ist dieses schon konstituiert, dann kann es anhängende Bestimmtheiten annehmen wie Klang und Geräusch, Geruch oder auch Gewicht und sonstige empirische Eigenschaften, die nicht auf eigenartige primitive Sinnesinhalte zurückführen.

*Note on the text.* The file has an editor's footnote marker after "Here" (*Hier*), which records that a new lecture probably began at this point, dated "31.V.07" in the margin. It is left out of the quotation.

The criterion for the division is a phrase that §20 has been preparing: "in the primary and proper sense" (*im primären und eigentlichen Sinn*). The violin's tone is referred to the violin but does not fill its space "in the primary and proper sense"; "an object must already be phenomenally there for a tone to be related to it", and "in that a certain mediacy is expressed" (Hua XVI 66–67). The word "primary" thus enters as an adverb of constitution, and the next paragraph explains why the adjectival pair cannot be used. In the tradition, primary qualities are those that belong to the thing as it is in itself, secondary ones those that exist only in a perceiver. Husserl's distinction cuts across that one. Colour, a secondary quality for Locke, is materializing; weight, close to the primary for the physicist, is merely attaching. Only the order of founding survives from the old terms.

The judgment on the terminology is a single adjective, *vergriffen*, which I render "worn out". It is a bookseller's word: a *vergriffenes* book is out of print, sold out. It also carries *sich vergreifen*, to take hold of the wrong thing. The terminology is used up by history and was mishandled in its use. The adjective claims nothing about Locke's arguments, and the commentary's gloss, that the tradition "thoroughly confused the relevant issues" (§20.6), says more than the German. Husserl grants the old pair "a genuine and very significant sense", in the subjunctive "here there would be" (*Hier wäre*): the sense is available, but the words are not.

The replacement is offered tentatively, "we will say, say" (*werden etwa sagen*), and it is a pair of German participles, "materializing" and "merely attaching" (*materialisierende und bloß anhängende*). The Latin stays for the strata the determinations make up, and it is hedged too: the attaching determinations "in a certain way" (*gewissermaßen*) make up a *materia secunda*. Then comes the claim that makes the distinction more than a classification. Materializing determinations "create the thing-concretum in the fundamental sense", "since spatial form by itself is nothing and can be nothing" (*da Raumform für sich nichts ist und nichts sein kann*). The causal "since" carries the anti-Kantian thesis of §14. Space is the form of thinghood, but a form that is nothing by itself; it exists only as filled. The *materia prima* is therefore not one property among others. It is what gives the form any being.

Husserl qualified the distinction in the same course and later rejected its central word. In §23, two lectures on, he finds that one "must be still somewhat more exact": the essential mark of the *materia prima* may lie "not in filling at all, but in constituting filling" (*nicht überhaupt in der Füllung, sondern in der konstituierenden Füllung*), and he moves temperature, which §20 had listed among the space-covering qualities, to the attaching side, since he named it with the materializing ones "not with a good conscience (if I named them at all)" (Hua XVI 79). The test is now founding, not covering: "the body must first be there for it to be able to appear warm or cold". His later note on p. 70 strikes the participle itself: "The expression materialization is unusable, since, looked at precisely, we are here constructing only the thing in its lowest stratum (phantom)" (*Der Ausdruck Materialisierung ist unbrauchbar …*, Beilage I, ad 70, 2 ff., Hua XVI 339). The note does not touch the order of founding. It denies that what §20 constitutes is matter in any sense: it is the phantom, the filled spatial body before causality and substance.

The correction was anticipated in §23's own wording, which already calls the materially filled body "the body of the thing (phantom)" (*Körper des Dinges (Phantom)*, Hua XVI 78). The distinction itself does its work there: it gives "the pregnant sense of the appearing side of the object", since the side is a filled surface of the *materia prima*. And the note on p. 69 extends it below perception: only certain sensuous data have a pre-empirical spread, "only certain physical data are thus enabled by their immanent essence to constitute thinghood in the primary sense", while data like tone "are by their essence apprehensible only in the form of attaching determinations" (Beilage I, ad 69, 37, Hua XVI 339). The pair that §20 introduced as a terminological substitute became, in Husserl's revision, a distinction among sensations as well as among determinations, and its Latin name outlived the German participle he had chosen to replace "primary".

## §29, Hua XVI 97: being-otherwise presupposes a ground of agreement

Reads [§29.10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#more-precise-determination-and-re-determination).

Re-determination, in which a later perception corrects an earlier one, can replace a determination only within an identity that persists. A thing can turn out to be otherwise than it was taken to be, but not otherwise in every respect, because conflict itself needs something on which the conflicting perceptions agree. §29 states this as an equation of two absurdities: "absolute being-otherwise in every respect is just as countersensical as absolute indeterminacy".

> If, when the back of our red sphere becomes visible, I find a spot not presupposed in seeing the front, and the red sphere was in the first perceptual step apprehended as uniformly red, then this at least agrees: that it is a sphere covered with a unitary coloration, red in general, "except for" the spot. The spot fits itself all the same into the unity of a total coloration that necessarily belongs to the apprehension; at this place it is another colour, but still a colour. If a conflicting deformation shows itself as well, it still agrees that it is a unitary body, into whose unitary form the deformation fits as a partial shape fits a total shape. We see here that absolute being-otherwise in every respect is just as countersensical as absolute indeterminacy. Conflicting positing behaves on the whole like more precise determination. Under all circumstances the new perception must fit the old, agree with it, namely according to the general.

German:

> Finde ich beim Sichtlichwerden der Rückseite unserer roten Kugel einen im Sehen der Vorderseite nicht mit vorausgesetzten Fleck <und> war die rote Kugel im ersten Wahrnehmungsschritt als gleichmäßig rot aufgefaßt, so stimmt doch dies, daß es eine Kugel ist, überdeckt mit einer einheitlichen Färbung, im allgemeinen rot, „bis auf“ den Fleck. Dieser fügt sich immerhin der Einheit einer Gesamtfärbung, die zur Auffassung notwendig gehört, ein; es ist an dieser Stelle eine andere Farbe, aber doch eine Farbe. Zeigt sich auch eine widerstreitende Deformation, so stimmt doch, daß es ein einheitlicher Körper ist, in dessen einheitlicher Form sich die Deformation einpaßt wie eben eine Partialgestalt einer Gesamtgestalt. Wir sehen da, daß absolutes Anderssein in jeder Hinsicht ebenso widersinnig ist wie absolute Unbestimmtheit. Es verhält sich überhaupt mit dem Ansatz durch Widerstreit ähnlich wie mit der näheren Bestimmung. Unter allen Umständen muß die neue Wahrnehmung zu der alten passen, zu ihr stimmen, nämlich dem Allgemeinen nach.

*Note on the text.* The angle brackets around ‹und› [and] mark an editor's addition, kept as the file has it.

The paragraph before states the rule in general form: "it belongs to the general essence of being-otherwise, of conflict, that it presupposes a ground of agreement" (*einen Boden der Übereinstimmung voraussetzt*, Hua XVI 97). The example then shows the ground at two levels. The spot is "another colour, but still a colour"; the deformation fits the body's form "as a partial shape fits a total shape". In each case the conflict replaces a species and keeps the genus. The quotation marks on "except for" (*„bis auf“*) mark the idiom that carries the structure. To say "red except for the spot" is already to say that the exception sits inside a whole that remains red "in general" (*im allgemeinen*). The connective "still" or "all the same" (*doch*, *immerhin*) recurs three times. It is the grammar of a conflict that does not reach the bottom.

The equation borrows its second term from §18, where Husserl had said that indeterminacy "is never absolute, complete indeterminacy; complete indeterminacy is a *nonsens*", but always a bounded one (Hua XVI 59). §29 pairs that thesis with its mirror. An apprehension cannot leave everything open, and a correction cannot replace everything. Both limits follow from one fact: every apprehension is apprehension of a thing, and "the idea of thinghood" prescribes the "general frame" within which sides can vary (Hua XVI 98). "Countersensical" (*widersinnig*) is the right word for this. The claim is not that total conflict never happens but that it cannot be thought as conflict: a perception that disagreed with the old in every respect would no longer be a perception of the same thing, and so would not contradict it at all.

The final sentence reduces the claim to a requirement of fit: "under all circumstances the new perception must fit the old, agree with it, namely according to the general" (*nämlich dem Allgemeinen nach*). The "namely" restricts the "must". Agreement is required only at the level of the genus, and within that frame Husserl lists the possibilities that remain: total agreement, super- and subordination of sense, determinate being-otherwise, further differentiation that may still leave the new moment "undetermined". The commentary renders the principle with "in any respect" (§29.10). The German says "in every respect" (*in jeder Hinsicht*), and the difference matters: it is the totality of the being-otherwise that is absurd, not being-otherwise in some given respect.

The principle has a weak point that the passage does not mark. The examples show that a spot and a deformation leave colour and body intact. They do not show that every conflict must leave something intact; the general "we see here" (*Wir sehen da*) draws the universal from two cases. The ground offered is essential, the idea of thinghood. But that ground decides only that a thing must have some colour and some shape. It does not decide that a perception which loses the thing altogether, a hallucination unmasked, is impossible. That case is not being-otherwise but not-being, and §29 does not discuss it.

§84 does. There the course's last lecture sorts disappointment into three modes. Re-determination ("the thing is, but not as it was apprehended") and change ("from moment to moment another, but always the same") both keep the thing's unity, the first by substitution within a lawful manifold that "remains on the whole the same", the second because the being-otherwise "from moment to moment" is "lawfully determined" (Hua XVI 286). "Quite otherwise than with being-otherwise and having-changed is it with not-being" (*Ganz anders … steht es mit dem Nichtsein*, Hua XVI 287). Not-being is the case of appearances that do not fit any unitary connection, like the landscape that appears in a picture on a wall that is not broken through. And §84 carries §29's word to the limit: if not-being "has its measure in being", "one would like to say" that "that absolutely nothing should be … is a countersense" (*ist ein Widersinn*, Hua XVI 288). The subjunctive "would like to say" (*möchte man sagen*) is the hedge §29 did not use, and the lecture goes on to doubt the extension (row 33). §29's principle holds within the experience of a thing. Whether it holds for the world as a whole is the question on which the course ends.

## §30, Hua XVI 102–104: the thing as "the identical" in an ordered continuity

Reads [§30.7–11](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#continuous-synthesis-of-manifold-perceptions).

In the kinetic synthesis the object is "the identical" (*das Identische*) of an ordered continuity of adumbrations. It is not reached by identifying one perception with another, as in Section I. It is constituted in a homogeneous flow whose order is real order, and it is fully given only in the "all-embracing unity" of every series in which it could be adumbrated. §30 states this in three places: the order of adumbrations, the expectant intentions that run through them, and the closing definition. Husserl later judged the middle one unsatisfactory.

> The order of the adumbrations is order in a continuity, therefore actual order, no arbitrarily interchangeable togetherness of mere collection. And it is order in a fused unity.
>
> […]
>
> If in a perception the series of appearances runs off in its continuous unity, then already the first onset of the change, so to speak the differential of movement, determines the "direction" of the course, and with that a system of continuously setting-in and self-fulfilling intentions is given. In normal perception they are intentions of expectation. (The series of appearances is pervaded by a certain teleology.) Each phase points to the following one. […] And each points forward: we feel ourselves drawn along in the stream of appearances, of objective adumbrations, from adumbration to adumbration; each points forward objectively in the continuity, and in pointing forward it is a presentiment of what is now coming, and the presentiment, the intimation, the intention is fulfilled. The one-sided view widens into the "all-sided" one.
>
> […]
>
> All these continuous series stand to one another in a lawful essential relation; they are themselves continuously mediated with one another, and only in the all-embracing unity of these series does the square actually come to "all-sided" complete givenness. It is what it is only as the identical in the systematic unity of these adumbrations or possibilities of adumbration.

German:

> Die Ordnung der Abschattungen ist Ordnung in einer Kontinuität, also wirkliche Ordnung, kein beliebig vertauschbares Zusammen bloßer Kollektion. Und es ist Ordnung in einer verschmolzenen Einheit.
>
> […]
>
> Läuft in einer Wahrnehmung die Erscheinungsreihe in ihrer stetigen Einheit ab, so bestimmt schon der erste Ansatz der Veränderung, sozusagen das Bewegungsdifferential, die „Richtung“ des Ablaufes, und damit ist gegeben ein System von stetig einsetzenden und sich erfüllenden Intentionen. Sie sind in der normalen Wahrnehmung Erwartungsintentionen. (Die Erscheinungsreihe ist durchherrscht von einer gewissen Teleologie.) Jede Phase weist auf die folgende hin. […] Und jede weist vorwärts: Wir fühlen uns im Strom der Erscheinungen, der gegenständlichen Abschattungen fortgezogen von Abschattung zu Abschattung, jede weist gegenständlich in der Kontinuität vorwärts, und im Vorweisen ist sie Ahnung von dem, was da nun kommt, und die Ahnung, die Andeutung, die Intention wird erfüllt. Der einseitige Anblick erweitert sich zum „allseitigen“.
>
> […]
>
> All diese kontinuierlichen Reihen stehen zueinander in gesetzmäßiger Wesensbeziehung, sie sind selbst miteinander kontinuierlich vermittelt, und erst in der allumfassenden Einheit dieser Reihen kommt das Quadrat wirklich „allseitig“ vollständig zur Gegebenheit. Es ist, was es ist, nur als das Identische in der systematischen Einheit dieser Abschattungen oder Abschattungsmöglichkeiten.

*Note on the text.* The three extracts are on Hua XVI 102, 103 and 104. The editor's footnote marker before "If" (*Läuft*) refers to Husserl's later note on p. 102, 36 ff., discussed below; it is left out.

The first extract sets the concept of order against a mere aggregate. The order of adumbrations is "actual order" (*wirkliche Ordnung*) because it is "order in a continuity", and it is "no arbitrarily interchangeable togetherness of mere collection". The "therefore" (*also*) makes continuity the ground of order: a continuum fixes a sequence, a collection does not. The same page then denies that the continuity is a chain of identifications. One "could speak of continuous identifying coincidence", "however, actual identification does not take place, but only a certain unity of homogeneous flow" that "includes in itself the possibility" of singling out phases and identifying them (Hua XVI 102). Section I had made identity-consciousness the form of the relation to an object (row 6). §30 places a unity beneath it that is not yet identification, of which identification is a possibility. That is the move Husserl's later note on §10 demands, "the synthesis of continuous unity-consciousness, to which alone evident identification is referred" (Beilage I, ad 28, Hua XVI 338).

The second extract describes how the continuity runs, and its vocabulary is the most figurative of the section. The first "differential of movement" fixes the "direction", in quotation marks; the intentions are "intentions of expectation" (*Erwartungsintentionen*) "in normal perception"; the series is "pervaded by a certain teleology", in a parenthesis. Then the language of feeling: "we feel ourselves drawn along", each adumbration is "a presentiment" (*Ahnung*) of what is coming, and "the presentiment, the intimation, the intention" is fulfilled. The three nouns slide from feeling to sign to act. The commentary calls the concept "the anticipatory intention (*vorgreifende Intention*)" (§30.7). That phrase does not occur in the file (whole-file grep). Husserl's word is "intentions of expectation", restricted to normal perception, and his figure for the forward pointing is presentiment, not anticipation (logged under Corrections in the key-passage list). The restriction matters. Expectation is one mode the intentions take, not their definition.

Husserl's later note falls exactly here. "The following unsatisfactory. One should set aside the 'intentions' = pointings (*Hinweise*) and first study the structure of the unity of apprehensions, and there only the occurrences of more precise determination, enrichment and illustration come into consideration" (Beilage I, ad 102, 36 ff., Hua XVI 339). The note equates "intentions" with "pointings", puts the first in quotation marks, and asks that both be set aside. It does not touch the first or third extract. What Husserl rejected was the description of the unity through a teleology of pointing; what he kept was the order of the continuity and the identical it constitutes. The commentary rates this block as the section's "most significant concept" (§30.7); the author's own judgment is that it is the weakest part.

The third extract widens the identity in two steps. A single closed series, the square from its appearance to its disappearance, is "no continuity that constitutes absolutely complete givenness". Only "the all-embracing unity" (*allumfassende Einheit*) of every possible series, rotations, approaches, displacements, gives the square "all-sidedly", and "all-sided" is in quotation marks, as it was in the second extract. The definition then adds a disjunction that changes its scope: the square is the identical in the systematic unity "of these adumbrations or possibilities of adumbration" (*oder Abschattungsmöglichkeiten*). The "or" moves from actual to possible series. The thing is defined through a system no perception runs through, and the quotation marks on "all-sided" concede that complete givenness is an idea. The commentary's "identical pole", corrected on 4 November, has gone; the German's neuter adjectival noun says only that something remains the same, and leaves open what kind of something it is.

The formula becomes the course's standing definition, and its subject is specified as the analysis descends. In §45 the thing is "the identical in the stereometric changes" (Hua XVI 157). In §54 it "is not merely the identical of this line", the actual series, but "the identical of this and at the same time of all possible lines, all possible image-continuities under all possible" kinaesthetic circumstances (Hua XVI 189, row 24). In §57 the oculomotor "side" is "exclusively the identical that constitutes itself in the possible total manifold of images" belonging to the variations of the eyes' circumstances (Hua XVI 203). In §67 the "object" would be "the identical in this system of displacements and rotations" (Hua XVI 237). The "possibilities of adumbration" of §30 are given their order by kinaesthesis: the "lawful essential relation" among the series that §30 asserts, Sections IV and V construct. The identical stays; the teleology that §30 used to connect the series is replaced by motivation.

## §33, Hua XVI 116–117: "neither for us nor for the dear Lord"

Reads [§33.8–10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-role-of-improper-appearance-in-the-process-of-more-precise-determination-the-ideal-of-adequate-perception).

Adequate perception of a thing would be a perception with no improper component, no unpresented back and nothing undetermined. §33 tests whether such a perception is even thinkable and answers that it is not, because what it would give is a filled field, and a field is not a space. The claim concerns the sense of thing and space, not the powers of a perceiver. The passage makes that point by giving the ideal to an infinite intellect and saying that it fails for "the dear Lord" too.

> What kind of givenness would this be? It would contain nothing of improper givenness, thus no back side, no inside would remain without presentation; it would contain nothing of indeterminacy, it would be throughout proper and fully determining appearance. Would we still have a difference between appearance and what appears, and the transcendence posited with it? The appearance would indeed be no mere adumbration; it would contain nothing of unfulfilled moments of apprehension pointing, as it were, beyond themselves. That seems at first quite thinkable, namely in the manner of really immanent perception, such as we have when we attend to the spread of the visual contents in the visual field and objectivate it as it is just given. An infinitely perfect intellect, one would like to fancy, has pure intuition of the thing and of the whole world. It has in its field of view such and such shapes, qualitatively filled in such and such a way, and those are the things. Our visual field, admittedly, is two-dimensional. Well, the infinite intellect has a three-dimensional field of view. That is "objective space"; this can be bounded in various ways, that is, undergo various divisions through separating spreads of quality, and the division belonging to each moment of time. The manifold of filled shapes: that is the "world" belonging to the moment of time in question. However, the matter has its great misgivings. A field of view is no space, and the excerpt of such a field no thing, neither for us nor for the dear Lord. How do things stand, then, with movement?

German:

> Welcher Art wäre diese Gegebenheit? Sie enthielte nichts von uneigentlicher Gegebenheit, also keine Rückseite, kein Innen bliebe ohne Darstellung; sie enthielte nichts von Unbestimmtheit, sie wäre durchaus eigentliche und voll bestimmende Erscheinung. Hätten wir noch einen Unterschied zwischen Erscheinung und Erscheinendem und die damit angesetzte Transzendenz? Die Erscheinung wäre ja keine bloße Abschattung, sie enthielte nichts von unerfüllten, über sich gleichsam hinausweisenden Auffassungsmomenten. Das scheint zunächst ganz wohl denkbar, nämlich in der Weise der reell immanenten Wahrnehmung, wie wir sie da haben, wenn wir auf die Ausbreitung der visuellen Inhalte im visuellen Feld achten und sie, wie sie gerade gegeben ist, objektivieren. Ein unendlich vollkommener Intellekt, möchte man phantasieren, hat von dem Ding und hat von der ganzen Welt reine Anschauung. Er hat in seinem Gesichtsfeld die und die qualitativ so und so ausgefüllten Gestalten, und das sind die Dinge. Allerdings, unser visuelles Feld ist zweidimensional. Nun, der unendliche Intellekt hat ein dreidimensionales Gesichtsfeld. Das ist der „objektive Raum“; dieser kann sich verschieden begrenzen, d.h. verschiedene Aufteilung durch trennende Qualitätsausbreitungen erfahren und die zu jedem Zeitmoment gehörige Aufteilung. Die Mannigfaltigkeit der ausgefüllten Gestalten — das ist die zu dem betreffenden Zeitmoment gehörige „Welt“. — Indessen, die Sache hat ihre großen Bedenken. Ein Gesichtsfeld ist kein Raum und der Ausschnitt eines solchen Feldes kein Ding, weder für uns noch für den lieben Gott. Wie steht es doch mit der Bewegung?

The ideal is built in conditionals and given its model at once. Adequate givenness "would contain nothing" improper or undetermined; "would we still have a difference between appearance and what appears?" The question answers itself: no. A perception without adumbration would abolish the transcendence that §6 posited. The paragraph then shows where this seems possible, "in the manner of really immanent perception", when we objectivate the spread of contents in the visual field "as it is just given". The ideal of adequate thing-perception is the immanent field taken for space. Everything that follows tests that substitution.

The voice shifts as the ideal is extended. "One would like to fancy" (*möchte man phantasieren*) assigns the ideal to an imagined interlocutor and marks it as a product of phantasy, not of the eidetic phantasy of §3 but of daydreaming. The infinite intellect is built from our case by one change, "Well" (*Nun*), from two to three dimensions. The quotation marks on "objective space" and "world" show the cost of the change: these are the field's divisions renamed. The construction is honest about being a construction, and its two most important words are the ones it cannot use without quotation marks.

The refutation is two sentences. "However, the matter has its great misgivings" (*hat ihre großen Bedenken*) is plain; then comes the formula. "A field of view is no space, and the excerpt of such a field no thing" states a difference of kind. "Neither for us nor for the dear Lord" (*weder für uns noch für den lieben Gott*) extends it to every possible perceiver. The colloquial phrase is chosen against the solemn "infinitely perfect intellect" a few lines earlier. By calling that intellect "the dear Lord", Husserl lowers it to the God of everyday speech and makes the point in the same breath: the reason the field is not space has nothing to do with whose field it is. The limit is not finitude. The commentary draws this consequence for the thesis of necessary inadequacy (§33.14); the wording carries it before any argument has been given.

The argument follows the question "How do things stand, then, with movement?" A thing moved without change "is an individually identical as a whole" (Hua XVI 118). A filled field only seems to allow this, because "we succumb to the obvious temptation" to take the change of presenting contents parallel to an appearing movement "also as movement". What shifts in the field is quasi-movement, and identity through it is identity of something presented, not of the field-content. The reply to the ideal is therefore the content-apprehension distinction of §15 carried into motion: a field can present a thing, it cannot be one. Even so, Husserl does not close the question in §33. "With that the ideal of adequate perception seems not yet dispossessed" (*noch nicht depossediert*, Hua XVI 118), and he asks whether a presentation running through the quasi-movement could be adequate. The refusal of the ideal is completed only in §35, from the infinity of space against the finitude of the field.

§34 sets the Lord aside again. Facing the infinity of space, Husserl says: "We leave the dear Lord out of play now" (*Den lieben Gott lassen wir jetzt aus dem Spiel*), and rests the argument on our evidence that an appearance cannot be divided into infinitely many pieces and that "all infinity must lead back phenomenologically to something like 'limitlessness in the progress of intuition'" (Hua XVI 119). The two remarks do different work. In §33 God is brought in to show that the limit is essential; in §34 he is dismissed because the evidence that settles the infinity question is ours. The course does not need a theology of the infinite intellect. It needs only the claim that whatever intellect there is would perceive things through fields, if it perceives things at all.

*Ideas I* turns the colloquialism into a thesis. The view that "God, the subject of absolutely perfect cognition and thus also of every possible adequate perception", has the perception of the thing in itself denied to us finite beings "is countersensical", because it implies that a spatial thing would be a real constituent of the divine stream of experience (§43, Hua III/1 89–90, read with CW2). And in §44: there always remains a horizon of determinable indeterminacy, and "no God can change anything about that, any more than about 1 + 2 = 3" (Hua III/1 92). The 1907 phrase, "for the dear Lord", is the seed of both. What is added in 1913 is the reason: not quasi-movement against movement, but the essential difference between immanent and transcendent being that §§9 and 13 of the lectures had drawn.

## §35, Hua XVI 121–122: presentation must economize

Reads [§35.2–7](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-field-as-finite-means-of-presentation-the-perception-of-things-as-necessarily-inadequate).

Necessary inadequacy, in §35, follows from a mismatch between space and field. Space offers infinitely many locations and movements; a field offers finitely many means of presentation. A finite stock can present an infinity only by being used again and again in an ordered series, and that is what "economize" (*haushalten*) names. Inadequacy is then not a deficiency in a perception but the form in which any finite means presents an infinite object.

> Space is an infinite manifold of possible locations and thereby offers a field of infinitely many possibilities of movements. Every thing is *a priori* movable as a filled spatial body, and *in infinitum*; therefore *a priori* the possibility must be guaranteed that movement be given, that is, come to presentation. Where are the means of presentation for locations and change of location *in infinitum*? It is certain that the field can offer only finitely many means of presentation, and that it cannot suffice here in two respects. […]
>
> […] in any case it is clear that the intensive and extensive infinity of space, in which every thing in its own way has a share, cannot come to presentation in an adequate manner. With limited means of presentation every possible presentation must economize, and it can do so only if again and again the same means of presentation, finite in their manifoldness, come into action and the infinity comes to presentation through the serial form of the arrangement of the operations. And necessarily connected with this is inadequacy: the single appearance can point to an infinity of possible operations and presentations, but as soon as it does so, it is, in the sense of the guiding ideal, precisely an inadequate one.

German:

> Der Raum ist eine unendliche Mannigfaltigkeit möglicher Lagen und bietet damit ein Feld unendlich vieler Möglichkeiten von Bewegungen. Jedes Ding ist *a priori* als erfüllter Raumkörper beweglich, und *in infinitum*; also *a priori* muß die Möglichkeit gewährleistet sein, daß Bewegung gegeben werde, d.h. zur Darstellung komme. Wo sind die Darstellungsmittel für Lagen und Lagenveränderung *in infinitum*? Es ist sicher, daß das Feld nur endlich viele Darstellungsmittel bieten kann und daß es in zweierlei Hinsicht hierbei nicht ausreichen kann. […]
>
> […] jedenfalls ist es klar, daß die intensive und extensive Unendlichkeit des Raumes, an der in seiner Weise jedes Ding Anteil hat, in adäquater Weise nicht zur Darstellung kommen kann. Mit beschränkten Darstellungsmitteln muß jede mögliche Darstellung haushalten, und sie kann es nur, wenn immer wieder dieselben und der Mannigfaltigkeit nach endlichen Darstellungsmittel in Aktion treten und die Unendlichkeit durch die Reihenform der Anordnung der Operationen zur Darstellung kommt. Und damit hängt notwendig Inadäquatheit zusammen: Die einzelne Erscheinung kann auf eine Unendlichkeit möglicher Operationen und Darstellungen hinweisen, aber sowie sie das tut, ist sie eben im Sinne des leitenden Ideals eine inadäquate.

*Note on the text.* The first extract opens §35 on Hua XVI 121; the second closes the argument on p. 122. The omitted stretch states the two respects, the intensive and the extensive, and is drawn on below.

The opening sentence turns on the word "field" used twice in two senses. Space "offers a field (*Feld*) of infinitely many possibilities of movements"; a few lines later "the field can offer only finitely many means of presentation". The first is a figure for a range of possibilities, the second the visual field of §33, the sensuous extent in which contents spread. The pun states the problem: the field of possible movements is infinite, the field of presenting contents is finite. The inference is set out in *a priori* steps, marked by the Latin twice in one sentence. Every thing is "*a priori* movable … *in infinitum*", "therefore *a priori*" the possibility that movement be presented "must be guaranteed". The "must" is a condition on constitution: if things are to be given at all, their movability must be presentable. Then the question, "Where are the means?", and the answer, "It is certain", which is the one premise the argument takes as evident.

The two respects are the intensive and the extensive. A piece of the field "cannot offer an inner infinity of distinguishable points", so even a body wholly in view is presented inadequately; and because the field has bounds, an object can leave it, and in continued approach "finally we no longer have a large enough piece of field" (Hua XVI 121–122). Husserl adds a prediction to the first: "it is to be foreseen that for purposes of presentation there will have to be an analogue of 'approach and recession'", and he speaks of "an expansion of adumbration, so to speak" (*Abschattungsdehnung sozusagen*). The word *Dehnung* (expansion), introduced with "so to speak", becomes a term of art in §§66–71, where expansion is "what constitutes the thing and space" (rows 27–28). §35 deduces the need for the modification that Section V describes. The deduction does not depend on what the modification is: "however we think this 'approach', whether as something the same as the familiar approach or only similar".

The conclusion lies in the household verb. "With limited means of presentation every possible presentation must economize" (*muß … haushalten*). *Haushalten* is to keep house, to manage a budget, to make limited resources last. The verb gives the inadequacy its positive form: the same finite means "come into action" "again and again" (*immer wieder*), and the infinity is presented "through the serial form of the arrangement of the operations" (*durch die Reihenform der Anordnung der Operationen*). "Operations" is a new word in the course. It is not yet said what they are, but the serial order of operations is what Sections IV and V will identify with kinaesthetic courses, the ordered system of circumstances under which the same field presents ever new aspects. The infinite is presented as a rule of repetition, not as a content.

The last sentence is careful about what it calls inadequate. The single appearance "can point to an infinity of possible operations and presentations", and "as soon as it does so, it is, in the sense of the guiding ideal, precisely an inadequate one". Inadequacy is relative to "the guiding ideal", the adequate perception of §33. Measured by that ideal, every appearance falls short; measured by what a thing is, it does what a presentation of a thing must do. The commentary's reading, that inadequacy is structural and not a defect (§35.12 and the row's reason), is correct, but it is the phrase "in the sense of the guiding ideal" that makes it so. The paragraph that follows qualifies the method as well: "this consideration was in a certain way an a priori one" (*in gewisser Weise eine apriorische*, Hua XVI 122), and the next step proceeds "as it were a posteriori", with "our" spatial intuition, for which it is "irrelevant whether thing-space is three- or four- or however-dimensional, further whether it is flat or curved", provided only "congruence in itself" and continuity (Hua XVI 123). The economy argument holds for any space a field could present.

This is the argument that §16's one-sidedness could not supply (row 11). §16 grounded inadequacy in "the means of presentation at our disposal" and would not "venture to assert" more. §35 derives it from the relation of any finite field to an infinite space, a relation that holds "neither for us nor for the dear Lord". The same section then draws from it "a very good argument against the field-theory of givenness, which takes adequate givenness as real being in perception" (Hua XVI 124). Husserl's later note on that page, that "originally the sense of this presentation was that no maximal points were assumed, merely increases in the appearances" (Beilage I, ad 124, 10 ff., Hua XVI 339), concerns the argument against maximal points, not the economy. The household verb stands unrevised.

## §38, Hua XVI 134: an "infinite task" Husserl would not call one

Reads [§38.8–9](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#clarity-and-distinctness-in-the-givenness-of-the-perceived-thing).

The final determination of a thing is never reached, because every appearance carries a priori possibilities of re-determination, of further kinetic determination and of new attached determinations motivated by experience. The neo-Kantians call this an infinite task. Husserl grants them the infinity and refuses the task: an absolute determination of a thing is "no reasonable goal", and determination must be bounded by interest. The passage turns on one phrase taken over and then declined, and it is a passage Husserl did not deliver.

> I think little will be said against this. Through our considerations it now becomes understandable in what respect the final determination of the object can rightly be called an infinite task by neo-Kantianism, or wherein the phenomenological grounds of this lie. For *a priori* there belong to every appearance possibilities of re-determination, of enrichment and so on; *a priori* the infinity of possible kinetic determinations, which on the side of the object find their mastery through geometry; but *a priori* also the possibility of infinitely many attached determinations which, characterized as actually belonging to the object, grow out of the connection of experience through experiential motivation. […] So there exist *a priori* infinitely many possibilities, and ever new possibilities, of determining the thing, which I would, however, not like to call an infinite task. Precisely because this state of affairs obtains, one will be able to call it no reasonable goal to determine any thing absolutely; the determination of things must be guided and bounded by reasonable practical interests.

German:

> Ich denke, dagegen wird sich wenig sagen lassen. Durch unsere Betrachtungen wird es nun verständlich, inwiefern die endgültige Objektbestimmung vom Neukantianismus mit Recht als unendliche Aufgabe bezeichnet werden kann bzw. worin die phänomenologischen Gründe davon liegen. Denn *a priori* gehören zu jeder Erscheinung Möglichkeiten der Neubestimmung, der Bereicherung usw., *a priori* die Unendlichkeit möglicher kinetischer Bestimmungen, die von Seiten des Objektes ihre Beherrschung durch die Geometrie finden, *a priori* aber auch die Möglichkeit unendlich vieler anhängender Bestimmtheiten, die, in der Charakteristik als dem Objekt wirklich zugehörige, aus dem Zusammenhang der Erfahrung durch Erfahrungsmotivation erwachsen. […] So bestehen *a priori* unendlich viele Möglichkeiten und immer neue Möglichkeiten der Dingbestimmung, die ich freilich nicht als unendliche Aufgabe bezeichnen möchte. Man wird es eben, weil diese Sachlage besteht, als kein vernünftiges Ziel bezeichnen können, irgendein Ding absolut zu bestimmen, die Dingbestimmung muß von vernünftig praktischen Interessen geleitet und begrenzt sein.

*Note on the text.* The editor reports a marginal note of Husserl's against the stretch from p. 133, line 28, to p. 134, line 37, which contains this passage: "Not read; cf. new lecture" (*Nicht gelesen; vgl. neue Vorlesung*; Beilage I, ad 133, 28 – 134, 37, Hua XVI 339). The passage was written but, by Husserl's account, not delivered.

The concession and the refusal are built from the same phrase. First "it now becomes understandable in what respect" (*inwiefern*) the final determination of the object "can rightly be called an infinite task by neo-Kantianism". The "in what respect" limits the "rightly": the neo-Kantians are right about something, and the lectures can say what. Then "or wherein the phenomenological grounds of this lie": Husserl supplies the ground they lack, a description of appearances, in place of their regulative idea of reason. The ground comes in three *a priori* clauses, each opening with the Latin: possibilities of re-determination and enrichment (the results of §§29–33), the infinity of kinetic determinations that geometry masters (§35), and the infinity of attached determinations that experience motivates (§20's *materia secunda*). The triple anaphora gathers Section III into one sentence.

The refusal is phrased as a preference about words. "Which I would, however, not like to call an infinite task" (*die ich freilich nicht als unendliche Aufgabe bezeichnen möchte*). *Freilich*, "admittedly" or "however", concedes the infinity in the act of declining the name. The reason follows with "precisely because" (*eben, weil*). The infinity that grounds the neo-Kantian phrase is what makes the phrase inappropriate: if absolute determination cannot be achieved, it is "no reasonable goal", and a task without a reasonable goal is not a task. The argument plays on the word *Aufgabe*, which means both a problem set and an assignment one takes on. Husserl hears in it the second sense: a task is something one sets oneself, and no one reasonable sets himself the impossible. Determination is therefore relative to "reasonable practical interests", which §§36–37 had shown at work in the optimal appearance relative to interest.

The passage stops short of three cases. The same infinity holds for the ideal of general determination, the "morphological 'laws'" of thinghood; "whether it is otherwise with regard to the exact natural laws of 'physics', that would need new investigation" (Hua XVI 134). The quotation marks on "laws" and "physics" leave the neo-Kantian home ground, mathematical natural science, untouched. Husserl's refusal concerns the perceived thing. Whether the object of physics is an infinite task he does not say.

The note "Not read; cf. new lecture" sends the reader to §39, which opens the next lecture, and there the refusal returns in a sharper form. "Should we be content with the 'infinite task'? Who will reasonably set himself a task whose solution can be achieved only through an infinite process, or, better put, whose solution by its essence cannot be achieved?" And: "if the task lies in producing absolutely complete givenness, then it is *a priori* unsolvable, it is an unreasonably posed task" (*eine unverständig gestellte Aufgabe*, Hua XVI 138). What was delivered, then, is not the concession of §38 but the refusal alone: the neo-Kantian phrase appears in quotation marks, as a question, and the phenomenological grounds are given only for rejecting it. The commentary reads §38 as Husserl's considered position (§38.8–9) and does not say that the passage was not given. A reading of the course as taught has to place the concession in the manuscript and the refusal in the lecture hall.

The understanding the passage reaches, an infinity of possible determination bounded in practice by interest, was not Husserl's last word on the phrase. In *Ideas I* the section on the subject is titled "Adequate givenness of the thing as idea in the Kantian sense" (§143, Hua III/1 330, title in the contents). What 1907 refused as a task, 1913 accepted as an idea. The two judgments are compatible: an idea in the Kantian sense is not a goal one sets oneself but a rule that prescribes the endless course of experience. Still, the 1907 refusal rests on the word *Aufgabe*, and the later formula keeps Kant's name while changing the noun.

## §41, Hua XVI 144: one givenness, two directions of judgment

Reads [§41.2, §41.7](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#attitude-toward-the-thing-and-attitude-toward-the-appearance-the-given-side-of-the-thing).

The appearance and the thing that appears are not two objects side by side. There is one absolute givenness, and two directions in which one can judge about it: toward the appearing thing, and toward the appearance. §41 says this in answer to an objection from Heinrich Hofmann, a student in the course, and the answer fixes the two attitudes, thing-directed and appearance-directed, that the course needs to describe its own procedure.

> At this account, whose evidence I cannot escape as often as I live through it, Herr Hofmann has taken offence. He is unable to make the quale of the phenomenological datum perception into an object for himself in the way, he thinks, one can the quale of a determinate tone. And he says: when I perceive a house, there is not, beside the physical thing house, also a psychic or phenomenological thing called perception. The latter sentence admittedly expresses a side-by-side of which we have never spoken. Naturally we have only one thing in the phenomenological sense, only one absolute givenness, and yet two evidently different directions of judgment: once about the appearing object, the other time about the appearance.

German:

> An dieser Darstellung, deren Evidenz ich mich nicht zu entziehen vermag, so oft ich sie durchlebe, hat Herr Hofmann Anstoß genommen. Er vermag nicht, das Quale des phänomenologischen Datums Wahrnehmung sich gegenständlich zu machen so wie, meint er, das Quale eines bestimmten Tones. Und er sagt: wenn ich ein Haus wahrnehme, gibt es neben dem physischen Ding Haus nicht noch ein psychisches oder phänomenologisches Ding, genannt Wahrnehmung. — Der letztere Satz drückt allerdings ein Nebeneinander aus, von dem wir nie gesprochen haben. Natürlich haben wir nur eines im phänomenologischen Sinn, nur eine absolute Gegebenheit und doch zwei evident verschiedene Richtungen des Urteils: einmal über den erscheinenden Gegenstand, das andere Mal über die Erscheinung.

*Note on the text.* "This account" refers back to the lecture before, most of which (§40, Hua XVI 139–141) is garbled or invented in the file. What Hofmann objected to cannot be read there; the reading relies on §41's own statement of the objection. The editor's footnote marker after "Hofmann" (identifying him as Husserl's student, whose dissertation appeared in 1913) is left out.

Husserl reports the objection fairly and in two parts. The first is about evidence: Hofmann "is unable" (*vermag nicht*) to make the quale of perception an object as he can the quale of a tone. The second is ontological and is given in direct speech: there is not "beside the physical thing house also a psychic or phenomenological thing called perception". Husserl answers only the second. The first, a report of what Hofmann fails to see, is met by Husserl's own report, "whose evidence I cannot escape as often as I live through it". Two testimonies of evidence stand against each other, and the passage does not adjudicate between them. It moves the dispute to ground where it can be settled, the question whether the course ever posited two things.

The reply turns on one noun. Hofmann's sentence "expresses a side-by-side (*Nebeneinander*) of which we have never spoken". The noun names the picture the objection rests on: two things in a row, a house and a perception, as there are two houses on a street. Husserl concedes the conclusion, "naturally we have only one", and denies that the course ever held the premise. The positive claim is built as a contrast: "only one absolute givenness and yet two evidently different directions of judgment" (*nur eine absolute Gegebenheit und doch zwei evident verschiedene Richtungen des Urteils*). The duality is moved from objects to judgments, and "evidently" (*evident*) claims for the difference of directions the evidence Hofmann denied to the perception as object. The counting words carry the argument: one givenness, two directions, and the phrase "once … the other time" (*einmal … das andere Mal*) gives the two as alternatives in time, not as coexisting things.

The paragraph that follows supplies the evidence through Hofmann's own example, the tone. Hearing the evenly sustained whistle of a steam whistle as I approach, I say it is the same, evenly continuing whistle; "that is the object". Attending to what is phenomenologically given, "I find for each time-piece of what appears an essentially different appearance; this quale is not the same" (Hua XVI 144). Hofmann granted that a tone's quale can be made an object. Husserl shows that in the tone too, quale and object come apart, so the difference between appearance and appearing is not peculiar to perception of houses. The tone was Hofmann's model of easy objectivation; it becomes Husserl's model of the two directions.

The course then works out the two directions as attitudes (*Einstellungen*), and the vocabulary shifts from judgment to attention. The first, "the 'natural' one", is the one that underlies describing the thing in words: "here is a green slope shot through with flowers". The second: "I attend to the 'appearance', it is my object: the side, that is, the adumbration" (Hua XVI 146). Between them Husserl analyses what the "side" is, distinguishing the slope itself from the side in which it presents itself, and within the side the spread of sensuous contents, what makes the spread a presentation, and what makes their unity appear "as something on the thing". Husserl's own later verdict on p. 146, 12 is one word, "Inexact" (*Ungenau*, Beilage I, Hua XVI 339), and on p. 149 he asks that "the difference between proper and improper appearance must be better taken into account in the foregoing pages" (Hua XVI 340). The two directions stood; their description he judged unfinished.

The commentary calls §41 the first statement of the noetic-noematic correlation (§41.14). The wording supports a narrower claim. The correlation of *Ideas I* is between act and meant object as meant; §41 distinguishes the appearance, a really immanent unity of sensation and apprehension, from the appearing thing. What it anticipates is the shift of regard, from the thing to its mode of appearing, not yet the noema. Its earlier ancestor is clearer. The editor points to the Fifth Logical Investigation, where the sensations "are not themselves intended": "I do not see colour-sensations but coloured things, I do not hear tone-sensations but the song of the singer" (LU V §11, Hua XIX/1 387). In 1901 the point was that the sensations are not objects of the act. Hofmann's objection pressed the converse, that the act is then no object either. §41's answer keeps the 1901 thesis for the natural attitude and adds the second direction, in which the appearance becomes an object without becoming a second thing.

Husserl's note at the head of the lecture states how much the course owed to the objection: "The lecture is determined by Hofmann's objections" (*Das Kolleg ist durch Einwände Hofmanns bestimmt*, Beilage I, ad 139, 9 ff., Hua XVI 339). The recapitulation of Chapter 7, in which the course reflects on its own method under the reduction, was written in reply to a student. The two directions of judgment are the course's account of what it has been doing all along when it moved between the house and its adumbrations.

## §46, Hua XVI 160–161: sensations that make presentation possible without presenting

Reads [§46.6–7](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#presentational-and-kinaesthetic-sensations).

Kinaesthetic sensations are the sensations of the moving eyes, head and hands, taken phenomenologically. They enter the apprehension of every outer thing, yet they present nothing: nothing in the thing corresponds to them, and they adumbrate no body. Their function is to make presentation possible. §46 introduces them by showing that the field-sensations of sight and touch, which do present, cannot constitute space by themselves, and it names them with a Greek coinage because the German word misleads.

> That is to say that the extensional moment of visual sensation, as also of tactile sensation, does adumbrate spatiality but does not suffice to make possible the constitution of spatiality, any more than the qualitative moment suffices for the constitution of the objective space-filling feature. New sensations are still needed, and we speak here of the sensations of movement, and these naturally have in the animating "apprehension" a quite different position and function from the presenting contents. They make presentation possible without themselves presenting. But I must remark terminologically at once that the word sensation of movement is unusable for us, since it is not meant that we sense the movement of the thing, or even only that the movement of the thing presents itself in them. As is well known, the word refers to the one who moves himself and wants to be understood psychologically. Excluding the psychological meaning, we will use the word kinaesthetic sensation, which as a foreign word is less misleading.

German:

> Damit ist gesagt, daß das extensionale Moment der Gesichtsempfindung wie auch der Tastempfindung zwar Räumlichkeit abschattet, aber doch zur Ermöglichung der Konstitution von Räumlichkeit nicht ausreicht, ebensowenig, als das qualitative Moment zur Konstitution des objektiven raumfüllenden Merkmals ausreicht. Es bedarf noch neuer Empfindungen, und wir sprechen hier von den Bewegungsempfindungen, und diese haben in der beseelenden ,,Auffassung" natürlich eine ganz andere Stellung und Funktion als die darstellenden Inhalte. Sie ermöglichen Darstellung, ohne selbst darzustellen. Doch muß ich terminologisch gleich bemerken, daß das Wort Bewegungsempfindung für uns unbrauchbar ist, da nicht gemeint sein soll, daß wir die Bewegung des Dinges empfinden oder auch nur, daß sich in ihnen Bewegung des Dinges darstellt. Bekanntlich bezieht sich das Wort auf den sich Bewegenden und will psychologisch verstanden sein. Wir werden unter Ausschluß der psychologischen Bedeutung das Wort kinästhetische Empfindung, das als Fremdwort weniger beirrend ist, verwenden.

The thesis sentence borrows the structure of the yellow sphere. Just as the qualitative moment of sensation, the shaded yellow, does not suffice for the objective colour without apprehension, so the extensional moment "does adumbrate spatiality but does not suffice" to make its constitution possible. The concessive *zwar … aber doch* (does … but still) keeps §14's term: extension in the field is an adumbration of space. What it lacks is not a further apprehension but a further kind of sensation. "New sensations are still needed" moves the analysis from the content-apprehension schema of §15, in which apprehension supplied the plus, to a second plus within sensation itself.

The formula for their function uses one verb twice: "they make presentation possible without themselves presenting" (*Sie ermöglichen Darstellung, ohne selbst darzustellen*). The paragraph before had prepared a double sense of the word. Presentation "in the widest sense", in quotation marks, covers "all sensation-contents" that enter the unity of appearance and undergo animating apprehension; the narrower sense, "projecting and thereby necessarily materializing presentation", covers only field-sensations (Hua XVI 160). Kinaesthetic sensations belong to presentation in the wide sense and not in the narrow. They "play an essential role in the apprehension of every outer thing", but nothing qualitative corresponds to them in the thing, "and they do not adumbrate the body either". "And yet, without their help there is no body, no thing" (Hua XVI 160). Husserl's later note on the paragraph makes the two senses explicit: "presentation in the narrower sense (projection …) and presentation in the wider sense. Not all sensation-data are presenting in the first sense" (Beilage I, ad 160, 16, Hua XVI 340). The formula of §46 is thus exact only with the narrow sense in its second half. The kinaesthetic sensations do not present in the sense of projection; they are presenting contents in the wider sense, and the course must say what their apprehension is.

The terminology follows the function. "Sensation of movement" (*Bewegungsempfindung*) is "unusable for us" because the compound suggests that what is sensed is movement, the thing's or one's own. "As is well known, the word refers to the one who moves himself" (*auf den sich Bewegenden*), and the psychology of the day used it for the felt motion of the limbs. Husserl wants neither sense. The sensations are not of a movement and do not present one; they are the data through whose courses movement will be constituted. The new word is chosen for its foreignness: "kinaesthetic sensation, which as a foreign word is less misleading" (*das als Fremdwort weniger beirrend ist*). The argument is about connotation, not etymology. Greek *kinēsis* (movement) and *aisthēsis* (sensation) say the same as the German, but a learned compound does not carry the everyday sense that makes the German compound mislead. The commentary records the coinage (§46.7). The point to add is that Husserl's claim for it is modest: the foreign word is "less" misleading, not exact, and it is chosen "excluding the psychological meaning", by stipulation.

The paragraph after narrows the claim further. Whether kinaesthetic sensations form "a fundamentally new basic genus of sensations" or belong with touch in a higher genus "is a question for a doctorate" (*eine Doktorfrage*, Hua XVI 161). The dismissive word sets the classification aside as a psychologist's question. What matters is the function: they appear "connected and, if one will, interwoven" with visual and tactile sensations, "but cannot mix with them, as if they could exchange function with them". The kinaesthetic sensations are defined by what they cannot do.

The course keeps the term and later loosens what it names. In §51 the kinaesthetic sensations become "the 'circumstances' (*Umstände*)" against the images as "the 'appearances'" (row 23). Husserl's note on a later page says that "orally I emphasized here, as very often, that it is not or need not be a question of a sort of simple and specifically peculiar sensations, and moreover that other things also belong under the title circumstances, such as open eye!" (Beilage I, ad 254, 34–38, Hua XVI 341). The open eye is no sensation of movement at all. By the end, the function that §46 assigned to a class of sensations is assigned to a title, "circumstances", that the class does not exhaust. A supplement to *Ideas II* still speaks of "the associative system of subjectively kinaesthetic movements" (*das assoziative System subjektiv kinästhetischer Bewegungen*) that motivates the associative system of the bodily thing (Hua IV, Beilagen, 381). The 1907 coinage lasted; the notion that it names a kind of sensation did not.

## §47, Hua XVI 162–163: the Body and the double apprehension

Reads [§47.3–6](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#insertion-of-the-kinaesthetic-sensations-into-the-body).

The Body (*Leib*) is a thing among things and also the bearer of the ego, in which sensations are localized. The same sensations that make outer things appear can be apprehended a second way, as had by the Body, and this "double apprehension" (*Doppelauffassung*) is what distinguishes the Body from every other thing. §47 states it in the course's existing categories: the localized sensations are "attached determinations", like the sound of the violin, only "of their own kind". That fit is the passage's weak point, and Husserl later said so.

> On the one hand the Body too is a thing, a physical thing like any other, namely insofar as it has its space and has filled it with proper and attached matter. It is a thing among other things; among them it has its changing location, it rests or moves like other things. On the other hand this thing is precisely Body, bearer of the ego; the ego has sensations, and these sensations are "localized" in the Body, partly in thought, partly immediately in the manner of appearance. In seeing I do not, admittedly, localize the visual appearances appearingly in my body, but I do localize the tactile sensations and the sensations interwoven with them, among them sensations of movement. The touching hand "appears" as having the tactile sensations. […]
>
> […] and yet it distinguishes itself appearingly from all other physical things, namely by a class of attached determinations that belong to it exclusively, and indeed in proper appearance. These are the "subjective" determinations. […] If we keep only to the sensations that have a thingly objectivating function, we find that they take on a double apprehension: once the one that lets the physical thing, and also the Body as physical thing, appear, and the other time the one that lets the Body appear as sensing, as the bearer of such and such sensations. So out of them come attached determinations of their own kind, subjective occurrences inserted into the Body, localized in it.

German:

> Einerseits ist der Leib auch ein Ding, physisches Ding wie irgendeines sonst, nämlich sofern es seinen Raum hat, und mit eigentlicher und anhängender Materie erfüllt hat. Es ist ein Ding unter anderen Dingen, zwischen ihnen hat es seine wechselnde Lage, es ruht oder bewegt sich wie andere Dinge. Andererseits ist dieses Ding eben Leib, Träger des Ich; das Ich hat Empfindungen, und diese Empfindungen werden im Leib ,,lokalisiert'' teils denkmäßig, teils unmittelbar erscheinungsmäßig. Sehend lokalisiere ich die Gesichterscheinungen allerdings nicht erscheinungsmäßig in meinem Körper, wohl aber die Tastempfindungen und die mit ihnen verflochtenen Empfindungen, darunter Bewegungsempfindungen. Die tastende Hand ,,erscheint'' als die Tastempfindungen habend. […]
>
> […] und zeichnet sich doch vor allen anderen physischen Dingen erscheinungsmäßig aus, nämlich durch eine Klasse anhängender Bestimmtheiten, die ihm ausschließlich, und zwar in eigentlicher Erscheinung zugehören. Es sind das die ,,subjektiven'' Bestimmtheiten. […] Halten wir uns bloß an die Empfindungen, die eine dinglich objektivierende Funktion haben, so finden wir, daß sie eine Doppelauffassung annehmen, einmal diejenige, die das physische Ding und auch den Leib als physisches Ding erscheinen läßt und das andere Mal diejenige, die den Leib als empfindenden, als den Träger der und der Empfindungen erscheinen läßt. So werden aus ihnen anhängende Bestimmtheiten eigener Art, subjektive und dem Leib eingelegte, in ihm lokalisierte Vorkommnisse.

*Note on the text.* The first extract is on Hua XVI 162, the second runs from p. 162 to p. 163. The editor's footnote marker after "attached determinations" (*anhängender Bestimmtheiten*), left out here, refers to Husserl's critical note discussed below.

The two sides of the Body are joined by "on the one hand … on the other hand" (*Einerseits … Andererseits*), and the second side begins with an emphatic "precisely" (*eben*): "this thing is precisely Body". The same noun phrase, "this thing", is subject of both. Husserl does not set a body beside a lived body; he gives one thing two characters, and the "Body" that the commentary capitalizes is *Leib*, against *Körper*, the word he uses one sentence later when he writes that I do not localize visual appearances "in my body" (*in meinem Körper*). The switch of nouns is exact. Visual appearances are not localized in the physical body; tactile sensations are localized in the *Leib*. Sight and touch differ here, and the asymmetry is stated with "admittedly … but" (*allerdings … wohl aber*). The difference between the senses carries the difference between the two nouns.

Quotation marks mark the words that must change sense to describe the Body. Sensations are "localized", in quotation marks, "partly in thought, partly immediately in the manner of appearance". The hand "'appears' as having" its sensations. Both verbs come from the vocabulary of things: a thing is localized, a thing appears. Here they are applied to sensations and to the having of sensations, and the quotation marks admit that the application is new. The example of the hands then shows the reversal in act. When the left hand touches the right, the appearance of each hand moving over the other is constituted; "at the same time, namely with a change of apprehension", the moving appears "in the other sense, which belongs only to the Body", and the same groups of sensations are, "with a change of attention and apprehension", apprehended "subjectivatingly" (Hua XVI 162). "Objectivating" and "subjectivating" are a pair of the course's own coining, and they name not two classes of sensations but two apprehensions of one class.

The categorization comes next. The Body is distinguished "by a class of attached determinations" that belong to it alone, "in proper appearance": the "subjective" determinations, in quotation marks. This puts the sensings of the hand into §20's category of the *materia secunda*, alongside sound and smell, determinations that presuppose a constituted thing and attach to it. The fit is formally right, since the subjectivating apprehension "presupposes the ego-Body already otherwise constituted" (Hua XVI 163). It is materially wrong, and Husserl's later note on this sentence says so: "But this 'attaching' is something essentially different from that of dead things" (*Dieses „Anhängen“ ist aber ein wesentlich anderes als das der toten Dinge*, Beilage I, ad 162, 30–36, Hua XVI 340). "Dead things" (*tote Dinge*) supplies the contrast the 1907 text lacked. A sound attaches to a violin; a sensation is had by a hand. The category of attached determinations has no room for having.

The passage looks beyond the course and then declines to go there. Out of the double apprehension "grows finally the introjection of all sensations and all appearances … into the ego and the ego-Body", as well as the possibility of inserting "psychic occurrences" into other physical things "and apprehending them as 'animated bodies'" (*„beseelte Leiber“*, Hua XVI 163). The second clause is the course's first mention of other subjects, and it follows from the Body's double apprehension: I can apprehend other bodies as sensing because I apprehend my own that way. "But that already goes beyond our line" (*Aber das geht schon über unsere Linie hinaus*). The course sets the subjectivating apprehension aside and studies "the physically objectivating function" of the kinaesthetic sensations alone. The file adds a parenthesis that reads, "(They are not essential for the appearance of physical thinghood.)" As printed it contradicts §46, where without kinaesthetic sensations "there is no body, no thing"; the context suggests that it refers to the subjectivating apprehension, but the file cannot settle the reference, and the reading does not rest on it.

The commentary reads §47 as the seed of *Ideas II* (§47.13). The corpus bears this out and shows what was changed. In *Ideas II* the same pressure-sensation of the hand on the table is apprehended "once as perception of the table-surface" and, "with another direction of attention, in actualization of another stratum of apprehension", as finger-pressure sensation; with hand touching hand "we have two sensations and each doubly apprehensible" (Hua IV 146–147). The double apprehension of 1907 survives almost word for word. What is new is the name for what the second apprehension yields: "specific occurrences of the Body of the kind we call *Empfindnisse*" [sensings], which "are lacking in 'merely' material things" (Hua IV 146). The coinage replaces "attached determinations of their own kind". It answers Husserl's own note: the Body's sensings are not attached as a thing's properties are, and the later text gives them a word of their own.

## §50, Hua XVI 176: the visual courses do not suffice

Reads [§50.7](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-sequence-of-images-in-the-case-of-eye-movement-and-in-the-case-of-the-movement-of-the-object).

The constitution of objective location is "essentially mediated" by kinaesthesis. The reason is that one and the same course of visual images can appear as rest or as movement, depending on whether the body moves, so the visual course alone cannot decide between them. §50 proves this with a pair of thought-experiments in which everything visual is held fixed and only the body varies. The conclusion is the indispensability thesis on which Chapter 9 rests.

> Performing certain movements, we have, say, the appearance of a constantly resting object-field, while a certain series of visual images, passing continuously into one another in such and such a way, runs off. Leaving our body in complete rest, we have, when the thing appears as resting, a single unchanged image. But the object can now set itself in movement, and in such a way that exactly the same series of visual images runs off as before with the body moving. But now movement appears, while before rest appeared, in spite of the identity of the course of appearance. We can also contrast the following: eye and body in general rest, and a resting object-field appears. The visual field of sensation is unchanged. Now let the body move and at the same time the object-field, but in such a way that it, so to speak, follows what is moving, namely so, and this is evidently a possibility, that the object-field always appears in exactly the same "appearance", presents itself in exactly the same visual field. With an unchanged visual field, then, rest appears once and movement once.
>
> We see from this that in fact the merely visual courses do not suffice for the apprehension, that they do not have in themselves the means to bring rest and movement to distinct appearance. But with that it is said that the constitution of objective location and objective spatiality is essentially mediated by the movement of the Body, phenomenologically speaking, by the kinaesthetic sensations, be it by constant ones, be it by running ones, by kinaesthetic courses.

German:

> Gewisse Bewegungen vollziehend, haben wir etwa die Erscheinung eines immerfort ruhenden Objektfeldes, wobei eine gewisse Serie stetig so und so ineinander übergehender visueller Bilder abläuft. Unseren Leib in völliger Ruhe belassend, haben wir, wenn das Ding als ruhend erscheint, ein einziges unverändertes Bild. Es kann nun aber das Objekt sich in Bewegung setzen und dabei so, das genau dieselbe Serie visueller Bilder abläuft wie vorhin bei bewegtem Leib. Aber nun erscheint Bewegung, während vorhin Ruhe erschien trotz der Identität des Erscheinungsverlaufes. Wir können auch folgendes gegenüberstellen: Auge und Leib überhaupt ruhen und es erscheint ein ruhendes Objektfeld. Das visuelle Empfindungsfeld ist unverändert. Nun bewege sich der Leib und zugleich das Objektfeld, aber so, daß er gewissermaßen dem sich Bewegenden nachgeht, nämlich so, und das ist offenbar eine Möglichkeit, daß das Objektfeld stets in genau gleicher „Erscheinung“ erscheint, sich in genau demselben visuellen Feld darstellt. Bei unverändertem visuellen Feld erscheint also einmal Ruhe und einmal Bewegung.
>
> Wir ersehen daraus, daß in Tat die bloß visuellen Verläufe für die Auffassung nicht ausreichen, daß sie die Mittel nicht in sich haben, Ruhe und Bewegung zu unterschiedener Erscheinung zu bringen. Damit ist aber gesagt, daß die Konstitution der objektiven Lage und objektiven Räumlichkeit wesentlich vermittelt ist durch die Leibesbewegung, phänomenologisch gesprochen, durch die kinästhetischen Empfindungen, sei es durch konstante, sei es durch verlaufende, durch kinästhetische Verläufe.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "das genau dieselbe" for *daß genau dieselbe* [that exactly the same] and "in Tat" for *in der Tat* [in fact]; both are kept as the file has them and rendered by their evident sense.

The two experiments are mirror images. In the first, the visual course varies identically in both cases, and the body varies: with the body moving, "exactly the same series of visual images" gives rest; with the body at rest and the object moving, it gives movement. In the second, the visual field is unchanged in both cases, and again the body varies: at rest, the unchanged field gives a resting object; with the body moving and the object moving with it, the unchanged field gives movement. "Exactly the same" (*genau dieselbe*, *genau gleicher*, *genau demselben*) recurs four times, and it carries the method. Everything visual is held identical, so whatever differs in what appears must come from outside the visual. The formula at the end, "once rest and once movement" (*einmal Ruhe und einmal Bewegung*), is the method's result in four words.

The second experiment needs a case that may not be possible, and the passage claims it with a parenthesis. The object-field must move so that the body "so to speak follows what is moving" (*gewissermaßen dem sich Bewegenden nachgeht*), and "this is evidently a possibility" (*das ist offenbar eine Möglichkeit*). "Evidently" is doing the work of an argument. The case is tracking: eyes and body follow a moving object so that its image stays fixed. That it occurs is plausible; that it is an essential possibility is asserted. The eidetic method of §3 permits phantasized examples, and the claim is that this one can be phantasized consistently. Nothing more is offered.

The inference has a gap that the connective covers. "We see from this that in fact the merely visual courses do not suffice": that much the experiments show. "But with that it is said" (*Damit ist aber gesagt*) that constitution is "essentially mediated" by the Body's movement. The step from "visual courses do not suffice" to "kinaesthetic sensations are needed" holds only if kinaesthesis is the sole remaining candidate. §46 had made that assumption; §50 does not argue it. What the experiments isolate is a difference in the body's state, and Husserl names that difference phenomenologically, "phenomenologically speaking, by the kinaesthetic sensations". The phrase translates the bodily movement of the experiments into the course's terms. Whether the difference could lie in something other than a sensation, such as the open eye that Husserl's later note counts among the "circumstances" (Beilage I, ad 254, 34–38, Hua XVI 341), §50 does not ask.

The last clause is the one the commentary passes over, and the next Chapter depends on it. Constitution is mediated by kinaesthetic sensations "be it by constant ones, be it by running ones" (*sei es durch konstante, sei es durch verlaufende*). The body at rest is not the absence of kinaesthesis but a constant kinaesthetic sensation, and in the first experiment the single unchanged image goes with it. That is what lets §51 treat every visual course as running under some kinaesthetic circumstance, *K* constant or *K* changing, and set up the correlation in which rest of the object is defined: an image that changes as the *K* change, in the way that belongs to that change (row 23). Without "constant", the kinaesthetic sensations would enter only when the body moves, and rest would have no kinaesthetic correlate.

§83 returns to the same configuration and finds that it does not decide by itself. In the carriage, kinaesthetic rest goes with moving images of the surroundings, and what appears is not "the surroundings move" but "I move (am moved)" (row 31). By §50's rule, constant *K* with changing images should give an object in movement. The carriage gives self-movement instead, and Husserl asks whether the carriage, with its states of movement, "takes over the function of kinaesthetic sensation" (Hua XVI 284). §50's experiments prove that the visual course is insufficient. They do not prove that the kinaesthetic sensations are sufficient, and the course's last Section shows a case where they are not.

## §51, Hua XVI 181: "circumstances" and "appearances"

Reads [§51.13–16](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#sequence-of-appearances-in-the-case-of-mere-oculomotor-changes).

The kinaesthetic sensations *K* and the visual images *f* do different work in perception. The images point to one another and are run through by a consciousness of unity; the kinaesthetic sensations do not point and only run off. Husserl names the difference with two words in quotation marks: the *K* are the "circumstances" (*Umstände*), the *f* the "appearances" (*Erscheinungen*). The relation between them is neither a unity like the images' nor a mere coexistence. It is a dependence in which a change of circumstances brings an expected change of appearances, and that dependence is what §54 will call motivation.

> Quite otherwise is it with the series of the *K*: they do not point to one another, they run off, but they are not bearers of intentions running through them of the kind the *f* have, not a consciousness of unity running through them. The reversal of the sequence of the *K* gives again a flow of intentions that run through the *f*: *f*ₐ, *f*꜀, *f*ᵦ, *f*ₐ, directed at and through one another, while the *K* run off in reverse, again without this distinction. We find in the appearances, then, two quite differently functioning moments. The *K* are the "circumstances", the *f* are the "appearances". With a determinate change of the circumstances <there follows> a determinate change of the appearances, and indeed a change that gives their intentions outflow, fulfilment, continuous consciousness of unity. The *K* and the *f* naturally do not form with one another a unity of the kind the *f* have with one another. The consciousness of unity goes only through the *f*, and not partly through the *K*, partly through the *f*. On the other hand the two are also not a mere togetherness, but rather such that when *K*₀ passes into *K*₁, *f*₀ passes expectantly into *f*₁, pointing to each new phase and fulfilling itself in it.

German:

> Ganz anders verhält es sich mit der Reihe der *K*; sie weisen aufeinander nicht hin, sie laufen ab, sie sind aber nicht Träger durch sie hindurchgehender Intentionen der Art wie sie die *f* haben, nicht ein durch sie gehendes Einheitsbewußtsein. Die Umkehrung der Abfolge der *K* gibt wieder einen Abfluß von Intentionen, die durch die *f* hindurchgehen: $f_a$, $f_c$, $f_b$, $f_a$, aufeinander und durcheinander gerichtet, während die *K* umgekehrt ablaufen wieder ohne diese Auszeichnung. Wir finden in den Erscheinungen also zwei ganz verschieden fungierende Momente. Die *K* sind die „Umstände“, die *f* sind die „Erscheinungen“. Bei bestimmter Änderung der Umstände <erfolgt> eine bestimmte Änderung der Erscheinungen, und zwar eine Änderung, die ihren Intentionen Abfluß gibt, Erfüllung, durchgehendes Einheitsbewußtsein. Die *K* und die *f* bilden miteinander natürlich auch nicht eine derartige Einheit, wie die *f* sie miteinander haben. Das Einheitsbewußtsein geht nur durch die *f* und nicht teils durch die *K*, teils durch die *f*. Beide sind andererseits auch nicht ein bloßes Zusammen, vielmehr so, daß, wenn $K_0$ in $K_1$ übergeht, $f_0$ erwartungsmäßig in $f_1$ übergeht, auf jede neue Phase hinweisend und sich in ihr erfüllend.

*Note on the text.* The reversed series reads "*f*ₐ, *f*꜀, *f*ᵦ, *f*ₐ" in the file. The forward series in the preceding paragraph runs "*f*ₐ to *f*_d", so the reversal should begin with *f*_d; the first index is probably a conversion error, and the reading does not depend on it. The angle brackets around ‹erfolgt› [there follows] mark an editor's addition.

The passage opens with a contrast, "quite otherwise" (*Ganz anders*), and the preceding paragraph supplies the other term. There *f*ₐ "points" to its continuous neighbours, with "points" (*„hinweist“*) in quotation marks, and intentions "run through the series *f*ₐ to *f*_d and are continuously fulfilled" (Hua XVI 180–181). The *K* are described by three negations: "they do not point to one another", they are "not bearers of intentions running through them", "not a consciousness of unity running through them". Between the negations stands one positive verb, "they run off" (*sie laufen ab*). That verb is all the *K* do. The reversal test confirms it. Run the *K* backwards and the *f* give a new flow of mutually directed intentions, while the *K* run off "again without this distinction" (*wieder ohne diese Auszeichnung*). Direction matters to the images, because their intentions have a sense of what comes next. It does not matter to the kinaesthetic series, which is the same series either way.

The naming sentence gives both terms quotation marks, and they distance different things. "Circumstances" is a new technical use of an ordinary word: not the conditions of an event in general, but the kinaesthetic state under which an image is given. "Appearances" is the course's oldest term, and the quotation marks restrict it here to the images, the visual moment, against "the appearances" in which "we find" both moments. The same sentence uses the word in two senses: the *K* and *f* are "two quite differently functioning moments" "in the appearances" (*in den Erscheinungen*), and then the *f* alone are "the 'appearances'". The quotation marks mark the narrowing.

The relation is fixed by excluding two alternatives. It is not the unity the *f* have among themselves: "the consciousness of unity goes only through the *f*". It is not "a mere togetherness" (*ein bloßes Zusammen*) either. What remains is a temporal conditional: "when *K*₀ passes into *K*₁, *f*₀ passes expectantly into *f*₁". The adverb "expectantly" (*erwartungsmäßig*) places the expectation in the image's transition, not in the kinaesthetic one. The *K* do not expect; the *f* expect under *K*. The next paragraph turns the conditional into a law of correlation: once *f*ₐ is given with *K*ₐ, "to every determinate change Δ*K*ₐ there belongs in temporal coincidence a determinate course of fulfilment Δ*f*ₐ, and to the mere duration of *K*ₐ the same duration of *f*ₐ" (Hua XVI 182). The second clause is §50's "constant" kinaesthesis in formal dress (row 22): rest of the *K* goes with rest of the image.

The passage's account of the *f* still uses the language of pointing that Husserl later judged unsatisfactory. The note on §30 asks that "the 'intentions' = pointings" be set aside (Beilage I, ad 102, 36 ff., Hua XVI 339), and here "points", in quotation marks, describes the images' unity. What survives the note is the asymmetry itself and the conditional. That conditional is what §54 states as motivation: the apprehension is fit to found the consciousness "if *K* were such and such, then the 'appearance' would be such and such" (Hua XVI 189, row 24), with "appearance" still in quotation marks. And at the end of the course it becomes the definition of real possibility: "if the appearance *E* occurs under the circumstances *K*, then to the *K*-course *K⌢K′* 'belongs' an appearance-course *EE′*" (row 34). The pair in quotation marks of §51 is the course's last word on existence.

The term "circumstances" outgrew the kinaesthetic sensations it was coined for. Husserl's later note on a page of Chapter 13 says that "orally I emphasized here, as very often, that it is not or need not be a question of a sort of simple and specifically peculiar sensations, and moreover that other things also belong under the title circumstances, such as open eye!" (Beilage I, ad 254, 34–38, Hua XVI 341). The open eye is not a sensation that runs off; it is a condition under which images are given at all. The function §51 assigns to the *K*, that of the term on which appearances depend without itself appearing, is what "circumstances" names. Which data or states fill the role is a further question, and the note answers it more broadly than §46 did.

## §54, Hua XVI 189: the halo of quasi-intentions

Reads [§54.9–12](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-consciousness-of-unity-in-the-sequences-of-images-and-in-the-kinaesthetic-sequences).

Each phase of a perception has, besides its actual "intention toward" the next image, a halo (*Hof*) of quasi-intentions: the intentions that would be motivated if the kinaesthetic circumstances took another direction. They are not actual intentions, but they are not nothing. They are melted into the actual intention and colour its character, and by them the appearance is the appearance of a thing. The thing is then the identical of every line of appearances that any kinaesthetic course could bring about, given in each phase of one line.

> This series of fulfilment, the actual one, is however only one among a manifold of possibilities, and this naturally comes into consideration not as an objective fact but phenomenologically, through the fact that every phase of apprehension contains in its essence the relation to all possibilities. The respective actual *b*-phase contains, besides the "intention toward" that fulfils *a parte ante* and itself soon fulfils itself *a parte post* in the continuity of the flow (with its linear direction), a halo of *quasi*-intentions. Under the given *K*-circumstances, with the motivations attached to the course of precisely these *K*, these are not "intentions toward" and not fulfilling and fulfilments. They are now only possible intentions, which means: if the *K* took another direction of change, then such and such new intentions would necessarily be motivated and actually present. Now, however, with the given direction of course of the *K*, they are not; in their place, however, corresponding moments of apprehension, which we there call *quasi*-intentions, are melted into the actual *b*-intentions and colour their character. […] Image points to changing image in a determinate line; but the thing is not merely the identical of this line (before, the line of non-change, here that of determinately characterized change), but it is the identical of this and at the same time of all possible lines, all possible image-continuities under all possible *K*-circumstances.

German:

> Diese Erfüllungsreihe, die aktuelle, ist aber nur eine unter einer Mannigfaltigkeit von Möglichkeiten, und dieses kommt natürlich nicht als objektive Tatsache in Betracht, sondern phänomenologisch dadurch, daß jede Auffassungsphase in ihrem Wesen die Beziehung zu allen Möglichkeiten enthält. Die jeweilige aktuelle *b*-Phase enthält neben der *a parte ante* erfüllenden und sich selbst *a parte post* in der Stetigkeit des Flusses alsbald erfüllenden ,,Intention auf“ (mit ihrer linearen Richtung) noch einen Hof von *quasi*-Intentionen. Diese sind unter den gegebenen *K*-Umständen bei den im Ablauf gerade dieser *K* anhängenden Motivationen nicht ,,Intentionen auf“ und nicht erfüllend und Erfüllungen. Sie sind jetzt nur mögliche Intentionen, das heißt aber: Wenn die *K* andere Richtung der Veränderung einschlügen, dann wären notwendig die und die neuen Intentionen motiviert und aktuell vorhanden. Jetzt aber, bei der gegebenen Ablaufrichtung der *K* sind sie es nicht, an ihrer Stelle aber <sind> ihnen entsprechende Auffassungsmomente, die wir da *quasi*-Intentionen nennen, den aktuellen *b*-Intentionen eingeschmolzen und ihren Charakter färbend. […] Bild weist auf sich änderndes Bild hin in bestimmter Linie; das Ding ist aber nicht bloß das Identische dieser Linie (vorhin der Unveränderung, hier der bestimmten charakterisierten Veränderung), sondern es ist das Identische dieser und zugleich aller möglichen Linien, aller möglichen Bildkontinuitäten unter allen möglichen K-Umständen.

*Note on the text.* *b* is Husserl's letter for the image (*Bild*) in this section; the commentary writes *i*. The angle brackets around ‹sind› [are] mark an editor's addition.

The passage begins by saying where the possibilities are. That the actual series is "only one among a manifold of possibilities" "comes into consideration not as an objective fact but phenomenologically", because "every phase of apprehension contains in its essence the relation to all possibilities". The contrast protects the halo from a misreading: the other possible series are not facts about the world that a theorist adds to the description. They are found in the phase itself, as a character of its apprehension.

The halo is defined by negation, then by a conditional, then by a metaphor. Negation: the quasi-intentions "are not 'intentions toward' and not fulfilling and fulfilments". Conditional: they are "only possible intentions, which means: if the *K* took another direction of change, then such and such new intentions would necessarily be motivated and actually present". The subjunctives *einschlügen* and *wären* (took, would be) carry the definition. A quasi-intention is the present trace of a counterfactual, with "necessarily" fixing the motivation between other *K* and other intentions. Metaphor: in place of the absent intentions, corresponding moments of apprehension "are melted into" (*eingeschmolzen*) the actual intentions "and colour their character" (*ihren Charakter färbend*). This last step is what the conditional alone could not give. A counterfactual is not an experience; a colouring is. The halo is how the unrealized possibilities are lived in the actual phase, and the prefix *quasi-*, which the course has used since §34 for movements in the field that are not real movements, marks intentions that are as-if intentions.

The noun "halo" (*Hof*) means a courtyard, the space around a building, and the luminous ring around the moon. The second paragraph uses it spatially: "surrounded by the halo of quasi-intentions" there is "a line of actual givenness (proper perception)" (Hua XVI 189). The image is a line drawn through a field. The actual course of perception is one line; the halo is the surrounding area of lines not taken. That is why the thing can be "the identical of this and at the same time of all possible lines". §30 defined the thing as the identical in the systematic unity of "adumbrations or possibilities of adumbration" (row 15). §54 gives the possibilities their order: they are the lines that different *K*-courses would motivate, and their system is the manifold of kinaesthetic circumstances. The commentary calls §54 the theoretical summit of the kinaesthetic analysis (§54.16). The wording shows why: the conditional of §51 has become a structure of every phase.

The same paragraph states the relation to the thing through §11's word family. The apprehension "has in its essence the characterization that makes it fit (*geschickt macht*) to enter into other series of fulfilment and to found the consciousness 'if *K* were such and such, then the "appearance" would be such and such'" (Hua XVI 189). In §11 a perception's relation to its object was its fittingness to found identity-consciousness with other perceptions (row 6). In §54 the fittingness is to enter other motivated series. The ideal classes of possible perceptions have become the halo, and the timeless sorting of §11 has become a counterfactual tied to circumstances. And the givenness of the thing is then stated as openness: in every phase the thing is given, but "it belongs to the essence of this givenness that it leaves infinitely many possibilities of new givenness open in a determinate way as motivated possibilities" (Hua XVI 189). "Motivated possibilities" (*motivierte Möglichkeiten*) is the phrase that §62 applies to the being of unperceived parts (row 26) and §84 to real possibility (row 34).

The word returns in Section V in the plural form of the same thought: each *K* is "surrounded by a halo of apprehension-colourings, of *quasi*-intentions", and the living intention of the appearance is surrounded "by a dependent halo of *quasi*-intentions, which first give the appearance its determinate character, as appearance of the thing" (Hua XVI 191). In *Ideas I* the word moves elsewhere. "Every thing-perception has such a halo of background intuitions" (*einen Hof von Hintergrundanschauungen*, §35, Hua III/1 72): the objects co-perceived but not attended to. The 1913 halo is a halo of actual but inattentive intuitions, the 1907 one a halo of possible intentions. The noun keeps the image of a surround; what surrounds the actual has changed from the motivated possible to the unattended present.

## §61, Hua XVI 217–218: the held-fast identity of the thing as the identity of its space

Reads [§61.8–11](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#preliminary-indication-of-the-constitution-of-space-space-as-the-ordered-context-of-the-thing).

Space, in §61, is the fixed order in which things are held fast. Each thing, once constituted in the field, is retained as identical with every part and point of its filled space; things never seen together are related through fixed orders that run through mediating things; and the unity of perception that joins them is a unitary course of perception, not one image. The identity of the thing and the identity of its space are one identity. The word that carries the passage is "fixed" (*fest*), with its verb "to hold fast" (*festhalten*).

> But since the field through its inner arrangement prescribes a fixed order to all images, and since the positing of unity follows the continuity in the modification of the single images and of their mutual orientations, there grows the consciousness of the fixedly ordered manifold of things and finally of the world. The held-fast identity of the thing is held-fast identity of its space, filled in such and such a way. In this identity every piece, however small, and every boundary of this piece, and every point in its relative location, is posited as identical and intentionally held fast as identical, and likewise every orientation of the thing to another thing visible together with it. The relation of the thing to things that do not come to presentation in one field with it is naturally only mediately determined. It is a fixed one, because fixed connections of order run from the thing through mediating things to the other thing. The absolute identity and immovability of the mediating orders means absolute identity, immovability of the relation of order between distant things that cannot be seen together. Every thing can indeed enter with every other into the unity of perception, and that belongs to the essence of all thinghood, without that it would have no sense; but the unity of perception is not unity of presentation in one point of time and one image-field, but a unitary course of perception that brings the one thing to appearance and, in fixed sequence according to order, a series of mediating things that finally terminate in the second thing.

German:

> Indem aber das Feld durch seine innere Anordnung allen Bildern eine feste Ordnung vorschreibt und indem die Einheitssetzung der Kontinuität in der Abwandlung der einzelnen Bilder und ihrer wechselseitigen Orientierungen folgt, erwächst das Bewußtsein der fest geordneten Dingmannigfaltigkeit und schließlich der Welt. Die festgehaltene Identität des Dinges ist festgehaltene Identität seines so und so erfüllten Raumes. In dieser Identität ist jedes noch so kleine Stück und jede Grenze dieses Stückes und jeder Punkt in seiner relativen Lage als Identisches gesetzt und als Identisches intentional festgehalten und ebenso jede Orientierung des Dinges zu einem anderen mit ihm zusammen sichtbaren Ding. Die Relation des Dinges zu Dingen, die nicht in einem Feld mit ihm zur Darstellung kommen, ist natürlich nur mittelbar bestimmte. Sie ist eine feste, weil feste Ordnungszusammenhänge von dem Ding durch vermittelnde Dinge zu dem anderen Ding laufen. Die absolute Identität und Unverrückbarkeit der vermittelnden Ordnungen bedeutet absolute Identität, Unverrückbarkeit der Ordnungsbeziehung zwischen entfernten, zusammen nicht zu schauenden Dingen. In die Einheit der Wahrnehmung kann jedes Ding mit jedem zwar treten, und das gehört zum Wesen aller Dinglichkeit, ohne das hätte sie keinen Sinn, aber die Einheit der Wahrnehmung ist nicht Einheit der Darstellung in einem Zeitpunkt und einem Bildfeld, sondern einheitlicher Wahrnehmungsverlauf, der das eine Ding zur Erscheinung bringt und in festem Ablauf der Ordnung nach eine Reihe von vermittelnden Dingen, die schließlich in dem zweiten Ding terminieren.

*Note on the text.* Husserl's critical notes say of p. 217, 30 ff., where this passage begins: "Supplement. Belonged to the earlier lecture, but was no longer read" (*Beilage. Gehörte zur früheren Vorlesung, wurde aber nicht mehr gelesen*; Beilage I, Hua XVI 340). The lecture that opens §61 had already summarized the previous one ("I sent ahead a general consideration"); the passage seems to be a leaf written for that earlier lecture and not delivered. How far the leaf extends the file does not show. The editor's footnote marker at "since" (*Indem*), which points to the note, is left out. The section title, "Space as the ordered context of things", is the editor's: the editor's introduction says that the headings are his, sometimes taken from Edith Stein's marginal titles ("Einleitung des Herausgebers" [editor's introduction], Hua XVI).

The first sentence gives the order a source and a direction. The field "through its inner arrangement prescribes a fixed order to all images": the visual field is already ordered, as the oculomotor field of §57 was. The positing of unity "follows the continuity in the modification" of images and their orientations. Out of the two "grows" (*erwächst*) the consciousness of "the fixedly ordered manifold of things and finally of the world". The verb is organic and the order cumulative: field, things, world. The world is the last term in a growth from the field, not a frame given in advance, and that is the course's answer to §14's form of intuition (row 8).

The second sentence states the identity thesis with a repetition: "the held-fast identity of the thing is held-fast identity of its space". The participle *festgehalten* (held fast) appears twice in the predicate and again in the next sentence, "intentionally held fast as identical". The thing's identity is not found; it is held, and what is held is the identity of the filled space, down to "every piece, however small", "every boundary", "every point in its relative location". The list carries §35's infinite divisibility into the object: the thing is identical as a continuum. And the copula makes thing and space one. The thing does not occupy a space that has an identity of its own; its identity is the identity of its filled space.

The mediacy argument then replaces simultaneity by order. A thing's relation to things "that do not come to presentation in one field with it" is "naturally only mediately determined", and yet "it is a fixed one, because fixed connections of order run" (*weil feste Ordnungszusammenhänge … laufen*) through mediating things. The "because" grounds fixity on fixity. The inference is valid only if the mediating orders are themselves fixed, and the next sentence asserts that they are, in the strongest terms: "absolute identity and immovability" (*Unverrückbarkeit*). The noun comes from *verrücken*, to shift a piece of furniture out of place. What it claims is that the order of things cannot be rearranged by the course of perception that traverses it. The passage does not argue for this immovability. It is the content of the thing-apprehension, the "holding fast" of the second sentence, applied to relations.

The last sentence states the consequence for the unity of perception, and its scope of negation is precise. Every thing can enter with every other into the unity of perception, "and that belongs to the essence of all thinghood, without that it would have no sense". But that unity "is not unity of presentation in one point of time and one image-field", "but a unitary course of perception" that runs from one thing through mediating things "that finally terminate in the second thing". What §33's infinite intellect wanted, a field in which all things are given at once, is replaced by a course. Space is the order of such possible courses. The sentence after the passage says so: to thing-constitution belongs "the evidence of the possibility" "with a determinate modification of the circumstances, of receiving a determinate series of images" (Hua XVI 218). The space of things is the system of what could be seen under which circumstances.

The passage thus redeems §14 and §35 in Section V's terms. §14 said that space is the form of thinghood; §61 shows it as the held-fast identity of filled space joined by fixed orders. §35 said that infinity must be presented through "the serial form of the arrangement of operations"; §61's "unitary course of perception" is that serial form, with the operations now named as kinaesthetic modifications. §62 then applies the thesis to the unperceived parts of a thing (row 26). That the passage was, by Husserl's note, not read in the lecture hall does not change what it states. It does mean that the course as delivered reached this thesis through the summary at the opening of §61 and the yew-alley of §62, not through these sentences.

## §62, Hua XVI 223: the being of the unperceived points to motivated courses

Reads [§62.10](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-new-entrance-of-images-into-the-field-elucidation-through-the-example-of-the-perception-of-a-yew-alley).

The parts of an object not now perceived are not thereby nothing. Their being "points to" possible perceptions, and specifically to motivated, firmly ordered courses of perception that would lead from the perceived parts to them under the corresponding kinaesthetic courses. §62 states this as the course's first thesis about the being of the unperceived, and it states it with two verbs that relate being to possibility without equating them.

> The determinacy of the order of space and the determinacy in the order of visibility and of the respective order of actual becoming-visible belong essentially together. And the being of parts of the object that are not perceived points to possible, and indeed motivated, courses of order of the confirming perceptions and in them of their presentations, which produce connections between those non-perceived parts and the actually perceived ones. To the essence of what exists at once and enduringly with the actually perceived there belongs the possibility of unitary and firmly ordered perceptions that lead over to it in determinate belonging to kinaesthetic courses that are to lead over.

German:

> Die Bestimmtheit der Raumordnung und die Bestimmtheit in der Ordnung der Sichtbarkeit und der jeweiligen Ordnung des aktuellen Sichtbarwerdens gehören wesentlich zusammen. Und das Sein von Objektteilen, die nicht wahrgenommen werden, weist hin auf mögliche, und zwar motivierte Ordnungsverläufe der ausweisenden Wahrnehmungen und darin ihrer Darstellungen, die Zusammenhänge herstellen zwischen jenen nicht wahrgenommenen und den aktuell wahrgenommenen Teilen. Zum Wesen des mit dem aktuell Wahrgenommenen zugleich und dauernd Seienden gehört die Möglichkeit einheitlicher und festgeordneter Wahrnehmungen, die zu jenem überführen in bestimmter Zugehörigkeit zu überführenden kinästhetischen Verläufen.

The first sentence joins two orders that the yew-alley has just shown running together. As I walk, tree *a* leaves the field and *f* enters; the ray through *a* "falls into the empty, but its intention is not lost, it remains melted into the new total appearance", and the emptying is "a motivated one" (Hua XVI 223). The order of space and the order of visibility "belong essentially together" (*gehören wesentlich zusammen*). The phrase is weaker than an identity. Spatial order is not the order of becoming visible, but neither can be determinate without the other.

The thesis sentence has the being of the unperceived as its subject and "points to" (*weist hin auf*) as its verb. The verb is the course's word for the intention of an appearance toward the next, used since §30 and still in §51. Here it is applied to being. The being of unperceived parts behaves like an intention: it refers beyond itself to perceptions that would confirm it. The qualifiers narrow what it refers to: "possible", "and indeed motivated" (*und zwar motivierte*), "courses of order" (*Ordnungsverläufe*), "of confirming perceptions" (*der ausweisenden Wahrnehmungen*). "Motivated" excludes arbitrary possibilities, the logically possible perceptions of anything; "of order" excludes isolated glimpses; "confirming" ties them to the identity of what is already held. The relative clause adds their function: they "produce connections" between the unperceived and the perceived parts.

The second sentence moves from reference to essence. "To the essence of what exists at once and enduringly (*zugleich und dauernd*) with the actually perceived there belongs the possibility" of such perceptions. "At once" makes the unperceived contemporary with the perceived; "enduringly" makes it persist when it is not seen. Neither is reduced to the possibility. The possibility "belongs to the essence" of the co-existing, which is a necessary condition, not a definition. The commentary reads the sentence as saying that the being of the unperceived "consists in" the motivated possibility of perceiving it, and calls this "constitutive verificationism" (§62.10). The two verbs of the source, "points to" and "belongs to the essence of", stop short of "consists in". The difference matters to the course's own ending, where real existence and real possibility are distinguished, not identified (rows 33–34). §62 states a thesis about what being involves; whether being is more than what it involves the passage leaves open (logged under Corrections in the key-passage list).

The final clause gives the possibility its circumstances. The perceptions lead over "in determinate belonging to kinaesthetic courses that are to lead over" (*in bestimmter Zugehörigkeit zu überführenden kinästhetischen Verläufen*). The repeated verb *überführen* (to lead over, to convey) links the two series: the perceptions lead over to the unperceived because the kinaesthetic courses would lead over. This is §51's conditional and §54's halo extended beyond the field (rows 23–24). In §54 the quasi-intentions concerned sides of a thing in view. In §62 they concern parts and things out of view, and the motivated courses are long: the walk down the alley. §61 had stated the order of these courses as the fixed context of things (row 25). §62 adds that the being of what that order contains, when it is not seen, is the being of a terminus of motivated perception.

The thesis returns at the end of the course as the distinction between real and mere possibility. At §84 real possibilities (*reale Möglichkeiten*) are selected from "phantastic" ones by motivation under circumstances, and the formula is §62's in general form: "if the appearance *E* occurs under the circumstances *K*, then to the *K*-course *K⌢K′* 'belongs' an appearance-course *EE′*" (Hua XVI 292, row 34). *Ideas I* restates §62 almost in its terms and hardens its negative side. A transcendence "that lacked connection through harmonious motivational connections with my respective sphere of actual perception would be a completely groundless assumption; a transcendence that lacked such in principle, a nonsense" (§45, Hua III/1 96, read with CW2). In 1907 the unperceived points to motivated perceptions. In 1913 an unperceived that pointed to none would be nonsense. The later text makes explicit what the earlier verb implied, and it still does not say that being consists in possibility: it says that being without such connection makes no sense.

## §66, Hua XVI 232–233: expansion is to be called expansion

Reads [§66.1–6](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#correction-of-a-misunderstanding-expansion-and-remoteness-not-identical).

Expansion (*Dehnung*) is a modification of the image in the field: its growth or contraction. Remoteness (*Entfernung*) is an objective relation of the thing to the perceiver. Every expansion has the objective significance of a change of remoteness, but the two are not the same, and calling the image-modification "remoteness" turned a definition into a false assertion. §66 corrects this at the opening of a lecture, and the correction is about words. It keeps expansion free to be what §§67–71 need, the phenomenological datum from which depth is constituted.

> I have to begin today by correcting myself. My account toward the end of the last lecture contained, when the modifications of the image-field corresponding to remoteness and approach were under discussion, an unsuitable terminology, which had the tendency to turn an assertion that had sense only as a definition into a false assertion. Every image-expansion has objectively the significance of a change of remoteness, and I then called the modification of image-expansion itself modification of remoteness. I then called mere remoteness that expansion which takes place without rotation in the field, without any change of orientation at all, and specifically the uniform expansion, since it gives every image-part the same modification of remoteness, that is, of expansion, and which is thus characterized also by modifying only the image-size while preserving the full similarity of the figure. But it turns out, although it is correct that modifications of remoteness go hand in hand throughout with modifications of expansion, that it is not permissible simply to equate expansion and remoteness terminologically. For what we objectively call mere remoteness is never and nowhere a uniform expansion.
>
> […] Accordingly we call expansion precisely expansion, and guard against speaking of modification of remoteness instead of expansion. How the one stands to the other will become clear in the further course.

German:

> Ich habe heute damit zu beginnen, mich selbst zu korrigieren. Meine Darstellung gegen Schluß der letzten Vorlesung enthielt, als von den Modifikationen des Bildfeldes die Rede war, die der Entfernung und Annäherung entsprechen, eine unpassende Terminologie, die die Tendenz hatte, eine Behauptung, die nur als Definition Sinn hatte, in eine falsche Behauptung zu verwandeln. Jede Bilddehnung hat objektiv die Bedeutung einer Entfernungsänderung, und ich nannte nun die Modifikation der Bilddehnung selbst Modifikation der Entfernung. Ich nannte dann diejenige Dehnung bloße Entfernung, die ohne Drehung im Feld, überhaupt ohne Änderung der Orientierung statthat, und speziell die gleichmäßige Dehnung, da sie jedem Bildteil die gleiche Entfernungsmodifikation, das ist Dehnungsmodifikation erteilt, die also dadurch auch charakterisiert ist, daß sie nur die Bildgröße bei Erhaltung der vollen Ähnlichkeit der Figur modifiziert. Aber es zeigt sich dabei, obschon es richtig ist, daß mit Dehnungsmodifikationen durchaus Entfernungsmodifikationen Hand in Hand gehen, daß es doch nicht statthaft ist, einfach Dehnung und Entfernung terminologisch gleichzustellen. Denn was wir objektiv bloße Entfernung nennen, ist nie und nirgends eine gleichmäßige Dehnung.
>
> […] Demnach nennen wir Dehnung eben Dehnung und hüten uns davor, statt von Dehnung von Entfernungsmodifikation zu sprechen. Wie eins zum anderen steht, wird sich schon im weiteren Gang klarstellen.

*Note on the text.* The editor records the marginal date "22.7.07" and the beginning of a new lecture at "today" (*heute*); the footnote marker is left out. The second extract closes the paragraph on Hua XVI 233.

The diagnosis is exact about what went wrong. The terminology "had the tendency to turn an assertion that had sense only as a definition into a false assertion". In the previous lecture, "mere remoteness" named a kind of image-modification: expansion without rotation, uniform, preserving "the full similarity of the figure". As a definition of a term for image-modifications this was harmless. But "remoteness" also names an objective relation, and in that sense the definition became a claim: that receding from an object is a uniform expansion of its image. That claim is false. The tendency lay in the word, which carried its objective sense into a definition meant to be phenomenological. Husserl's later note on the earlier page is harder on himself: "False! and unfortunately delivered, but corrected in the next lecture" (*Falsch! und leider vorgetragen, aber in der nächsten Vorlesung verbessert*, Beilage I, ad 229, 34 ff., Hua XVI 341).

The passage keeps the true part and names the false one. "It is correct" that modifications of remoteness "go hand in hand throughout" with modifications of expansion; "it is not permissible simply to equate" them "terminologically". The adverb "simply" (*einfach*) and "terminologically" restrict the error to the naming. Then the counterexample, introduced as a ground: "For what we objectively call mere remoteness is never and nowhere a uniform expansion." The next paragraph gives the alley, whose image as one approaches "obviously" does not expand evenly in all its parts, and then an a priori argument: uniform expansion cannot occur in the manifold of rest, since then all points of the object "would always appear equally far from the reference point of remoteness, the imaginary ego-point, which is however a determinate point of objective space, and that is obviously impossible" (Hua XVI 233). A second later note qualifies the earlier text again: "That is false. Homogeneous expansion would occur only if a sum of objects equidistant from me receded from me or approached me equally" (Beilage I, ad 230, 6–12, Hua XVI 341). The note does not contradict §66. It states the one case in which the earlier description would hold, and so confirms that it does not hold of receding in general.

The resolution is a tautology used as a rule: "Accordingly we call expansion precisely expansion" (*Demnach nennen wir Dehnung eben Dehnung*). The sentence makes no claim about things. It fixes a word to a datum and forbids a substitution, "we guard against speaking of modification of remoteness instead of expansion". What the relation between the two is "will become clear in the further course". The tautology is the method of the section: by refusing the objective name, Husserl keeps the phenomenological datum available for the constitution of the objective relation. If expansion were already remoteness, depth would be presupposed in the analysis that is to constitute it.

The correction prepares the next sections' central claim. In §67 expansion is what brings thinghood "to a higher level, to a new dimension"; displacement and rotation give "no full things", and "only when expansion is added do we have the full presentational material capable of presenting space" (row 28). §68 then asks whether expansion is not "a limitless manifold, so to speak an *ἄπειρον* [boundless]". §35 had foreseen the need: "an analogue of 'approach and recession'" and "an expansion of adumbration, so to speak" (row 17). The word that §35 introduced with a hedge becomes in §66 a term held against its objective neighbour, and in §71 "the constitutive function of expansion (approach and recession)" names in its parenthesis the objective relation that expansion constitutes and does not denote.

The commentary describes the self-correction accurately (§66.1–6). Two things can be added from the source. The correction is aimed at a mechanism, a word that turns a definition into an assertion, and that mechanism is the one §1 objected to in "theory" and §14 in "form of intuition": a term whose received sense commits the speaker to a claim. And the correction did not settle the matter for Husserl, whose later notes mark the earlier pages false twice and add the condition under which uniform expansion would occur.

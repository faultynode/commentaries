# Husserl, *Thing and Space*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md) works through the 1907 lectures section by section. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding goes in the rest of the course.

The main commentary is keyed to Richard Rojcewicz's English translation, *Thing and Space: Lectures of 1907*, which is not in the corpus. The readings quote the German original instead, Husserliana XVI, *Ding und Raum. Vorlesungen 1907*, edited by Ulrich Claesges (1973), from the converted file `sources/husserl/husserl-hua-16-ding-und-raum-vorlesungen-1907.md` in `faultynode/sources`. The § numbers are the same in both editions. Passages are cited by § and Hua XVI page, and unit references such as "§11.3" name the third paragraph of the commentary under §11. All English renderings of the German are my own working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows. They are not Rojcewicz's, and where a reading questions the commentary's English, it cannot tell whether the commentary or the translator is responsible.

The file has limits that bear on more than one reading. Its page numbers come from marker comments, which can fall in mid-paragraph, so a page can be one out; the marker for p. 64 reads "6". Several stretches are garbled or invented and are never quoted: a fragment in §1 (p. 5), the opening of §6 (p. 17), the opening of §14 (pp. 42–43), §16 pp. 51–52, most of §40 (pp. 139–141, where the file also lacks the § heading), §69 pp. 241–242, and a block of invented English mathematics after §84. Where one of these touches a passage, the reading says so. The file keeps Husserl's italics, so emphasis can be argued from with care. Beilage I prints the critical notes Husserl later wrote on his own manuscript, keyed to page and line ("ad 51, 10 ff."). The main commentary never uses them; several readings do, because they record what Husserl came to think of the passage at hand.

## §1, Hua XVI 4: the refusal of "theory"

Reads [§1.3](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#the-world-of-natural-experience-and-the-world-of-scientific-theory).

For Husserl in 1907, a "theory" explains and grounds: it derives what is given from something else, as mathematics and natural science do. The lectures do something else. They clarify (*aufklären*) the essence of what experience gives, starting from its lowest forms, and nothing in that field needs explaining in the theorist's sense. The passage declines the obvious name for the course, "theory of experience", and the refusal is a statement of method before any method has been shown.

> I can now designate the theme of the following lectures in a few words. It concerns the foundational parts of a future phenomenology of experience: a clarification of the essence of experiential givenness, starting from the nearest and first beginnings and carried from there as deep and as far as possible, at least in its lower forms and levels. If one insisted on seeking support in the ways of speaking now current, one would have to speak of a theory of experience. But on the one hand I have, as already with the word epistemology [theory of knowledge], my misgivings about talk of theory, which fits mathematical and natural-scientific explanation and grounding, but certainly fits nowhere where there is nothing in this sense to explain and to ground; it does not fit even the experiencing of the morphologizing or typifying sciences, let alone that of phenomenology. Besides, since Cohen and the Marburg School applied the title "theory of experience" to Kant's critique of experiential knowledge, it has taken on a breadth that spans just about all the problems of pure theoretical reason; and we are not so bold here as to want to face and treat them all.

German:

> Ich kann nun mit wenigen Worten das Thema der nachfolgenden Vorlesungen bezeichnen. Es handelt sich um die grundlegenden Partien einer künftigen Phänomenologie der Erfahrung, um eine von den nächstliegenden und ersten Anfängen ausgehende und von da aus möglichst tief und weit geführte Aufklärung des Wesens der Erfahrungsgegebenheit, mindestens in ihren niederen Formen und Stufen. Wollte man durchaus Anlehnung an die jetzt üblichen Redeweisen suchen, so wäre von Theorie der Erfahrung zu sprechen. Doch habe ich einerseits, wie schon bei dem Worte Erkenntnistheorie, meine Bedenken gegen die Rede von Theorie, die auf mathematische und naturwissenschaftliche Erklärung und Begründung paßt, aber sicher nirgends paßt, wo es in diesem Sinne nichts zu erklären und zu begründen gilt, schon nicht auf das Erfahren der morphologisierenden oder typisierenden Wissenschaften, geschweige denn auf das der Phänomenologie. Zudem hat der Titel ,,Theorie der Erfahrung“, seit Cohen und die Marburger Schule ihn auf Kants Kritik der Erfahrungserkenntnis angewandt haben, eine Weite angenommen, die so ziemlich alle Probleme der reinen theoretischen Vernunft umspannt; und so kühn sind wir hier nicht, uns allen stellen und alle behandeln zu wollen.

The positive name comes before the refused one. The course is "a phenomenology of experience" and, in apposition, "a clarification (*Aufklärung*) of the essence of experiential givenness". "Theory of experience" enters only in a counterfactual: "if one insisted on seeking support in the ways of speaking now current" (*Wollte man durchaus Anlehnung … suchen*). The subjunctive keeps the phrase at arm's length before the objection to it is even stated. The two objections that follow are not of the same kind, and the commentary runs them together. The first is about the word "theory" (*die Rede von Theorie*): it belongs where something is "to be explained and grounded" (*zu erklären und zu begründen*), and the paired infinitives define theory by its task. The second is about a title already occupied, "theory of experience" in quotation marks, which the Marburg School stretched over the whole of Kant's critique. Only the first is a principle. The second is a matter of scale, and Husserl answers it with irony, "we are not so bold".

The scope of the first objection is wider than phenomenology. Theory does not fit "even the experiencing of the morphologizing or typifying sciences" (*schon nicht … geschweige denn*): the sciences that describe shapes and types, botany or anatomy, already do without explanation in the mathematical sense, "let alone" phenomenology. The graded construction places phenomenology at the far end of a line of descriptive disciplines, not outside science altogether. The parenthetical "as already with the word epistemology" (*wie schon bei dem Worte Erkenntnistheorie*) shows that the misgiving is old and is about a word. Husserl objects to "theory" even inside the name of the discipline he had been practising.

The same week's lectures state the principle. In the fourth of the five introductory lectures, published as *Die Idee der Phänomenologie*, phenomenology "proceeds by seeing, clarifying, determining sense and distinguishing sense" and "does not theorize and mathematize; that is, it carries out no explanations in the sense of deductive theory" (*Sie theoretisiert und mathematisiert nicht; sie vollzieht nämlich keine Erklärungen im Sinne der deduktiven Theorie*, Hua II 58–59). The §1 passage applies that rule to the course's own title. Two editorial facts in Hua XVI make the refusal concrete. The 1904/05 course had been announced as "Main Parts of a Phenomenology and Theory of Knowledge" (*Hauptstücke aus einer Phänomenologie und Theorie der Erkenntnis*, editor's note, Hua XVI 123); the 1907 course was announced as "Main Parts of Phenomenology and Critique of Reason" (*Hauptstücke aus der Phänomenologie und Kritik der Vernunft*, editor's introduction). Between the two, "theory" left the title and "critique" took its place. And the heading of Section I, "The Foundations of a Phenomenological Theory of Perception", stands in the editor's angle brackets: the word Husserl refuses in §1 was put back over his first Section by Claesges.

The paragraph that follows sets the refusal against the "very high goals" (*sehr hohe Ziele*) it postpones: the constitution of scientific reality by deduction, induction and logically mediated knowledge. The course will cultivate "the field of experiential phenomena", and "we will be doing well" if later workers can attempt the higher forms. The modesty is a claim about order, made good at the end of §1, where Husserl argues that one may begin "from below" (*von unten*) without fear of a phenomenological game indifferent to science, because all mediate grounding rests on immediate givenness. "Theory" is refused at the bottom of the edifice, not banished from it.

The word returns rarely in the lectures, and each return confirms the rule. At §14 the "image-theory" (*Bildertheorie*) is a theory in the refused sense, a construction that explains perception by a copy inside it, and it "has its absurdities" (Hua XVI 45). At §35 Husserl calls his result "a very good argument against the field-theory of givenness" (Hua XVI 124). At the end of §82 he admits that the "full theory" of judgments about movement and rest is not exhausted, because "only of this", the intuition of movement and rest, "have we attempted a clarification" (*Nur von dieser haben wir ja eine Aufklärung versucht*, Hua XVI 278). The last return pairs the two words §1 set apart. Theory names what lies beyond the course; clarification names what the course did.

## §3, Hua XVI 13–14: "nothing existing and yet a being"

Reads [§3.6–9](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#essential-cognition-of-perception-on-the-basis-of-phantasized-perceptions).

The examples on which the course rests are singular essences. A perception taken as an example, whether actually lived or only phantasized, is neither a psychological fact nor an abstraction. It is "this singular perception there", given absolutely, and its existence does not count. The formula for it is a paradox: "nothing existing and yet a being". The paradox licenses the course's method, because it lets a phantasized perception do everything an actual one does, and it fixes what the course's evidence is about.

> But even this positing of existence that takes place here in reflection, the positing as *cogitatio*, as actual, now existing perception, stays out of play. It has nothing to say here. Phantasy-presentifications of perceptions do us the same service, insofar as they set perceptions before our eyes and we then in fact see, and can grasp with evidence as a givenness, what we want to grasp, namely what the essence of perception is, what something like "perception" means. In the dignity of givenness as actual lived experience over against mere presentification we are not interested here, any more than we are interested at all in the constitution of the formations of consciousness that make up the evidence we are now carrying out step by step. In evidence, in the sphere of pure self-givenness, we investigate the essential peculiarities of perception. But what we investigate are precisely these, and not the evidence that itself makes up the investigating here. The investigation of the phenomenological constitution of these evidences belongs, of course, to another stratum of problems.
>
> I have also already pointed out earlier that the givens at our disposal in the examples are singular essences. If the existence of the example perceptions is out of play, even their existence as *cogitatio*, if merely phantasized perceptions, which wholly lack the positing of existence, fully suffice, then what is given here in the absolute sense is nothing existing and yet a being, namely in each case a singular essence (this singular perception there, whether it exist or not). To these singular givens the evident essential generalizations of a higher level relate; for example, we draw from them the universal essence "perception in general", which is singularized in them in such and such a way.

German:

> Aber selbst diese Existentialsetzung, die in der Reflexion hier statthat, die Setzung als *cogitatio*, als aktuelle, jetzt seiende Wahrnehmung bleibt außer Spiel. Sie hat hier nichts zu sagen. Phantasievergegenwärtigungen von Wahrnehmungen tun uns dieselben Dienste, sofern sie uns Wahrnehmungen vor Augen stellen und wir nun in der Tat das sehen, mit Evidenz als Gegebenheit fassen können, was wir fassen wollen, nämlich das Wesen von Wahrnehmung ist, was dergleichen wie „Wahrnehmung“ meint. Für die Dignität der Gegebenheit als aktuelles Erlebnis gegenüber der bloßen Vergegenwärtigung interessieren wir uns hier nicht, ebensowenig, als wir uns überhaupt für Konstitution der Bewußtseinsgestaltungen interessieren, die die Evidenz ausmachen, welche wir jetzt schrittweise vollziehen. In der Evidenz, in der Sphäre reiner Selbstgegebenheit erforschen wir die wesentlichen Eigentümlichkeiten der Wahrnehmung. Aber was wir erforschen, sind eben diese und nicht die Evidenz, die das Forschen hier selbst ausmacht. Die Untersuchung der phänomenologischen Konstitution dieser Evidenzen gehört natürlich einer anderen Problemschicht an.
>
> Ich habe auch schon früher darauf hingewiesen, daß die Gegebenheiten, über die wir in den Beispielen verfügen, singuläre Essenzen sind. Ist die Existenz der Beispielswahrnehmungen außer Spiel, selbst die Existenz als *cogitatio*, genügen vollgültig auch bloß phantasierte Wahrnehmungen, denen die Existenzsetzung gänzlich fehlt, so ist das hier im absoluten Sinn Gegebene nichts Existierendes und doch ein Seiendes, nämlich jeweils eine singuläre Essenz (diese singuläre Wahrnehmung da, mag sie existieren oder nicht). Auf diese singulären Gegebenheiten beziehen sich die evidenten Wesensverallgemeinerungen höherer Stufe; z.B. entnehmen wir ihnen das allgemeine Wesen „Wahrnehmung überhaupt'', das sich in ihnen so und so singularisiert.

*Note on the text.* The file has an editor's footnote marker after "the same service" (*dieselben Dienste*); the footnote refers the reader to two critical notes of Husserl's in Beilage I, discussed below. The marker is left out of the quotation.

The paradox depends on splitting two German words that ordinary usage keeps together. "Existing" (*Existierendes*) here means posited as actually there, whether as a physical thing or as a *cogitatio* lived now. "Being" (*Seiendes*) means whatever can be given and judged about. The conditional sentence builds the split in two steps: if even the existence of the example "as *cogitatio*" is out of play, and if phantasized perceptions "fully suffice" (*genügen vollgültig*), then what remains is a being without existence. The adverb carries weight. A phantasized perception is not a second-best example that will do for want of a real one. It is "fully valid", because existence was never part of what the analysis looks at. The first paragraph had already said the same of actual perception in reflection: its positing of existence "has nothing to say here" (*hat hier nichts zu sagen*).

The parenthesis gives the singular essence its form of words: "this singular perception there, whether it exist or not" (*diese singuläre Wahrnehmung da, mag sie existieren oder nicht*). The demonstrative "this … there" keeps the individual; the concessive subjunctive cancels its existence. A singular essence is thus not yet a universal. The universal "perception in general" is drawn from such givens at "a higher level" (*höherer Stufe*) and is "singularized in them". The course works on two levels of essence at once, and the commentary's gloss, that the method is what "later phenomenology calls free variation in phantasy" (§3.8), skips the lower one. Variation compares examples to reach the universal; the singular essence is what each example already is before any comparison.

The first paragraph also draws a line that Husserl later rejected. The course is interested neither in the "dignity" of actual over phantasized givenness nor in "the constitution of the formations of consciousness that make up the evidence"; that belongs "to another stratum of problems". Beilage I prints two notes on this page. On line 10 Husserl wrote "*Nota bene*, so already in 1907, but probably much earlier" (*Nota bene also schon 1907, aber wohl schon viel früher*), dating the method of phantasized examples back before the course. On lines 15–25, where the exclusion stands, he wrote "No! The whole further procedure consists precisely in that" (*Nein! Das ganze weitere Verfahren besteht gerade darin*), and pointed to "the correction in the next lecture" at Hua XVI 19 (Hua XVI 337). That page is the opening of §7, where the method of strata turns on the question the exclusion set aside: "how are evident assertions possible about an objectivity that is not actually given in the phenomenon?" (Hua XVI 20). The singular-essence thesis survives the note. The refusal to study how evidence is constituted does not, and by Husserl's own account the correction came in the very next lecture.

The thesis was stated a few days before in the introductory lectures. There an evident grasp of essence "points back to singular intuition", "but not therefore to singular perception", and "for the consideration of essence, perception and phantasy-presentation rank quite equally" (*Für die Wesensbetrachtung rangiert Wahrnehmung und Phantasievorstellung ganz gleich*, Hua II 69). The phantasy-tone "does *not* exist", and that fact "belongs to another consideration". What §3 adds is the ontological formula. Hua II says that existence is irrelevant; §3 says what is left when it is, a being that does not exist.

The course then uses the singular essence as its working unit. In §10 the perceptions of a house from inside and outside, considered "in phenomenological reduction or, as we can now also say, in self-posings, and indeed as singular essences" (*als singuläre Wesen*), each look different, "and yet we say, with a certain evidence, that they present the same house" (Hua XVI 26). The identity-consciousness that §§10–11 analyse joins singular essences, not lived acts, and in §11 Husserl imagines having "all singular perception-essences before our eyes" (*alle singulären Wahrnehmungsessenzen*, Hua XVI 31) in order to sort them into immanent and transcendent. The paradox of §3 is what lets that thought-experiment count as evidence: an infinite collection of perceptions, none of which need exist, is still a collection of beings.

## §5, Hua XVI 15–16: *Perzeption*, perception without taking-for-true

Reads [§5.8](../commentaries/husserl/husserl-thing-and-space-commentary.md#presence-in-the-flesh-and-belief-perception-perzeption-and-position-taking).

*Perzeption* is Husserl's name for perception with belief taken out: the phenomenon of an object standing there in the flesh, before any position is taken on whether it exists. The German word for perception, *Wahrnehmung*, contains the word for "true" (*wahr*), and Husserl gets the restricted concept by taking that word apart. What is left once the "true" is removed is the stratum that §§14–43 analyse as appearance.

> The concept of perception is often restricted in such a way that it excludes what is properly to be called taking-for-true (let alone actual true-taking), that is, excludes the character of belief, the character of standing there in a believed way. That has its advantages and disadvantages. In any case, the concept that is narrower in content (or wider in extension) needs a name that holds it fast. We will say *Perzeption*, and then speak, say, of perceptual belief (perception in the normal sense), perceptual disbelief, doubt and so on. Yet where these differences of the new characters, which we call differences of position-taking, are irrelevant, and where we can do without the distinction altogether, we will keep to speaking of perception, so that we leave it open whether we have mere *Perzeptionen* or *Perzeptionen* with position-takings and with other phenomenal characters indifferent to us. At bottom, then, it is *Perzeptionen* that are analysed here, but it is more agreeable to use the familiar German expression, provided only that care is taken that its ambiguities do not mislead.

German:

> Öfters wird der Begriff der Wahrnehmung so beschränkt, daß er das eigentlich so zu nennende Für-wahr-Nehmen (geschweige denn das wirkliche Wahr-Nehmen) ausschließt, nämlich ausschließt den Charakter des Glaubens, den Charakter des in glaubhafter Weise Dastehens. Das hat seine Vorteile und Nachteile. Jedenfalls bedarf es für den inhaltlich beschränkteren (bzw. den umfangsweiteren) Begriff einer festhaltenden Benennung. Wir werden sagen Perzeption und dann etwa sprechen von perzeptivem Glauben (Wahrnehmung im normalen Sinn), perzeptivem Unglauben, Zweifel usw. Doch werden wir, wo diese Unterschiede der neuen Charaktere, die wir als Unterschiede der Stellungnahme bezeichnen, irrelevant sind und es überhaupt ohne Scheiden geht, dabei bleiben, von Wahrnehmung zu sprechen, so daß wir es offen lassen, ob wir bloße Perzeptionen haben oder Perzeptionen mit Stellungnahmen und mit sonstigen für uns gleichgültigen phänomenalen Charakteren. Im Grunde genommen werden also hierbei die Perzeptionen analysiert, aber es ist angenehmer, den vertrauten deutschen Ausdruck zu gebrauchen, wofern nur Sorge getragen ist, daß seine Vieldeutigkeiten nicht beirren.

The paragraph before prepares the split with a rhyme. §5 opens by setting "in the flesh" (*leibhaft*) against "credible" (*glaubhaft*), two adjectives with one suffix. In ordinary perception the two are "fused" (*verschmolzen*); the unmasked hallucination and the case of doubt pull them apart, and reduction then separates "presence in the flesh, which is fundamentally essential to perception as such", from "credibility, which can be added and can be missing" (Hua XVI 16). The suffix makes the two look like coordinate features. The argument makes them unequal: one belongs to the essence, the other is an addition.

The hyphens then do to the noun what the rhyme did to the adjectives. "Taking-for-true" (*Für-wahr-Nehmen*) is belief: to take something as true. "True-taking" (*Wahr-Nehmen*), "actual" (*wirklich*), is the stronger reading of the word, a taking of what is in fact true, a perception that succeeds. The parenthesis "let alone" (*geschweige denn*) ranks them. If the restricted concept leaves out belief, it leaves out success all the more. Neither hyphenated form is a word of ordinary German; they are the word *Wahrnehmung* read as a compound, and English "perception" has no part that means "true". The split also shows why the restriction is coherent. A concept that excludes belief excludes nothing that the phenomenon of presence needs, because the hallucination still presents its object in the flesh.

Husserl does not claim the restriction as his own. "The concept of perception is often restricted" (*Öfters wird … beschränkt*) reports a usage, and he weighs it coolly: it "has its advantages and disadvantages". What he adds is the name, a "holding-fast" one (*festhaltende Benennung*), and the parenthesis "narrower in content (or wider in extension)" states the logic of the abstraction: a concept with fewer marks covers more cases, hallucinations and doubted appearances included. The commentary has Husserl "propose" the Latin term for a concept of his own (§5.8). The source has him name a concept already in use.

The last sentences undo the terminology as soon as it is set up. Where position-taking does not matter, the course will "keep to speaking of perception", because the familiar German word is "more agreeable" (*angenehmer*). "At bottom", it is *Perzeptionen* that are analysed. The rest of the course follows this rule. *Wahrnehmung* stays the working word, and *Perzeption* appears where the difference from belief is the point, as in §15's mannequin, where "two *Perzeptionen* stand in conflict" (Hua XVI 46), or where the analysis is "referred to mere *Perzeption*" because position-taking is irrelevant to it.

The restriction has a limit that §9 sets at once. In absolutely giving perception, "we cannot speak of a *Perzeption* that is separable by itself and combined now with belief or disbelief, now with doubt": the having and the positing of the self-posing perception "cannot be separated analytically" (Hua XVI 25). *Perzeption* is therefore a concept of outer, presenting perception only. Only where an object is presented and not itself given can presence in the flesh survive the loss of belief. Husserl's later note on §9 strikes at the other half of the rhyme: "No! In the flesh = impressional; that makes no sense here" (*Nein! leibhaft = impressional; das gibt hier keinen Sinn*, Beilage I, ad 23, 9–10, Hua XVI 338). The note narrows *leibhaft* to a mode of impression, and so takes from §5 the assumption that presence in the flesh is one character common to every perception.

§15 then ties *Perzeption* to the course's two governing concepts. *Perzeption* is perception "in its ordinary word-sense", "only that we do not take position-taking in with it"; it is "contents of apprehension taken in their apprehension"; and since a hallucination also has an appearance, "appearance is evidently nothing other than *Perzeption*" (*Also ist Erscheinung offenbar nichts anderes als Perzeption*, Hua XVI 49). The pun of §5 thus founds the course's subject matter. Everything Sections II to V say about appearances, sides and adumbrations is said of *Wahrnehmung* with its *wahr* set aside, and §43 has to bring belief-positing back explicitly (Hua XVI 151) before the analysis can speak of the thing's being.

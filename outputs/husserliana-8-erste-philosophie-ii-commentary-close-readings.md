# Husserl, *Erste Philosophie* (1923/24), Second Part: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the block of the [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md) that it extends. The German is quoted from the Husserliana edition of the second part of the lectures *Erste Philosophie* (Hua VIII, ed. Rudolf Boehm, 1959) in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-8-erste-philosophie-ii-theorie-der-phanomenologischen-reduktion.md`). The commentary has no § numbers and cites no pages; it proceeds lecture by lecture, and a unit there is a block under a lecture heading ("Lecture 31.17" is the seventeenth block under Lecture 31; Lecture 53 has two sub-headings, a and b). Headings here give the lecture and the Hua VIII page, taken from the page markers in the file. A marker can sit inside a long line, so a page can be off by one.

All English renderings of Husserl, and of the Greek and Latin he uses, are working renderings. They stay close to the German so that its structure shows, and they are not taken from a published translation.

The file is on the corpus's list of texts to check before quoting, and four cautions affect more than one reading. First, invented text was removed from it by hand, and the excision left invisible seams; one seam has lost real text. Hua VIII 157, the heading and first page of Lecture 51, is missing: the file breaks off in Lecture 50 and resumes on p. 158. No reading quotes or relies on that page. Second, Husserl's footnotes and the editor's notes are often printed far from their markers, and note numbers restart on each page; notes are matched to their markers by content, and each reading says where a note it quotes actually stands. Third, emphasis survives but unevenly. Italics are present throughout, but some lectures have hardly any, and Husserl's spaced type comes through as bold, as spaced letters, or as both. The readings argue from emphasis where the file has it, never from its absence, and never from which of the three forms a word takes. Fourth, many lectures are set as hard-wrapped print lines with the print's end-of-line hyphens, and the print's margin line numbers sometimes leak into the text. Quotations join words broken at a line end and drop a stray margin number; both are noted where they occur. Some instances of the Greek *epoché* and of one other term are LaTeX garble in the file, and are noted where a passage contains them.

## Lecture 28, Hua VIII 7: the ego as first summit

Reads [Lecture 28, block 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-28-the-idea-of-philosophy-in-history-and-the-motivational-situation-of-the-subject-beginning-to-philosophize).

The transcendental ego is the first theme of philosophical knowledge, but it is not where philosophy begins. Husserl separates two orders. In the order of the will, the philosophizing subject is its own theme from the first moment, since it begins by resolving to want absolutely justified knowledge. In the order of knowledge, the same subject becomes its theme only "in further consequence", when a method has taught it to apprehend itself as transcendental. The ego is first in rank and late in time: it is the result of the meditations, not their premise. Everything Section Two does to reach the ego depends on keeping these orders apart.

> If, accordingly, the philosophizing ego must become a theme of its will for itself, in order to be able to attain a philosophy, then it holds, but only in further consequence, that it must also become for itself its first theme of knowledge: namely, that on the basis of a certain methodical apperception it must grasp itself as transcendental or pure ego, and find in this ego the basic field of its theoretical work. "In further consequence", I said; for that no longer belongs itself to the very first beginning, but belongs already to the result-content of the meditations, as the first summit to which they lead.

German:

> Muß danach das philosophierende Ich für sich selbst zum Willensthema werden, um eine Philosophie erzielen zu können, so gilt es, aber erst in weiterer Folge, daß es für sich selbst auch zu seinem ersten Erkenntnisthema werden muß, nämlich daß es sich auf Grund einer gewissen methodischen Apperzeption als transzendentales oder reines Ich erfassen und in diesem dann das Grundfeld seiner theoretischen Arbeit finden muß. „In weiterer Folge” — sagte ich; denn das ist nicht mehr selbst zum allerersten Anfang gehörig, sondern gehört schon zum Ergebnisgehalt der Meditationen, als der erste Gipfelpunkt, zu dem sie führen.

The sentence pairs two compounds on one stem: "theme of the will" (*Willensthema*) and "theme of knowledge" (*Erkenntnisthema*). The same subject stands in both, and only the mode of its being a theme changes. The conditional carries the order. "If" the ego must be a theme of its will, "then it holds" that it must also be a theme of knowledge, and the inserted "but only in further consequence" (*aber erst in weiterer Folge*) splits the consequence from the antecedent in time. A reader who drops the insertion hears the Cartesian thesis that the ego is the first thing known. With it, the sentence qualifies what "first theme of knowledge" (*ersten Erkenntnisthema*) seems to say on its own: first in the system of knowledge, but reached second. The adjective "first" and the adverb "only" (*erst*) share a stem, and the sentence sets them a line apart.

Husserl then quotes himself. "'In further consequence', I said" stops the lecture to underline a phrase just spoken, and the gloss converts a temporal qualifier into a thesis about the work's structure. The ego belongs to the "result-content" (*Ergebnisgehalt*) of the meditations. The image he gives it is "the first summit to which they lead" (*der erste Gipfelpunkt, zu dem sie führen*). The image is chosen against the one he has just reported of Descartes. Two pages earlier, Descartes leads himself and the reader to the ego cogito "as the Archimedean point of philosophy and its method" (*als dem archimedischen Punkt*, Hua VIII 5). An Archimedean point is a fulcrum one stands on before lifting anything. A summit is reached by climbing, and "first" promises others beyond it. The two figures restate the hermeneutic verdict of the preceding page, that Descartes found the basic form of the beginning and misunderstood its sense: he took the summit for the fulcrum.

The paragraph before the passage gives the ground for the order. Philosophy cannot arise from naive cognition, only "from free self-reflections or free self-determinations" (*aus freien Selbstbesinnungen bzw. aus freien Selbstbestimmungen*) of the knower. The pairing of reflection with determination makes the will primary: the subject turns to itself first as one who wants something, and its knowledge of itself is a means to that wanting. The paragraph after the passage draws the consequence for the beginner's life. The resolution founds a "habitual form of life of the philosopher in the making" (*Lebensform des werdenden Philosophen*), and that form is "a life of knowledge in perfect and constant self-responsibility". Lectures 29 and 30 develop that form of life before any epoché is mentioned. The passage is the hinge on which the course postpones the ego: it lets Husserl spend two lectures on vocation and resolution without seeming to have strayed from first philosophy.

The order holds through the course, and it holds in an unexpected way. When the ego does appear, in Lecture 38, it is not reached as the planned goal of the critique of the world. Husserl's review says the apodictic critique of mundane experience "subsequently took on" a second function besides its own (*noch eine sehr folgenreiche zweite Funktion nachträglich angenommen*, Hua VIII 77): through its result it made visible "the transcendental subjectivity hidden from me before". The ego "became visible in a turn of theoretical interest" (*wurde in einer Wendung des theoretischen Interesses nun sichtlich*, Hua VIII 76), not as what survived the critique. "Subsequently" redeems "in further consequence" more strictly than Lecture 28 promised. The ego is a result, but not the result the critique was aimed at; the critique was aimed at the world, and the ego came into view as a by-product of its failure. The Archimedean point also returns, now in Husserl's own voice. At the end of Lecture 36, after the negative result of the critique of experience, the beginner says "I am still searching for the Archimedean point on which I can rely absolutely" (*Ich suche noch nach dem archimedischen Punkt*, Hua VIII 69). Husserl lets the beginner want Descartes' fulcrum. What the beginner gets, two lectures on, is a field "opened" as "the theme for a transcendental critique" that "I must first supply" (Hua VIII 76), a summit from which more climbing is visible.

The phrase "in further consequence" stays in Husserl's vocabulary as the mark of a derived stage. The science of origins of Lecture 31 is to explore what is ultimately original "and in further consequence teach us" how all knowledge can be brought to its final form (Hua VIII 29–30). In Lecture 37 the distinction between two senses of "I" brings "in further consequence" two senses of "my psychic life", "self-experience" and "self-knowledge" (Hua VIII 71). In each case the phrase marks an order of dependence, not of value. That is the sense Lecture 28 fixes for it by quoting it. The passage thus sets the terms on which the whole course can be read: the ego is the first theme of philosophy and the last thing the beginner reaches, and the gap between those two firsts is the theory of the reduction.

## Lecture 30, Hua VIII 19: primal founding as self-creation

Reads [Lecture 30, block 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-30-pure-cultural-disposition-in-general-and-the-primal-founding-of-a-philosophical-radicalism).

A primal founding (*Urstiftung*) is an act that institutes something lasting which did not exist before it. In Lecture 30 the thing instituted is the philosopher himself. Other vocations can grow from inclinations a person already has, and a later decision only ratifies them. The philosopher has no prior inclination to ratify: the resolution that makes him a philosopher is the first moment at which he exists as one. Husserl calls such a resolution an "original self-creation". The word enters the course hedged, and it leaves the course as a term for how any lasting sense arises in consciousness.

> With the philosopher the matter stands entirely otherwise. He *necessarily* needs a *resolution* of his own, one that first and originally creates him as a philosopher at all: a primal founding, so to speak, which is original self-creation. No one can drift into philosophy.

German:

> Ganz anders steht die Sache bei dem Philosophen. Er bedarf *notwendig* eines eigenen, ihn als Philosophen überhaupt erst und ursprünglich schaffenden *Entschlusses*, sozusagen einer Urstiftung, die ursprüngliche Selbstschöpfung ist. Niemand kann in die Philosophie hineingeraten.

The paragraph is three sentences long, and its last sentence takes its verb from the paragraph before. There, a young person's love of art can turn into practice and specialize in landscape painting, "and so he may drift unnoticed into the profession, without a solemn decision, so to speak" (*und so mag er unvermerkt in den Beruf hineingeraten, ohne eine sozusagen feierliche Entscheidung*). "No one can drift into philosophy" (*Niemand kann in die Philosophie hineingeraten*) negates that clause word for word. The commentary's "slip into" catches the sense; the repetition shows the logic. The painter's "may" (*mag*) becomes the philosopher's "cannot" (*kann … nicht*), and the decision the painter could do without becomes one the philosopher "*necessarily* needs".

The two italics divide the work. "Necessarily" (*notwendig*) claims that the difference is one of essence, not of biography: there could be no philosopher who drifted in. "Resolution" (*Entschlusses*) names what makes the difference. The participial phrase between them says what the resolution does, and it stacks three qualifiers on one verb: it creates him "as a philosopher at all", "first", and "originally" (*ihn als Philosophen überhaupt erst und ursprünglich schaffenden*). The painter's later choice of career, by contrast, had "the character of a mere confirmation" of a habitual will "that had already grown naturally" (*des ohnehin schon natürlich erwachsenen habituellen Lebens- und Tatwillens*). The opposition is between growing (*erwachsen*) and creating (*schaffen*). What grows has a subject before it; what is created in this sense does not, since the creator and the created are the same.

Then the word itself, and it is hedged. "So to speak" (*sozusagen*) introduces *Urstiftung* as a figure, the same particle the previous paragraph put before the painter's "solemn" decision. The relative clause that follows, "which is original self-creation" (*die ursprüngliche Selbstschöpfung ist*), defines the figure by a second coinage rather than by an analysis. The prefix "primal" (*Ur-*) and the adverb "originally" (*ursprünglich*) say the same thing twice: this founding is not the renewal of an earlier one. The paragraph does not say what a founding institutes, beyond the person. Lecture 28 had already used the verb for the result: the resolution founds "the habitual form of life of the philosopher in the making" (*die durch diesen Entschluß gestiftete habituelle Lebensform*, Hua VIII 7), and Lecture 29's title is "On the founding of the habitual form of life". What is founded is a habit of the will that persists after the act.

The next paragraph gives the reason, and the reason is narrower than the claim. "That is because" (*Das liegt daran*) the naivety of the love of knowledge "is a necessary first" (*ein notwendig Erstes ist*) and carries an imperfection hidden from itself, which scepticism first exposes. Philosophy, unlike art and positive science, begins by breaking with a naivety that every knower starts in; a break of that kind cannot happen unnoticed, so a philosopher cannot drift in. That shows why philosophy needs an explicit decision. It does not show that the decision creates its subject from nothing, since the one who decides is already a knower, already troubled by scepticism and already moved by the "pure love of knowledge". The phrase "self-creation" claims more than the argument supplies. What it does supply is a discontinuity in the will: the person exists before the decision, the philosopher does not.

Lecture 30 itself fills the decision out three pages later. It is "a great life-resolution of its own" (*eines eigenen großen Lebensentschlusses*): to take up the venture and stake one's life on it, to seek science in the radicalism of the ultimate "out of oneself and in association with the like-minded" (*aus sich heraus und im Verband mit Gleichgesinnten*, Hua VIII 22). The self-creation is solitary in its act and communal in its aim from the first statement of it.

The word then changes its subject. In Lecture 49, ideal objects such as the numbers produced by counting have "their primal founding" in acts, and they persist as "lasting precipitates of validity" (*bleibende Geltungsniederschläge*) that make up our "ideal 'worlds'" (Hua VIII 152). In Lecture 54, what I take as truly existing is said to adhere to the whole motivational nexus of my life, "and especially to the contents of sense and appearance that have grown up in my primal founding and become habitual" (*den darin in meiner Urstiftung erwachsenen und habituell werdenden Sinnes- und Erscheinungsgehalten*, Hua VIII 185). The structure of Lecture 30 is kept: an act institutes, and what it institutes lasts as a habit. But two things have shifted. The *sozusagen* is gone, and *Urstiftung* now names a moment in the genesis of any sense. And Lecture 54 joins founding to growing (*erwachsen*), the verb Lecture 30 set against it. Once the founding act is generalized from the will to constitution, the opposition between a life that grows and a life that creates itself no longer holds: what is founded grows, and the philosopher's resolution becomes the explicit, willed case of what every constituting life does.

The *Crisis* takes the term from the individual to history. Its §15 calls us heirs and co-bearers of the will that runs through modern philosophy, "and we are so from a primal founding which is, however, at the same time a re-founding and modification of the Greek primal founding" (*aus einer Urstiftung, die aber zugleich Nachstiftung und Abwandlung der griechischen Urstiftung ist*, Hua VI 72). Lecture 30's "original" self-creation allowed no such qualification: it was the act before which the philosopher was nothing. Read from the *Crisis*, the individual's resolution is itself a re-founding, the uptake of a will already instituted. The lecture's "in association with the like-minded" is the opening the later text widens. In 1923/24 the beginning philosopher still has to found himself; the *Crisis* makes his founding an inheritance.

## Lecture 31, Hua VIII 29–30: archaeology as the science of origins

Reads [Lecture 31, block 7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-31-natural-and-transcendental-apodictic-and-adequate-evidence).

"Archaeology" here means the science of origins, taken at its word: a logos of the *archai*, the sources from which every sense and every validity springs. Its object is not a past but something present in every act of knowing and hidden there, the "origin" concealed in the ordinary evidence of the positive sciences. Its distinctive evidence is what Husserl calls "transcendental clarity about origins", set against the natural evidence that does the work of knowing without seeing its own source. The word is an image for that relation: what an archaeology uncovers lies inside what everyone already has.

> So we gain the highest guiding goal-idea of all cognitive endeavours. At the same time, this idea, as it has grown up for us in evident motivation, by no means yet has the value of an explicit guiding conception of this science of what is original and from what is original. In other words, here at the beginning we do not yet dispose of a clear and distinct prototype of a philosophy, but only of the formal-general methodical guiding thought: that under this title a science is necessary which, as one truly to be called an "archaeology", is to explore systematically that ultimately original which encloses in itself all origins of being and of truth, and in further consequence is to teach us how, from this primal source of all opinions and validities, every cognition can be brought into the highest and final form of reason, that of absolute foundation and absolute justification, of the last conceivable clarification of sense and certification of right; into a form, then, in which it can be originally acquired with absolutely good conscience as finished, as not only valid but "final", and now set aside as settled once and for all. It thereby takes its place in the circle of fixed possessions of knowledge, which can then at any time be reactivated in their original-genuine shape and absolutely defended in their necessarily invariable finality; defended, then, not merely out of the naive clarity commonly called "evidence" (natural evidence, evidence of positivity), but out of the higher-level evidence of transcendental clarity about origins, in which the origin of the cognitive accomplishment hidden in the evidence of positivity, with its horizon of motivation that determines and limits its primal right, is uncovered and so understood from the origins.

German:

> So gewinnen wir die oberste leitende Zweckidee aller Erkenntnisbemühungen. Dabei hat diese Idee, so wie sie uns in evidenter Motivation erwachsen ist, keineswegs schon den Wert einer expliziten Leitvorstellung von dieser Wissenschaft vom Ursprünglichen und aus dem Ursprünglichen. M.a.W., wir verfügen hier am Anfang noch nicht über ein klares und deutliches Urbild einer Philosophie, sondern nur über den formal-allgemeinen methodischen Leitgedanken: daß unter diesem Titel eine Wissenschaft notwendig sei, die als eine wahrhaft zu nennende „Archäologie“ jenes Letztursprüngliche und alle Ursprünge des Seins und der Wahrheit in sich Beschließende systematisch erforschen und in weiterer Folge uns lehren soll, wie aus dieser Urquelle aller Meinungen und Geltungen jedwede Erkenntnis in die höchste und letzte Vernunftform gebracht werden kann, in die der absoluten Fundamentierung und der absoluten Rechtfertigung, der letzterdenklichen Aufklärung des Sinnes und Ausfertigung des Rechtes; in eine Form also, in der sie mit absolut gutem Gewissen als fertige, als nicht nur gültige, sondern „endgültige“ ursprünglich erworben und nun als ein für allemal erledigt beiseitegestellt werden kann. Sie gliedert sich damit ein in den Kreis der festen Erkenntnisbesitztümer, welche dann jederzeit wieder in der ursprüngsechten Gestalt reaktiviert und absolut vertreten werden können in ihrer notwendig invariablen Endgültigkeit; vertreten also nicht bloß aus der naiven Klarheit, die da gemeiniglich „Evidenz“ heißt (natürliche Evidenz, Evidenz der Positivität), sondern aus der höherstufigen Evidenz der transzendentalen Ursprungsklarheit, in welcher der in der Evidenz der Positivität verborgene Ursprung der Erkenntnisleistung mit ihrem urrechtbestimmenden und -begrenzenden Motivationshorizont aufgedeckt und so aus den Ursprüngen verstanden ist.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "ursprüngsechten", which is not a German word; the sense is plainly "ursprungsechten" (genuine to its origin), and the rendering above gives that sense. The reading does not rest on the word.

The paragraph translates its one foreign word into German before and after using it. Around "archaeology" stands a family of compounds in "primal" and "origin" (*Ur-*, *Ursprung*): the science "of what is original and from what is original" (*vom Ursprünglichen und aus dem Ursprünglichen*), the "ultimately original" (*Letztursprüngliche*), "all origins of being and of truth", "this primal source" (*Urquelle*), knowledge "originally acquired", its "original-genuine shape", its "primal right" (*urrechtbestimmenden*), "transcendental clarity about origins" (*Ursprungsklarheit*), and "understood from the origins". The Greek *arche* is the stem the German family glosses. The phrase "one truly to be called" (*eine wahrhaft zu nennende*) and the quotation marks work together: the marks say the word is not used in its common sense, and "truly" says that its proper sense is the etymological one, a science of *archai*, not of buried things. The commentary reads "archaeology" as Husserl's term for a regressive inquiry into constitutive strata. Nothing in this paragraph speaks of strata. The one verb that belongs to digging comes at the very end: the origin is "uncovered" (*aufgedeckt*). And what is uncovered is not beneath positive evidence but "hidden in" it (*in der Evidenz der Positivität verborgene*). The image is of a source concealed in what flows from it, not of layers to be cut through.

That is the point the paragraph serves in Lecture 31. Two kinds of evidence are introduced in its last clause. "Natural evidence", which Husserl puts in quotation marks as what is "commonly called" evidence, is a "naive clarity": it gives its object and does not show its own accomplishment. The higher-level evidence sees that accomplishment, "with its horizon of motivation that determines and limits its primal right". An evidence can therefore be complete in its own line and still not know the limits of its right. The archaeology does not add new objects to positive knowledge. It adds knowledge of where positive knowledge comes from, and so of how far it reaches. The third pair of quotation marks, "final" (*„endgültige“*) against merely "valid" (*gültige*), states the result in a pun the English loses: a cognition is final (*end-gültig*) when its validity has been traced to its end, which is its origin.

The sentence also hedges the whole programme, and the hedges matter more than the image. We have "not yet" a clear prototype of philosophy, "only" a "formal-general methodical guiding thought". The science "is necessary" and "is to" (*soll*) explore and teach. Everything after "archaeology" is in the mode of what it is to do. The next paragraph says why: we decide for the idea practically, and only by working it out "can we have it itself and thereby first know what we really intended". Lecture 31 then takes the higher evidence up under another name. The adequacy that the following blocks examine, and the apodicticity defined at Hua VIII 35, are the forms the "absolute justification" of this paragraph has to take. The archaeology states the goal; the theory of evidence asks what could satisfy it.

The edition shows the word is a late addition. The apparatus records that the printed stretch from Hua VIII 29, line 16, to 30, line 20, follows Landgrebe's typescript (B) and Husserl's later handwritten revision of it (C), and that the phrase "as one truly to be called an 'archaeology'" is an insertion in C (*als bis „Archäologie" Einf. C*, Hua VIII 516–517). The lecture manuscript of 1923/24 (A), which the apparatus quotes, put the same thought in Greek: a universal science "which systematically derives all the special sciences apodictically from the true *archai*" (*welche systematisch die sämtlichen Einzelwissenschaften aus den wahren ἀρχαί apodiktisch ableitet*). The revision did two things. It made the *archai* a single source that "encloses in itself all origins", and it changed the relation from derivation to exploration and clarification. The sciences are no longer to be deduced from first principles, as the A text's "apodictically derives" suggests; their evidence is to be understood from its origin. The word "archaeology" arrives with that change. It names a science that goes back into what the sciences already do, where the earlier text had named a system built down from axioms.

Husserl kept the word. In the corpus it next appears as a heading he wrote in May 1932 on the envelope of a group of time manuscripts: "Phenomenological archaeology. Inquiry back to the ego and the subjective in its originality. Primal originality and recollections-presentifications" (*Phänomenologische Archäologie. Rückfrage auf das Ich und das Subjektive in der Originalität*, Hua XV 707). By then the word carries its own direction, "inquiry back" (*Rückfrage*), and it is attached to the analysis of the living present. Both features are already in the revised sentence of Hua VIII: the source is hidden in present evidence, and the science goes back to it. What the sentence does not yet have, and what the commentary's gloss supplies from later, is the picture of strata. In 1923/24 the archaeology is a science of the source; whatever layers it finds are still to come.

## Lecture 31, Hua VIII 33–34: adequacy as an idea

Reads [Lecture 31, block 14](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-31-natural-and-transcendental-apodictic-and-adequate-evidence).

An evidence is adequate when nothing in what is meant goes beyond what is given itself. Lecture 31 has just made adequacy the measure of all justification, and in this passage Husserl allows that it may be an idea: a goal approached in a series of ever more perfect evidences and never reached. If so, there are two things one can mean by "evidence" of such a goal. One is the evidence that one is approaching it, and through that the evidence that it exists as an idea. The other is having it itself. The first is available; the second never is. The passage calls this an ambiguity "grounded in essence", and the whole later separation of apodicticity from adequacy starts from it.

> For perhaps it would turn out that such self-givenness is a mere "idea", in a sense analogous to that in which we so call pure red: seen red is, we think, always only red in a more or less imperfect purity, belonging to series of increase in whose perceptual traversal we approach pure red, although in the end we still remain (more or less) distant from it. Beyond the last-seen member of such a series a further increase is always possible, conceivable; on the other hand, in the progress itself we have the evidence precisely of the approach to "the" pure red that remains distant ("pure red"); and with that, in an essentially grounded ambiguity of speech, an evidence of the being of this idea — as idea — although not an evidence, a self-having, of pure red simply. Perhaps there lies in each and every evidence, as self-giving, as consciousness of grasping what is meant as "it itself", a certain relativity, such that, wherever we speak of an adequate evidence and are certain of it as such, there is present only a similar, and perhaps continuous and freely continuable, process of increase of relative evidences; thus a consciousness of steady and free approach to a goal co-included in consciousness, which becomes evident as such — that is, only as idea — while despite the evidence of the approach it remains — and evidently — unreached. — Here too further questions arise at once, whether infinite regresses do not threaten, and so on. However, they cannot be questions of the beginning.

German:

> Vielleicht würde sich nämlich herausstellen, daß solche Selbstgegebenheit eine bloße „Idee“ ist; in einem analogen Sinn, in dem wir das reine Rot so nennen: gesehenes Rot ist, meinen wir, jederzeit nur Rot in einer mehr oder minder unvollkommenen Reinheit, Steigerungsreihen angehörig, in deren wahrnehmendem Durchlaufen wir uns dem reinen Rot annähern, obschon wir ihm schließlich doch (mehr oder minder) fern bleiben. Immer ist über das Letztgesehene einer solchen Reihe hinaus eine weitere Steigerung möglich, denkbar; andererseits haben wir im Fortschreiten selbst die Evidenz eben der Annäherung an „das“ fernbleibende „reine Rot“; damit aber, in einer wesensmäßig begründeten Doppeldeutigkeit der Rede, eine Evidenz vom Sein dieser Idee — als Idee —, obschon nicht eine Evidenz, ein Selbsthaben des reinen Rot schlechthin. Vielleicht liegt nun in aller und jeder Evidenz als Selbstgebung, als Bewußtsein, Gemeintes als „es selbst“ zu erfassen, eine gewisse Relativität, derart daß, wo immer wir von einer adäquaten Evidenz sprechen und ihrer als solcher gewiß sind, nur ein ähnlicher und dabei ev. kontinuierlicher und frei fortzuführen der Steigerungsprozeß relativer Evidenzen vorliegt, somit ein Bewußtsein stetiger und freier Annäherung an ein bewußtseinsmäßig also mitbeschlossenes Ziel, das als solches — also nur als Idee — evident wird, während es trotz der Evidenz der Annäherung doch — und evidenterweise — unerreicht bleibt. — Auch hier erheben sich alsbald weitere Fragen, ob nicht unendliche Regresse drohen usw. Indessen sie können nicht Fragen des Anfangs sein.

*Note on the text.* The passage runs across the page break from Hua VIII 33 to 34 ("fern" / "bleiben"), and the text reads on without loss. The file has "frei fortzuführen der Steigerungsprozeß", a word split by the conversion; the sense is "frei fortzuführender" (freely continuable), as rendered.

Everything turns on the word "evidence" (*Evidenz*), and the passage uses it in both senses it distinguishes. In the progress we have "the evidence precisely of the approach"; with it we have "an evidence of the being of this idea — as idea —, although not an evidence, a self-having, of pure red simply". The apposition "an evidence, a self-having" (*eine Evidenz, ein Selbsthaben*) fixes the strong sense by a synonym, and the dashes around "as idea" fence off the weak one. The ambiguity is "of speech" (*der Rede*), but it is "essentially grounded" (*wesensmäßig begründet*): not a careless double use that better terminology could remove, but one the matter forces. Whoever approaches an idea really does see it as the goal of the approach, and so really has an evidence "of" it. That is why the same word must serve. The passage ends by turning the ambiguity into a single paradoxical clause: the goal "remains — and evidently — unreached" (*doch — und evidenterweise — unerreicht bleibt*). The unreachability is itself given with evidence. It is not a failure of the progress but part of what the progress shows.

The quotation marks do as much as the dashes. "Idea" (*„Idee“*) is marked on its first use, as a word whose sense here is technical and borrowed. The marks then split the phrase "the pure red that remains distant" in two, „das“ fernbleibende „reine Rot“, and put the definite article in quotation marks by itself. The article is what would make pure red an object one could have. Husserl distances it. Pure red is "the" goal of the series only in the sense in which a limit is the end of a series that has no last member. Finally, "it itself" (*„es selbst“*) is marked in the next sentence, when the relativity is extended to all evidence: the formula for self-givenness, which Lecture 31 had made the measure of all justification, is now itself quoted.

The argument rests on an analogy, and the passage says so. Self-givenness may be an idea "in a sense analogous to" (*in einem analogen Sinn*) that in which pure red is one. The example is chosen for its clarity, but it is a case of a sensory quality varying in degree, where increase is easy to picture. The step to "each and every evidence" (*aller und jeder Evidenz*) is not argued. It is introduced with a second "perhaps" (*Vielleicht*), and the claim about red itself is hedged with "we think" (*meinen wir*). Two "perhaps" clauses carry the paragraph, and the verb of the first is conditional: it "would turn out" (*würde sich … herausstellen*). Husserl is not asserting that adequacy is an idea. He is admitting that his measure might be unattainable, at the very moment he has adopted it.

That admission is what the passage contributes to the lecture. Immediately before it, the principle of evidence was set out as a demand to "pass over to the limit of evidence" (*zum Limes der Evidenz übergehen*, Hua VIII 32) and to make "the absolute optimum" of self-giving "the last measure". A limit is what a series approaches, and the passage draws the consequence the earlier word already held: the optimum may never be had. Immediately after, the beginner states his aim again, now with a restriction: evidence "and at the highest adequately evident … as far as such a thing is at all to be possible" (*soweit dergleichen irgend möglich sein soll*, Hua VIII 34). The deferral of the regress questions, "they cannot be questions of the beginning", lets the beginning go on without solving them. But it cannot go on with adequacy alone as its warrant, and a page later apodicticity enters as a property of adequate evidence (Hua VIII 35). The concession here is what makes that second concept necessary: if adequacy may be an infinite task, the beginning needs an evidence whose certainty does not wait on its completion.

The course redeems the passage at its end, and in its vocabulary. In Lecture 51 Husserl describes the pure life of the ego, taken universally, as "a limit-idea lying at eternal distance" (*eine in ewiger Ferne liegende Limes-Idee*), which itself encloses "an infinity of limit-forms and infinitely distant points", and which I can know only "in relative determinacy and approach" (Hua VIII 163). The "distance" (*fern*) and "approach" (*Annäherung*) of pure red have become the mode of givenness of transcendental subjectivity itself. What Lecture 31 offered as a perhaps about evidence in general is there asserted of the field the reduction opens. That is why the equivalence of adequacy and apodicticity, asserted two pages after this passage, cannot survive the course.

*Ideas I* had used the same concept with a narrower reach. There the stream of experience as a whole is grasped not as a single experience is, but "in the manner of an idea in the Kantian sense", and it is nevertheless "an absolutely indubitable givenness", whose indubitability "has a quite different source" from that of single experiences, which "come to pure givenness in immanent perception" (§83; `husserl-hua-3-1-…`, checked against Kersten's translation in `husserl-cw2-…`). §143 says the same of the adequate givenness of a thing. In 1913 the idea marks the limit of givenness for things and for the stream as a whole, while the single experience is had itself. Lecture 31's "perhaps … each and every evidence" puts that exception in doubt. It also takes over the other half of §83 without yet naming it: an evidence can be indubitable while its object is given only as an idea. Lecture 31 calls that indubitability apodicticity two pages later, and spends the rest of the course learning that it does not require adequacy.

## Lecture 31, Hua VIII 35: apodicticity defined by the test of negation

Reads [Lecture 31, block 17](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-31-natural-and-transcendental-apodictic-and-adequate-evidence).

Apodicticity, as Lecture 31 introduces it, is a reflective property: an evidence is apodictic when the attempt to negate or doubt what it gives shows, with evidence, that the non-being or doubtfulness of that thing is impossible. Husserl first presents it as a property of adequate evidence, discovered by testing adequate evidence. He then asserts the converse, that every apodictic evidence is adequate, and treats the two words as interchangeable. The definition by the test of negation survives the course. The converse does not, and its wording shows how little was done to earn it.

> One more thing is to be remarked here as characteristic of an adequate evidence. It comes out in the test of passing through negation or doubt. If I try to negate an adequate evidence or to posit it as doubtful, then — and again in adequate evidence — the impossibility of the non-being or of the doubtfulness of what is evident, of what is grasped from absolute self-giving, leaps out. We can also designate this property of adequate evidence as its **apodicticity**. Evidently, conversely, every **apodictic** *evidence* is **adequate**. We can therefore use both expressions as equivalent, and in particular prefer the one or the other according as we lay special weight on adequation or on apodicticity.

German:

> Noch eines ist hier zu bemerken als Charakteristikum einer adäquaten Evidenz: Es tritt hervor in der Probe des Durchgangs durch Negation oder Zweifel. Versuche ich, eine adäquate Evidenz zu negieren oder als zweifelhaft anzusetzen, so springt, und wieder in adäquater Evidenz, die Unmöglichkeit des Nichtseins oder des Zweifelhaftseins des Evidenten, des aus absoluter Selbstgebung Erfaßten hervor. Wir können diese Eigenheit adäquater Evidenz auch als ihre **Apodiktizität** bezeichnen. Offenbar ist umgekehrt jede **apodiktische** *Evidenz* **adäquat**. Wir können daher beide Ausdrücke als äquivalente gebrauchen und insbesondere den einen oder <den> anderen bevorzugen, je nachdem wir eben auf die Adäquation <auf> die Apodiktizität besonderen Wert legen.

*Note on the text.* The angle brackets are the editor's supplements, kept as the file has them. The note marker after the last sentence belongs to the editor's reference to Beilage VIII ("The apodictically absolutely given as presupposition of all cognitive striving"), which the file prints in Lecture 35 (Hua VIII 58), far from its marker. The bold marks Husserl's spaced type in the print; it is consistent here and the reading uses it.

The definition is procedural. Apodicticity "comes out in the test" (*tritt hervor in der Probe*) of a "passage through negation or doubt" (*Durchgangs durch Negation oder Zweifel*). The first-person conditional, "If I try to negate", makes it an experiment the meditator performs, and the verb for the result, "leaps out" (*springt … hervor*), says the result is not inferred but seen. What is seen is an impossibility, "of the non-being or of the doubtfulness of what is evident". The clause inserted in the middle of the sentence, "and again in adequate evidence" (*und wieder in adäquater Evidenz*), carries the whole construction. The test is itself an evidence, and it is adequate. Apodicticity is thus defined at one remove: it is what a second, reflective evidence finds when it tries to cancel the first. That matches the passage two paragraphs earlier, where adequacy too was known "only in a second, the reflective evidence, which must itself again be adequate" (Hua VIII 33).

The third sentence names the property, and the naming is modest: "We can also designate this property of adequate evidence as its apodicticity". The noun is defined as a property (*Eigenheit*) of a kind of evidence already in hand. Nothing yet allows an apodictic evidence that is not adequate, because apodicticity has been introduced only as something adequate evidence has.

The fourth sentence reverses the direction, and it carries the paragraph's only claim that goes beyond a definition. "Evidently, conversely" (*Offenbar ist umgekehrt*) every apodictic evidence is adequate. The adverb does the arguing. No test is described that would show an apodictic evidence to be adequate, and the preceding paragraph had just allowed that adequacy may be "a mere 'idea'", a goal approached and never reached (Hua VIII 33–34). If that is so, the converse makes apodicticity an idea as well, and the test of negation could never be passed by anything actually given. The paragraph does not face this. The print's emphasis falls on the three terms of the converse (*apodiktische*, *Evidenz*, *adäquat*), not on the "evidently" that would need defending. The last sentence draws a consequence about words: "We can therefore use both expressions as equivalent" (*beide Ausdrücke als äquivalente gebrauchen*). "Therefore" (*daher*) licenses a usage, and the choice between the words is left to emphasis, "according as we lay special weight" on one or the other. A definitional equivalence would not leave a choice of emphasis. The phrasing concedes that the two words pick out different aspects even while it says they pick out the same evidences.

The passage gives Lecture 31 its link to Descartes. The next paragraph says that "this reflection" leads to "the principled sense of the Cartesian maxim of indubitability" (Hua VIII 35): to reject whatever gives the least occasion for doubt is to admit only what passes the test of negation. The test is the phenomenological form of Descartes' rule. The equivalence lets Husserl run the rule and the ideal of adequate self-givenness together, so that the Cartesian beginning and the principle of all principles seem to ask for one thing.

The course undoes the equivalence and keeps the test. Lecture 33 defines apodictic indubitability with no word about adequacy: "where I see as I see, and holding fast that I see so", I cannot even think the possibility that what is seen is not, or is otherwise (Hua VIII 50). Lecture 51 describes the pure life of the ego as a "limit-idea" never had in full (Hua VIII 163), while the existence of that life is never doubted. By then the field the reduction opens is apodictically certain in its existence and inadequately given in its content, which is just what the converse ruled out. Husserl's own critical notes date the trouble back to this page. Beilage II gives a note on the stretch from Hua VIII 31 to 36: instead of these pages, "the principle of indubitability should first have been introduced in the same vague way in which it first appears in Descartes' Meditations, and not at once in the sharpening to the principle of apodictic indubitability" (*nicht sogleich in der Verschärfung zum Prinzip der apodiktischen Zweifellosigkeit*). The sharpened principle is needed only once pure subjectivity is reached, where apodictically certain givens must be told from others; until the distinction of transcendental and apodictic reduction at Hua VIII 80, "talk of an 'apodictic' reduction or critique should be avoided" (Hua VIII 311). The later Husserl's objection is to the timing, not to the test.

The *Cartesian Meditations* state the separation as doctrine. In §6, adequate evidence is an idea of perfection, "where it may remain open whether it does not in principle lie at infinity" (*ob sie nicht prinzipiell im Unendlichen liegt*), and apodicticity is a different perfection of "higher dignity", which "can occur also in inadequate evidences" (*sie kann eventuell auch an inadäquaten Evidenzen auftreten*, Hua I 55–56). §9 recalls that remark and applies it: "adequacy and apodicticity of an evidence need not go hand in hand", and transcendental self-experience offers only "a core of properly adequately experienced" content, the living self-present, within a presumptive horizon (Hua I 63). Lecture 31 had all the elements of §6 on two consecutive pages: the doubt that adequacy is ever reached, and a definition of apodicticity by the test of negation. What it lacked was the step that keeps them apart, and the "evidently" of the converse is where it failed to take it.

## Lecture 32, Hua VIII 36: the absolute ideal as a principle of the beginning

Reads [Lecture 32, block 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-32-the-possible-starting-points-i-am-and-i-as-beginning-philosopher-i-am-and-this-world-is).

The absolute ideal of justification is the demand that every judgment be justified by adequate evidence, with apodicticity as its test. Lecture 32 adopts it as the guiding principle of the beginning, not because it has been shown to be the only form of justification, but because nothing else is yet known. The passage admits that other forms may later prove legitimate, and still lays the ideal down "in any case". Husserl's marginal "Why?" asks for the reason that the sentence does not give. His longer note in Beilage II answers by sketching the alternative the lecture set aside: justification by a progress that approaches an ideal pole, with apodicticity and adequacy as distinct stages of evidence.

> Perhaps in the further course of our meditations we will have to distinguish *many kinds of* peculiar forms of justification which do not themselves and directly have the character of adequate evidence; but perhaps we can then still show that in them all adequate evidence must function as the last source of justification, if the justification is to count as actual and serious. For the present, however, we know nothing yet of such things, and in any case the absolute ideal of justification is prescribed to us as the guiding principle of the beginning.

German:

> Vielleicht werden wir in dem weiteren Gang unserer Meditationen *vielerlei* eigenartige Rechtfertigungsgestalten unterscheiden müssen, die nicht selbst und geradezu den Charakter adäquater Evidenz haben; vielleicht aber, daß wir dann doch zeigen können, daß in ihnen allen die adäquate Evidenz als letzte Rechtfertigungsquelle fungieren muß, damit die Rechtfertigung als wirkliche und ernstliche gelten könne. Vorläufig aber wissen wir von dergleichen noch nichts, und jedenfalls als Leitprinzip des Anfangs ist uns das absolute Ideal der Rechtfertigung vorgezeichnet.

*Note on the text.* The editor's note reporting Husserl's marginal "Why?" (*„Warum?“*) against the last sentence is printed a few lines below the paragraph, under a different number than its marker; it is matched by content and by Beilage II's reference "ad 36, 29–31". The longer note quoted below stands in Beilage II (Hua VIII 311–312).

The paragraph is built of two "perhaps" clauses and a "but". The first opens the possibility that justification has "many kinds" of forms, and the print italicizes *vielerlei*, the one word that threatens the ideal. The second "perhaps" closes the threat again: even then adequate evidence might function in all of them "as the last source of justification". The two clauses balance a plurality of forms against a unity of source, and neither is asserted. Then comes the turn: "For the present, however, we know nothing yet of such things" (*Vorläufig aber wissen wir von dergleichen noch nichts*). Ignorance of the alternatives is made to stand in for an argument against them. The last clause draws its conclusion with "in any case" (*jedenfalls*), a particle that concedes the preceding reasoning has not settled the matter, and states it in the passive with no agent: the ideal "is prescribed to us" (*ist uns … vorgezeichnet*). Who or what prescribes it the sentence does not say. It could be the idea of philosophy adopted in Lecture 31, or the beginner's resolution, or the nature of justification itself. Only the last would make the ideal binding, and the passage has just conceded that it does not know what justification in general requires.

That is the joint the marginal "Why?" (*„Warum?“*) strikes. It asks for the agent of "prescribed". The commentary reads the query correctly as aimed at the weak point of the argument. The wording shows where the weakness lies: the step from "perhaps there are other forms" to "the absolute ideal in any case" uses the beginner's ignorance as a premise.

The paragraphs around the passage show what the ideal was meant to do. The lecture's first paragraph calls it "a formal and in fact unsurpassable ideal" (*ein formales und in der Tat unübersteigliches Ideal*) and adds a procedure that will matter later: applying it may allow one "to restrict a given evidence to its content of what is to be absolutely justified, and so to produce from a non-adequate an adequate, but restricted, evidence" (Hua VIII 36). The next paragraph is experimental: "This absolute ideal of adequation we want, then, to try to carry out … We want to see how far we get with it." The passage sits between the claim that the ideal cannot be surpassed and the admission that it is a trial. Its last sentence turns the trial into a rule for the beginning, and the following paragraph at once asks which adequate evidences must come first. The method of restriction in the opening paragraph is what the course will actually use. It becomes the apodictic reduction of Lecture 38, which isolates within transcendental experience what can be absolutely justified (Hua VIII 80).

Beilage II answers the query in a note headed "On the same: on the course of thought" (*Ebendazu: Zum Gedankengang*). Its first line restates the principle in a new form: "Principle of adequate knowledge. Principle of evidence with the stages apodicticity and adequation" (*Prinzip der Evidenz mit den Stufen Apodiktizität und Adäquation*). The two words that Lecture 31 had declared equivalent are now stages. Then it asks the question the lecture closed off: "Should I hold no knowledge to be absolutely justified that does not have and reach the goal of adequation? Or should I also admit knowledge, cognitive processes, with certain methodical accomplishments, such that the *progressus* in its *method* and accomplishment is guaranteed by apodictic and adequate principles, and I so reach, not actual adequation in the cognitive spheres concerned, but *approach* to the goal: the goal an ideal pole?" (Hua VIII 311). The "many kinds of forms of justification" of the lecture have become a definite candidate: a method that guarantees approach without arrival. The note goes further. "Perhaps there lies in the same, under the title of empirical science, also a kind of apodicticity that I do not yet survey in advance", and world-science might be "through and through apodictic" if it posits not "the world simply" but "world-presumption", remaining "relatively apodictic" in every change (*relativ apodiktisch*, Hua VIII 311).

The note turns the lecture's first "perhaps" into a programme and drops its second. Adequate evidence is no longer required as the last source within every form of justification; apodictic principles that govern an infinite approach are enough. That is the form the course itself reaches at its end, where transcendental life is a "limit-idea" known "in relative determinacy and approach" (Hua VIII 163). The note's closing outline confirms the order the course follows: first the proof that external experience "is not apodictic, and of course not adequate"; then what the critique of mundane experience "constantly presupposed", the experiencing ego, as the transition to the ego cogito; then "the apodictic critique of the ego cogito" (Hua VIII 311–312). It also names a shortcut: one could go "directly to transcendental subjectivity", as in the Kant address (the editor refers to *Kant and the Idea of Transcendental Philosophy*, in Hua VII), and criticize external experience "only quite briefly, or not at all". The "Why?" against this sentence is thus the seed of the later Husserl's verdict on the whole first way. The absolute ideal was prescribed to the beginning before the beginning knew whether it needed it.

## Lecture 32, Hua VIII 42: "I am" as the principle of all principles

Reads [Lecture 32, block 14](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-32-the-possible-starting-points-i-am-and-i-as-beginning-philosopher-i-am-and-this-world-is).

The "principle of all principles", in *Ideas I*, was a rule: whatever originary intuition gives is to be accepted as it gives itself, and only within those limits. In Lecture 32 the title passes to a proposition, "I am". The shift changes what a principle is. A rule of evidence can ground indefinitely many first statements; a first proposition is one statement on which a philosophy is to rest. The passage proposes "I am" for that place, and it does so as a view one "can hold", with the world held out of validity until the claim is argued. Section Two is the argument.

> However illuminating this in fact very natural consideration may appear, and however much the "I am" presents itself as a contingent and in no way privileged particular of the experienced existence of the world, one can nevertheless hold the view, and perhaps with very much better reasons, that rather the proposition "I am" must be the true principle of all principles and the first proposition of all true philosophy. One can in fact show that the whole discussion devoted to the indubitability of the world's existence, or to the right of world-experience, does not pass the test of genuine apodicticity; and that therefore, for a philosophy to be grounded absolutely, the universe, taken in its full totality, must remain out of validity, that is, must remain in question until further notice, as lacking absolute grounding, exactly like the propositions of all world-sciences and the sciences in general.

German:

> Indessen so einleuchtend diese in der Tat sehr natürliche Überlegung erscheinen mag und so sehr das „Ich bin“ sich als eine zufällige und gar nicht bevorzugte Besonderheit der erfahrenen Weltexistenz darbietet, man kann doch die Ansicht vertreten, und vielleicht mit sehr viel besseren Gründen, daß vielmehr der Satz „Ich bin“ das wahre Prinzip aller Prinzipien und der erste Satz aller wahren Philosophie sein muß. Man kann in der Tat zeigen, daß die ganze Ausführung, die der Unbezweifelbarkeit der Weltexistenz bzw. dem Rechte der Welterfahrung gilt, die Probe echter Apodiktizität nicht besteht; und daß somit für eine absolut zu gründende Philosophie das Weltall, in seiner vollen Allheit genommen, außer Geltung bleiben, d.h. bis auf weiteres genau so als absoluter Begründung entbehrend in Frage bleiben muß wie die Sätze sämtlicher Weltwissenschaften und die Wissenschaften überhaupt.

*Note on the text.* The note marker at the end of the passage belongs to the editor's reference to Beilage IX, "Empirical critique of experience and experiential science and transcendental critique of the same", which the file prints at Hua VIII 58.

The first sentence concedes before it claims. Its subordinate clauses grant the natural view twice: the consideration is "in fact very natural", and "I am" "presents itself" (*sich … darbietet*) as "a contingent and in no way privileged particular" of the world's existence. The verb is chosen with care. The natural view is not refuted; the self is said to show itself that way. The main clause then shifts from the first person plural of the lecture to the impersonal "one" (*man*): "one can nevertheless hold the view" (*man kann doch die Ansicht vertreten*). The phrase names a position in a dispute. The insertion "and perhaps with very much better reasons" (*und vielleicht mit sehr viel besseren Gründen*) claims superiority and hedges it in the same breath. Only inside the "that"-clause does the modal harden into "must" (*sein muß*). The structure is that of a thesis announced for defence: it is not yet the lecture's own assertion, but the content of a view the lecture will argue for.

The thesis uses two names for the same place, "the true principle of all principles and the first proposition of all true philosophy". The word "proposition" (*Satz*) is the hinge. "I am" is called a *Satz* twice, and its rival is "the propositions of all world-sciences" (*die Sätze sämtlicher Weltwissenschaften*). The adjective "true" is placed on both nouns: the "true" principle against the one the natural view would choose, and "all true philosophy" against the sciences. The principle is now a statement with a content, set in competition with other statements. That is the change from *Ideas I*.

*Ideas I* §24 had stated the "principle of all principles" as a rule about intuition: "that every originarily giving intuition is a source of legitimacy for knowledge, that everything that offers itself to us originarily in 'intuition' … is simply to be accepted as what it gives itself as, but also only within the limits in which it gives itself there". Every statement that does no more than express such givens is, §24 continues, "an absolute beginning", a *principium* (`husserl-hua-3-1-…`, checked against Kersten's translation in `husserl-cw2-…`). There were as many beginnings as originary givens, and a perception of a thing was one of them, within its limits. Lecture 32 keeps the title and narrows it to one proposition because it has changed the test. The measure is no longer originary giving but "the test of genuine apodicticity" (*die Probe echter Apodiktizität*), the test of negation set up in Lecture 31. The perception of a thing gives its object originarily and fails that test, as the next paragraph begins to show ("every perception of a spatial thing is inadequate", Hua VIII 43). Under the new test, the many *principia* of 1913 collapse into one candidate.

The second sentence moves from "one can hold" to "one can in fact show" (*Man kann in der Tat zeigen*), and states the result Section Two will reach. Its last clause gives the status of the world, and gives it in two forms joined by "that is" (*d.h.*). The world "must remain out of validity" (*außer Geltung bleiben*), that is, "remain in question until further notice … as lacking absolute grounding" (*bis auf weiteres … als absoluter Begründung entbehrend in Frage bleiben*). The gloss defines being out of validity as being in question, and it limits both in time and in reason: "until further notice", and because absolute grounding is lacking, not because the world is doubted. The comparison fixes the sense: the world is to be treated "exactly like" (*genau so … wie*) the propositions of the sciences, which Lecture 30 had already put "out of validity" for the beginning philosopher. The universe of experience is given the status of a scientific theory not yet justified. The verb "remain" (*bleiben*) is used twice; nothing is done to the world, it is only not let in.

The passage closes Section One and sets the agenda of Section Two. Lecture 33 must make good the claim that world-experience fails the test of apodicticity, and does so through the "irremovable contingency" of "the world is". But the course does not deliver the thesis as stated. When "I am" is recovered in Lecture 38, Husserl insists that it "no longer meant the I of natural I-talk" (*nicht mehr das Ich der natürlichen Ich-Rede gemeint*), "as if I, the man, had saved myself from the epistemological end of the world" (Hua VIII 75–76). The "I am" of Lecture 32 is the proposition the natural view called "a particular of the experienced existence of the world". The proposition that becomes first is the same sentence with a different subject. And it is first only as a field "for a transcendental critique", which must still be supplied (Hua VIII 76). The principle of all principles turns out to need its own apodictic critique, which Lecture 53 finally demands. The passage's hedges, "one can hold", "perhaps", are more accurate than its "must".

## Lecture 33, Hua VIII 48–49: the world dissolving into a "swarm"

Reads [Lecture 33, block 7](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-33-the-irremovable-contingency-of-the-proposition-the-world-is).

The "swarm" (*Gewühl*) is what experience would become if its harmony broke down for good: appearances that no longer fit together, so that no perception anticipates anything and no thing appears. Husserl uses the image to show that the harmonious style of world-experience is a fact, not a necessity. Its dissolution is conceivable, and what is conceivable cannot be excluded with apodictic certainty. The word is Kant's, and Husserl marks it as Kant's. Kant used it for a possibility that the transcendental ground of unity rules out; Husserl uses it for a possibility that nothing rules out.

> But is this harmonious unity-structure of universal world-perception, so we now ask, an apodictic necessity? Is it not an open possibility, and a constantly open possibility, that this unity-structure (which is itself only an empirical presumption) dissolves, that experience no longer runs in the manner of continuing correction and of a continuing continuity of corrections that all fit together? That it must run so is, even if we were already apodictically certain of the fact that it has run so up to now, anything but an apodictic certainty. It is, so we shall have to tell ourselves, conceivable that a continuity of appearances that fit together might dissolve, in Kantian terms, into a mere "swarm" of appearances. Of course the single perceptions would then lose their anticipating belief; they would change their whole systematic structure of anticipation; their pre-meanings and co-meanings, which belong to them as normal external perceptions and which in the normal course of perception take on the character of expectations, would lose their hold and their force, their certainty of belief. If expectations are disappointed again and again, then nothing more is expected; anticipation falls away. That would mean, however, that external perceptions would come to an end; the course of perception would lose its character as a course of perceptions of things. Things and world would no longer be there perceptually for the one experiencing, neither as actually experienced nor as ready for experience, as accessible at any time through freely activated perceiving. What would at best remain would be a memory of a world that had appeared in an earlier experience, for a time thoroughly harmonious, and had been believed in certainty, but whose continued validity would now have lost every experiential motive. If every actual external perception were lacking, and in addition the actual readiness for perception, the certainty of "I can" seek and find spatial things by seeing, hearing and so on, then the former world-belief would be completely uprooted. Every representation of a world would have the character of an arbitrary fiction, and each would stand equal to every other as such a fiction.

German:

> Ist aber diese harmonische Einheitsstruktur der universalen Weltwahrnehmung, so fragen wir nun, eine apodiktische Notwendigkeit? Ist es nicht eine offene Möglichkeit, und eine beständig offene Möglichkeit, daß diese Einheitsstruktur (die selbst nur eine empirische Präsumtion ist) sich auflöst, daß die Erfahrung nicht mehr in der Weise fortgehender Korrektur und einer fortgehenden Kontinuität allzusammenpassender Korrekturen verläuft? Daß sie so verlaufen muß, ist, selbst wenn wir schon des Faktums, daß sie bisher so verlaufen sei, apodiktisch gewiß wären, nichts weniger als eine apodiktische Gewißheit. Es ist, so werden wir uns sagen müssen, denkbar, daß sich eine Kontinuität zusammenpassender Erscheinungen, kantisch gesprochen, in ein bloßes „Gewühl“ von Erscheinungen auflöse. Freilich würden dann die einzelnen Wahrnehmungen ihren vorgreifenden Glauben verlieren, sie würden ihre ganze systematische Vorgriffsstruktur ändern; ihre ihnen als normalen äußeren Wahrnehmungen zugehörigen Vormeinungen und Mitmeinungen, die im normalen Wahrnehmungsablauf den Charakter von Vorerwartungen annehmen, würden ihren Halt und ihre Kraft, <ihre> Glaubensgewißheit verlieren. Wenn sich immer und immer wieder Vorerwartungen enttäuschen, dann wird eben nichts mehr erwartet, der Vorgriff fällt weg. Das besagte aber, mit den äußeren Wahrnehmungen hätte es sein Ende, der Wahrnehmungsverlauf verlöre seinen Charakter als Verlauf von Dingwahrnehmungen. Es wären nicht mehr Dinge und Welt wahrnehmungsmäßig für den Erfahrenden da, weder als aktuell erfahren noch als erfahrungsbereit, als durch ein frei sich betätigendes Wahrnehmen allzeit zugänglich. Was bestenfalls übrig bliebe, wäre eine Erinnerung an eine in einer früheren, zeitweise durchaus einstimmigen Erfahrung erschienene und in Gewißheit geglaubte Welt, deren Fortgeltung nun aber jedes Erfahrungsmotiv eingebüßt hätte. Fehlte jede aktuelle äußere Wahrnehmung, fehlte zudem die aktuelle Wahrnehmungsbereitschaft, die Gewißheit des: „Ich kann“ sehend, hörend usw. Raumdingliches suchen und finden, so wäre der frühere Weltglaube völlig entwurzelt. Jede Weltvorstellung hätte den Charakter einer willkürlichen Fiktion, und jede stünde jeder anderen als solche Fiktion gleich.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph runs across the page break from Hua VIII 48 to 49 ("ihren Halt und ihre" / "Kraft"), and reads on without loss; "<ihre>" is the editor's supplement.

The passage turns on modality, and its verbs carry it. The opening questions oppose "apodictic necessity" (*apodiktische Notwendigkeit*) to "an open possibility, and a constantly open possibility". The doubling insists that the possibility is not one moment's worry but belongs to experience at every moment. The parenthesis gives the reason in a word: the unity-structure "is itself only an empirical presumption" (*selbst nur eine empirische Präsumtion*). The next sentence separates fact from necessity with a concession that costs the opponent nothing: even if the past course of experience were apodictically certain, "that it must run so" would not be. The claim is then put in the mode Husserl needs: "it is conceivable" (*denkbar*), introduced by "so we shall have to tell ourselves" (*so werden wir uns sagen müssen*). The necessity has moved from the world to the admission. What is necessary is that we grant the conceivability, and the subjunctive of the dependent clause, "might dissolve" (*auflöse*), keeps the dissolution itself a mere possibility.

The phrase "in Kantian terms" (*kantisch gesprochen*) and the quotation marks around "swarm" set the word apart as borrowed. In Kant's first edition the word stands in a counterfactual. The unity of synthesis according to empirical concepts "would be wholly contingent, and, if these were not grounded on a transcendental ground of unity, it would be possible that a swarm of appearances filled our soul without experience ever arising from it" (*so würde es möglich sein, daß ein Gewühle von Erscheinungen unsere Seele anfüllte*, A 110–111, `kant-kritik-der-reinen-vernunft-1781.md`). For Kant the swarm is the possibility that would hold if the transcendental ground were missing, and the point of the passage is that the ground is not missing. For Husserl the swarm is a possibility that holds, period: "the irremovable contingency" of the lecture's title. He takes Kant's word and drops Kant's "if not". The contingency Kant raised in order to exclude it is the contingency Husserl affirms. The commentary is right that Husserl claims less than Kant; the wording shows how. He claims nothing about the conditions of unity, only that the fact of unity is a fact.

The second half of the paragraph describes the swarm from inside, and its subjunctives describe a structure, not an event. The single perceptions "would lose their anticipating belief" and "their whole systematic structure of anticipation". The middle sentence changes mood: "If expectations are disappointed again and again, then nothing more is expected; anticipation falls away" (*dann wird eben nichts mehr erwartet, der Vorgriff fällt weg*). It is in the indicative, because it states a law of expectation, not a hypothesis about the world. From that law the rest follows: without anticipation there is no perception of things, and without that, no world "neither as actually experienced nor as ready for experience". The certainty of "I can" seek and find spatial things is named as part of what would be lost, and its loss is what "uproots" world-belief (*völlig entwurzelt*). What would remain "at best" is a memory of a world whose validity "would have lost every experiential motive", and the end of the paragraph states the consequence: every representation of a world would be "an arbitrary fiction", each "equal to every other". The final clause is exact. The swarm does not make the world false; it makes the world one fiction among others, with no experience to decide between them.

The passage closes the analysis of world-perception that opens Lecture 33. The preceding paragraphs had shown experience as a self-correcting harmony that makes the world "an idea", "necessarily to be posited … as long as this form is given" (Hua VIII 48). The conditional "as long as" is what this paragraph takes up: it asks whether the form must be given and answers that it need not. The next paragraph draws the conclusion that the existence of the world is not apodictic, and blocks the retreat to existence without content: "where nothing … of the being-so … can be given adequately, there no room is left for an apodicticity of mere existence" (Hua VIII 49).

The swarm returns twice in Lecture 36, and the second return widens the argument. Surveying "the whole of my mundane experience", the meditator recognizes again "the possibility of that transformation into a mere 'swarm' of appearances, in which every unity of belief dissolves" (Hua VIII 64). Three pages later the possibility of a "senseless swarm" (*sinnlosen Gewühl*) is joined by another "which should not be overlooked": that "despite the harmony" in which experience has run and still runs, the horizons of perceivability that never became actual perception lack the fulfillability that was "constantly and indubitably presumed". Then "the world need not be, need never have been" (Hua VIII 67). The swarm is the dramatic case, in which experience visibly falls apart. The second case is quieter and more radical: the world's non-being would not have to show itself as disorder at all. Lecture 33's Kantian image is thus the first of two forms of the world's contingency, and Lecture 36 makes it the less important one. What both show is that the being of the world is presumptive through and through. What the swarm alone shows, and what the commentary picks out, is that even if the world fell apart, something would still be going on: a course of appearances and a memory of a world, the residue the reduction will make its field.

## Lecture 33, Hua VIII 50: empirical and apodictic indubitability

Reads [Lecture 33, block 9](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-33-the-irremovable-contingency-of-the-proposition-the-world-is).

There are two ways of being unable to doubt. In the empirical way I cannot doubt because nothing speaks against what I experience: doubt needs a counter-motive, and there is none. In the apodictic way I cannot even think that what I see is not or is otherwise, for as long as I see it and hold fast that I see it so. The world of perception is indubitable in the first way and not in the second. That gap is what Husserl calls the "cognitive contingency" of the world, and it is compatible with complete certainty. Section Two depends on it, because the reduction needs a world that is certain and yet not apodictically so.

> Let us note the following. Every fact, and so also the fact of the world, is as fact, as is generally granted, **contingent**; this implies: if it is at all, it could still be otherwise and perhaps also not be. Whether and how far this really holds of *every* fact does not concern us here. But attention should be drawn to the fact that for the existence of the world an entirely different kind of contingency is in question here. *While* I perceive and in general experience the world, and perceive it in however great a perfection, *while* it is thus conscious to me in unbroken certainty as self-given, as one whose existence I simply cannot doubt, it nevertheless has a constant *cognitive contingency*, and that in the sense that this *bodily self-givenness* never in *principle excludes* its non-being. A *double indubitability* also comes to stand out here. Where I perceive in unbroken harmony, I believe, and I cannot arbitrarily turn belief into unbelief. So long as nothing speaks against experienced existence, I do not doubt, but I also cannot doubt. In the essence of doubt lies the "Something speaks against it". *Apodictic* indubitability, however, means something else and much more; it means: where I see as I see, and holding fast to this, that I see so, there I cannot even think the possibility that what is seen is not or is otherwise; thus also, say, the possibility that it should afterwards turn out that what is seen is not. Such a thing I can think; I can see it with insight as a possibility only if I think in addition that I do not see and see in this apodictic way. Otherwise for the seeing of external experience. It is evidently compatible with it that what is perceived is not. In this, then, consists an irremovable contingency of the proposition: "The world is".

German:

> Beachten wir hier folgendes; Jedes Faktum, und so auch das Weltfaktum, ist als Faktum, wie allgemein zugestanden, **kontingent**; darin liegt: wenn es überhaupt ist, es könnte doch anders sein und vielleicht auch nicht sein. Ob und wieweit das wirklich für *jedes* Faktum gilt, geht uns hier nichts an. Aber aufmerksam sei darauf gemacht, daß für die Weltexistenz hier eine ganz andersartige Kontingenz in Frage ist. *Während* ich die Welt wahrnehme und überhaupt erfahre, und in noch so großer Vollkommenheit wahrnehme, *während* sie also für mich in ungebrochener Gewißheit als selbstgegeben bewußt ist, als eine, an deren Existenz ich schlechthin nicht zweifeln kann, hat sie doch eine beständige *Erkenntniskontingenz*, und zwar des Sinnes, daß diese *leibhafte* *Selbstgebenheit* ihr *Nichtsein prinzipiell* nie *ausschließt*. Auch eine *doppelte* *Zweifellosigkeit* kommt hier zur Abhebung. Wo ich in ungebrochener Einstimmigkeit wahrnehme, glaube ich, und ich kann nicht willkürlich den Glauben in Unglauben verwandeln. Solange gegen erfahrenes Dasein nichts spricht, zweifle ich nicht, ich kann aber auch nicht zweifeln. Im Wesen des Zweifels liegt das ,,Es spricht etwas dagegen''. Die *apodiktische* Zweifellosigkeit besagt aber anderes und sehr viel mehr, sie besagt: wo ich sehe, wie ich sehe, und dies festhaltend, daß ich so sehe, da kann ich mir nicht einmal die Möglichkeit, daß das Gesehene nicht sei oder anders sei, denken; somit etwa auch die Möglichkeit, daß sich hinterher herausstelle, daß das Gesehene nicht sei. Dergleichen kann ich mir denken; ich kann es nur einsehend als Möglichkeit erschauen, wenn ich mir dazu denke, daß ich nicht sehe und in dieser apodiktischen Art sehe. Anders für das Sehen der äußeren Erfahrung. Ist evident verträglich, daß Wahrgenommenes nicht sei. Darin besteht also eine unaufhebbare Kontingenz des Satzes: ,,Die Welt ist''.

*Note on the text.* The passage is set as hard-wrapped print lines; words broken at line ends are joined here ("Erkenntniskontin-genz", "Nichtseinprinzi-piell", the latter run together in the file as "*Nichtseinprinzi* - *piell*" and separated here as "*Nichtsein prinzipiell*", following the sense). Two places in the file do not read as finished German, and the reading rests on neither. "Dergleichen kann ich mir denken" (Such a thing I can think) contradicts "nicht einmal … denken" (cannot even think) two lines above unless it is taken with what follows: I can think it only if I think in addition that I do not see. And "daß ich nicht sehe und in dieser apodiktischen Art sehe" (that I do not see and see in this apodictic way) needs "or not in this apodictic way" for its sense; the commentary reads it so. "Ist evident verträglich" lacks its subject ("Es"). The apparatus records no variant at either point, so the file cannot settle whether a word was lost in conversion. The editor's note at the end of the paragraph, which refers to Beilage XI, "On the possibility of the non-existence of the world", is printed at Hua VIII 58.

The paragraph sets two contingencies apart before it sets two indubitabilities apart. The first is the contingency every fact has "as fact", printed in spaced type (**kontingent**) and dismissed: whether it holds of every fact "does not concern us here". The second is new and gets a coinage, "cognitive contingency" (*Erkenntniskontingenz*). It is not a property of the world's being but of the world's being known: the world could be certain and still be contingent in this sense. The emphasis falls on "while" (*Während*), italicized twice. The contingency holds during the experience, not after it or in a sceptic's afterthought: at the moment of "unbroken certainty", "bodily self-givenness" (*leibhafte Selbstgebenheit*) does not exclude non-being "in principle". The sentence assembles the strongest terms of the theory of evidence for the world (self-given, bodily, certain, indubitable) and then denies them the one thing they would need to be apodictic.

The two indubitabilities are told apart by two uses of "cannot". Of the first: "I do not doubt, but I also cannot doubt" (*zweifle ich nicht, ich kann aber auch nicht zweifeln*). The "cannot" is motivational, and the next sentence gives its ground in a quoted formula: "In the essence of doubt lies the 'Something speaks against it'". Doubt is defined by its motive. Where nothing speaks against, doubt has nothing to start from, and so I am unable to doubt. Of the second: "I cannot even think the possibility" (*kann ich mir nicht einmal die Möglichkeit … denken*). "Not even" (*nicht einmal*) marks the step. Empirical indubitability is the absence of reasons for doubt; apodictic indubitability is the absence of a thinkable alternative. "Means something else and much more" (*anderes und sehr viel mehr*) claims both: a different kind, and a higher degree.

The clause that defines the apodictic is reflexive, and it carries a condition. "Where I see as I see, and holding fast to this, that I see so" (*wo ich sehe, wie ich sehe, und dies festhaltend, daß ich so sehe*): the seeing has to be held in view as the seeing it is. Apodicticity is not a property of the object alone but of a seeing that keeps hold of itself. The following sentence draws the consequence. The non-being of what is seen can be thought, but only "if I think in addition that I do not see" in this way. The necessity is therefore conditional on the seeing: while I see so, the contrary is unthinkable. Whether the seeing itself could fail is not asked here. That question is the apodictic critique of transcendental experience, which Lecture 38 sets as a task and Lecture 53 finally takes up. The definition already contains the place where that critique will have to begin.

The external seeing is placed against this in two short sentences, the second without a subject in the file: "Otherwise for the seeing of external experience. [It] is evidently compatible with it that what is perceived is not." The world's non-being is not merely not excluded; its compatibility with perception is itself evident. The apparatus records that "the seeing of external experience" replaces the manuscript's "the empirical seeing, that of external experience" (*das empirische Sehen, das der äußeren Erfahrung*, Hua VIII 518). The revision drops "empirical" and so speaks of external experience as a seeing too, in the same word used for the apodictic case. That sharpens the contrast: both are seeings, and only one of them excludes its contrary.

The distinction has to be defended at once, and Lecture 34 does so against the reading that would turn the world's contingency into a sceptical worry. "The existence of the world is completely *indubitable*, and this indubitability lies enclosed in world-perception itself". What the argument shows is that "this perfect empirical certainty, this empirical indubitability, nevertheless, as empirical, leaves the possibility open that the world is not" (Hua VIII 55). The pair empirical/apodictic does all the work of that defence. Husserl's own critical notes show where he thought the distinction came from. Against his later objection that the principle of indubitability should have been introduced in a vaguer form, he wrote: "No, I have no particular reason to doubt that the world is. But if I ask myself whether it is so completely certain that …, and consider, say, mathematical evidence, I become aware that here one must distinguish between empirical and apodictic indubitability" (Beilage II, Hua VIII 311). The distinction is reached by comparison: the "seeing" of Lecture 31's "2 < 3" is the model against which the seeing of the world is measured and found contingent. That is also its limit. Apodictic indubitability is defined here on the model of an evidence of essence, and Section Two will look for it in an evidence of existence, the "I am". Whether the reflexive clause can hold for a stream of experience that flows off as it is seen is what the later lectures have to settle.

## Lecture 34, Hua VIII 53–54: transcendental semblance

Reads [Lecture 34, block 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-34-transcendental-and-empirical-illusion-on-the-objection-from-madness).

Transcendental semblance (*transzendentaler Schein*) is the possibility that the world as a whole, bodily given and harmoniously confirmed, is nothing: a semblance with no truth beneath it. Empirical semblance is always semblance of something, a misperception that a further perception corrects, so that asking what is really there makes sense and can be answered. Transcendental semblance would leave no ground from which to ask. The term names a possibility, never an actual illusion, and its whole content lies in what it would make senseless.

> To be sure, we now have given a bodily existing world; we live, and have lived until now, as memory tells us, in a harmony of experience that conserved, or always restored, the concordance of the confirmation of all perceptions. This past and still continuing style of the whole of mundane perception necessarily motivates in itself the anticipating belief that it will go on so. And so we therefore believe, and so we must believe; the belief belongs constantly to the universal structure of streaming world-perception. On the other hand, it is still an empirical belief, a mere anticipation, which always leaves it open that it may not be confirmed, that the structure of the course of consistent correction may dissolve, as we showed last time. So while we still live in mundane experience and in the harmoniously confirmed belief of perception, and look at it, stating it, we can nevertheless see the possibility that this bodily perceived world could be a mere *semblance*, and not, as it self-evidently is, at all times mere appearance of a world true in itself that is sheltered in it as idea. To be sure, a semblance of a special kind, a *transcendental semblance*, is in question here, well distinguished from every empirical *semblance*, the semblance in the common sense. While we stand on the ground of empirical certainty of the world, that is, have the world given in normal experience, an empirical semblance meets us here and there, a semblance for whose explanation, for whose truth that at all times lies at its basis and is only hidden, we can ask. That this question has a sense, is a **decidable** question, is then certain to us in advance. Entirely otherwise for transcendental semblance. That the world, the one just now bodily given, should in truth not be, that this bodily given should be a semblance, a transcendental semblance, is a constantly open possibility. But here it belongs to the semblance we called transcendental that to seek a correction through a corresponding truth, or to ask for a true being to be set in the place of this non-existing world, would be wholly senseless. That would not be much different from our asking what exists as a true thing in place of the water nymph that we had just freely imagined, how we could correct this nothing so that it became a something, the true thing that falsely appears to us as a nymph.

German:

> Freilich, jetzt haben wir gegeben eine leibhaft daseiende Welt, wir leben und lebten bisher, wie uns die Erinnerung sagt, in einer Erfahrungsharmonie, die die Einstimmigkeit der Bestätigung aller Wahrnehmungen konservierte bzw. immer wieder herstellte. Dieser vergangene und noch fortgehende Stil der gesamten mundanen Wahrnehmung motiviert in sich notwendig den Vorglauben, daß es weiter so bleiben wird. Und so glauben wir daher, und so müssen wir glauben; der Glaube gehört beständig mit zur universalen Struktur der strömenden Weltwahrnehmung. Andererseits ist es doch ein empirischer Glaube, eine bloße Antizipation, die es immerzu offen läßt, daß er sich nicht bestätige, daß die Verlaufsstruktur konsequenter Korrektur sich auflöse: wie wir das letzthin ja gezeigt haben. Also während wir noch in der mundanen Erfahrung und im einstimmig sich bestätigenden Wahrnehmungsglauben leben und auf ihn konstatierend hinblicken, können wir doch die Möglichkeit einsehen, daß diese leibhaft wahrgenommene Welt ein bloßer *Schein* sein könnte, und nicht, wie sie das selbstverständlich ist, allzeit bloße Erscheinung einer in ihr als Idee geborgenen an sich wahren Welt. Freilich, ein Schein besonderer Art, ein *transzendentaler Schein* ist hier in Frage, wohlunterschieden von jedem empirischen *Schein*, dem Schein im gemeinen Sinne. Während wir auf dem Boden der empirischen Weltgewißheit stehen, also normal erfahrend die Welt gegeben haben, tritt uns da und dort ein empirischer Schein entgegen, ein Schein, nach dessen Erklärung, nach dessen ihm jederzeit *z u* *Grunde* liegenden und nur verborgenen Wahrem wir fragen können. Daß diese Frage einen Sinn hat, eine **entscheidbare** Frage ist, ist uns dann im voraus sicher. Ganz anders für den transzendentalen Schein. Daß die Welt, die soeben leibhaft gegebene, in Wahrheit doch nicht sei, daß dieses leibhaft Gegebene ein Schein, ein transzendentaler Schein sei, ist eine ständig offene Möglichkeit. Aber hier gehört zu dem Schein, den wir transzendental nannten, daß nach einer Korrektur durch eine entsprechende Wahrheit zu suchen bzw. nach einem wahren Sein zu fragen, das anstatt, an Stelle dieser nicht seienden Welt zu setzen sei, völlig sinnlos wäre. Das wäre nicht viel anders, als wenn wir fragen würden, was denn an Stelle der Nixe, die wir uns soeben frei fingiert hatten, als wahres Ding existiere, wie wir dieses Nichts umkorrigieren könnten, daß es zum Etwas würde, dem Wahren, das uns fälschlich als Nixe erscheint.

*Note on the text.* The paragraph runs across the page break from Hua VIII 53 to 54 ("Aber hier gehört zu dem" / "Schein") and reads on without loss. The file sets "*z u  *Grunde**" with broken markup; it is given here as the print's spaced and italic emphasis on "zu Grunde" (at the basis), which the reading does not rest on.

The paragraph sorts three words that share a stem: appearance (*Erscheinung*), semblance (*Schein*), and the "common" semblance of empirical error. The world "self-evidently is" (*wie sie das selbstverständlich ist*) "mere appearance of a world true in itself that is sheltered in it as idea" (*bloße Erscheinung einer in ihr als Idee geborgenen an sich wahren Welt*). Appearance is the normal mode of the world's givenness, and "mere" here takes nothing away: the true world is not behind the appearing world but "in" it, "sheltered" (*geborgen*) as the idea toward which its corrections converge, the idea set out in Lecture 33. Semblance is the opposite: what is given would not be the appearance of anything. The sentence that names the possibility also says what it would cancel, and the italics fall on *Schein*, not on *Erscheinung*.

Empirical semblance is defined by a question. One can ask for its explanation, for the truth that "at all times lies at its basis" (*jederzeit zu Grunde liegenden*) "and is only hidden". The print stresses "at the basis" with spaced type and "decidable" (**entscheidbare**) with emphasis: the question has a sense and an answer, and "that is then certain to us in advance". The advance certainty is the point. Lecture 33's man in the fog, corrected into a tree stump, is the model: the correction replaces one thing with another inside a world that both belong to. Transcendental semblance is defined by the same question becoming senseless. To ask for "a true being to be set in the place of this non-existing world" (*an Stelle dieser nicht seienden Welt*) assumes a world in which the replacement would stand. The doubling "instead, in the place of" (*anstatt, an Stelle*) presses on the spatial picture that has no application.

The water nymph (*Nixe*) is meant to show the senselessness, and the analogy is weaker than its job. A nymph "that we had just freely imagined" was never posited as real: we know it is a fiction, so there is no error to correct. The world in the hypothesis is given "bodily" and believed "as we must believe". The analogy holds at one point only: in both cases there is no ground from which a correction could start, because "this nothing" (*dieses Nichts*) cannot be "corrected" (*umkorrigieren*) into "a something". The coined verb names exactly what empirical correction does and transcendental semblance would forbid. What the analogy does not show is how a semblance that is believed with necessity could be known as semblance at all. Husserl does not claim that it could. He claims only that its possibility is "constantly open" (*ständig offene Möglichkeit*), and the paragraph says nothing about recognizing it.

The term is Kant's, and Husserl does not say so here. Kant's Dialectic sets transcendental illusion against "empirical illusion (e.g. optical)", which occurs in the empirical use of otherwise correct rules, and locates transcendental illusion in principles "whose use is not at all directed to experience, in which case we would at least have a touchstone of their correctness" (*einen Probierstein ihrer Richtigkeit*, A 295–296). Transcendental illusion, for Kant, is unavoidable and persists even "after it has been uncovered and its nullity clearly seen by transcendental critique" (A 296–297). Husserl keeps Kant's empirical side almost unchanged: within experience, error has a touchstone, and the question is decidable. He inverts the transcendental side. For Kant the illusion lies beyond experience, in reason overstepping it; for Husserl it would be experience itself, the whole of it, with nothing beyond. For Kant the illusion is actual and is uncovered by critique; for Husserl it is a bare possibility, and nothing could uncover it. The commentary calls this an inversion. The wording shows that Husserl inverts by taking over Kant's very criterion: the touchstone Kant found in experience is what the world as a whole would lack.

The next paragraphs of Lecture 34 make the result explicit: "with our empirically indubitable perceptual certainty of the existence of the world, the proposition that this world is a pure nothing, a mere transcendental semblance, is *compatible*" (Hua VIII 55). The term then becomes the hinge of the course's thought-experiments. In Lecture 36 the world "need be no more than a transcendental semblance", "an absolutely empty possibility against which the whole of experience speaks, … and for which absolutely nothing speaks" (Hua VIII 67). In Lecture 37, "had it pleased God … to create, instead of the actual world, only a transcendental world of semblance", "I would remain exactly who I am, in my pure selfhood" (Hua VIII 73). And in Lecture 39 the remembered Schloßberg, "my body, my walking legs", may be "transcendental semblance" while the remembering and the past perceiving remain (Hua VIII 85). Each time, the possibility subtracts the world and leaves the experiencing. That is what the term was made for. Transcendental semblance is the form in which the course thinks the world away without doubting it, and so the first form of the epoché.

## Lecture 35, Hua VIII 62–63: appresentation and perception through interpretation

Reads [Lecture 35, block 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-35-on-the-doctrine-of-empathy).

The other's psychic life is "appresented": meant along with his body, which I perceive as originally as my own, but never itself given. Husserl distinguishes this from the anticipation in every thing-perception. The unseen back of a thing is meant as something I could go round and see; the other's experience is indicated in a way that "neither demands nor permits" a perception that would redeem it. What results is still perception, but perception "through original interpretation". The phrase is Husserl's own name for the act, and his note sets it against the received word "empathy", which he calls "not very apt".

> In my own case, the thing-body is perceived in an original way, but so too is the psychic that embodies itself there, and as it embodies itself. The psychic is indeed my own. By contrast, the other's bodily thing is indeed perceived in my spatial surroundings, and quite as originally as mine; not so, however, the psychic embodied in it. It is not really and properly self-given, but only appresentatively co-meant. In this respect there is a likeness to that anticipation through which in every external perception something co-perceived is included as meant to be there itself, as for instance the invisible back of a seen thing. But the analogy is not a full one; it is indication, but not an anticipation that could become a grasping of the thing itself. This indicating intention does not demand and does not make possible a redeeming perception, as is the case with all the indicating moments within the perception of spatial things. The perception of the *other's* body is rather, so we shall have to say, by its own essence perception through original interpretation.
>
> [Husserl's note to the end of the next paragraph:] This experience through interpretation is nowadays usually, and not very aptly, called "empathy".

German:

> … ist der dingliche Leib, aber auch das Psychische, das sich da verleiblicht, und so wie es sich verleiblicht, in ursprünglicher Weise wahrgenommen. Das Psychische ist ja mein eigenes. Hingegen ist zwar der fremde Leibkörper in meiner räumlichen Umwelt wahrgenommen, und ganz ursprünglich wie der meine; nicht so jedoch das in ihm verleiblichte Psychische. Es ist nicht wirklich und eigentlich selbstgegeben, sondern nur appräsentativ mitgemeint. In dieser Hinsicht besteht Ähnlichkeit mit jener Antizipation, durch welche in jeder äußeren Wahrnehmung Mitwahrgenommenes, als selbst mit da Gemeintes, beschlossen ist; wie etwa die unsichtbare Rückseite eines gesehenen Dinges. Aber die Analogie ist keine volle; es ist Indikation, aber kein Vorgriff, der zum Selbstgriff werden könnte. Diese indizierende Intention fordert und ermöglicht nicht eine einlösende Wahrnehmung, wie das bei allen vordeutenden Momenten innerhalb der raumdinglichen Wahrnehmung der Fall ist. Die *fremd*-*leibliche* Wahrnehmung ist vielmehr, so werden wir sagen müssen, ihrem eigenen Wesen nach Wahrnehmung durch ursprüngliche Interpretation.
>
> Wenig passend heißt diese Erfahrung durch Interpretation neuerdings gewöhnlich ,,Einfühlung".

*Note on the text.* The quotation begins mid-sentence, at the top of the stretch the commentary's block covers; the paragraph runs across the page break from Hua VIII 62 to 63 and reads on without loss. Husserl's note is attached to the following paragraph's claim that this combination of seeing and interpreting is "a basic form of experience of its own, which is naturally still to be called perception"; it is printed directly below that paragraph.

The passage defines appresentation by three oppositions, each sharper than the last. First, "not really and properly self-given, but only appresentatively co-meant" (*nicht wirklich und eigentlich selbstgegeben, sondern nur appräsentativ mitgemeint*). The adverb attaches the prefix "co-" (*mit-*) to a meaning, not to a perceiving: the other's life is meant along with his body, and "only" meant. Second, the likeness with the back of a thing is granted and then limited: "it is indication, but not an anticipation that could become a grasping of the thing itself" (*es ist Indikation, aber kein Vorgriff, der zum Selbstgriff werden könnte*). The rhyme of "anticipation" and "self-grasp" (*Vorgriff*, *Selbstgriff*) states the normal course of thing-perception in two words: what is grasped ahead becomes grasped itself. Indication (*Indikation*), a Latinate word set against the native pair, names a reference that does not lead to its object. Third, the indicating intention "does not demand and does not make possible a redeeming perception" (*fordert und ermöglicht nicht eine einlösende Wahrnehmung*). The double verb says two things. Empathy does not call for fulfilment, so it is not an empty intention left unfulfilled; and it could not be fulfilled, so the lack is essential, not a defect of my position. The financial image in "redeeming" (*einlösende*) fits: the back of a thing is a promissory note that a walk round the thing cashes; the other's life is no note at all.

The conclusion is introduced with Husserl's formula for a concession forced by the matter, "so we shall have to say" (*so werden wir sagen müssen*), and it joins two words that pull apart: "perception through original interpretation" (*Wahrnehmung durch ursprüngliche Interpretation*). "Interpretation" says that the other's life is read off from his body; "original" says that the reading is not an inference from something prior. The next paragraph grounds the originality: it "lies grounded in" the relation back to "my own primal bodiliness" (*Urleiblichkeit*), in which I have "the primal experience of an incorporation of the subjective in something appearing as a thing". The originality of the other's perception is borrowed from mine. I cannot help apprehending the body there, which behaves like mine, as one in which something subjective is embodied, and so as the body of a subjectivity "that must live itself in the 'I am', but that I myself am not".

The note gives the act its name and refuses the received one. Husserl calls it "this experience through interpretation" (*diese Erfahrung durch Interpretation*), the phrase of the text, and says that it is "nowadays usually, and not very aptly, called 'empathy'" (*Wenig passend heißt diese Erfahrung durch Interpretation neuerdings gewöhnlich ,,Einfühlung"*). The quotation marks and "nowadays usually" mark the word as current usage, not his choice. "Not very aptly" is explained by what the passage has just said: feeling oneself into another suggests a living-through of his experience, and the whole point is that his experience is never given to me. The note names no author. The commentary's attribution of the term to Lipps and its remark that Husserl "continues to use" it "for want of a better" are not in the source; the lecture's title, which puts "empathy" in quotation marks (*Zur Lehre von der „Einfühlung“*), is the editor's. Husserl's own description, "experience through interpretation", is the one the reading should follow.

The passage serves Lecture 35's answer to the "objection from madness". Other people's experience cannot confirm the world independently of mine, because I have it only through an interpretation founded on my perception of their bodies. Their testimony is a further layer of my own world-experience, and it shares that experience's contingency. The lecture then calls empathy "a secondary form of perception" and says it has "its own modes of confirmation": "the self-confirmation of interpreting perception takes place again through interpretation" (Hua VIII 63). Interpretation is confirmed by further interpretation, never by a perception of what is interpreted.

The course does not keep "perception" for this act. In Lecture 47 empathy is a presentification (*Vergegenwärtigung*) that implies another ego (Hua VIII 135). In Lecture 54 the claim of Lecture 35 is reversed in so many words: confirmation of the other's subjectivity is "a confirmation through merely concordant indication, mutually confirming in its motivation", distinguished by its roots in self-giving experience "and so an 'appresenting' indication"; "an original experience, an actual perception, I have only of myself" and of what I perceive directly (Hua VIII 187). The word "indication" from Lecture 35 survives, now with "appresenting" in quotation marks; the word "perception" is withdrawn. The *Cartesian Meditations* state the structure in Lecture 35's terms and explain the word. The appresentation of the back of a thing includes "the possibility of confirmation through corresponding fulfilling presentation (the back becomes the front)", while for the appresentation of the other this "must be a priori excluded", as "the very word appresentation (making conscious as co-present) indicates" (*wie es schon das Wort Appräsentation (Als-mitgegenwärtig-bewußt-machen) andeutet*, §50, Hua I 139). The distinction between anticipation and indication is the same; the 1929 text derives it from the word. In 1923/24 it was derived from the matter, and called perception through interpretation.

## Lecture 36, Hua VIII 66: solipsism "in a good sense"

Reads [Lecture 36, block 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-36-transcendental-solipsism-the-negative-result-of-the-critique-of-mundane-experience).

The solipsism Lecture 36 adopts is a rule for the order of critique. A universal critique of experience may not presuppose that other subjects exist, because their existence is part of what it puts in question; it can know them only as experienced in my experience. It is solipsism "in a good sense" because it claims nothing about who exists, only about what may be used as a premise. The passage names, in the same breath, the fault the rule prevents: the "specifically epistemological circle" of using in a particular argument what the critique has made questionable in general. The rule is provisional, and the course's later uses of the word show it hardening before it is overcome.

> From such considerations I recognize, then, that a universal critique of experiences in general, which is incumbent on me as beginning philosopher, or could ever be incumbent on me, can only be one that is *solipsistic* in a good sense: that it is possible only as a critique of my experiences, which knows other subjects and their experiences only as experienced ones of my experiences and, as standing critically in question, does not presuppose them as existing. In so far as I may later ever have ground and occasion to exercise a philosophical critique on other people's experience, that can make sense only after I have already exercised a critique entering primarily into the mediacies of my universal experience. If other people's experiencing is there for me only as experiencing that indicates itself through interpretive mediacy, indicating itself in what I experience directly, then in any case the primal ground of all critique must be the investigation of these mediacies, experienced and to be followed in my own consciousness. — Besides all this, our long consideration of the objection from possible madness has also had the good of making us attentive, once and for all, to the danger of the specifically epistemological circle, which consists in using in a particular case, while arguing and without noticing it, what stands in question in the generality of the critical theme. —

German:

> Aus solchen Betrachtungen erkenne ich also, daß eine universale Kritik der Erfahrungen überhaupt, die mir als anfangendem Philosophen obliegt, oder je obliegen könnte, nur eine in gutem Sinne *solipsistische* sein kann, daß sie nur als eine Kritik meiner Erfahrungen möglich ist, die andere Subjekte und ihre Erfahrungen nur als erfahrene meiner Erfahrungen kennt und, als kritisch in Frage stehend, nicht als seiend voraussetzt. Sofern ich späterhin je Grund und Anlaß haben mag, an fremder Erfahrung eine philosophische Kritik zu üben, so kann das nur Sinn haben, nachdem ich eine primär in die Mittelbarkeiten meiner universalen Erfahrung eingehende Kritik schon geübt habe. Ist fremdes Erfahren für mich nur da als durch interpretatorische Mittelbarkeit sich indizierendes Erfahren, sich indizierend an direkt von mir Erfahrenem, so muß jedenfalls der Urgrund aller Kritik die Untersuchung dieser in meinem Bewußtsein selbst erfahrenen und zu verfolgenden Mittelbarkeiten sein. — Neben all dem hat unsere lange Überlegung des Einwandes von der möglichen Verrücktheit auch das Gute gehabt, uns ein für allemal auf die Gefahr des spezifisch erkenntnistheoretischen Zirkels aufmerksam zu machen, der darin besteht, daß man argumentierend, und ohne es zu merken, in Besonderheit benützt, was in der Allgemeinheit des kritischen Themas in Frage steht. —

The italic falls on "solipsistic" and the qualification follows it at once: "in a good sense" (*in gutem Sinne*). The phrase concedes that the word has a bad sense and claims a good one, and the rest of the sentence defines the good one by three restrictions. The critique is possible "only as a critique of my experiences"; it knows others "only as experienced ones of my experiences" (*nur als erfahrene meiner Erfahrungen*); and it "does not presuppose them as existing" because they are "standing critically in question". The doubled genitive, "experienced ones of my experiences", is awkward on purpose: others enter the critique as contents of mine, not as co-critics. And the ground of the restriction is stated as a status, not a doubt: they are not presupposed because they are "in question", the status Lecture 32 gave the whole world.

Two features of the wording make the rule stronger than the lecture's own example needs. The critique is the one incumbent on me "or that could ever be incumbent on me" (*oder je obliegen könnte*): not a feature of this beginner's situation but of any universal critique of experience. And the second sentence turns the rule into an order of time: a critique of other people's experience "can make sense only after" (*kann das nur Sinn haben, nachdem*) the critique of the mediacies of mine. The third sentence supplies the reason from Lecture 35, in its vocabulary. Other people's experiencing is there for me only "as experiencing that indicates itself through interpretive mediacy" (*durch interpretatorische Mittelbarkeit sich indizierendes*), "indicating itself in what I experience directly". Lecture 35's "perception through original interpretation" has become "interpretive mediacy". The word "mediacy" (*Mittelbarkeit*) is new and decisive: what Lecture 35 still called a kind of perception is here classed with the mediate, and "the primal ground of all critique" (*Urgrund aller Kritik*) must be the investigation of these mediacies "in my own consciousness". The solipsism is a consequence of the theory of empathy, not an independent decision.

The last sentence, set off by dashes, draws a lesson from the madness objection that outlasts it. The circle "consists in using in a particular case, while arguing and without noticing it, what stands in question in the generality of the critical theme" (*in Besonderheit benützt, was in der Allgemeinheit des kritischen Themas in Frage steht*). The opposition of particular and general is the whole definition. The objector who appeals to other people's sanity uses one case of the world, other people, to judge the world as a whole. "Without noticing it" makes the circle a danger, not a fallacy anyone commits on purpose, and "once and for all" (*ein für allemal*) makes it a standing guard. Lecture 37 opens by applying it to Husserl himself: have I, by leaving my own experience uncriticized, "really committed an **epistemological circle**?" (Hua VIII 71). The circle named here is the charge the next lecture must answer.

The passage closes the negative result. The preceding paragraph has just shown that within the hypothesis of a "senseless swarm" there is "no possibility of other people any more, mad or sane, to whom I could appeal" (Hua VIII 66). The following paragraph begins "After the conclusion of this meditation on mundane experience" and turns to what can be used from its result (Hua VIII 67). The solipsism is thus the methodological form of that result: the critique has nothing to stand on but my experience, and others count only as what that experience indicates.

The word returns three times, each time with a different qualifier. In Lecture 53b, after the reduction, it is no longer "in a good sense" but "transcendental", and it is stated as a necessity with a hedge. "A transcendental phenomenology can therefore, seemingly, be possible only as *transcendental egology*. As phenomenologist I am necessarily a solipsist, though not in the ordinary ridiculous sense, which is rooted in the natural attitude, but just in the transcendental one" (*Als Phänomenologe bin ich notwendig Solipsist, obschon nicht im gewöhnlichen lächerlichen Sinn … aber eben doch im transzendentalen*, Hua VIII 174). The good sense of Lecture 36 has become the transcendental sense, the bad sense "ridiculous", and the whole is "seemingly" (*scheinbar*) so. Two pages later the possibility is stated in its strongest form: "it *seems* conceivable that I am alone" (Hua VIII 176). In Lecture 54, finally, the solipsism is a predicament: if all true being is an ideal pole formed in my transcendental life, "then we stand in an uncomfortable situation — precisely that of transcendental solipsism" (*in einer unbehaglichen Situation*, Hua VIII 185). Between Lecture 36 and Lecture 54 the word passes from a rule of method to a thesis to an embarrassment. What Lecture 36 called "in a good sense" was a restriction on premises. The later lectures have to show that it was only that, and that the restriction does not become a doctrine about what there is.

## Lecture 37, Hua VIII 71: two meanings of "I"

Reads [Lecture 37, blocks 3–4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-37-the-apodictic-certainty-of-the-worlds-possible-non-existence-and-the-setting-off-of-the-transcendental-life-of-subjectivity).

The charge of a circle is answered by splitting a word. "I" in ordinary speech means the human being, an object in the world among others. But the same speech also names the one who finds that object, together with everything else: the subject for whom the world as a whole is there. If the critique of the world presupposed only the second "I", it presupposed nothing the critique had put in question, and there was no circle. The passage introduces this distinction as two meanings, and in the form of questions. What it does not yet do is show that the two meanings name two things. That is left to the thought-experiment that follows, and the wording keeps the two steps apart.

> It may be that such a critique, a critique above all of *self-awareness*, of *self-perceiving*, which gives the mundane critique its ground, is needed as well, and I already suspect that it may be of great importance. But what is first in *question* here is this: have I, by such an omission, *really* committed an **epistemological circle**? Is it not rather *clear* that here two *meanings* of I, and in further consequence of "my psychic life", "self-experience" and "self-knowledge", begin to stand out from one another?
>
> I, according to my ordinary talk of I, means I, the *human* **I**. Taken concretely and in full, I am an animated body, a psychophysical reality belonging to the world, the totality of realities. I am an *object* of my mundane experience among others. Must I not *separate* from this the I that is here the *subject* of the experience, the ego-subject for the ego-object? Considered more exactly: I, who live through a continuous world-experiencing, find this manifold yet unified world before me, and so, as the subject who finds everything, am precisely the subject for all objects, for the universe. As ordered into it I also find myself before me, that is, myself as object, this human I with all its "soul-life", belonging psychophysically to this physical body that I call mine, and embodying itself objectively in it. And this whole psychophysical reality is what it is in world-space, woven into the manifold concatenations of causality by which all worldly realities are <connected> with one another, directly or mediately, dependent on one another in space, causally bound to one another.

German:

> Es mag sein, daß solcher Kritik, einer Kritik vor allem des *Selbstinneseins*, *Selbstwahrnehmens*, das der mundanen Kritik ihren Boden gibt, ebenfalls bedarf, und ich ahne schon, daß sie von großer Wichtigkeit sein dürfte. Aber was hier zunächst in *Frage* ist: Habe ich durch solches Unterlassen *wirklich* einen **erkenntniskritischen Zirkel** begangen? Ist es nicht vielmehr *klar*, daß hier zweierlei *Bedeutungen* von Ich, und in weiterer Folge von ,,mein psychisches Leben'', ,,Selbsterfahrung'' und ,,Selbsterkenntnis'' sich abzuheben beginnen?
>
> Ich, gemäß meiner gewöhnlichen Ich-Rede, besagt Ich, das *Menschen*-**Ich**. Konkret voll genommen, bin ich beseelter Leib, psychophysische Realität, zur Welt, dem All der Realitäten gehörig. Ich bin ein *Objekt* meiner mundanen Erfahrung unter anderen. Muß ich davon nicht *scheiden* dasjenige Ich, das hierbei das *Subjekt* der Erfahrung ist, das Ichsubjekt für das Ichobjekt? Genauer überlegt: Ich, der ich ein kontinuierliches Welterfahren vollziehend durchlebe, finde diese mannigfaltig-einheitliche Welt vor und bin so als allvorfindendes Subjekt eben das Subjekt für alle Objekte, für das Weltall. Als diesem eingeordnet finde ich auch mich selbst vor, d.i. mich als Objekt, dieses menschliche Ich mit all seinem ,,Seelenleben'', psychophysisch zugehörig zu diesem physischen Leib, den ich den meinen nenne, sich in ihm objektiv verleiblichend. Und diese ganze psychophysische Realität ist, was sie ist, im Weltraum, eingeflochten in die mannigfachen Verkettungen der Kausalität, durch welche alle weltlichen Realitäten miteinander unmittelbar oder mittelbar <verknüpft>, im Raum aufeinander angewiesen, aneinander kausal gebunden sind.

*Note on the text.* The file splits "*Bedeu* - *tungen*" at a former line end, and sets the last word of "*Menschen* - I c h" in spaced letters; both are joined here, and the spaced type is given in bold, as in the other readings. The angle brackets are the editor's supplement. The passage begins on Hua VIII 71; the paragraph before it runs across the page break from 70.

The first paragraph concedes one thing in order to deny another. A critique "of *self-awareness*, of *self-perceiving*" (*des Selbstinneseins, Selbstwahrnehmens*) may well be owed, and Husserl "already suspects" its importance; that is the naivety the preceding paragraph admitted. But "what is first in *question*" is the circle, and the italics fall on the words that carry the denial: "*really*" (*wirklich*) and "*clear*" (*klar*). The second italic claims more than the sentence form allows. The distinction is put as a question, "Is it not rather clear …?", and the verb is inchoative: the two meanings "begin to stand out from one another" (*sich abzuheben beginnen*). Husserl asserts the evidence of a distinction whose terms he has not yet drawn. The phrase "in further consequence" (*in weiterer Folge*), which Lecture 28 used to put the ego after the will to philosophize, here carries the split from the pronoun to three phrases in quotation marks: "my psychic life", "self-experience", "self-knowledge". The quotation marks say that each of these phrases, used throughout the critique of the world, has been ambiguous all along. Lecture 38 will need exactly this: a self-experience that is not psychological.

The second paragraph defines the ordinary meaning first, and the verb is definitional: "I, according to my ordinary talk of I, means" (*besagt*) "the *human* I". The default sense of the word is the worldly one. The human I is then described in the vocabulary of the critique just finished: "animated body" (*beseelter Leib*), "psychophysical reality", belonging to "the totality of realities", "an *object* of my mundane experience among others". Each term places it inside what Lectures 33 and 34 showed need not exist. The other meaning is introduced by a question with an italicized verb, "Must I not *separate*" (*scheiden*), and named by a pair of coinages that differ only in their second element: "the ego-subject for the ego-object" (*das Ichsubjekt für das Ichobjekt*). The preposition does the defining. The subject-ego is not a second entity alongside the human ego but the one "for" whom the ego-object is there.

"Considered more exactly" (*Genauer überlegt*) then gives the subject-ego its scope. Husserl coins "the all-finding subject" (*allvorfindendes Subjekt*) and makes it "the subject for all objects, for the universe". The same verb, "find before me" (*vorfinden*), serves both meanings. As subject I find the world before me; "as ordered into it" (*als diesem eingeordnet*) I "also find myself before me", as object. The object-ego is one of the things the subject-ego finds. This asymmetry is the answer to the circle: the critique questioned what is found, and the finding is not among the found. The paragraph's last sentence piles up the worldly ties of the human ego (world-space, "concatenations of causality", "causally bound") because each is a tie that the annihilation of the world would cut.

The inference is weaker than "clear" makes it look, and the next paragraph says so. "Naturally this is not an accidental equivocation" (*Natürlich handelt es sich hier nicht um eine zufällige Äquivokation*, Hua VIII 71): passing from subject-ego to object-ego and back, I must recognize "in apodictic evidence" that they are identical, and must "incorporate" whatever reflection finds into the human being (Hua VIII 71–72). Two meanings of one word, with one referent, settle nothing about the circle, since the subject who critiques would still be the human being in the world. Husserl's way out is not semantic. "But despite all this … I cannot escape the distinction" (Hua VIII 72), because the thought-experiment shows that if the world were not, "not nothing would remain" (Hua VIII 72–73). Only then does the vocabulary change from meanings to being: "my human existence" (*mein menschliches Dasein*) against "my transcendental being" (*mein transzendentales Sein*, Hua VIII 73). The block in the commentary names the two meanings "the empirical and the transcendental ego"; the passage itself does not use "transcendental". It distinguishes subject from object, and the word "transcendental" is earned two pages later, by the annihilation argument, not by the distinction of meanings.

The passage is the hinge of Lecture 37. Before it stands the charge: I practised self-knowledge throughout the critique, though I belong to the world (Hua VIII 70). After it comes the identity that seems to undo the distinction, and then the thought-experiment that restores it. Landgrebe's summary in Beilage I records the step in the same order: the circle seems to arise, "but it is a matter of *two meanings of I*" (*zweierlei Bedeutungen von Ich*), the ego-object found in the world by the ego-subject, "which remains spared by its annihilation" (Hua VIII 73 in the summary, src L4154). Lecture 38 draws the methodological consequence. Nobody needs to discover his natural ego, but "transcendental subjectivity had first to be discovered", and Husserl stresses the verb: "I emphasize: *discover*" (*Ich betone: entdecken*, Hua VIII 78–79). The ordinary sense of "I" is given with every "I perceive"; the second sense is not a meaning one can read off ordinary speech, and needs a method. The phrase "begin to stand out" at Hua VIII 71 thus anticipates a discovery that the lecture's semantic framing cannot itself make.

The distinction does not stay a pair. Lecture 40 splits the ego again, within transcendental life, into a reflecting and a reflected ego ("Reflection as splitting of the ego", *Reflexion als Ichspaltung*, Hua VIII 87), and the identity of the two is there the problem, not the starting point. In the *Crisis* the pair returns as a paradox the theory must solve, and the subject is plural: "How is a component of the world, its human subjectivity, to constitute the whole world?" (*Wie soll ein Teilbestand der Welt, ihre menschliche Subjektivität, die ganze Welt konstituieren*, Hua VI §53, whose heading names "being a subject for the world and at the same time being an object in the world"). What Hua VIII 71 presented as a clear distinction of two meanings that a beginner cannot miss, the *Crisis* calls a paradox that arises from "the power of the self-evidence of the natural objective attitude" (*der Macht der Selbstverständlichkeit der natürlichen objektiven Einstellung*, Hua VI §53). The early version underestimates the identity that the next paragraph of Lecture 37 concedes. The later one starts from it.

## Lecture 38, Hua VIII 75–76: not a residuum but made visible

Reads [Lecture 38, block 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-38-the-field-of-transcendental-experience-as-theme-of-a-transcendental-critique).

The transcendental ego was not left over by the critique of the world. It became visible. The two descriptions differ in what they make of the critique. On the first, the critique works like a sieve: it removes what is doubtful and keeps what passes, and what passes is certified by passing. On the second, the critique removes the world and certifies nothing; what it does is turn the critic's attention from the world to the experiencing of it, where the ego was all along. Husserl corrects the first description by the second within a single sentence, and draws the consequence that the ego so found has still to be criticized. The passage withdraws from Lecture 37 the one thing its thought-experiment seemed to have earned, a certified ego.

> But when I was afterwards led back to "I am" and kept it after this critique, what was meant by it was no longer the I of natural talk of I, as if I, the human being, had rescued myself there from the epistemological end of the world. What was left over was rather I, the transcendental I; and this precisely because, as subject of the whole of world-knowledge, I did not belong to the known world, and on its exclusion became aware of myself in my purity. With this a highly significant advance had been made, a very promising horizon opened. Admittedly, the retaining of transcendental self-experience with its transcendental self by no means yet meant retaining <it> as an apodictic residuum of the apodictic critique of the world. Of that, after all, nothing at all was left, and nothing of the validity of the experience that gives it. But the being of this experience, and with it I as subject of the experiencing of this experience, now became visible in a turn of theoretical interest; it became visible that a self-experience was left over, namely remained unaffected by the mundane critique, and that, when I put the whole world out of validity, I myself lie constantly ready for myself as theme of experiences and other cognitions. Since in this way transcendental experience and the field of transcendental subjective being and life are opened, it is also opened as theme for a transcendental critique. This I must admittedly first supply, if I am to remain consistent with my philosophical principle of beginning, to let only apodictic evidences count as beginning. That, then, is a great task that still lies ahead of me.

German:

> Wenn ich nachher aber zum ,,Ich bin'' zurückgeführt wurde und es nach dieser Kritik übrig behielt, so war hiermit nicht mehr das Ich der natürlichen Ich-Rede gemeint, als ob ich, der Mensch, mich da aus dem erkenntniskritischen Weltuntergang gerettet hätte. Sondern übrig war ich, das transzendentale Ich; und das gerade, weil ich als Subjekt der gesamten Welterkenntnis nicht zur erkannten Welt gehörte und bei ihrer Ausschaltung meiner in meiner Reinheit inne wurde. Eben damit war ein höchst bedeutsamer Fortschritt vollzogen, ein vielversprechender Horizont eröffnet. Freilich, das Übrigbehalten der transzendentalen Selbsterfahrung mit ihrem transzendentalen Selbst besagte noch keineswegs ein Übrigbehalten <seiner> als apodiktisches Residuum der apodiktischen Kritik der Welt. Von dieser blieb ja gar nichts übrig, und nichts von der Geltung der sie gebenden Erfahrung. Aber das Sein dieser Erfahrung und damit Ich als Subjekt des Erfahrens dieser Erfahrung wurde in einer Wendung des theoretischen Interesses nun sichtlich; es wurde sichtlich, daß eine Selbsterfahrung übrig, nämlich durch die mundane Kritik unbetroffen blieb und daß, wenn ich die ganze Welt außer Geltung setze, ich selbst für mich als Thema von Erfahrungen und sonstigen Erkenntnissen immerzu bereit liege. Indem so die transzendentale Erfahrung und das Feld des transzendentalen subjektiven Seins und Lebens eröffnet ist, ist es auch als Thema für eine transzendentale Kritik eröffnet. Diese muß ich freilich allererst liefern, wenn ich bei meinem philosophischen Anfangsprinzip konsequent bleibe, als Anfang nur apodiktische Evidenzen gelten zu lassen. Das ist also eine große Aufgabe, die mir noch bevorsteht.

*Note on the text.* The passage runs across the page break from Hua VIII 75 to 76 ("Resi-" / "duum"); the file breaks the paragraph after "gerettet hätte" without a blank line, and it is read as one paragraph. The angle brackets are the editor's. A note marker follows "Horizont eröffnet" in the file, and the list for this passage took it to carry Husserl's marginal remark "That can be intended from the outset" (*Das kann von vornherein gewollt sein*). The evidence points elsewhere. The editor's notes for these pages are printed together inside Lecture 40, in page order: Beilage XIV, Beilage XV, the marginal remark, then two notes on Husserl's later criticism. The file has markers at the end of Lecture 37, at "Horizont eröffnet", and at the end of the first sentence of the next paragraph ("erschaubar zu machen"), where the critique is said to have acquired a "second function". In that order the remark falls on the second-function sentence. Landgrebe's summary in Beilage I hangs the same remark on the same sentence ("die (zweite) Funktion", with the note "Randbemerkung Husserls: ,Das kann von vornherein gewollt sein'"), and Beilage II gives its place as "ad 76, 17–26", which fits that sentence if the file's page marker for 77 is set a paragraph too early. The reading below takes the remark with the sentence about the second function.

The first sentence denies the natural reading of "I am" in the vocabulary of Lecture 37. What was kept "was no longer the I of natural talk of I" (*nicht mehr das Ich der natürlichen Ich-Rede*): the "ordinary talk of I" (*gewöhnliche Ich-Rede*) of Hua VIII 71 returns to name what is set aside. The image it rejects is precise: "as if I, the human being, had rescued myself from the epistemological end of the world" (*aus dem erkenntniskritischen Weltuntergang gerettet hätte*). "End of the world" (*Weltuntergang*) is the apocalyptic word for the thought-experiment, and "rescued" makes the human ego a survivor, the angel-of-death picture Lecture 37 refused. The reason given for the transcendental ego's remaining is not that it resisted doubt but that it never belonged to what was doubted: "as subject of the whole of world-knowledge, I did not belong to the known world". The critique did not reach it because it was the critic.

The correction turns on the word family of remaining. The paragraph uses "left over" (*übrig*) and "retaining" (*Übrigbehalten*) six times, and the vocabulary is Husserl's own from Lecture 37; it is not wrong but it invites a picture. "Admittedly" (*Freilich*) introduces the picture to reject it: retaining the ego "by no means yet meant" (*besagte noch keineswegs*) retaining it "as an apodictic residuum of the apodictic critique of the world". The definitional verb (*besagte*) says that the word "retaining" was being misread, not that a claim has changed. The double "apodictic" in the rejected phrase marks the error. A residuum of an apodictic critique would inherit the critique's apodicticity, as if what survived a test of necessary doubt were thereby indubitable. And the reason given is blunt: "Of that, after all, nothing at all was left" (*Von dieser blieb ja gar nichts übrig*). The critique of the world had one result, negative, and the ego is not part of it.

What replaces the residuum is a verb of seeing, repeated: the being of this experience "now became visible" (*wurde … nun sichtlich*), "it became visible that …". The cause is "a turn of theoretical interest" (*eine Wendung des theoretischen Interesses*). The phrase moves the transcendental ego from the result of an argument to the object of an attention, and anticipates Section Three's analysis of the reflecting ego as "uninterested" in the being of what it reflects on. The last image is of availability, not survival: when I put the world out of validity, "I myself lie constantly ready for myself as theme" (*ich selbst für mich als Thema … immerzu bereit liege*). "Lie ready" belongs to a field, not to a remnant, and the next sentence names it one: "the field of transcendental subjective being and life" (*das Feld des transzendentalen subjektiven Seins und Lebens*). Only a field can be "opened as theme for a transcendental critique"; a residuum certified by the critique would need none. "Admittedly" returns (*freilich*) to concede that this critique has not been supplied, and "first" (*allererst*) puts it before anything apodictic can be claimed for the ego. The task is deferred to Lecture 53a.

The passage sits between the summary of the way just taken and its methodological gloss. The paragraph after it gives the critique of the world "a second, very consequential function", acquired "afterwards" (*nachträglich*): to make visible "the transcendental subjectivity previously hidden" (Hua VIII 76–77). This is where Husserl's later remark belongs. If the critique of the world served, in fact, to make the ego visible, and if what makes it visible is a turn of interest rather than the critique's result, then "that can be intended from the outset". The remark draws the consequence of the passage read here: once the ego is no residuum, the critique of the world is no longer necessary to reach it, and a second way is possible. Beilage II spells it out: "I can … also go directly to transcendental subjectivity", criticizing outer experience "only quite briefly, or not at all" (Hua VIII 312). Section Three takes that way, from the reduction of single acts (Hua VIII 81 ff.).

Husserl's own later wording hardens the correction into a ban on the word. A note of Beilage XVIII (Hua VIII 432) says that talk of a phenomenological "residuum" (*Residuum*), like talk of "excluding" the world, "is better avoided", because it suggests that the world falls out of the phenomenological theme. That is a second objection to the word, about the world rather than the ego. The *Crisis*, criticizing Descartes, puts the first one as a thesis: "The *ego* is not a residuum of the world, but the absolutely apodictic positing that is made possible only through the epoché" (*Das ego ist nicht ein Residuum der Welt, sondern die absolut apodiktische Setzung*, Hua VI 81); it is the soul that is "the residuum of a prior abstraction". The two later texts agree with Hua VIII 75–76 against the title of *Ideas I* §49, "Absolute consciousness as residuum of world-annihilation". They disagree with it about apodicticity. Here the ego is opened for an apodictic critique that "still lies ahead"; in the *Crisis* it is the "absolutely apodictic positing". The 1923/24 lecture withholds exactly what the *Crisis* asserts, and its reason is the one the word "visible" carries: what has only become visible has not yet been tested.

## Lecture 38, Hua VIII 80: phenomenological and apodictic reduction

Reads [Lecture 38, block 10](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-38-the-field-of-transcendental-experience-as-theme-of-a-transcendental-critique).

Two operations that Descartes performed as one are here given two names and an order. The phenomenological (or transcendental) reduction gains a domain of experience: transcendental self-experience. The apodictic reduction tests the evidence of that experience for what in it cannot be cancelled. The second needs the first, as any critique needs something to criticize; the first does not need the second. Separating them lets Husserl hold that transcendental experience is open and usable while its apodicticity is still undecided. The passage presents the separation as a choice of method, made "deliberately", and the word that makes it possible is "field".

> I said that for *Descartes* transcendental subjectivity, the *ego cogito*, results at once as absolutely indubitable being. In contrast with the possibility of the experienced world's non-being, or, as *Descartes* prefers, the possibility of doubting it, the *ego cogito* stands out as what is absolutely indubitable for me. I, by contrast, have deliberately detached the method of transcendental reduction, in the present exposition, from the question of the apodictic validity of transcendental self-knowledge. I now distinguish this transcendental reduction, or phenomenological reduction, from the apodictic reduction connected with it. The latter designates a task first made possible by the phenomenological reduction. Before I exercise apodictic critique, I must have a field of critique, here a realm of experience, and this one, that of transcendental self-experience, I have only thanks to the method of phenomenological reduction.

German:

> Ich sagte, daß sich für *Descartes* die transzendentale Subjektivität, das *ego cogito*, sofort als absolut zweifelloses Sein ergibt. Im Kontrast mit der Nichtseinsmöglichkeit der erfahrenen Welt, oder wie *Descartes* bevorzugt, der Zweifelsmöglichkeit, hebt sich das *ego cogito* als absolut für mich Unzweifelbares ab. Ich habe demgegenüber mit Vorbedacht die Methode der transzendentalen Reduktion in der jetzigen Darstellung von der Frage der apodiktischen Gültigkeit der transzendentalen Selbsterkenntnis abgelöst. Ich unterscheide jetzt diese transzendentale Reduktion oder phänomenologische Reduktion von der mit ihr sich verknüpfenden apodiktischen Reduktion. Die letztere bezeichnet eine durch die phänomenologische Reduktion erst ermöglichte Aufgabe. Bevor ich apodiktische Kritik übe, muß ich ein Feld der Kritik, hier ein Erfahrungsreich haben, und dieses, das der transzendentalen Selbsterfahrung, habe ich erst dank der Methode der phänomenologischen Reduktion.

The paragraph opens by restating Descartes in two words that matter: the *ego cogito* results "at once" (*sofort*) as "absolutely indubitable being" (*absolut zweifelloses Sein*). "At once" is the fusion. For Descartes the same step that finds the ego certifies it. Husserl marks a second difference in passing: the world's "possibility of non-being", his own formula from Lectures 33–37, against Descartes' "possibility of doubting", which Descartes "prefers". The parenthesis matters. The critique of Section Two concerned the world's possible non-being, a claim about the world, not the critic's state of doubt; Husserl's ego stands out against that possibility, Descartes' against his doubt. The verb, "stands out" (*hebt sich … ab*), is the one Lecture 37 used for the two meanings of "I" that "begin to stand out" (Hua VIII 71). For Descartes the standing out is already the proof.

"By contrast" (*demgegenüber*) introduces Husserl's procedure, and three qualifiers make it a decision, not a discovery. "Deliberately" (*mit Vorbedacht*) says that the separation was intended through Lectures 33–38. "In the present exposition" (*in der jetzigen Darstellung*) restricts the claim to this way of presenting the method; it does not say that the reduction is independent of apodicticity in every presentation. And "detached" (*abgelöst*) is a verb of separation by handling, the word for peeling one thing off another to which it adhered. The adhesion was historical and Cartesian. "I now distinguish" (*Ich unterscheide jetzt*) then names the result: "this transcendental reduction or phenomenological reduction" on one side, "the apodictic reduction connected with it" (*mit ihr sich verknüpfenden*) on the other. The reflexive participle keeps the two tied: the apodictic reduction is not an alternative method but one that attaches to the first. The chapter title the editor gave this section, "Transcendental, phenomenological and apodictic reduction" (*Transzendentale, phänomenologische und apodiktische Reduktion*, in angle brackets in the file, Hua VIII 69), lists three names; the passage reduces them to two by an "or".

The asymmetry is carried by "first" (*erst*), used twice. The apodictic reduction "designates a task first made possible" (*eine … erst ermöglichte Aufgabe*) by the phenomenological reduction, and the field is had "only thanks to" (*erst dank*) its method. The phenomenological reduction is a method; the apodictic reduction is a "task" (*Aufgabe*), the word Lecture 38 used two pages earlier for the critique still owed ("a great task that still lies ahead of me", Hua VIII 76). The last sentence gives the reason as a general rule about critique: "Before I exercise apodictic critique, I must have a field of critique, here a realm of experience". "Field" (*Feld*) and "realm of experience" (*Erfahrungsreich*) are words for a domain one can survey and work in, and they carry the weight of the argument. If what the reduction yields is a field, it can be explored before it is certified, as natural experience was explored for centuries before Lecture 33 criticized it. If it were a premise, as the *ego cogito* is for Descartes, it would be useless until certified.

The inference has a gap the wording passes over. The rule says that apodictic critique needs a field, which is true of any critique. It does not show that the field can be had without any apodicticity at all. Husserl's own next paragraph concedes as much. It is "hardly avoidable and in any case useful" (*kaum vermeidlich und jedenfalls nützlich*) to carry out "a piece of this apodictic reduction, the beginning that forces itself on us at once" together with the first look at the new sphere (Hua VIII 80). The separation is clean as a distinction of tasks and porous in practice: Section Three opens with the apodictic core of the living present.

The passage closes the account of the Cartesian way. The preceding paragraph names the method "the *Cartesian method* of transcendental reduction" and says it brings out what lay hidden in the *Meditations*, "hidden from *Descartes* himself" (Hua VIII 80). The separation is what Descartes missed. What follows announces the programme of Section Three: a part of the apodictic reduction, and "questions of a possible variation of the method of phenomenological reduction", that is, the second way (Hua VIII 80).

The distinction returns as a working rule and as a debt. A note to Beilage XVII warns: "Not to be overlooked: that here we do not hold to apodictic evidence, but renounce apodictic reduction and perform only phenomenological reduction, as reduction to the 'stream of experience'" (*sondern auf apodiktische Reduktion verzichten und nur phänomenologische Reduktion … vollziehen*, Hua VIII 413). The verb is "renounce" (*verzichten*): the separation licenses phenomenological work that makes no apodictic claim. The debt falls due in Lecture 53a, whose editor's heading names "the philosophical task of an apodictic critique of transcendental experience". There the "apodictic evidence of the *ego cogito*" is "only a beginning and not an end", and memory, on which the transcendental past depends, "deceives often enough"; "so there still stands before us the great task of the **apodictic critique of transcendental experience**" (Hua VIII 169). The task is still "great", and still before us, ninety pages later. The *Cartesian Meditations* repeat the order and make the deferral explicit in a section title: "Necessity of first excluding the problems of the range of transcendental knowledge" (Hua I §13, p. 67). The "great task of a critique of transcendental self-experience" is there "a task of a higher level", which presupposes that we have first followed "the evidence of harmoniously progressing transcendental experience, functioning in a certain way naively" (*der in gewisser Weise naiv fungierenden Evidenz*, Hua I 67–68). In 1923/24 the separation was a choice "in the present exposition"; in 1929 it is a "necessity", and the field explored before its critique is explicitly called naive. Lecture 53a gives that naivety its name.

## Lecture 39, Hua VIII 85: the double transcendental reduction

Reads [Lecture 39, block 4](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-39-the-full-content-of-universal-transcendental-self-experience-transcendental-present-past-and-future).

A memory can be reduced twice. The first reduction takes the remembering as my present experience: with world-belief inhibited, "I remember" remains, reflectively perceived, now. The second goes into what is remembered and finds there, not the past walk as a worldly event, but my past perceiving, willing and doing, which the memory carries with it as "I perceived". Bracketing the remembered world does not cancel the remembered life. The result is a transcendental past, and with it the claim that the field opened in Lecture 38 is a stream with a history rather than a present point. The wording shows how much the second reduction takes on trust.

> Here I at once become aware that a *memory* yields me something *transcendental* in a twofold way. On one hand, I remember, and if I put the universe out of play, or inhibit the whole belief of experience that concerns it, this reflectively perceived "I remember" remains to me as my present experience. On the other hand, in this present experience my past walk on the Schloßberg presentifies itself to me. Admittedly, of the past event, which as an action of my human person was a psychophysically real event in the world, I may make no use in judgment; in so far as my recollection is belief in this real past, it, this belief, is put out of play by my phenomenological exclusion. But on closer view there lies co-included in my "I remember" an "I have perceived"; and in the past action: "I have willed and done". Though the Schloßberg, my body, my walking legs and so on may, as *past being*, never have happened, may be transcendental semblance, however it may stand with their being, the whole continuity of perceiving through which path and goal counted for me perceptually as actuality, and the striving, willing, doing that was, together with the perceiving, my acting experience, is not cancelled by the abstention from judgment on mundane being. So I have not only the "I remember" given now as present transcendental experience, but, included in it, the memory of my past transcendental life. That evidently holds for every memory. Each evidently admits a *double transcendental reduction*, one of which yields the memory as my transcendental present experience, while the second, reaching in a remarkable way into the reproductive content of the memory, discloses a piece of my past transcendental life.

German:

> Hier werde ich alsbald darauf aufmerksam, daß eine *Erinnerung* mir doch in doppelter Weise *transzendentales* ergibt: Einerseits erinnere ich mich, und wenn ich das Weltall außer Spiel setze bzw. den gesamten Erfahrungsglauben, der es betrifft, inhibiere, so bleibt mir dieses reflektiv wahrgenommene „Ich erinnere mich“ als mein jetziges Erleben. Andererseits vergegenwärtigt sich mir in diesem jetzigen Erleben mein vergangener Schloßbergspaziergang. Freilich, von dem vergangenen Geschehen, das als ein Handeln meiner menschlichen Person ein psychophysisch-reales Geschehen in der Welt war, darf ich keinen Urteilsgebrauch machen; sofern meine Wiedererinnerung Glaube an diese reale Vergangenheit ist, ist sie, ist dieser Glaube durch meine phänomenologische Ausschaltung außer Spiel gesetzt. Aber näher besehen liegt doch in meinem „Ich erinnere mich“ mitbeschlossen ein „Ich habe wahrgenommen“; und in der vergangenen Handlung: „Ich habe gewollt und getan“. Mag der Schloßberg, mein Leib, meine gehenden Beine usw. als *vergangenes Sein* ungeschehen, transzendentaler Schein sein, mag es mit seinem Sein wie immer stehen, die ganze Kontinuität des Wahrnehmens, durch das Weg und Ziel für mich wahrnehmungsmäßig als Wirklichkeit galt, und das Streben, Wollen, Tun, das in eins mit dem Wahrnehmen mein handelndes Erleben war, ist durch die Urteilsenthaltung über das mundane Sein nicht aufgehoben. Also nicht nur habe ich als gegenwärtiges transzendentales Erleben jetzt gegeben das „Ich erinnere mich“, sondern in ihm beschlossen die Erinnerung an mein vergangenes transzendentales Leben. Das gilt offenbar für jede Erinnerung. Jede läßt evidenterweise eine *doppelte transzendentale Reduktion* zu, deren eine die Erinnerung als mein transzendentales gegenwärtiges Erleben ergibt, während die zweite, in merkwürdiger Weise in den reproduktiven Gehalt der Erinnerung hineingreifend, ein Stück meines vergangenen transzendentalen Lebens enthüllt.

*Note on the text.* The passage stands on Hua VIII 85, after the example's opening on p. 84. In the next sentence, which runs over to p. 86, the file repeats the end of a word across the page break ("Reduktion." / "tion, so erschaue ich"); the reading does not quote it.

The first reduction is stated in the vocabulary of Lecture 38: put the universe "out of play", "inhibit" the belief of experience, and "I remember" "remains" (*bleibt*). Its object is a present act, "reflectively perceived", and nothing in it goes beyond the living present. The second reduction is introduced by a reflexive verb that gives the initiative to the past: my walk "presentifies itself to me" (*vergegenwärtigt sich mir*). "Admittedly" (*Freilich*) then takes back what the reflexive seemed to offer. The walk as "a psychophysically real event in the world" may not be used, and the sentence names exactly what is excluded: "in so far as my recollection is belief in this real past, it, this belief, is put out of play". The self-correction "it, this belief" (*ist sie, ist dieser Glaube*) narrows the exclusion from the memory to the belief in it. It is that narrowing which leaves room for the second reduction.

The second reduction finds its object by a verb of implication: in my "I remember" there lies "co-included" (*mitbeschlossen*) an "I have perceived". The quotation marks set the two first-person sentences side by side, present and perfect. What is implied is not the walk but my having perceived it, and the action adds a second perfect, "I have willed and done". The concessive sentence that follows is the hinge. "Though the Schloßberg, my body, my walking legs … may, as *past being*, never have happened" (*ungeschehen … sein*): the italics fall on "past being", and the coinage "never have happened" turns the possible non-being of the world, Lecture 33's discovery, into the possible non-being of the past world. Against that, "the whole continuity of perceiving through which path and goal counted for me … as actuality" "is not cancelled" (*nicht aufgehoben*). "Counted as" (*galt*) is the word for validity, and it is in the past tense: what the second reduction keeps is the past validity of the past world for me, the having-counted, not the world that counted.

Two adverbs carry the claim further than the analysis does. "That evidently (*offenbar*) holds for every memory", and each memory "evidently" (*evidenterweise*) admits the double reduction. The generalization is from one example to every memory, and the evidence is asserted twice in two sentences. Yet the passage also calls the second reduction one that reaches into the memory's content "in a remarkable way" (*in merkwürdiger Weise … hineingreifend*). "Remarkable" admits what "evidently" passes over. The first reduction works on what I perceive now. The second works on what I perceive only through the memory, so that its result, a "piece of my past transcendental life", is given only as reliably as the memory gives it. The exclusion was limited to "belief in this real past"; the belief that I did perceive, in the past, is not excluded. It cannot be, if the second reduction is to yield anything, and the passage does not raise the question. Lecture 53a does: "my transcendental past is certain to me, but does not memory, to which alone I owe it, deceive often enough?" (Hua VIII 169).

The passage stands at the turn from the present cogito to the stream. Lecture 39 has just said that memory and, "in a way", expectation are experience too (Hua VIII 84). The sentence after the passage follows the chain of recollections back to the present and forward into pasts ever more remote, and finds my transcendental life reaching back into an "endless past" (Hua VIII 85–86). The future gets an analogous double reduction in the next paragraph, with the reservation that expecting is not seeing again (Hua VIII 86). Without the double reduction the field would be the point Descartes' ego occupies; with it, the field has a time-form of its own, which Lecture 39 announces at its close.

The structure returns, generalized, in Section Three. Lecture 46 looks back on its analyses of presentifying acts (memory, expectation, depiction, phantasy) and states their point: their intentional relation "is not a simple one, as it appears at first", for in each one must distinguish the straight look at what is presentified from a second direction of view (Hua VIII 131). It adds that "for memory and expectation we had already shown it fleetingly in the first, Cartesian reduction", that is, here. Beilage XX, Husserl's later critique of his reductions of 1907 and 1910, dates the device and so corrects the impression the lecture gives. The 1910 lectures, he writes, already rested "on the demonstration of the double reduction that all presentifications can undergo, namely reduction of the presentifications as present experiences and reduction 'in' the presentifications" (*auf dem Nachweis der doppelten Reduktion, die alle Vergegenwärtigungen erfahren können*, Hua VIII 434). The 1910/11 text survives in the corpus, and its wording anticipates the lecture's: "Then there appeared the most remarkable thing, that every experience admits a double phenomenological reduction, once the one that brings it to pure immanent seeing, and the other time the one that is practised on its intentional content and object" (*Da zeigte sich nun das höchst Merkwürdige, dass jede Erfahrung eine doppelte phänomenologische Reduktion zulässt*, Hua XIII 178, Text Nr. 6, §34). "Remarkable" is there in 1910, and so is "every". What changes by 1923/24 is the name and the use. "Phenomenological" has become "transcendental", and in 1910 the device served the discovery of other egos (Hua XIII §39 derives "other phenomenological egos through double phenomenological reduction"); in 1923/24 it first serves the ego's own past. Lecture 47 will extend it to empathy, the use with which 1910 began.

## Lecture 40, Hua VIII 90: latent and patent

Reads [Lecture 40, block 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-40-reflection-as-splitting-of-the-ego-and-the-identity-of-the-ego-in-the-streaming-living-present).

The ego that performs an act is not aware of itself performing it; it becomes an object for itself only when a further ego reflects on it, and that further ego is in the same condition. Husserl needs a name for the condition, and the names to hand are wrong because they describe it as a loss: forgetting presupposes prior awareness, and "unconscious" is ambiguous. He replaces them with a pair, latent and patent, that describes it as a mode. Latency comes first; patency is something that happens to a latent ego, and only through another latent ego. The terminological remark turns a description of reflection into a law with a remainder: whatever reflection discloses, the disclosing is not disclosed.

> One more terminological remark: talk of *self-forgetfulness* is not apt, since, according to the normal talk of forgetting, a conscious awareness would have had to precede, on which the forgetting of what had already been in awareness would have to follow. But obviously the mode of self-forgetfulness, considered in itself, is here the preceding one. On the other hand the expression "ego unconscious of itself" does not fit either, by virtue of the ambiguities of talk of consciousness. We could rather speak of a *latent* ego, and in contrast of a *patent* one. Accordingly we should have to say: an ego, a waking ego, an ego performing an act, becomes patent, and an act itself becomes patent, only through the appearance of an ego reflecting on it, which is in its turn latent. Further: becoming patent in this way is possible for every latent ego, and so also for that of every reflection. It consists in the reflecting ego's being the performer of an act that makes the previously latent ego the object of the act, the intentional object.

German:

> Noch eine terminologische Bemerkung: Die Rede von *Selbstvergessenheit* ist nicht passend, da ja, der normalen Rede von Vergessen gemäß, ein bewußtes Innesein vorangegangen sein müßte, dem das Vergessen des schon Innegewesenen zu folgen hätte. Offenbar ist aber hier der Modus der Selbstvergessenheit, an sich betrachtet, der vorangehende. Andererseits paßt auch der Ausdruck ,,seiner selbst unbewußtes Ich'', vermöge der Vieldeutigkeiten der Rede von Bewußtsein, nicht. Eher könnten wir von einem *latenten* Ich sprechen, und demgegenüber von einem *patenten*. Wir hätten danach zu sagen: Patent wird ein Ich, ein waches, ein Vollzugsich eines Aktus, und patent wird ein Aktus selbst nur durch Auftritt eines darauf reflektierenden Ich, das seinerseits latent ist. Ferner: Solcher Art Patentwerden ist für jedes latente Ich, also auch das jeder Reflexion möglich. Es besteht darin, daß das reflektierende Ich vollziehendes eines Aktus ist, der das vordem latente zum Objekt des Aktus, zum intentionalen Objekt macht.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "Vollzugsicch"; the obvious misspelling of "Vollzugsich" (performing ego) is corrected silently in the quotation.

The objection to "self-forgetfulness" (*Selbstvergessenheit*) is an argument from the grammar of the word, and Husserl says so: "according to the normal talk of forgetting" (*der normalen Rede von Vergessen gemäß*). To forget, one must have known; the word implies an order of time, awareness first and forgetting after. The lecture has used the word freely for three pages: the ego absorbed in perceiving the house is "self-forgotten", and so is the reflecting ego of the second level (Hua VIII 89–90). The remark does not withdraw the description but its implied history. "Obviously" (*Offenbar*) the order is the reverse: "the mode of self-forgetfulness, considered in itself, is here the preceding one" (*der vorangehende*). "Considered in itself" (*an sich betrachtet*) separates the mode from the word: what the word calls a lapse is in fact the original condition. The second expression, "ego unconscious of itself", is refused for a different reason, and more briefly: "the ambiguities of talk of consciousness". Husserl does not say which. The phrase would make the performing ego "unconscious" while its act is a consciousness of the house, so the refusal saves the word "consciousness" for intentional life.

The replacement is put tentatively, "We could rather speak" (*Eher könnten wir … sprechen*), and the two words are the only italics in the remark after the rejected "*self-forgetfulness*". Latent and patent are a Latin pair, hidden and open, and they bring no history with them: something latent need never have been patent. That is the point of choosing them. They also bring no deficiency. A latent ego is not a defective conscious ego but one that is functioning and not thematized. The pair is then defined at once by an asymmetry. An ego "becomes patent" (*wird … patent*), so patency is an event; latency is what it is before the event and needs no verb. And the event has a condition: "only through the appearance of an ego reflecting on it, which is in its turn latent" (*nur durch Auftritt eines darauf reflektierenden Ich, das seinerseits latent ist*). "In its turn" (*seinerseits*) makes the law self-applying. Every disclosure is made by an undisclosed ego.

"Further" (*Ferner*) adds the universality and the mechanism. "Becoming patent" (*Patentwerden*), now a noun, "is possible for every latent ego, and so also for that of every reflection". No ego is in principle hidden; each can be reached by one more reflection. The last sentence defines the event in the vocabulary of intentionality: the reflecting ego performs an act "that makes the previously latent ego the object of the act, the intentional object". Becoming patent is being made an intentional object. The definition has a cost the remark does not draw. If patency is objecthood, then the ego as performer, the ego that functions, is patent only as what it was, never as what it is doing now. The iteration that Lecture 40 has just described ("each new, higher reflection brings a new ego as performing subject on stage", Hua VIII 89) is the form of that cost. The remark gives it its terms: an open series of disclosures, each with a latent remainder.

The remark sits between the description of the iterated reflection and the question that occupies the rest of Lecture 40: why speak of "the same ego" when every act has its own ego-pole, and why use the image of a "splitting" (*Spaltung*), which implies a unity divided, "as a split tree trunk need not yet be split into completely separate pieces" (Hua VIII 90)? The answer comes in the next paragraph, and it uses the new word. In higher reflection I can bring into one view "a reflecting ego that has itself already become patent" (*ein selbst schon patent gewordenes reflektierendes Ich*), and see that "the 'many' act-poles are evidently in themselves the same ego" (Hua VIII 91). Identity is seen only across patent egos, from a latent one.

The pair then does work across the course. At the opening of Lecture 42 the reflection that becomes uninterested starts "from the naive-latent performance of 'I perceive the house'" (*vom naiv-latenten Vollzug*, Hua VIII 98), so "latent" now qualifies the naive attitude as such. In Lecture 44 phantasy has its own latency: I make patent in phantasy the reflections "through which my previously latent act would have been disclosed" (*mein vordem latenter Akt*, Hua VIII 117). In Lecture 52 the word has become a programme: the horizons of every present and past consciousness "are to be unfolded according to their latent implications" (*nach ihren latenten Implikationen*, Hua VIII 165). Beilage XVI, attached to this lecture by the editor, divides the term and gives it a second, social sense: "a double latency of the ego for itself", first "the original latency of the subject not yet developed into 'self-consciousness'", then "the latency of the human ego that is act-subject" (Hua VIII 409); the second is the one at issue here, and the Beilage puts "self-forgotten" back into quotation marks. The *Crisis* keeps the structure and changes the word. When the functioning "we-subjectivity" becomes thematic, its acts become thematic too, "though always with a remainder that is unthematic, that remains so to speak in anonymity, namely as the reflections functioning for this thematic" (*in Anonymität bleibt*, Hua VI 111, §28). That is the law of Hua VIII 90 with "anonymity" for latency, and with the remainder, which the 1923/24 remark leaves implicit in "in its turn latent", now said outright.

## Lecture 42, Hua VIII 105: value-ception

Reads [Lecture 42, block 11](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-42-the-most-general-concept-of-interest-of-attitude-of-theme).

A value is given in feeling, not in perception. Perceptions present the artwork as a thing; feeling, built on them, presents it as a value, and when feeling is fulfilled, in full enjoyment, the value is there "itself". Husserl names the fulfilling act on the model of perception: as perception (*Wahrnehmung*) gives things, "value-ception" (*Wertnehmung*) gives values. The coinage claims that values have their own mode of intuitive givenness and their own evidence, and it fixes an order: a theoretical interest in values can begin only when a value has already been felt. In Lecture 42 the point serves a theory of interest. The ego can live in a valuing interest as it lives in a cognitive one, and passing from the one to the other is a change of attitude.

> But the interest can also be an *interest of feeling*, an interest valuing in the widest sense, the intention value-thematic, the theme a value-theme, a theme of feeling. The ego now wants to live itself out in valuing. It wants, say, to progress from the anticipatory valuing, an anticipatory liking of the work of art first seen fleetingly, to the fulfilled or ever more richly fulfilled value, and finally, in the completed fulfilment of the founding valuation, to attain the value itself; that is, it wants to attain in itself, in the mode of full and pure enjoyment of art (the synthetic unification of value-intentions continuously fulfilling one another), the aesthetic object as this concrete value, as *energeia* of the earlier mere *dynamis*, as the final and actual self-giving and self-having of the value, which is nothing other than the pure and saturated enjoyment of art. This takes place, then, not in judging but in valuing, and in so far as perceptions play their essential role in it, they function only as serving acts, as presuppositions, under-positings, ground-positings for the valuing feelings and feelings-out founded in them. The value itself in its value-truth is not perceived but, as it were, value-ceived; what perception achieves for the mere thing, value-ception achieves for value. It is the fulfilment-mode of valuing feeling. The aesthetic beholder lives in valuing interest, and only through a turning of interest, a change of attitude as change of interest, does he pass over, as we said earlier, into being-positing experience and theoretical contemplation, for example as scholar of art; but this can begin only when the aesthetic interest has been exercised and the value, in it the *telos* of feeling, lies ready, so to speak, as [valued], value-ceptively [self-valued], for a theoretical interest to be directed to it, and first for a perception directed at the aesthetic formation, the perception of the value-object. —

German:

> Das Interesse kann aber auch ein *Gemütsinteresse* sein, ein im weitesten Sinn wertendes Interesse, die Intention eine wertthematische, das Thema ein Wertthema, Gemütsthema. Das Ich will sich nun wertend ausleben. Es will etwa über das vorfühlende Werten, ein vorfühlendes Gefallen an dem zuerst flüchtig gesehenen Kunstwerk zum erfüllten oder sich immer reicher erfüllenderen Wert fortschreiten und schließlich in der vollendeten Erfüllung der fundierenden Wertung den Wert selbst erzielen, d.i. es will in sich, im Modus des vollen und reinen Kunstgenusses (der synthetischen Einigung der sich kontinuierlich ineinander erfüllenden Wertintentionen), das ästhetische Objekt als diesen konkreten Wert in sich erzielen, als *ἐνέργεια* der früheren bloßen *δύναμις*, als letzte und wirkliche Selbstgabe und Selbsthabe des Wertes, die eben nichts anderes ist als der reine und gesättigte Kunstgenuss. Das vollzieht sich also nicht im Urteilen, sondern im Werten, und sofern Wahrnehmungen dabei ihre wesentliche Rolle spielen, so fungieren sie nur als dienende Akte, als Voraussetzungen, Untersetzungen, Grundsetzungen für die in ihnen fundierten wertenden Fühlungen und Erfühlungen. Der Wert selbst in seiner Wertwahrheit wird nicht wahrgenommen, sondern gleichsam wertgenommen; was für das bloße Ding die Wahrnehmung leistet, leistet für den Wert die Wertnehmung. Sie ist der Erfüllungsmodus des wertenden Fühlens. Der ästhetische Beschauer lebt im wertenden Interesse, und erst durch Umwendung des Interesses, Änderung der Einstellung als Interessenänderung, geht er, wie wir früher gesagt haben, über ins seinssetzende Erfahren und theoretische Betrachten, z.B. als Kunstwissenschaftler; dies kann aber erst einsetzen, wenn das ästhetische Interesse sich betätigt hat und der Wert, darin das *τέλος* des Gemüts, als erweiterter, wertnehmend selberwerteteter sozusagen bereit liegt für ein darauf zu richtendes theoretisches Interesse, und zunächst für eine auf das ästhetische Gebilde gerichtete Wahrnehmung, die Wahrnehmung des Wertobjekts. —

*Note on the text.* Near the end the file has "als erweiterter, wertnehmend selberwerteteter", which is not good German. The first word is probably an OCR error for "erwerteter" (valued, attained in valuing): Lecture 43 speaks of what acts "value-ceptively" confer on "what is felt and valued" (*ihrem fühlend Erwerteten*, Hua VIII 108). The second looks like a garbled "selbst erwerteter" or "selbstgewerteter". The rendering puts both in brackets, and the reading does not rest on them. The Greek is clean.

The English coinage has to stand in for a German one that works by substitution. *Wahrnehmung*, perception, is built from *wahr*, true, and *nehmen*, to take: a taking-as-true. Husserl replaces the first element with *Wert*, value, and gets *Wertnehmung*, a taking-as-valuable. He introduces it first as a verb and with a hedge: the value "is not perceived but, as it were, value-ceived" (*nicht wahrgenommen, sondern gleichsam wertgenommen*). "As it were" (*gleichsam*) flags the new word. The noun follows in a strict proportion, "what perception achieves for the mere thing, value-ception achieves for value", and then a definition, "the fulfilment-mode of valuing feeling" (*der Erfüllungsmodus des wertenden Fühlens*). The phrase that precedes it, "the value itself in its value-truth" (*in seiner Wertwahrheit*), carries the same substitution in reverse: value has a truth of its own, and the coinage says where that truth is had. The play on *wahr* is the argument. If perception is a taking of what is true, value-ception is a taking of what is valuable as it is, and feeling is credited with its own evidence.

The vocabulary of evidence confirms it. The fulfilled enjoyment is "the final and actual self-giving and self-having of the value" (*Selbstgabe und Selbsthabe des Wertes*). Self-giving is Husserl's word for evidence in the sphere of cognition, and here it is applied without change to feeling. The progression follows the pattern of perceptual fulfilment as well: from "anticipatory valuing" (*vorfühlendes Werten*), a liking of the work "first seen fleetingly", to the "ever more richly fulfilled value", to the "completed fulfilment". The Aristotelian terms give the end-state its form. The aesthetic object reached in enjoyment is the "*energeia* of the earlier mere *dynamis*", the actuality of what the anticipatory liking had only as possibility, and the value is "the *telos* of feeling" (*das τέλος des Gemüts*). The Greek turns a sequence of acts into a movement toward an end that is reached. A value fulfilled is not an inference from a pleasant state but the state's completion.

Two features of the wording keep perception in its place. The perceptions involved "function only as serving acts" (*nur als dienende Akte*), and Husserl stacks three nouns for their role, "presuppositions, under-positings, ground-positings" (*Voraussetzungen, Untersetzungen, Grundsetzungen*). The series descends spatially (pre-, under-, ground-) and every member contains "positing" (*-setzung*): perceptions posit the thing on which the feeling is built, but they do not give the value. And the feelings built on them are doubled, "feelings and feelings-out" (*Fühlungen und Erfühlungen*), the prefix of attainment answering the prefix of anticipation in "anticipatory liking" (*vorfühlend*). Value-ception is the "feeling-out" in which the value is reached.

The inference rests on one case and an analogy. The case is aesthetic enjoyment, where the value is plausibly present in the enjoying; the proportion with perception is then stated for "value" without qualification. And "as it were" concedes that the analogy is an analogy. Lecture 42 does not argue that every value has a fulfilment-mode in feeling, or that the "value-truth" of a value is settled by fulfilment as a thing's being is by perception. It makes the claim needed for the theory of interest: that valuing has its own goal and its own completion, and so can be a theme as cognition is.

That is the passage's work between its neighbours. Before it, cognitive interest has been described as aiming at "being" in verifying determination, with the scientist's habitual "professional attitude" (Hua VIII 104). The valuing interest is the second kind, and the order the passage sets is the point of the comparison: the scholar of art begins "only when the aesthetic interest has been exercised" (*erst einsetzen, wenn das ästhetische Interesse sich betätigt hat*) and the value "lies ready" (*bereit liegt*) for a theoretical look. The phrase is the one Lecture 38 used for the ego after the reduction, which "lies constantly ready" as theme (Hua VIII 76). A theme is what lies ready for an interest. After the passage, the lecture turns to "reflections of feeling" such as regret and conscience (Hua VIII 105), in which the reflecting act is itself a valuing.

The coinage is then put to the use the course needs, in Lecture 43. When I reflect on my enjoyment of an artwork, I am normally "co-interested" in its value, a co-performer of the acts "through which the artwork is there for me not only as an existing thing but as an artwork" (Hua VIII 107). The phenomenologist abstains from that co-performance. He is not "the ego that, in its value-ceiving doing (*in seinem wertnehmenden Tun*), wins validity for feeling (*Gemütsgeltung*) for the value-form of the picture", and he can be interested purely in the fact that the acts confer "value-ceptively on what they value-ceive (*wertnehmend ihrem Wertgenommenen*), on what is felt and valued, the validity of something beautiful" (Hua VIII 107–108). Value-ception, which Lecture 42 presented as a fulfilment, is here a conferral of validity, and so falls under the epoché as perception does. The analogy with perception that Lecture 42 left as "as it were" becomes structural: values, like things, are validities, and bracketing reaches both. The commentary finds the term again at the end of Lecture 50. That passage stood on Hua VIII 157, the page lost from the file: the file breaks off in the sentence "We can include under the concept of validity of being all other validities too, such as those from" (*Wir können dabei unter den Begriff der Seinsgeltung auch alle anderen Geltungen, wie die aus*, Hua VIII 156), and resumes on p. 158. That return cannot be checked here. The word was not new in 1923. *Ideas II* already uses it, glossed in the same way: an intuitive valuing "plays the role of a value-'perception' (in our parlance: value-ception), in which the value-character is originally given intuitively itself" (*die Rolle einer Wert-„Wahrnehmung“ (in unserer Rede: Wertnehmung)*, Hua IV 187, §50), with the example of a violin's tone that moves the feeling. "In our parlance" marks it as Husserl's own established term. What Lecture 42 adds is not the coinage but its place: value-ception as the end of an interest, and so as the ground on which a change of attitude, and finally the epoché, can operate.

## Lecture 43, Hua VIII 108: a "phenomenological" epoché, by no means transcendental

Reads [Lecture 43, block 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-43-the-possibility-of-a-pure-interest-in-subjective-being-in-phenomenological-epoch-and-reflection).

The word "epoché" enters the body of the course here, in Lecture 43, and it enters already restricted. A reflecting ego that refuses to share the positings of the acts it reflects on can perform an abstention on any single act. That abstention is "phenomenological" because what it yields is the pure act; but it is so only provisionally, in a sense that will prove "restricted" and "by no means transcendental". The sentence names the method and limits it in one breath, and the limit is what Section Four will have to overcome. What it does not say is why the restricted epoché is not transcendental. The juxtaposition of "restricted" and "by no means transcendental" suggests that the restriction to single acts is the reason, and that suggestion is the point Husserl later rejected.

> In this way I can, with respect to every act that I performed straightforwardly, perform a "phenomenological" epoché, as we can already say here in a provisional sense — in a sense, as will be shown, restricted and *by no means transcendental*.

German:

> In dieser Art kann ich in Bezug auf jeden Akt, den ich geradehin vollzog, eine „phänomenologische“ ἐποχή vollziehen, wie wir schon hier in einem vorläufigen Sinn sagen können — in einem, wie sich zeigen wird, beschränkten und *keineswegs transzendentalen Sinn*.

*Note on the text.* The file sets the Greek word as LaTeX garble (`$\text{\epsilon}\pi\text{o}\chi\text{\eta}$`), here and in the next sentence; it is given in Greek as the print has it. The italics fall on "keineswegs transzendentalen Sinn" in the file, the noun included.

Until this sentence the course has had no single word for its method. Section One spoke of the Cartesian "overthrow" (*Umsturz*); Section Two of putting the world "out of play" (*außer Spiel setzen*), "out of validity", of "inhibiting" belief, of "abstention from judgment" (*Urteilsenthaltung*, Hua VIII 85), and Lecture 38 of the "phenomenological reduction". The Greek word appears in the file's body text for the first time at this point (the only earlier hit is the editor's lecture title, if the title is his). It is introduced with three hedges. "Phenomenological" stands in quotation marks: the epoché is called by the name of the discipline, but the marks hold the name at a distance. "As we can already say here in a provisional sense" (*wie wir schon hier in einem vorläufigen Sinn sagen können*) makes the naming an anticipation: "already" (*schon*) concedes that the name belongs properly to something later. And the dash introduces a restriction announced as a result to come: "as will be shown" (*wie sich zeigen wird*).

The scope of the sentence is set by "with respect to every act that I performed straightforwardly" (*in Bezug auf jeden Akt, den ich geradehin vollzog*). The epoché is available for every act, but it is performed act by act. "Straightforwardly" (*geradehin*) names the naive performance that Lecture 40 called latent: the act is done, and the abstention comes after, in reflection. The tense (*vollzog*, performed) marks this. The epoché is the reflecting ego's refusal to take over the reflected ego's positings, and the next sentence defines it so: an epoché "that refuses to all thematic objects of the respective acts the interest of validity" (Hua VIII 108). It is the attitude of the "uninterested spectator" of Lecture 41, now named.

The italic falls on the denial, "*by no means transcendental*" (*keineswegs transzendentalen Sinn*), and "by no means" is stronger than "not yet". The two adjectives are joined by "and" (*beschränkten und keineswegs transzendentalen*): restricted and not transcendental. Nothing in the sentence says that the second follows from the first. But nothing else in Lecture 43 explains the denial, and the restriction to single acts is the only limit the passage names. The preceding paragraph has stated the yield of the reflective abstention "now in the fullest generality": the purely subjective is what it is "even if" the act-objects "were in *truth not*", "prior to any critical question of their right" (Hua VIII 108). That is the formula of Section Two's transcendental residue, reached without the hypothesis of the world's non-being. The denial in the next sentence holds it back from being transcendental, and the only visible ground is that it is gained one act at a time while the world as a whole remains valid behind each act.

Section Three keeps the reservation and softens it. Lecture 46, summing up the single-act analyses, calls the reflecting ego's finding "the phenomenologically pure act; this purity is a phenomenological one, but admittedly only in a first and still imperfect, not yet transcendental sense", "only phenomenological purity in the sense of an empirical psychology" (Hua VIII 128). "By no means" has become "not yet" (*noch nicht*), and the gap is now named: what the single-act epoché yields is pure psychology. Husserl's later hand went further. Beside those two sentences he wrote "No!", and he struck "not yet transcendental" (the editor's note, printed in the file inside Lecture 48, at Hua VIII 146). Lecture 48 then takes the restriction as the problem to be solved: how to pass from the psychological reduction of single acts to a universal epoché (Hua VIII 139 ff.), and Lecture 51 performs the universal epoché.

Beilage XXIII, Husserl's "Critique of the false presentation of the difference between psychological and transcendental reduction in the winter lecture of 1923/24", removes the ground the passage implied. "It is not to be seen why the emphasis is laid on continuous reduction of the single acts" (Hua VIII 445). The psychologist who wants his own pure soul "must in fact practise a *universal epoché* with respect to the world", and this universal epoché is still "for me as psychologist only a means of purification": "my positive universal field, on which I stand, … is the world", held "in final validity" (*in Endgeltung*, Hua VIII 445). A universal epoché can therefore be psychological, and the difference from the transcendental cannot be the difference between single and universal. It lies in whether the world remains the ground on which the purified soul is placed. Read against the Beilage, the sentence of Lecture 43 is right in its verdict and misleading in its implied reason. The single-act epoché is not transcendental, but not because it is single. It is not transcendental because the reflecting ego still counts itself, and the act it purifies, as part of a world that stays valid. The provisional name "phenomenological", in quotation marks, was the honest part of the sentence.

## Lecture 43, Hua VIII 111: the bracket as index

Reads [Lecture 43, block 8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-8-erste-philosophie-ii-commentary.md#lecture-43-the-possibility-of-a-pure-interest-in-subjective-being-in-phenomenological-epoch-and-reflection).

To bracket an object is not to stop seeing it but to see it with a mark attached. The mark is an index that says, of this object, that its validity is not shared: it counts only as the object of the act that confers validity on it. The object stays in the field of consciousness with all its determinations; what changes is the form in which it is valid for the one who reflects. The paragraph makes this a matter of having: the phenomenologist no longer "has" the object in the normal sense, but has it constantly in a modified sense. And it answers, through the split of the ego, how one subject can at once see the world and refuse its validity.

> This important point must be well attended to. One must beware of misinterpreting the *talk of phenomenological exclusion* of the respective objects, of their *putting out of play* (which is a justified correlative expression of the putting out of play of acts and interests). The being, the value, the purpose that I as phenomenological observer no longer "have" in the normal sense, I nevertheless have constantly in another and modified sense. What is excluded from my validity and from my interest in validity in general has not thereby vanished from my field of consciousness; only it is given to me, the phenomenologist (as against me as naturally attuned observer, knower, appraiser, productive worker), in a fundamentally altered way, and thanks to the method through which I became a phenomenologist. On the side of the objective we designate this method as the *method of bracketing*. We attach to the object, as it were, an excluding bracket, an index that says: here I will inhibit every letting-count-along, every interest in being, interest in value and so on; I will let the object count only as intentional object of its act, of the act that assigns it validity; I will be interested only in the act and in what it itself posits as object, and as object so characterized thematically. If I carry this out, I have the phenomenologically pure subjective and in it its object in the modified form of validity of the merely intentional object of its act. So I do not become object-blind as through a self-hypnosis (and certainly not, when I pass over to the exclusion of the whole world, blind to this whole), but I remain seeing for everything. But in the splitting of the ego I am established precisely as at once simply seeing and practising pure self-knowledge, and everything simply seen is there in the modification of the bracket, and *seen as bracketed*. —

German:

> Auf diesen wichtigen Punkt ist wohl zu achten. Man muß sich hüten, die *Rede vom phänomenologischen Ausschalten* der jeweiligen Objekte, von ihrem *Außer-Spiel-setzen* (was ein berechtigter korrelativer Ausdruck ist des Außer-Spiel-setzens der Akte und Interessen) zu mißdeuten. Das Sein, den Wert, den Zweck, den ich als phänomenologischer Betrachter nicht mehr im normalen Sinn „habe“, habe ich doch in einem anderen und modifizierten Sinn beständig. Das aus meiner Geltung und aus meinem Geltungsinteresse überhaupt Ausgeschaltete ist darum nicht aus meinem Bewußtseinsfelde verschwunden; nur ist es für mich, den Phänomenologen (gegenüber mir als natürlich eingestelltem Betrachter, Erkenner, Schätzer, werktätigem Arbeiter) in einer grundwesentlich geänderten Weise gegeben, und dank der Methode, durch die ich Phänomenologe wurde. Am Objektiven bezeichnen wir diese Methode als *Methode der Einklammerung*. Wir bringen am Objekt gleichsam eine ausschaltende Klammer an, einen Index, der sagt: Hier will ich jedes Mitgeltenlassen, jedes Seinsinteresse, Wertinteresse usw. inhibieren, ich will das Objekt nur als intentionales seines Aktes, des Aktes, der ihm Geltung zumißt, gelten lassen; mich eben nur für den Akt und was er selbst als Objekt, und als thematisch so charakterisiertes Objekt setzt, interessieren. Führe ich das aus, so habe ich das phänomenologische reine Subjektive und darin sein Objekt in der modifizierten Geltungsgestalt des bloß intentionalen Objekts seines Aktes. Ich werde also nicht wie durch eine Selbsthypnose objektblind (etwa gar, wenn ich zur Ausschaltung der ganzen Welt übergehe, für diese ganze ganze blind), sondern für alles bleibe ich sehend. Aber in der Ichspaltung bin ich eben etabliert als zugleich schlicht Sehender und als reine Selbsterkenntnis Übender, und alles schlicht Gesehene ist da in der Modifikation der Klammer, und *als eingeklammert gesehen*. —

*Note on the text.* The file reads "Erkennner", silently corrected to "Erkenner" (knower). In the parenthesis it reads "für diese ganze ganze blind", with "ganze" doubled; the print probably has "für diese ganze blind" (blind to this whole) or a noun lost after the first "ganze". The rendering follows the first; the reading does not rest on the parenthesis. The paragraph stands on Hua VIII 111.

The paragraph opens as a guard against a misreading of its own vocabulary. The "talk of phenomenological exclusion" and of "putting out of play" (*Ausschalten*, *Außer-Spiel-setzen*, both italicized) is "justified", but only as the "correlative expression" for what is done to acts and interests. What is put out of play, strictly, is my taking part in an act's validity; the object is put out of play only by correlation. The guard is needed because the words name a removal. "Excluding" is literally switching off, and an object switched off would no longer be there. The next sentence denies exactly that, with quotation marks: what I no longer "have" (*„habe“*) in the normal sense, "I nevertheless have constantly in another and modified sense". The verb is the same; the quotation marks say that "have" has two senses, and the paragraph turns on the second.

The method is then renamed "on the side of the objective" (*Am Objektiven*): there it is the "*method of bracketing*" (*Methode der Einklammerung*). The bracket is introduced with "as it were" (*gleichsam*), and at once glossed as "an index" (*einen Index*). The image is borrowed from notation. A bracket in a formula leaves its contents unchanged and changes how they enter the whole; an index is a mark attached to something that remains what it is. Husserl gives the index a voice, "an index that says" (*der sagt*), and what it says is in the first person and the language of will: "here I will inhibit", "I will let the object count only as intentional object of its act". The bracket is a resolution of the reflecting ego written onto the object. Its content is a restriction of validity, not a negation: the object counts "only as intentional object of its act, of the act that assigns it validity" (*der ihm Geltung zumißt*). The result is named in the most exact phrase of the paragraph, "the modified form of validity of the merely intentional object" (*die modifizierte Geltungsgestalt des bloß intentionalen Objekts*). Validity is not removed; its form is changed.

"Self-hypnosis" (*Selbsthypnose*) names the caricature with care. A self-hypnotized subject is "object-blind" (*objektblind*) by his own doing: he makes himself not see what is there. The epoché is also self-performed, which is why the caricature tempts, and the paragraph separates the two by what they do to seeing. Hypnosis removes the object from view; bracketing keeps it in view and changes its validity. The parenthesis extends the point to the universal case, to the "exclusion of the whole world", which the course has not yet reached by this way, and so promises that the universal epoché of Lecture 51 will not be a world-blindness either. "But I remain seeing for everything" (*sondern für alles bleibe ich sehend*).

The last sentence says how. "In the splitting of the ego (*Ichspaltung*) I am established precisely as at once simply seeing and practising pure self-knowledge." "At once" (*zugleich*) carries the unity, "established" (*etabliert*) its stability, and the split carries the difference: the lower ego sees simply, and believes; the reflecting ego practises self-knowledge and refuses to share the belief. The final phrase gives both in one participle: everything is there "*seen as bracketed*" (*als eingeklammert gesehen*). The italics fall on the outcome: the seeing is not reduced, it is qualified. The inference leans on the split. The paragraph says that "I" remain seeing, and it is the lower ego that sees with belief; the upper ego sees what the lower ego sees, through its act, and only so. Whether the phenomenologist "sees" the house or sees the seeing of it is the question the word "at once" settles by fiat.

The paragraph answers a problem Lecture 40 had raised against the reduction itself. There Husserl asked: have I not bracketed the house's existence, "put this belief of naive perception 'out of play'? If I take its belief from the perception, it is no longer perception" (Hua VIII 87). Taking the belief away is not the point, he answered, only putting it "out of action, out of play"; "but does not doing so mean doing something to the perception?" The present paragraph supplies the answer that Lecture 40 left open. Nothing is done to the perception: the belief stays with the perceiving ego, and the bracket is attached by the other. The next paragraph moves to new kinds of acts, pictures and phantasy, to test the method (Hua VIII 111), and Lecture 44 brackets the validity that phantasy confers in its own way (Hua VIII 115–116).

The bracket's later fortunes are told in its time-words. In Lecture 48 Husserl looks back on the single-act reductions and finds their limit in duration: only the objectivities posited in the respective acts "were bracketed, and only temporarily" (*nur vorübergehend*), "to gain the pure act-contents", while "the world with its humans remains valid in the background as acquired habitual possession" (Hua VIII 140). The bracket of Lecture 43 was an index attached to one object, for the length of one reflection. In Lecture 50 the universal epoché refuses every validity "the force of counting for me, in the manner of bracketing" (*in der Weise der Einklammerung*, Hua VIII 153). The device is the same; its scope and its permanence have changed. What the paragraph of Lecture 43 establishes holds through the change: the bracketed is seen, and what the bracket alters is validity, never visibility.

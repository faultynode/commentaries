# Heidegger, *Prolegomena zur Geschichte des Zeitbegriffs*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md) maps the argument of the whole course at equal depth. Each reading here instead follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Heidegger means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds that meaning, what work the passage does between what precedes and what follows it, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the course and in his other works.

The text is the German of GA 20, the Marburg lecture course of summer 1925, edited by Petra Jaeger (Klostermann). Passages are cited by § and by the printed page, "GA 20 n". Unit references such as "§2.3" name the third paragraph of the main commentary under § 2. All English renderings are my working translations, close enough to show the structure under discussion. They are not Theodore Kisiel's published translation (*History of the Concept of Time*), which is cited by name where it differs in a way that matters. Other works are cited by their Gesamtausgabe volume and page, *Sein und Zeit* by the Niemeyer page ("SZ n"), and works held in the corpus only in English by the translation and its German page where the file gives one.

The German comes from a converted file that is not wholly reliable. Page numbers are taken from running heads and can be one page out. Ten pages (27, 46, 47, 85, 87, 130, 137, 301, 417, 419) were recovered from the PDF's text layer and have lost their italics, so no reading argues from the absence of emphasis on them. Line-end hyphenation in the file is joined silently. Any other damage is noted where it occurs, and none of it is repaired.

## §1, GA 20 2–3: productive logic

Reads [§1.3](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 1.

For Heidegger, "productive logic" is logic that goes ahead of a science and discloses the field it will study. It lays open, in its constitution of being, the field a science is to work in, before the science has cut its objects out of that field, and it hands the science the basic structure of those objects. Logic in this sense is not a theory of correct thinking, and not a theory of science that studies how an existing science has built its concepts. It is the disclosure of the matter itself, carried out for the sake of a possible science. The first paragraphs of the course work toward this definition, and the rest of the course carries it out, though not on the objects for which it was announced.

> Because reality, nature as well as history, can be reached only, as it were, by leaping over the sciences, this prescientific, properly philosophical disclosing of these realities becomes what I call *productive logic*: an anticipatory disclosure and conceptual penetration of possible object-domains for the sciences. Unlike traditional theory of science, it does not run along behind a determinate fact, a contingent, historically given science, and examine that science for its structure. It is a logic that leaps ahead into the primary subject-field of a possible science and, by disclosing the constitution of being of this subject-field, first makes available the basic structure of the possible object of this science. That is the process of *original* logic, as Plato and Aristotle brought it out, though only within very narrow limits. Since then the concept of logic has been buried and no longer understood.

German:

> Weil zur Wirklichkeit — Natur sowohl wie Geschichte — nur vorgedrungen werden kann gewissermaßen im Überspringen der Wissenschaften, wird dieses vorwissenschaftliche, eigentliche philosophische Erschließen dieser Wirklichkeiten zu dem, was ich als *produktive Logik* bezeichne, vorausgehende Erschließung und begriffliche Durchdringung möglicher Gegenstandsgebiete für die Wissenschaften, die nicht, wie traditionelle Wissenschaftstheorie, hinter einem bestimmten Faktum einer zufälligen, historisch vorgegebenen Wissenschaft herläuft und sie auf ihre Struktur untersucht, sondern eine Logik, die vorausspringt in das primäre Sachfeld einer möglichen Wissenschaft und durch Erschließung der Seinsverfassung dieses Sachfeldes die Grundstruktur des möglichen Gegenstandes dieser Wissenschaft erst bereitstellt. Das ist der Prozeß der *ursprünglichen* Logik, wie sie von *Plato* und *Aristoteles* herausgestellt wurde — allerdings nur in ganz engen Grenzen. Seither ist der Begriff der Logik verschüttet und nicht mehr verstanden worden.

The definition rests on a pair of terms that § 1 set up in its opening paragraph: the object-domain (*Gegenstandsgebiet*) and the subject-field (*Sachfeld*). A science's object-domain is what it has made thematic. The subject-field is the whole of the matter from which that domain was "cut out" (*herausgeschnitten*, GA 20 2). Heidegger begins with a doubt: "it is not settled" (*es ist nicht ausgemacht*) whether an object-domain gives the subject-field at all, and whether historical knowledge "ever sees history in its historicity" (*die Geschichte in ihrer Geschichtlichkeit*, GA 20 2, :361). Productive logic is the answer to that doubt. It works at the level of the *Sachfeld*, and its result, the basic structure of possible objects, is handed down to the level of the *Gegenstandsgebiet*. The phrase "possible object-domains" (*möglicher Gegenstandsgebiete*) makes the order exact: the domain is still only possible, because the field has not yet been disclosed.

Heidegger's verbs then give this order a direction. The noun he chooses for the work, disclosing (*Erschließen*, *Erschließung*), occurs three times in the passage. Its counterpart in the opening paragraph is what stays "closed off" (*verschlossen*) to a science, and the unity the split between nature and history keeps "covered" (*verdeckt*). Disclosure is what a science cannot do for itself, and the verbs of motion place it accordingly. Reality is reached by pressing forward (*vordringen*). Productive logic "leaps ahead" (*vorausspringt*) into the field. Theory of science "runs along behind" (*hinter … herläuft*) a science that already exists. The two disciplines face opposite ways on one line, and the line is the factual science. What theory of science trails is "a determinate fact" (*Faktum*), the word of the neo-Kantians whom § 4 describes as having "for its proper object the fact of science" (*das Faktum der Wissenschaft*, GA 20 21, :608). Heidegger qualifies the fact as "contingent" and "historically given", and that is enough to take away its authority. The decisive verb comes last: productive logic "first makes available" (*erst bereitstellt*) the basic structure of the object. The "first" (*erst*) states the priority. The science does not have its object until the logic has disclosed the field.

The grammar of the definition shows that this is a redefinition of logic, not a discovery within logic. The philosophical disclosing "becomes what I call productive logic" (*wird … zu dem, was ich als produktive Logik bezeichne*). Heidegger does not argue that logic was always such a disclosing. He names the disclosing "logic" and then defends the name with a history: this was the "original" logic of Plato and Aristotle, since "buried" (*verschüttet*). In §9 the same word names the most dangerous kind of covering-up, something once discovered that has fallen back into concealment and survives only as semblance (GA 20 119). §9 also explains why the name fits. *Logos* means letting something be seen from itself (*apophainesthai*), and "-logy" in "phenomenology" is taken in that sense (§9.4). Logic in the original sense is already disclosure, and productive logic is that sense recovered.

The surrounding paragraphs show why § 1 needs the concept. Directly after the passage, Heidegger reads the "crisis of the sciences" as the sciences themselves calling for it. The real crisis is that "the basic relation of the individual sciences to the matters they question has become questionable" (GA 20 3–4). In that crisis, "scientific research gains a philosophical tendency"; the sciences "say that they need an original interpretation which they cannot provide themselves" (GA 20 4, :412). The survey that follows, of mathematics, physics, biology, history and theology, repeats one verb for each discipline: each is "pressing forward" (*vordringen*) to its matters, and "everywhere we see the pressing forward to an original understanding of the matters themselves" (GA 20 6, :429). This is the passage's own verb. The sciences press toward their subject-fields and cannot reach them, and productive logic is the name for reaching them. Phenomenology, the passage concludes, "has the task of making the subject-fields intelligible *before* scientific treatment" (GA 20 3). The concept thus turns the course's subtitle, "prolegomena to a phenomenology of history and nature", into a programme: the prolegomena are what productive logic must settle before it can disclose history and nature themselves.

The course never reaches history and nature. Its first section turns to Dasein, and § 1 already says why. The sciences "are nothing other than concrete possibilities of human Dasein itself, of expressing itself about its world and about itself" (GA 20 6, :429). A logic that discloses the field of a science must therefore first disclose the being whose possibilities the sciences are. When the analytic reaches its result in §32, it states the method in terms that repeat the definition: phenomenological research is "interpretation of beings toward their being", and its aim is "the concrete foundation for every possible research into being" (§32.2). Productive logic, announced for nature and history, is carried out on Dasein.

The concept runs through Heidegger's work of these years. In the 1923 course *Ontology: The Hermeneutics of Facticity* a note already pairs "the productive logic of the sciences" with the "concrete sojourns and abodes in life" that they develop (GA 63, appendix, van Buren's translation). The course of the following winter, *Logic: The Question of Truth* (1925–26), opens by setting a "philosophizing logic" against school logic. School logic "began *as* a decline". It comes from a time "when philosophy had already lost its character as a productive force", and it keeps "the content of an original philosophical questioning that was alive in Plato and Aristotle" (GA 21 13, Sheehan's translation). The same lecture says a science advances only through "a revision of basic concepts", with Einstein as the example (GA 21 17). That is the crisis of GA 20 § 1 seen from inside. *Sein und Zeit* gives the definition its final form. Research into basic concepts "must run ahead of the positive sciences, and it *can*"; it is "productive logic in the sense that it as it were leaps forward (*vorspringt*) into a determinate region of being" (SZ 10). Then comes the example that shows what the term had meant all along: Kant's transcendental logic is "an a priori logic of the subject-matter of the region of being 'nature'" (*apriorische Sachlogik des Seinsgebietes Natur*, SZ 11). The *Sach-* of *Sachlogik* is the *Sach-* of *Sachfeld*. A productive logic is a logic of the matter, not of thought about the matter. Read this way, Kant's *Critique* becomes the model case, and the Kant interpretations of 1927–29 (GA 25, GA 3) follow from that reading. Between 1925 and 1927 one claim also hardens. GA 20 grants Plato and Aristotle the original logic "only within very narrow limits". In *Sein und Zeit* their work is "proof" (*Beweis*, SZ 10) that such research is possible.

## §2, GA 20 8: the history of the question of being as decline

Reads [§2.3](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 2.

The understanding this passage carries is decline (*Verfall*). The history of the question of being is not a neutral sequence of answers. It is a decline, and the decline is not an accident of intellectual history but follows from the way the questioner is. In a single sentence the passage moves the word from the history of a concept to "the being of Dasein". The course then spends thirteen sections earning that move.

> But then the history of the concept of time is the history of the discovery of time and the history of its conceptual interpretation; that is, this history is the history of the question of the being of beings, the history of the attempts to uncover beings in their being, carried by the understanding of time prevailing at each stage, by the stage reached in the conceptual working-out of the phenomenon of time. So the history of the concept of time is, more exactly, in the end *the history of the decline and the history of the mutilation of the ground-question* of scientific research into the being of beings: a history of the incapacity to pose the question of being radically anew and to work it out anew in its first foundations, an incapacity grounded in the being of Dasein.

German:

> Die Geschichte des Zeitbegriffs ist dann aber die Geschichte der Entdeckung der Zeit und die Geschichte ihrer begrifflichen Interpretation; d. h. diese Geschichte ist die Geschichte der Frage nach dem Sein des Seienden, die Geschichte der Versuche, das Seiende in seinem Sein zu entdecken, getragen von dem jeweiligen Verständnis der Zeit, von der jeweiligen Stufe der begrifflichen Ausarbeitung des Zeitphänomens. So ist die Geschichte des Zeitbegriffs genauer am Ende *die Geschichte des Verfalls und die Geschichte der Verstümmelung der Grundfrage* wissenschaftlicher Forschung nach dem Sein des Seienden: Geschichte des Unvermögens, die Seinsfrage radikal neu zu stellen und in ihren ersten Fundamenten neu auszuarbeiten — ein Unvermögen, das im Sein des Daseins gegründet ist.

The sentence works by repeating "the history of" (*die Geschichte*) and changing what follows it. The history of a concept becomes the history of a discovery. Through "that is" (*d. h.*) it becomes the history of the question of being. With "so" (*so*) it becomes the history of a decline. The repetition keeps the subject constant while its content is replaced, and the reader is carried from a scholarly topic, how time has been conceived, to a philosophical thesis about why being has not been understood. Each "is" is an identification rather than an inference. Heidegger says as much in the next sentence, where he calls the account a "wholly external characterization" (*ganz äußerliche Kennzeichnung*, GA 20 8).

Three nouns give "decline" its content. *Verfall* is a falling-away from a height. *Verstümmelung* (mutilation) is damage to a body that goes on living, so the question of being is not lost but survives maimed. *Unvermögen* (incapacity) is the inability to pose the question "anew", which means the question was once posed and cannot now be posed again. Together they describe a question that is still present in the inherited concepts and can no longer function as a question. Kisiel's "distortion" for *Verstümmelung* misses this. A distortion misrepresents something; a mutilation leaves it present and incapable, and incapacity is exactly what the colon goes on to name. The two qualifiers, "more exactly" (*genauer*) and "in the end" (*am Ende*), add that the verdict is read backward from the present. The decline shows only to a questioner who stands at its end.

The final clause carries the weight: the incapacity is "grounded in the being of Dasein". Dasein has not been introduced. It arrives as the questioning being only in §17. The clause promises that the decline will turn out to be a feature of the questioner, not of the texts, and the noun *Verfall* is the vehicle of that promise. In §13f the course comes back to it with the verb. The two neglects of Husserlian phenomenology, of the being of the intentional and of the sense of being, "are not accidental negligences of the philosophers"; in them "the history of our own Dasein reveals itself", and "Dasein, in its way of being of falling (*Verfallen*), which it does not itself escape, first comes to its being precisely in rising up against it" (GA 20 179, :2704). The question "has fallen silent since Aristotle, and so fallen silent that one no longer knows that it has fallen silent" (GA 20 179, :2700). That is the mutilation of § 2 described from inside: the question is handled in inherited concepts and no longer heard as a question. When the Main Part opens, Heidegger recalls his own sentence and hedges it. The history of the attempts to determine beings in their being "would perhaps be" (*sei vielleicht*) the history of the decline (§14, GA 20 191, :2815). The external characterization has served its purpose; what is to replace it is the analysis.

Decline also shapes the plan of the course. The history of the concept of time was to run backward from Bergson through Kant and Newton to Aristotle, and § 3 explains the order. Each station is only a "relative" transformation, because "at bottom the concept of time as Aristotle grasped it is retained" (GA 20 11). A decline is a continuity of the same concept, ever further from its source, so its history has to be read toward the source. The Main Part gives the decline its existential name, falling (§29), and the newly written block on the "fore-" structure states the consequence for historical research. Great historians reach more original conceptions because they carry out "the destruction of what was once uncovered in a certain way, but has fallen again" (§31.7, GA 20 418).

*Sein und Zeit* §6 assembles these pieces into a method. Dasein "is its past" and grows up into a traditional interpretation of itself (SZ 20); ontology "falls prey to the tradition" (*der Tradition verfällt*) that makes its concepts self-evident; and a "destruction" of the traditional content of ancient ontology is required, carried out "on the guiding thread of the question of being" (SZ 21–22). What GA 20 § 2 calls the history of decline, SZ calls the tradition to be destroyed. The verb in "der Tradition verfällt" is the verb of § 2's noun. The course of the following winter applies the same figure to logic, which "began *as* a decline" (GA 21 13). Between 1925 and 1927 "decline" stops being a judgment on history and becomes the name for how the question of being is handed down. It thereby becomes the reason a destruction is needed at all.

## §2, GA 20 9–10: phenomenological research before the division into historical and systematic

Reads [§2.4](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), fourth paragraph under § 2.

Here "phenomenological" names a sphere of research, not a method set beside history and system. The sphere lies "before" the academic division of philosophy into historical and systematic work. The passage claims that the question of being forces research into this sphere, because the question has a history that belongs to it. To ask the question is already to stand in that history. The passage is phrased as a condition, and the rest of the course is meant to show that the condition holds.

> This opinion remains plausible as long as one believes that a systematic philosophical discussion is possible in a radical sense without being, in its innermost ground, historical. But should that be the case, should it turn out that precisely the ground-question of philosophical research, the question of the being of beings, compels us to press forward into an original sphere of research that lies *before* the traditional division of philosophical work into historical and systematic knowing, then the *prolegomena* to the investigation of beings in their being are to be won only by way of *history*; that is, the manner of research is *neither historical nor systematic, but phenomenological*.

German:

> Diese Meinung bleibt so lange einleuchtend, als man glaubt, eine systematische philosophische Erörterung sei in einem radikalen Sinne möglich, ohne daß sie im innersten Grunde historisch sei. Sollte das aber der Fall sein, sollte es sich ergeben, daß gerade die Grundfrage der philosophischen Forschung, die Frage nach dem Sein des Seienden, dazu zwingt, in eine ursprüngliche Forschungsphäre vorzudringen, die *vor* der traditionellen Aufteilung der philosophischen Arbeit in historisches und systematisches Erkennen liegt, dann sind die *Prolegomena* zur Erforschung des Seienden in seinem Sein nur auf dem Wege über die *Geschichte* zu gewinnen, d. h. die Forschungsart ist *weder historisch noch systematisch, sondern phänomenologisch*.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "zu." at the foot of p. 9 and resumes "gewinnen" on p. 10 (:458). The conditional "Sollte … dann sind … zu gewinnen" needs the continuation, so the full stop is a conversion artifact and is left out of the quotation. The italics fall on pp. 9 and 10, neither of them a text-layer page.

The understanding is carried by one word set against another, and the passage's surface contradiction shows where they part. The prolegomena are to be won "only by way of *history*" (*Geschichte*, italicized), and yet the research is "*neither historical*" (*historisch*). The "that is" (*d. h.*) presents the second clause as a restatement of the first. The way runs through *Geschichte*, history as what has happened, here the history of the question of being itself. What the research is not is *historisch* in the sense the preceding paragraph gave the word: the doxographical "historiography" (*Historie*) of the concept of time, a "collection of opinions" that "remains blind" until what it asks about has been clarified (GA 20 9). One kind of history is the way; the other is a discipline the way does not belong to. The italicized "before" (*vor*) names the place where the two meet: a sphere prior to the division of labour in which historiography and system become separate subjects. "Phenomenological" is the name for that sphere.

§ 1 has already given the vocabulary for this distinction. Heidegger doubted there whether "historical knowledge of historical reality is ever able to see history in its historicity" (*ob die historische Erkenntnis der geschichtlichen Wirklichkeit die Geschichte in ihrer Geschichtlichkeit je zu sehen vermag*, GA 20 2, :361). That sentence holds *historisch* and *geschichtlich* apart and names *Geschichtlichkeit* (historicity). Measured against it, the first sentence of the present passage is loose. It says a systematic discussion must be "in its innermost ground historical" (*historisch*), where § 1's usage would require *geschichtlich*. The looseness is local. The passage relies on the distinction § 1 drew, and its own claim depends on keeping the two apart. Kisiel renders *historisch* throughout as "historiological", which is right for the second and third occurrences. In the first, it makes Heidegger say that systematic philosophy must be historiographical at its innermost ground, which is not his point.

The syntax keeps the claim in suspense. "Should that be the case, should it turn out that…" (*Sollte … sollte es sich ergeben*) governs everything up to the colon, so the phenomenological character of the research depends on what the question of being will "compel" (*zwingt*). The verb repeats the pressing forward (*vordringen*) of § 1. In § 1 the sciences pressed forward toward their subject-fields; here the question of being presses philosophy forward into its own. The next paragraph makes it an aim of the course to show the necessity "from the thematic content of the matters to be investigated and not from some arbitrarily invented idea of philosophy or a so-called philosophical standpoint" (GA 20 10). It closes by calling the separation of the historical clarification from the analysis of the phenomenon of time "only didactic" (*nur didaktisch getrennt*, :462).

The course redeems the conditional in its own construction. The Preparatory Part is a history, of phenomenology's breakthrough from Brentano to Husserl, and at the same time a systematic argument. The editor's afterword warns against reading it as "a merely historical sketch" (GA 20 445, :5210). The immanent critique of §§10–13 is the turning point. Phenomenology fails to ask about the being of its own field because the tradition, Descartes and the definition of the human as *animal rationale*, decides that being in advance. A systematic question, what consciousness is, turns out to be answerable only through a history, how the question of being came to be decided without being asked. §13f names the history it means: the neglects reveal "the history of our Dasein itself", history understood "not as the totality of public events" but as "the manner of happening of Dasein itself" (*die Geschehensart des Daseins selbst*, GA 20 179, :2704). This is *Geschichte* in the sense the passage needs. §9 adds the formal reason. "Phenomenology" is a "methodological" title that names the *how* of research, not its subject-matter (§9.5), so it can name a manner of research prior to the split between historical and systematic subjects. The fore-structure described in §31d supplies the hermeneutic reason: every interpretation, historical interpretation included, stands in a fore-having and fore-sight it must make its own, so historiography is never finished, and the history that philosophy goes through is its own way of being (§31.6–7).

*Sein und Zeit* states the distinction the passage depends on and draws the methodological consequence. Historicity (*Geschichtlichkeit*) "means the constitution of being of the 'happening' of Dasein as such", and historiography (*Historie*) is possible "only because" Dasein is "determined by historicity in the ground of its being" (SZ 20). Since the question of being is itself "characterized by historicity", working it out requires the "destruction" of the tradition (SZ 20, 22). The "manner of research" that GA 20 calls "neither historical nor systematic" becomes, in 1927, the joining of the existential analytic to a destruction of the history of ontology, the two halves of the plan of *Sein und Zeit*. Kisiel's note at this point in his translation makes the same connection, from the GA 20 distinction to that plan.

## §4, GA 20 26: Brentano's intentional in-existence as in-dwelling

Reads [§4.11](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), eleventh paragraph under § 4.

Heidegger first presents intentionality as Brentano's "intentional in-existence", and he hears in it in-dwelling (*Innewohnen*): something objective dwells within the psychic experience. Intentionality, on this account, is a relation inside the psyche, the mark that sets psychic phenomena apart from physical ones. The course takes over the structure and spends the rest of its length turning it round, until the dwelling is no longer the object's in the psyche but Dasein's in the world.

> So the question arises: what is the nature of psychic phenomena as against physical ones? Brentano poses this question in the first book of the *Psychology*. He says that psychic phenomena are distinguished from all physical ones by nothing so much as by this: that something objective dwells within them. Should there, therefore, be differences within the field of psychic phenomena, they must be differences with respect to this basic structure of dwelling-within, differences in the way in which something is objective to the experiences. These differences, in which something is objective to the various experiences (in representing the represented, in judging the judged, in willing the willed), form, according to these differences, the chief class-differences within psychic phenomena. This basic structure of the psychic, that something objective dwells within every experience, Brentano designates as *intentional in-existence*.
>
> *Intentio* is an expression of scholasticism and means as much as: *directing oneself toward*.

German:

> So entsteht die Frage: Welches ist die Natur der psychischen Phänomene gegenüber den physischen? Diese Frage stellt *Brentano* im ersten Buch der Psychologie. Er sagt, die psychischen Phänomene unterscheiden sich von allen physischen durch nichts so sehr als dadurch, daß ihnen etwas Gegenständliches innewohnt. Sollte es deshalb Unterschiede innerhalb des Feldes der psychischen Phänomene geben, so müssen es Unterschiede sein hinsichtlich dieser ihrer Grundstruktur des Innewohnens, Unterschiede in der Weise, in welcher den Erlebnissen etwas gegenständlich ist. Diese Unterschiede, in denen den verschiedenen Erlebnissen etwas gegenständlich ist, im Vorstellen das Vorgestellte, im Urteilen das Geurteilte, im Wollen das Gewoll, je nach diesen Unterschieden bilden sich die vorzüglichsten Klassenunterschiede innerhalb der psychischen Phänome. Diese Grundstruktur des Psychischen, daß jedem Erleben etwas Gegenständliches innewohnt, bezeichnet *Brentano* als *intentionale Inexistenz*.
>
> *Intentio* ist ein Ausdruck der Scholastik und besagt soviel wie: *sich richten auf*.

*Note on the text.* This is p. 26, which audits as partial, and the file drops letters here: "Gewoll" for *Gewollte* and "Phänome" for *Phänomene*. They are quoted as the file has them and do not bear on the reading. The next page, p. 27, comes from the text layer; the italics above fall on p. 26 and are not affected.

The paraphrase builds the understanding by translation. Brentano's Latinate term, "intentional in-existence", appears only in the last sentence. Before it, Heidegger has said the same thing three times in plain German: something objective "dwells within" (*innewohnt*); the basic structure is "dwelling-within" (*Innewohnen*); something objective dwells within "every experience". Whether *innewohnen* is Brentano's own word cannot be checked, because the *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* is not in the corpus. As Heidegger presents it, the technical term arrives already interpreted. "In-existence" means that the object is *in* the experience, and the "in" is that of dwelling.

What dwells within is "something objective" (*etwas Gegenständliches*), a nominalized adjective, not "an object". The phrasing leaves open whether what is in the experience is the thing itself or the thing as meant, and the ambiguity is Brentano's. In §5 Heidegger says that Brentano "wavered" over his "intentional object", between the being itself and the how of its being-grasped (§5.10). The grammar of the passage already holds the place of that wavering. The argument then turns on a "therefore" (*deshalb*). Because in-dwelling divides the psychic from the physical, any differences *within* the psychic "must" be differences in the manner of in-dwelling. Nothing makes that necessary. The step follows only from Brentano's maxim, stated just before, that a classification must take its principle from the "nature" of the matter. Heidegger glosses "nature" here as "what a thing is, seen from itself" (*von ihm selbst her*, GA 20 26), which is the formula §9 will use for the phenomenon. So the maxim is read as phenomenological before the word has been explained. In-dwelling thus becomes a single structure that both marks the psychic off and articulates it from within, and the three classes, representation, judgment and interest, are its modes.

The context shows what Heidegger values in this and what he will refuse. Before the passage, Brentano is the one who freed psychology from natural-scientific method: "'empirical' means here not inductive in the sense of the natural sciences, but as much as factual, non-constructive" (GA 20 25, :629). After it, Brentano's founding thesis follows, that every psychic phenomenon is a representation or rests on one (§4.11), and then the line to Husserl. In §5 the structure seen here is called a phenomenological *discovery*, because "crude acquaintance" with it differs from understanding its sense (§5.1). Brentano had the in-dwelling. What he lacked, according to §5.10, is the *intentum*, the being in the how of its being-intended, and above all any question about what intentionality is the structure *of*. He identified it with the psychic, which "he took in the traditional Cartesian sense". The plain gloss on *intentio*, "an expression of scholasticism", answers in advance the charge §5 rejects: that phenomenology inherited a "dark, metaphysical, scholastic concept" (§5.1). Heidegger grants the provenance and reduces it to a word-meaning, "directing oneself toward", which §5 repeats as "literally" (*dem Wortsinne nach*, GA 20 37).

In-dwelling returns at the centre of the analytic with its direction reversed. In §19 Heidegger derives "in" from *innan*, "to dwell, *habitare*", and "I am" from "alongside" (*bei*). In-being means dwelling-alongside the world as what is familiar, and it is "nothing spatial" (GA 20 213, §19.3). In Brentano the object dwells in the experience. In the analytic, Dasein dwells in the world. The figure survives the critique; what changes is who dwells and where. The newly written close of §31 completes the reversal: seen from care, "what was grasped as intentionality in phenomenology … is fragmentary, a phenomenon seen only from outside". The bare directing-toward must be set back into care's structure of being-ahead-of-itself-in-being-alongside (§31.9, GA 20 420). The passage's in-dwelling and §19's dwelling-alongside meet in that structure.

Heidegger's later summary confirms the arc and names its stake. In 1928 he writes that for Brentano intentionality "remains an internal affair of psychology and gains no fundamental significance". Husserl widened it to the essence of consciousness and reason, but "the insight into intentionality does not go so far as to see at the same time that grasping this structure as an essential structure of Dasein must revolutionize the whole concept of the human" (GA 26 167). That revolution is what GA 20 carries out between § 4 and § 31, and Brentano's in-dwelling is where it starts.

## §4, GA 20 31: a work's self-interpretation and what it in fact achieves

Reads [§4.13](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), thirteenth paragraph under § 4.

The passage rests on one understanding: a work's self-interpretation (*Selbstauslegung*) can fall short of what the work in fact achieves, and the interpreter must read the achievement, not the self-interpretation. Heidegger states this as a verdict on one sentence of Husserl's. It is also the rule by which the whole course reads phenomenology, and by which Heidegger will later read Kant.

> To blame for this misunderstanding is, in a certain way, the self-interpretation that Husserl for his part gave of his work in the introduction to this volume: "Phenomenology is descriptive psychology." This self-explication of his own work is wholly inappropriate to what is worked out in it. In other words, when Husserl wrote the introduction to these investigations, he was not able properly to survey what he had in fact laid down in this volume. Only two years later did he himself set this misinterpretation right, in the *Archiv für systematische Philosophie*, 1903.

German:

> Schuld an diesem Mißverständnis ist in gewisser Weise die Selbstinterpretation, die *Husserl* seinerseits in der Einleitung zu diesem Bande von seiner Arbeit gegeben hat: »Phänomenologie ist deskriptive Psychologie«. Diese Selbstauslegung des eigenen Werks ist dem, was darin erarbeitet wird, ganz unangemessen. Mit anderen Worten, *Husserl* war, als er die Einleitung zu diesen Untersuchungen schrieb, nicht imstande, eigentlich das zu übersehen, was er faktisch in diesem Bande vorgelegt hatte. Erst zwei Jahre später hat er diese Fehlinterpretation selbst richtig gestellt, im »Archiv für systematische Philosophie«, 1903.

The vocabulary divides into two columns. On one side is what Husserl said about his work: a "self-interpretation" (*Selbstinterpretation*), then a "self-explication" (*Selbstauslegung*), finally a "misinterpretation" (*Fehlinterpretation*). On the other is what he did: "what is worked out in it" (*was darin erarbeitet wird*) and what he "in fact laid down" (*faktisch … vorgelegt*). The verdict measures the first against the second, and the measure is fitness: the self-interpretation is "wholly inappropriate" (*ganz unangemessen*) to the work. The pivot of the explanation is *übersehen*. In this sentence it means "to survey": Husserl was "not able properly to survey" what he had done. The word also means "to overlook". Heidegger does not play on it here, but the structure he describes is overlooking. A researcher who cannot survey the content of his own research has, in that respect, overlooked it.

Two qualifiers keep the verdict from being a dismissal. The self-interpretation is to blame only "in a certain way" (*in gewisser Weise*), for the readers share the blame: just before, the Munich circle had taken the *Investigations* for "improved descriptive psychology", and Natorp had read the second volume as a relapse into psychologism (GA 20 30–31). And Husserl corrected the formula "himself", two years later. The self-interpretation is therefore not the author's last word, and the gap between saying and doing can close. The paragraph that follows turns the verdict into a rule of reading. The *Investigations* demand "step by step, explicitly intuitive presentation and controlling exhibition" of their matters; one cannot "simply take out results" without perverting the sense of the investigations (GA 20 32). A work of this kind is understood by repeating what it does, and that is why Heidegger declines to summarize it and works out its discoveries instead (§4.13). The verdict on the formula and the refusal to summarize express the same understanding.

The rule then governs the whole Preparatory Part, and the course states it in the vocabulary of this passage. §8b is headed "The self-understanding of phenomenology" (*Das Selbstverständnis der Phänomenologie*, GA 20 109). It rejects once more the reading of phenomenology as "descriptive psychology" (:1679). It holds that the definition just won must be understood from "what lies in it as task", not from what is said about it, just as one would not interpret physics from astrology (§8.3). The immanent critique applies the same measure to Husserl's later work. Phenomenology, in determining its own field, is "*unphenomenological*! — that is, *supposedly* phenomenological!" (*vermeintlich*, GA 20 178, :2677). Here the self-interpretation is no longer a sentence in an introduction but the self-understanding of the research as such, and the gap between saying and doing has become the gap between "phenomenological" and "supposedly phenomenological". The 1903 correction did not close that gap. It changed the label and left in place the traditional determination of the being of consciousness that §§10–13 expose.

In the Main Part, interpretation (*Auslegung*) becomes a structure of Dasein (§28c), and the newly written §31d gives the rule of this passage its ground. Every interpretation stands in a fore-having, fore-sight and fore-conception, and must ask "whether what it has taken … was explicitly appropriated or only picked up by chance" (§31.6). A self-interpretation can fall short of its work because interpreting oneself is itself an interpretation, with a fore-structure that may have been taken over from the tradition and never made one's own. Husserl's formula is a case: he interpreted his discovery through the inherited horizon of "the psychic".

Heidegger later states the rule as a principle of his own interpretations, and he borrows it from Kant. The Kant book of 1929 quotes Kant's complaint about historians who, "over the word-research into what [earlier philosophers] have said", cannot see "what they wanted to say". Heidegger then adds: "in order to wrest from what the words say what they want to say, every interpretation must necessarily use violence" (GA 3 202). The 1925 passage is the mild form of the same principle, applied to a living author who had corrected himself. In 1929 it has become a declared method. The violence is the hard form of the claim that a work's self-interpretation does not have the last word on its achievement.

Heidegger gives the formula in guillemets as a quotation but cites no page, and he names the 1903 correction only by journal and year. The corpus holds the *Logical Investigations* in German only for volume I (Hua XVIII). The English volumes translate the second edition of 1913, in which Husserl revised the passages on the nature of phenomenology, so they do not show the first-edition wording. Heidegger's report therefore cannot be checked against Husserl here, and nothing in the reading depends on its exact wording.

## §5, GA 20 40: intentionality as intentional from the outset

Reads [§5.4](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), fourth paragraph under § 5.

Intentionality, Heidegger argues here, is not a relation that a perception enters when an object happens to exist. It is the structure of the comportment itself: perceiving is "intentional from the outset" (*von Hause aus intentional*), whether or not what it perceives is there. The point looks trivial. It reverses the order of explanation that both the critics of phenomenology and the tradition behind them take for granted. They begin from a psychic event and ask whether something outside answers to it. Heidegger begins from a directedness and asks how an event could ever lack it. The same phrase later carries the whole analytic of Dasein.

> Let us call the hallucination to mind. One will say: the automobile is not really present, so there is no correlation between the physical and the psychic; only the psychic is given. But is the hallucination not, after all, by its sense hallucination, a supposed perception *of* an automobile? Is not this supposed perceiving too, which is without a real relation to a real object, precisely as such a directing-oneself-toward a supposedly perceived? Is not the deception itself, as such, a directing-oneself-toward, even if the real object is factually not there?
>
> It is not the case that a perception would first become intentional through a physical thing entering into relation with the psychic, and would no longer be intentional if this real thing does not exist. Rather, the perception in itself, whether correct or deceptive, is intentional. Intentionality is not a property that would be added to perception and belong to it in certain cases; rather, as perception it is *intentional from the outset*, quite apart from whether the perceived is really present or not. Indeed, only because perception as such is a directing-oneself-toward something, because intentionality makes up the structure of the comportment itself, can there be anything like deceptive perception and hallucination.

German:

> Vergegenwärtigen wir uns die Halluzination: Man wird sagen, das Automobil ist realiter gar nicht vorhanden, es besteht also keine Zuordnung zwischen Physischem und Psychischem, sondern nur Psychisches ist gegeben. Aber ist die Halluzination nicht doch ihrem Sinne nach Halluzination, vermeintliche Wahrnehmung von einem Automobil? Ist nicht auch dieses vermeintliche Wahrnehmen, das ohne reales Verhältnis zu einem realen Objekt ist, gerade als solches ein Sich-richten-auf ein vermeintlich Wahrgenommenes? Ist nicht die Täuschung selbst als solche ein Sich-richten-auf, auch wenn das reale Objekt faktisch nicht da ist?
>
> Es ist nicht so, daß eine Wahrnehmung erst dadurch intentional würde, daß ein Physisches zum Psychischen in Beziehung tritt und daß sie nicht mehr intentional wäre, wenn dieses Reale nicht existiert, sondern die Wahrnehmung in ihr selbst, ob rechte oder Trugwahrnehmung, ist intentional. Intentionalität ist nicht eine Eigenschaft, die der Wahrnehmung zugegeben würde und ihr in gewissen Fällen zukommt, sondern sie ist als Wahrnehmung *von Hause aus intentional*, ganz abgesehen davon, ob das Wahrgenommene realiter vorhanden ist oder nicht. Ja gerade nur deshalb, weil Wahrnehmung als solche ein Sich-richten-auf etwas ist, die Intentionalität die Struktur des Verhaltens selbst ausmacht, kann es so etwas wie Trugwahrnehmung und Halluzination geben.

*Note on the text.* The page markers around this passage are out of order in the file (37, 39, 38, 40), and two paragraphs earlier the text breaks off mid-sentence ("bei näherem Zusehen ist es ein", :771) before resuming with "Seit *Descartes* weiß man". The passage itself sits whole on p. 40.

The two paragraphs are built as a question and its answer. The first is three rhetorical questions, each beginning "Is not…" (*Ist nicht…*), and each moves the hallucination one step closer to the structure it is supposed to lack. Hallucination is "by its sense" hallucination: the word already names a perceiving *of* something. The "supposed perceiving" is "precisely as such" a directing-toward. Finally "the deception itself, as such" is a directing-toward. The repeated "as such" (*als solches*, *als solche*) moves the argument from the fact of the case, whether there is an object, to its sense, what kind of comportment this is. The critics' objection lives entirely on the side of the fact; the questions answer it on the side of the sense.

The second paragraph states the reversal in the grammar of its "first" (*erst*) and its "only because" (*gerade nur deshalb*). The rejected view is that perception "would first become intentional" when something physical enters into relation with it. The subjunctive *würde* marks it as a hypothesis Heidegger is reporting, not one he holds. Against it stands an indicative: perception "in itself … is intentional". The last sentence turns the critics' evidence round. Hallucination was offered as proof that perception need not be intentional; it becomes possible "only because" perception is a directing-toward. What was a counterexample becomes a confirmation. The decisive phrase is italicized: perception is *von Hause aus intentional*. The idiom means "by nature, from the outset", but it is built on *Haus*, home, and it says where intentionality belongs. It is not "added" (*zugegeben*) from outside, as a property is, and it is not a "correlation" (*Zuordnung*) set up between two places, inside and outside. It is at home in the comportment. The noun Heidegger uses for what has this structure is chosen to the same end: not "experience" (*Erlebnis*) or "psychic process", the critics' words, but "comportment" (*Verhalten*). Intentionality "makes up the structure of the comportment itself".

The passage answers the objection set out just before it, which Heidegger attributes to Rickert and "many others". On that view, intentionality smuggles in two presuppositions: that the psychic gets out of itself to something physical, which "has been forbidden since Descartes", and that a real object always answers to a psychic process, which illusion refutes (GA 20 39). Both presuppositions picture intentionality as a bridge between an inside and an outside, and both ask whether the bridge reaches. The passage does not answer that question. It denies that there are two banks. The next paragraph draws the conclusion in a sentence that names what the course will spend thirteen sections on: "the being of the comportment itself is a directing-oneself-toward" (*das Sein des Verhaltens selbst ist ein Sich-richten-auf*, GA 20 40). Heidegger calls this "the first, perhaps still quite empty determination". It is empty because it says what intentionality is not, a supervening relation, without yet saying what the comportment is whose being it constitutes. That gap is the question of §§11–13.

"From the outset" returns at the decisive points of the analytic, and each time its subject is larger. In §25 the orientation of space leads to "a fundamental proposition": because a region can be discovered only by an oriented Dasein, "Dasein itself is from the outset already *being-in-the-world*" (*von Hause aus schon In-der-Welt-sein*, GA 20 315, :4102). In §28 Dasein "brings its There along from the outset" (*bringt sein Da von Hause aus mit*), "not in the sense of a dead property" (GA 20 349, :4410). The structure of the argument stays the same. Something that seemed to be a relation added to a self-contained thing, perception to an object, Dasein to a world, Dasein to its There, turns out to be the way that thing is. What changes is the bearer. In § 5 it is the comportment of perceiving; in §§25 and 28 it is Dasein. The phrase follows the course's path from the intentionality of acts to the being of the one who acts, and §31f draws the conclusion that the directing-toward of § 5 is "a phenomenon seen only from outside", to be set back into care (§31.9).

Heidegger repeated the argument two years later almost word for word, and the repetition shows what had changed. In *The Basic Problems of Phenomenology* (1927) the hallucination is of elephants in the lecture hall rather than an automobile over the students' heads, and the conclusion is the same: "the intentional relation does not first arise through the actual presence of the objects but lies in the perceiving itself, whether it is free of deception or deceives itself" (GA 24 85). The opening diagnosis, though, is now stated in the vocabulary of the analytic. To treat intentionality as a relation that first "comes to" a subject when an object appears is to treat it as "a relation present-at-hand between two things present-at-hand" (*einer vorhandenen Beziehung zwischen zwei Vorhandenen*, GA 24 84). In 1925 the objection was answered by attending to the sense of the comportment. In 1927 it is diagnosed as a mistake about the kind of being in question: the critics read intentionality as present-at-hand. The step between the two is the analytic of GA 20 itself.

## §5, GA 20 60–61: the intentum, the being in the how of its being-intended

Reads [§5.9](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), ninth paragraph under § 5.

The *intentum* (the intended) is not the being that is intended. It is that being "in the how of its being-intended": the chair as perceived, the bridge as remembered, the house as pictured. Intentionality is complete only when the directing-toward (*intentio*) and this how (*intentum*) are seen to belong together. The "how" is the understanding the passage carries, and it becomes the course's term for the dimension phenomenology works in. The passage adds a warning about Husserl's names for the pair, noesis and noema: the words already interpret the directing-toward as theoretical knowing.

> We thus have a peculiar belonging-together between the way of being-intended, the *intentio*, and the *intentum*, where *intentum*, the intended, is to be understood in the sense brought out: not the perceived as a being, but the being in the how of its being-perceived, the *intentum* in the how of its being-intended. Only with the how of being-intended that belongs to every *intentio* as such has the basic constitution of *intentionality* come into view at all, though only provisionally.
>
> In phenomenology, *intentio* is also understood as meaning (*Vermeinen*). A connection holds between the meaning and the meant, or *noesis* and *noema*. *Noein* means to apprehend, simply to grasp: the apprehending itself and the apprehended in the way of its being-apprehended. I point to these terms because they contain not only a terminology but already a determinate interpretation of the directing-oneself-toward. Every directing-oneself-toward, fear, hope, love, has the character of directing-oneself-toward that Husserl designates as *noesis*. Insofar as *noein* is taken from the sphere of theoretical knowing, an interpretation of the practical sphere in terms of the theoretical results here.

German:

> Wir haben so eine eigentümliche Zugehörigkeit zwischen der Weise des Intendiertseins, der *Intentio*, und dem *Intentum*, wobei *Intentum*, das Intendierte, zu verstehen ist in dem herausgestellten Sinne, nicht das Wahrgenommene als Seiendes, sondern das Seiende im Wie seines Wahrgenommen seins, das *Intentum* im Wie seines Intendiertseins. Erst mit dem zu jeder *Intentio* als solcher gehörigen Wie des Intendiertseins ist überhaupt, obzwar nur vorläufig, die Grundverfassung der *Intentionalität* in den Blick gekommen.
>
> Intentio wird in der Phänomenologie auch als Vermeinen gefaßt. Ein Zusammenhang besteht zwischen Vermeining und Vermeinten oder *Noesis* und *Noema*. *Noein* heißt vernehmen, schlicht erfassen, das Vernehmen selbst und das Vernommene in der Weise seines Vernommenseins. Ich weise auf diese Termini hin, weil darin nicht nur eine Terminologie, sondern auch schon eine bestimmte Interpretation des Sich-richtens-auf enthalten ist. Jedes Sich-richten-auf, Furcht, Hoffnung, Liebe hat den Charakter des Sich-richtens-auf, den *Husserl* als *Noesis* bezeichnet. Sofern *noein* aus der Sphäre des theoretischen Erkennens genommen wird, ergibt sich hier eine Deutung der praktischen Sphäre aus der theoretischen.

*Note on the text.* The file has "Wahrgenommen seins" with a space and "Vermeining" for *Vermeinung*; both are conversion errors and are quoted as they stand. The page break between pp. 60 and 61 falls just before the second paragraph.

One small word does the defining: "how" (*Wie*). It appears three times in two sentences, each time as a noun with an article: "the being in the how of its being-perceived", "the *intentum* in the how of its being-intended", "the how of being-intended that belongs to every *intentio*". German can make an interrogative adverb into a noun, and Heidegger uses that resource to name a dimension that has no ordinary name. The opposition is set out in one line: "not the perceived as a being, but the being in the how". The same being is meant on both sides of the "but". What changes is the respect in which it is taken. The paragraph before the passage lists the hows: the perceived in its perceivedness (*Wahrgenommenheit*), the pictured in its pictoriality (*Bildlichkeit*), the presentified in the manner of presentifying, the emptily meant in the manner of empty meaning (GA 20 60). The suffix *-heit* turns each mode of intending into a property of the intended. Perceivedness belongs to the chair only as perceived, and that is precisely what the *intentum* is.

The second paragraph turns from the thing named to the names. Husserl's terms, noesis and noema, translate the pair, and Heidegger glosses them through their Greek verb: *noein* "means to apprehend, simply to grasp" (*vernehmen, schlicht erfassen*). His point is in the next clause. The terms contain "not only a terminology but already a determinate interpretation". Fear, hope and love are directings-toward, and calling them all "noesis" reads them through a verb "taken from the sphere of theoretical knowing". The practical is interpreted from the theoretical. Heidegger then sets the objection aside, calling the terminology "harmless for our purposes". The concession is local, for this paragraph only. The objection itself becomes a thesis of the Main Part: knowing is "a founded mode" of being-in-the-world, not its basic one (§20.6). §29b traces how the theoretical gaze arises from curiosity, Aristotle's "all humans by nature stretch toward seeing" (§29.2). The warning about *noein* is the first sign of that critique, made at the level of a word.

The passage completes the first discovery. § 5 began by insisting that intentionality is the structure of comportment and not a relation added to it (row 6). It then turned to the side of the object: the chair as environmental thing, as natural thing, as thinghood, and finally as perceived, in its bodily presence (§5.5–5.7). The passage gathers those analyses into the formula of mutual belonging. "Only" (*erst*) with the how does intentionality come into view, and even then "only provisionally" (*obzwar nur vorläufig*). The next paragraph uses the result against Brentano, who "saw the *intentio* … but not the *noema*, the *intentum*" (§5.10, row 8). The *intentum* is what separates the phenomenological discovery from Brentano's acquaintance with the structure. That is why § 5 can call it a discovery at all.

The how becomes the course's name for phenomenology's own dimension. In § 9 the name "phenomenology" says "nothing about the material content" of its objects; it names "only the *how*", the manner in which something is thematic (§9.5). In §10 the reduction is described in the passage's own words: phenomenological analysis takes the object of an act "in the how of its being-meant", so that the perceived is meant "not directly as such, but in the how of its being" (*im Wie seines Seins*, GA 20 135, :2028). The critique of §§11–12 then asks whether Husserl, having found the how of being-intended, ever asked about the how of being of the intentional itself. In §18 the analytic takes the how as its whole programme: not the look of the being that we are, "not the what of what it consists of, but *the how of its being and the characters of this how*" (*das Wie seines Seins*, GA 20 207, :3078). The word that in § 5 named the mode in which a chair is given ends by naming the way Dasein is. The course's path from the intentum to the analytic runs through this one word.

The suspicion of *noein* has a long life in Heidegger's work. *Sein und Zeit* writes that the primacy of "intuition" and "thinking" in the tradition is already a remote derivative of understanding (SZ 147). When it returns to intentionality in a note to §69b, it promises to show "that and how the intentionality of 'consciousness' is *grounded* in the ecstatic temporality of Dasein" (SZ 363–64). The pair that GA 20 presents as the "basic constitution of intentionality" is, by 1927, a structure to be founded in something deeper, and the theoretical cast of its Greek name is part of why.

## §5, GA 20 61–62: what intentionality is the structure of

Reads [§5.10](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), tenth paragraph under § 5.

The understanding this passage carries is a question, not a doctrine: *what is intentionality the structure of?* Brentano left it "undiscussed", and Heidegger finds the same omission in phenomenology "still today". The passage sets out the omission as the place where phenomenology itself, not its critics, has gone wrong. Whoever refutes phenomenology by criticizing Brentano "gives the theme out of hand". That idiom returns in §12, turned against Husserl, and the question it guards is answered only at the end of the analytic.

> Brentano saw in intentionality the *intentio*, *noesis*, and the variety of its modes, but he did not see the *noema*, the *intentum*. He remained uncertain in determining what he called the "intentional object". … The pure setting-off of the how of being-intended does not come about in Brentano at all; that is, intentionality as such, as a structural whole, is not set off in his work. But this means further: intentionality is taken together with the being whose character it is determined to be; it is identified with the psychic. Brentano also left undiscussed what intentionality is properly supposed to be the structure of, and this because he took the psychic into his theory in the traditional sense of what is immanently perceivable, immanently conscious (in the sense of Descartes' theory). … This is a moment that has not been overcome even in phenomenology today. Today too intentionality is simply taken as a structure of consciousness or as a structure of acts, of the person, and again these two realities, of which intentionality is supposed to be the structure, are taken over traditionally. … One may not, then, in order to refute phenomenological intentionality, simply criticize Brentano! With that one gives the theme out of hand from the start.

German:

> *Brentano* sah an der Intentionalität die *Intentio*, *Noesis*, und die Verschiedenheit ihrer Weisen, aber er sah nicht das *Noema*, das *Intentum*. Er blieb unsicher in der Bestimmung dessen, was er »intentionales Objekt« nannte. … zur reinen Abhebung des Wie des Intendiertseins kommt es bei *Brentano* überhaupt nicht, d. h. die Intentionalität als solche, als Strukturganzheit, ist bei ihm nicht abgehoben. Das besagt aber weiter: Intentionalität ist zugleich mit dem Seienden, als dessen Charakter sie bestimmt wird; sie wird mit dem Psychischen identifiziert. Unerörtert ließ *Brentano* auch, wovon eigentlich die Intentionalität Struktur sein soll, und zwar insofern, weil er das Psychische im traditionellen Sinne des immanent Wahrnehmbaren, des immanent Bewußten (im Sinne der *Descartes*’schen Theorie) in seine Theorie aufnahm. … Das ist ein Moment, das auch in der Phänomenologie heute noch nicht überwunden ist. Auch heute wird einfach die Intentionalität als Bewußtseinsstruktur gefaßt oder als Struktur der Akte, der Person, wobei wiederum diese beiden Wirklichkeiten, wovon Intentionalität Struktur sein soll, traditionell aufgenommen sind. … Man darf also nicht, um die phänomenologische Intentionalität zu widerlegen, einfach *Brentano* kritisieren! Damit gibt man von vornherein das Thema aus der Hand.

*Note on the text.* The passage runs across the page break between pp. 61 and 62 (:1092–:1097). The omitted sentences are the four meanings of the perceived recalled in passing, the efforts of Husserl and Scheler to get past the psychic, and the promise of a more radical working-out; the reading cites them where it uses them.

The passage moves its question through three phrasings of "structure of what" (*wovon … Struktur*), and the phrasing does the arguing. The first is Brentano's: intentionality "is identified with the psychic", because it is "taken together with the being whose character it is determined to be". The structure is fused with its bearer, so the bearer cannot be questioned through the structure. The second states the omission: Brentano "left undiscussed what intentionality is properly supposed to be the structure of" (*Unerörtert ließ Brentano auch, wovon eigentlich die Intentionalität Struktur sein soll*). The emphatic placing of "undiscussed" at the head of the sentence and the modal "supposed to be" (*sein soll*) mark the bearer as something assumed, not found. The third extends the charge to "phenomenology today": consciousness, act and person, "of which intentionality is supposed to be the structure", are "taken over traditionally" (*traditionell aufgenommen*). The same *sein soll* recurs. Across the three, a defect of Brentano's psychology becomes a defect of phenomenology, and the reason is the same each time. The bearer of the structure comes from the tradition (the Cartesian "immanently perceivable") and not from the matters.

The final sentence turns this into a rule against the critics, and its idiom matters. Whoever refutes phenomenology by criticizing Brentano "gives the theme out of hand" (*gibt … das Thema aus der Hand*). To give something out of hand is to surrender what one holds. The critics surrender the theme because they attack what Brentano built under intentionality, his psychology, and miss the structure itself. So the passage does two things at once. It defends intentionality against Rickert's charge of "metaphysical dogma" by locating the dogma in the unasked question of its bearer, not in the structure. And it names that same unasked question as phenomenology's own open flank. The defence and the critique have one source: what is dogmatic is "what is built under the structure or left unquestioned beneath it" (§5.10).

In its context the passage closes the first discovery and prepares the critique of chapter three. Just before it, the *intentum* has been set off (row 7). This is what Brentano lacked, and it is why intentionality can be called a phenomenological discovery and not an inheritance. Just after it, § 5 ends by insisting that the enigma of how being-intended relates to the being itself must be held open, not covered with theory (§5.10). The question of the bearer is planted here and harvested in §§11–13. There the four determinations of pure consciousness are shown to be taken from consciousness's role as grasped, given, constituting and ideated, not from its being (§11.6). And §12 turns the passage's idiom against Husserl himself: "in the sense of the reduction, the very ground is given out of hand (*aus der Hand gegeben*) on which alone one could ask about the being of the intentional" (GA 20 151, :2268, row 15). The phrase that in § 5 rebuked the critics for surrendering the theme now rebukes the reduction for surrendering the ground. Phenomenology, read by its own maxim, makes its critics' mistake about itself.

The course's answer comes at the close of the analytic. With care worked out, Heidegger reaches "the place at which we can critically repeat what we heard about intentionality in the introductory considerations". Intentionality as phenomenology grasped it is "fragmentary", "a phenomenon seen only from outside", and its bare directing-toward must be set back into care's structure of being-ahead-of-itself-in-being-alongside (§31.9, GA 20 420). What intentionality is the structure of turns out to be Dasein, whose being is care. *Sein und Zeit* pushes the question one level further down. Intentionality is no longer the structure of a being at all but is "grounded" in "the ecstatic temporality of Dasein" (SZ 363–64). In 1928 Heidegger compressed the whole arc into a judgment on Husserl: "the question of the being constituted as consciousness is not further posed" (GA 26 167). That is the question this passage first asked of Brentano.

## §6, GA 20 77–78: the surplus of the "is"

Reads [§6.2](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 6.

Every perceptual assertion says more than perception can show. In "this chair is yellow and upholstered" the chair, its yellowness and its upholstery can be seen; the "this", the "and" and above all the "is" cannot. Heidegger calls this a "surplus of intentions" (*Überschuß an Intentionen*). The surplus is where being first shows up in the course, as what is meant in every assertion and seen in no perception. The passage poses the problem that categorial intuition answers. Being must be given somehow if the assertion is to be fulfilled at all, and it cannot be given as the chair is.

> We have, on the one hand, what simple perception gives: the full material content of the really found thing (chair); and then the assertion about it: "This chair is yellow and upholstered." This S is P and Q. The question is: does this assertion find full fulfilment in the perceived? … More precisely, one must ask in detail: is the "this", the "is", the "and" exhibitable perceptually in the thing? The chair, the being-upholstered and the being-yellow I can see, but the "this", "is", "and" I shall never in all eternity see as I see the chair. In the full perceptual assertion there lies a *surplus of intentions*, whose exhibition cannot be supplied by simple perception of the thing.
>
> … "Being" is no real moment in the chair, as the wood, the weight, the hardness, the colour are, or the upholstery or the screws. "Being", as Kant already said, meaning *real-being*, is no real predicate of the object, and this holds also of being in the sense of the copula. Evidently there is also no adequation between assertion and perceived; the latter remains behind in material content what the former asserts of it. The assertion expresses what is not to be found perceptually at all. Must the idea of an adequate fulfilment of assertions as such not then be given up, and with it the idea of truth?

German:

> Dazu vergegenwärtigen wir uns: Wir haben einmal das, was die schlichte Wahrnehmung gibt: den vollen Sachgehalt der real vorfindlichen Sache (Stuhl) und dann die Aussage darüber: »Dieser Stuhl ist gelb und gepolstert«. Dieses S ist P und Q. Die Frage ist: Findet diese Aussage volle Erfüllung im Wahrgenommenen? … Genauer ist im einzelnen zu fragen: Ist das »dieses«, das »ist«, das »und« wahrnehmungsmäßig an der Sache ausweisbar? Den Stuhl, das Gepolstertsein und das Gelbsein kann ich sehen, aber das »dieses«, »ist«, »und« werde ich in alle Ewigkeit nie sehen, so wie ich den Stuhl sehe. In der vollen Wahrnehmungsaussage liegt ein *Überschuß an Intentionen*, deren Ausweisung durch die schlichte Sachwahrnehmung nicht bestritten werden kann.
>
> … Das ›Sein‹ ist kein reales Moment im Stuhl, wie das Holz, die Schwere, Härte, Farbe auch nichts an ihm ist wie das Polster oder die Schrauben. ›Sein‹ – sagte schon *Kant* und meinte dabei das *Realsein* – ist kein reales Prädikat des Gegenstandes, das gilt auch vom Sein im Sinne der Kopula. Offensichtlich besteht auch keine Adaequation zwischen Aussage und Wahrgenommenem, dieses bleibt an Sachgehalt hinter dem zurück, was jene von ihm aussagt. Die Aussage drückt aus, was wahrnehmungsmäßig gar nicht vorfindlich ist. Muß damit nicht die Idee einer adaequaten Erfüllung der Aussagen überhaupt aufgegeben werden und damit die Idee der Wahrheit?

*Note on the text.* "Supplied" renders *bestritten* in its older sense of "met, covered" (as one meets a cost), which the context requires. The passage runs from p. 77 to p. 78 (:1276–:1283); the omitted sentences restate the question and test a simpler nominal phrase, "the yellow, upholstered chair", which the reading cites.

The passage isolates being by quotation marks. The small words of the assertion are cited, not used: »dieses«, »ist«, »und«. In the second paragraph "being" itself is in distancing marks: *›Sein‹*. The marks take the words out of the sentence about the chair and hold them up as items of their own, and that is the passage's operation: it turns the means of saying something into something said. The list of what can be seen is built to make the contrast exact. Heidegger does not write "yellow and upholstered" but "the being-upholstered and the being-yellow" (*das Gepolstertsein und das Gelbsein*). The nominalized forms carry a "being" inside them, and the next paragraph takes it out. Even the bare attribute "yellow" in "the yellow chair" means, fully spelled out, "the yellow-*being* chair" (*der gelbseiende Stuhl*, GA 20 78). Wherever an assertion characterizes, being is already meant.

The emphasis falls on the impossibility. "I shall never in all eternity see" (*in alle Ewigkeit nie sehen*) is hyperbole, and it is precise: no improvement of perception will supply the "is". Kant is cited to fix what kind of impossibility it is. Being is "no real predicate", no *realitas*, no content of the thing. Heidegger adds two qualifications that change Kant's thesis while borrowing it. Kant meant "*real-being*" (*Realsein*), existence. Heidegger extends the thesis to "being in the sense of the copula" (*Sein im Sinne der Kopula*). Kant's point about existence becomes a point about every "is". The question at the end is put with a weight the course does not mean to bear out: "must … the idea of truth" be given up? It is rhetorical, and the paragraph after it warns against "drawing conclusions, which is always a suspect thing in philosophy" (GA 20 78). The surplus is not a scandal for truth but a pointer to a second kind of givenness.

The passage sits at the hinge of § 6. Before it, Heidegger has set up the vocabulary of fulfilment: an intention is fulfilled when what it means is given, and an assertion is true when what it says "covers" (*Deckung*) what is given (GA 20 77). The surplus is what this vocabulary cannot yet accommodate. After it, Heidegger rejects the empiricist way out, by which what is not sensory must be subjective and so found by reflection on consciousness, "as one has argued in English empiricism since Locke" (GA 20 79). He then gives Husserl's answer from the Sixth Investigation. The concepts of being and state-of-affairs originate "not in reflection on judgments … but in the judgment-fulfilments themselves" (GA 20 79). The "is" is a correlate of certain acts, given in a non-sensory perception, the categorial (§6.3–6.4). The surplus is therefore the precise point at which intentionality, the first discovery, is needed for the second. Without it, what is not sensory would have to be inner; with it, the not-sensory can be an object of its own kind. Heidegger states the dependence outright: "one does not know what one is doing" if one accepts categorial intuition and dismisses intentionality as a myth, "for both are one and the same" (GA 20 80).

The "is" that cannot be seen is the thread that leads to the question of being. Its first consequence is the identification of ontology and phenomenology (§6.9, row 10). Its second comes in § 15, where the question of being starts from the same word. We "always already live in an understanding of the 'is'" without being able to say what it means, and even this "vague pre-understanding is *still an understanding*" (GA 20 192, :2867). § 6 showed the "is" to be given in every assertion and seen in none. § 15 takes that givenness without comprehension as the starting point of the question. In §22 Kant's thesis returns in its original setting, as the consequence of Descartes' admission that being "does not affect us" (GA 20 237, :3423). The thesis that § 6 borrowed to free the copula becomes evidence of how the tradition made being inaccessible.

In 1927 Heidegger devoted the first chapter of the first part of *The Basic Problems of Phenomenology* to "Kant's thesis: being is not a real predicate" (GA 24 §§7–9). He read it there as a thesis about the being of the present-at-hand and traced it to its ground in intentional comportment. The fullest testimony to what the surplus meant for him came almost fifty years later. The protocol of the 1973 Zähringen seminar records his account of categorial intuition. The "is" is "a 'surplus' in relation to the sensuous affections", yet it is "given *in the same manner*" as they are. "In order to unfold the question concerning the meaning of being, being must be *given*." Husserl's achievement was this "making present of being, which is phenomenally present in the category", and with it, Heidegger is reported as saying, "I finally had the ground" (GA 15, Zähringen seminar, 1973, English translation). The protocol also records the limit he found in Husserl, which GA 20 §13 had already drawn: having reached being as given, Husserl "does not inquire any further into it".

## §6, GA 20 98: scientific ontology as phenomenology

Reads [§6.9](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), ninth paragraph under § 6.

The claim of this passage is an identity: "scientific ontology is nothing other than phenomenology". Ontology here is not a branch of metaphysics with its own subject-matter. It is the search for a "way of research" (*Forschungsart*) that could exhibit being and the categories instead of deducing them, and phenomenology, through categorial intuition, is that way found. The identity turns a discovery about acts into a claim about philosophy as such. The rest of the course spends itself on the question it opens: if phenomenology is ontology, why has it not asked about being?

> Further, the objectivity that gives itself in such acts is itself the objective way in which reality itself can become objective more properly. With the exhibition of the categorial structure the idea of objectivity is widened, in such a way that this objectivity itself now becomes exhibitable in the investigation of the corresponding intuition in its content. In other words: in the phenomenological research breaking through here, the way of research is won that the old ontology sought. There is no ontology *alongside* a phenomenology; rather, scientific ontology is nothing other than phenomenology.

German:

> Weiter ist diese in solchen Akten sich gebende Gegenständlichkeit selbst die gegenständliche Weise, in der die Realität selbst eigentlicher gegenständlich werden kann. Mit dem Aufweis der kategorialen Struktur ist die Idee der Objektivität erweitert, so zwar, daß diese Objektivität selbst nun in der Durchforschung der entsprechenden Anschauung in ihrem Gehalte aufweisbar wird. Mit anderen Worten: In der damit durchbrechenden phänomenologischen Forschung ist die Forschungsart gewonnen, die die alte Ontologie suchte. Es gibt keine Ontologie *neben* einer Phänomenologie, sondern wissenschaftliche Ontologie ist nichts anderes als Phänomenologie.

The identity is reached in three steps, and each step changes the subject. The first sentence is about objectivity: what is given in categorial acts is "the objective way in which reality itself can become objective more properly". The second is about the idea of objectivity, which is "widened" (*erweitert*) and becomes "exhibitable" (*aufweisbar*) in intuition. The third, introduced by "in other words" (*Mit anderen Worten*), is about research: the "way of research" that "the old ontology sought" has been "won". "In other words" claims that the third sentence restates the second. It does not. It moves from what is given to the method of a discipline, and from the present of phenomenology to the past of ontology. The sentence works by the verb "sought" (*suchte*) in the past tense, set against "won" (*gewonnen*). Ontology appears as a search that lacked its method, and phenomenology as the method that completes it. Phenomenology inherits ontology's question; ontology inherits phenomenology's access.

The final sentence states the identity twice, once negatively and once positively, and the emphasis falls on the negative. "There is no ontology *alongside* (*neben*) a phenomenology" rejects the picture of two disciplines, one supplying the objects and the other the method. "Nothing other than" (*nichts anderes als*) then states strict identity. The qualifier "scientific" (*wissenschaftliche*) limits it. The claim is not that every ontology is phenomenology, but that ontology done as research, exhibiting what it claims, is. The ontology that deduces its categories from a table of judgments, or constructs them, is excluded by the word. Kant is the obvious target, since § 6 has just contrasted the categorial forms as "objects that become visible *in* the acts" with Kant's forms of the understanding (§6.8).

The passage is the last word of the second discovery. Just before it, Heidegger has drawn two lesser consequences. Categorial intuition opens "the concrete path of an exhibiting and genuine research into categories". It also settles, provisionally, the old quarrel over universals, which went wrong by denying the *objectivity* of the universal along with its reality (GA 20 98). The identity of ontology and phenomenology gathers both consequences into one claim. Immediately after it, Heidegger justifies the order of the discoveries: categorial intuition is "a concretion of the basic constitution of intentionality", and the third discovery, the a priori, rests on both (GA 20 98–99). The identity is therefore not a slogan added to § 6 but the point toward which the discoveries were ordered.

The rest of the course tests the identity against phenomenology's practice and finds the practice wanting. If phenomenology is scientific ontology, it must be able to ask about the being of its own field. §§11–13 show that it did not: it determined the being of consciousness from the tradition and made "categorial primal distinctions" without asking about the sense of being that guides them (§13.8). The title of the Main Part's first chapter registers the result in reverse. The source titles it "The phenomenology grounded in the question of being" (*Die in der Seinsfrage gegründete Phänomenologie*, GA 20 183, :2732). § 6 had made ontology into phenomenology. Chapter one makes phenomenology depend on the question of being. Phenomenology is ontology only once it is grounded in that question. §32 states the method that results: "phenomenological research is interpretation of beings toward their being" (§32.2).

*Sein und Zeit* keeps the identity and grounds it as § 6 did not. "Ontology is possible only as phenomenology" (SZ 36). "Ontology and phenomenology are not two different disciplines alongside others belonging to philosophy. The two titles characterize philosophy itself according to its object and its way of treatment" (SZ 38). The "alongside" of GA 20 (*neben*) returns. So does the word that limited the claim in 1925: philosophy is "universal phenomenological ontology, proceeding from the hermeneutic of Dasein" (SZ 38). The closing phrase names what GA 20 had to discover between § 6 and § 32. Phenomenology becomes ontology only by way of an analytic of the being that asks about being.

## §7, GA 20 99: the a priori as "the earlier"

Reads [§7.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 7.

The a priori, for Heidegger, is "what in something is always already the earlier". Read from its name, the oldest term of philosophical rigour is a term of time. The passage uses this to explain why the third discovery is treated briefly: its sense cannot be clarified until time itself is understood, and time is what the course is looking for. The understanding is a promise. The "earlier" of the a priori is to be made intelligible from time, and the word "already", which the passage introduces in passing, will carry that promise through the whole analytic.

> The working-out of the sense of the a priori is the third discovery that we owe to the beginnings of phenomenology. The characterization of this discovery will be kept shorter: first, because the a priori, despite essential insights in phenomenology itself, is still little clarified; second, because it remains to a large extent bound up with traditional questions; and third, above all, because the clarification of its sense presupposes the understanding of precisely what we are seeking: time.
>
> The last point is already clear from the clarification of the name: a priori — prius — πρότερον — earlier; a priori — what is from before — what already is from earlier. The a priori is that in something which in it is always already the earlier. That is a quite formal determination of the a priori. It has not yet been said what the something is at which something like an earlier is to be found. A priori is a title in which something like a temporal sequence lies, though it remains very faded, indeterminate and empty.

German:

> Die Herausarbeitung des Sinnes des Apriori ist die dritte Entdeckung, die wir den Anfängen der Phänomenologie verdanken. Die Charakteristik dieser Entdeckung sei kürzer gehalten, erstens weil das Apriori trotz wesentlicher Einsichten in die Phänomenologie selbst noch wenig geklärt ist, zweitens weil es noch weitgehend mit traditionellen Fragestellungen verknüpft bleibt und drittens vor allem, weil die Klärung seines Sinnes gerade das Verständnis dessen voraussetzt, was wir suchen: Zeit.
>
> Das letztere wird schon aus der Klärung des Namens deutlich: a priori — prius — πρότερον — früher; a priori — was von vorher — was von früher her schon ist. Das Apriori ist das an etwas, was daran schon immer das Frühere ist. Das ist eine ganz formale Bestimmung des Apriori. Es ist noch nicht gesagt, was das Etwas ist, an dem so etwas wie ein Früheres vorfindlich wird. Apriori ist ein Titel, darin so etwas wie eine Zeitfolge liegt, obzwar diese sehr verblaßt, unbestimmt und leer bleibt.

The first paragraph gives three reasons and saves the decisive one for last with "above all" (*vor allem*). The last clause ends on a single word after a colon: "what we are seeking: time" (*was wir suchen: Zeit*). It is the first time in the course that time is named as the object of the search rather than as the topic of the announced history. The second paragraph proves the reason by etymology, and the proof is laid out as a chain of dashes: a priori, prius, πρότερον, earlier. Latin, comparative, Greek, German follow one another without verbs. The dashes present the equivalences as self-evident, and the chain ends in an ordinary German word for a relation in time, *früher*. The second chain unfolds that word into a phrase: "what is from before — what already is from earlier" (*was von früher her schon ist*). Here a new word enters, *schon* (already), and the definition that follows doubles it: the a priori is what is "always already the earlier" (*schon immer das Frühere*).

The passage's caution about its own result is precise. It calls the determination "quite formal" and says it leaves open "what the something is" at which an earlier is found. The temporal sequence in the title is "very faded, indeterminate and empty" (*sehr verblaßt, unbestimmt und leer*). Three adjectives concede that etymology cannot settle the sense of the a priori. They also keep the claim in force: the sequence is faded, but it is there. An argument from a word's history is weak, and Heidegger does not rest the doctrine on it. He rests on it only the claim that the a priori's sense is a question for the analysis of time. That is all the course needs from this paragraph.

The context shows why the a priori must be cut loose from the subject before time can explain it. The paragraphs that follow trace how the a priori became a character of knowing since Descartes and Kant. It became what is "available first and solely in the subject", "inner knowledge" (GA 20 100). Phenomenology shows instead that "the a priori is not a title of comportment but a title of being", indifferent to subjectivity. It is "structurally earlier" in the being of the real and of the ideal alike, and graspable in simple intuition (§7.3, GA 20 101). This is the step that makes the passage's "earlier" usable. If the a priori were earlier only in the order of knowing, its time would be the time of a subject's acts. As a title of being, its "earlier" belongs to the structure of beings, and the question becomes what kind of time that is. § 7 closes by naming the risk: the discovery of the a priori is identical with the discovery of the concept of being in Parmenides and Plato, so that "one can speak with a certain right of a *Platonism* within phenomenology" (GA 20 102, :1590). The a priori carries the Greek sense of being with it. Time will be needed to loosen that sense.

The "already" of the passage is the word through which the promise is kept. Care, in §31, is "being-ahead-of-itself-in-*already*-being-alongside" (*Sich-vorweg-sein-im-schon-sein-bei*, §31.2). Being-in-the-world is "always already" alongside a world (§19.4). The understanding of being is "always already there" (§15.1). The analytic describes every structure of Dasein as already in place before any particular comportment, which is to say *a priori*, and it does so in the temporal adverb that § 7 found inside the term. At the end of the course the adverb receives its ground. The being-guilty co-posited in running ahead is "the being of the ownmost *having-been*" (*Ge*wesensein), and that is the past (§36.1, row 41). The "already" of the a priori turns out to be the having-been of Dasein's time.

Heidegger made the connection explicit two years later. In the introduction to *The Basic Problems of Phenomenology* the a priori is the "earlier" of being before beings, and "the earlier is a determination of time, but one that does not lie in the time-order of the time we measure with the clock" (GA 24 27). In the last part of the course the point is made from the analytic's own vocabulary. "A priori means 'from what is earlier'", and in all the preceding explications "we needed no word more often than the expression 'already'", in phrases such as "lies at the basis in advance already" and "must always already be understood in advance". With all these temporal terms "we mean what the tradition since Plato calls the a priori" (GA 24 461). The faded temporal sequence of GA 20 § 7 has become, by 1927, the "temporality" (*Temporalität*) of being. And the word "already", which § 7 introduced in passing, is now named as the sign of it.

## §9, GA 20 111–12: the phenomenon as what shows itself

Reads [§9.2](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 9.

A phenomenon, for Heidegger, is "what shows itself": a being as it shows itself from itself, in a distinctive way of being encountered. It is not an appearance of something hidden behind it. Semblance, the being's showing itself as what it is not, is a modification of this self-showing and presupposes it. The understanding is carried by the Greek middle voice, and the course's whole sense of method depends on it. If "phenomenon" meant appearance, phenomenology would be a science of surfaces. Because it means self-showing, phenomenology is the way to the matters themselves.

> φαινόμενον is the participle of φαίνεσθαι; this middle meaning says: *to show itself*; φαινόμενον is thus what shows itself. The middle φαίνεσθαι is a formation from φαίνω: to bring something to the day, to make visible in itself, to place in the light. φαίνω has the stem φα — φῶς, the light, the brightness, that in which something can be manifest, visible in itself. As the meaning of phenomena one must hold fast: φαινόμενα, what shows itself. …
>
> … φαινόμενον can mean as much as semblance only because semblance is a modification of φαινόμενον in the first sense. More sharply formulated: only because φαίνεσθαι means to show itself can it also mean to show itself only so, as, only to look like. Only insofar as something by its sense at all pretends to show itself can it give itself out as …; only what pretends to be manifest can be semblance. Indeed, that is the *sense of semblance: pretension* to the manifest, but precisely not being it. Hence precisely *φαινόμενον* as semblance shows that the sense of phenomenon says: the being manifest in itself, itself; semblance by contrast is a pretended self-showing. Phenomenon therefore means *a way in which the being is encountered in itself, showing itself*.

German:

> φαινόμενον ist das Partizip von φαίνεσθαι; diese mediale Bedeutung besagt: *sich zeigen*; φαινόμενον ist also das, was sich zeigt. Das Medium φαίνεσθαι ist eine Bildung von φαίνω: etwas an den Tag bringen, sichtbar machen an ihm selbst, in die Helle stellen. φαίνω hat den Stamm φα — φῶς, das Licht, die Helle, das, worin etwas offenbar, an ihm selbst sichtbar sein kann. Als Bedeutung von Phänomenen ist festzuhalten: φαινόμενα, das, was sich selbst zeigt. …
>
> … φαινόμενον kann nur deshalb soviel wie Schein besagen, weil Schein eine Modifikation von φαινόμενον im ersten Sinne ist; schärfer formuliert: nur weil φαίνεσθαι sich zeigen besagt, kann es auch besagen: sich nur so zeigen wie, nur so aussehen wie. Nur sofern etwas seinem Sinne nach überhaupt prätendiert, sich zu zeigen, kann es sich ausgeben als . . .; nur was offenbar zu sein prätendiert, kann Schein sein — ja, das ist der *Sinn von Schein: Prätention* des Offenbaren, aber es gerade nicht sein. Daher zeigt gerade *φαινόμενον* als Schein, daß der Sinn von Phänomen besagt: das an ihm selbst offenbare Seiende selbst; Schein dagegen ist ein angebliches Sichzeigen. Phänomen meint daher *eine Begnisart des Seienden an ihm selbst, sich selbst zu zeigen*.

*Note on the text.* "Begnisart" (:1739) is a dropped syllable for *Begegnisart* (way of being encountered); the reading depends on the word and gives it in full, noting the error here. The Greek in this paragraph is clean Unicode. A few lines further on, in § 9b (:1773), the Greek is garbled LaTeX and cannot be quoted from the file.

Everything rests on a grammatical voice. Heidegger names it: φαίνεσθαι is a "middle" (*Medium*, *mediale Bedeutung*), and the German reflexive renders it: *sich zeigen*, to show itself. The middle voice makes the being both the one that shows and the one shown. It shows itself, and it does so "in itself" (*an ihm selbst*) and, as § 9b adds, "from itself" (*von ihm selbst her*). Nothing stands behind it to do the showing, and no subject does the showing to it. The etymology then extends the sense. The active φαίνω means "to bring to the day, to place in the light", and the stem leads to φῶς, light, "that in which something can be manifest". The phenomenon is what shows itself in a brightness that belongs to the showing.

The second paragraph secures this sense against its rival by the logic of "only because" (*nur deshalb … weil*, *nur weil*). Semblance (*Schein*) is the case in which a being shows itself as what it is not, the good that only "looks" good. It might seem to make "phenomenon" ambiguous between showing and seeming. Heidegger turns the ambiguity into a founding relation: φαινόμενον can mean semblance "only because" it first means self-showing. The key word is borrowed and pointed: semblance is "pretension" (*Prätention*). Only what claims to be manifest can fail to be what it claims, so "only what pretends to be manifest can be semblance". Semblance is a pretended self-showing. Pretension presupposes the thing pretended, so semblance "shows precisely" the original sense. The paragraph ends by giving that sense a new name, not Greek but Heidegger's own: the phenomenon is "a way in which the being is encountered" (*Begegnisart*). Self-showing is a mode of encounter, not a property of things.

The definition is aimed at a confusion the next paragraphs name. "No word has caused such devastation and confusion in philosophy" as "appearance" (*Erscheinung*), and "mere appearance" (GA 20 112). An appearance, a symptom, shows itself and thereby "refers" (*verweist*) to something that does not show itself. Appearance is a referring, and it is possible only because what refers shows itself, that is, is a phenomenon. When the referring is taken "ontically", as a relation between two beings, appearance and the thing behind it, the appearing is degraded to "mere appearance", then confused with semblance, and "on this confusion traditional epistemology and metaphysics live" (GA 20 114). The definition is thus the precondition of everything § 9 then says about method. The phenomenon has no "behind". What can be the matter of phenomenology is "first and mostly covered" (*verdeckt*), so phenomenology is "the work of freeing letting-be-seen", the dismantling of coverings (§9.6). The understanding of the phenomenon as self-showing makes covering-up, not hiddenness behind appearances, the counter-concept of phenomenology.

The new name, "way of being encountered", carries the definition into the Main Part. There the analysis of world is an analysis of encounter. Concern "lets the world be encountered" (*begegnen lassen*), the encounter-structure of the world is "the how of the being in which the being called world shows itself *from itself*" (§21.2–21.3), and §23 is the "positive exhibition" of these encounter-characters. Reference, which § 9 assigned to appearance as its mark, becomes in §23 the encounter-structure of the environing world itself. Things show themselves *in* references, and "reference" "will henceforth be used as a term" (GA 20 251, :3576). The world shows itself through referring, so referring and self-showing turn out to belong together. The light of the etymology returns too. In §31c, uncoveredness gives the concept for the old idea of a natural light (*lumen naturale*) in the human being: Dasein "is lit in itself" (*in sich selbst gelichtet*), while a stone is neither light nor dark (§31.5, GA 20 412). The brightness in which the phenomenon shows itself is Dasein's own.

*Sein und Zeit* keeps the definition and its new name almost word for word. "*Phenomenon* (the showing-itself-in-itself) means a distinctive way in which something is encountered (*eine ausgezeichnete Begegnisart von etwas*). *Appearance*, by contrast, means a referential relation that is in beings themselves" (SZ 31). The 1925 lecture is the place where the formula was worked out. The SZ formula adds one word, "distinctive" (*ausgezeichnete*). The phenomenon is not just any way of being encountered but the one in which the being shows itself as it is.

## §9, GA 20 115–16: logos as letting be seen

Reads [§9.4](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), fourth paragraph under § 9.

*Logos*, for Heidegger, is not "science of" and not, in the first place, speech as sound. It is a letting-be-seen: *apophainesthai*, showing something from itself so that discourse makes manifest what it is about. With this sense, the two halves of the word "phenomenology" turn out to say one thing: to let what shows itself be seen from itself. The passage also carries a restriction that the course later lifts. The *logos* of "-logy" is taken as the theoretical, apophantic *logos*, discourse that grasps a matter "and only" that.

> The expression λόγος goes back to λέγειν. In the word-combinations named, theology, biology and so on, -logy means as much as science of. … But λόγος does not properly mean science; going back to λέγειν it means discourse, discourse of something. … How do the Greeks determine the sense of λέγειν, to discourse? Λέγειν does not mean simply making words and reciting words; rather, the sense of λέγειν is δηλοῦν, to make manifest, to make manifest namely that about which discourse is held and how it is to be discoursed about. Aristotle determines the sense of λόγος more precisely as ἀποφαίνεσθαι: letting something be seen in itself, and that — ἀπό — from itself. In discourse, insofar as it is genuine, what is said is to be drawn — ἀπό — from what is discoursed about, so that the communicating discourse, in its content, in what it says, makes manifest what it discourses about. That is the strict, functional sense of λόγος as Aristotle worked it out.
>
> In concrete performance, discourse has the character of speaking, that is, of vocal utterance in words. In this respect the λόγος is φωνή, voice. But this character does not make up the essence of the λόγος; on the contrary, the character of the φωνή is determined from the proper sense of the λόγος as ἀποφαίνεσθαι, from what discourse properly is: showing, letting see.

German:

> Der Ausdruck λόγος geht auf λέγειν zurück. In den genannten Wortverbindungen Theologie, Biologie usw. besagt -logie soviel wie Wissenschaft von. … λόγος besagt aber eigentlich nicht Wissenschaft, sondern im Rückgang auf λέγειν bedeutet es Rede, Rede von etwas. … Wie bestimmen die Griechen den Sinn von λέγειν — reden? Λέγειν besagt nicht einfach Worte machen und Worte hersagen, sondern der Sinn des λέγειν ist das δηλοῦν, offenbar machen, offenbar machen nämlich das, worüber in der Rede geredet wird und wie darüber geredet werden soll. Genauer bestimmt Aristoteles den Sinn des λόγος als ἀποφαίνεσθαι — sehen lassen von etwas an ihm selbst und zwar — ἀπό — von ihm selbst her. In der Rede soll, sofern sie echt ist, das, was geredet wird — ἀπό —, aus dem, worüber geredet wird, geschöpft sein, so daß die redende Mitteilung in ihrem Gehalt, in dem, was sie sagt, und dem, worüber sie redet, offenbar macht. Das ist der strenge, funktionale Sinn des λόγος, wie ihn Aristoteles herausgearbeitet hat.
>
> Im konkreten Vollzug hat die Rede den Charakter des Sprechens, d. h. der stimmlichen Verlautbarung in Worten. Der λόγος ist in dieser Hinsicht φωνή — Stimme. Dieser Charakter macht aber nicht das Wesen des λόγος aus, sondern umgekehrt, der Charakter der φωνή bestimmt sich aus dem eigentlichen Sinn des λόγος als ἀποφαίνεσθαι, aus dem, was die Rede eigentlich ist – aufzeigende, sehenlassende.

The passage defines *logos* by stripping off two inherited meanings and fixing a third. The first to go is "science of", the meaning "-logy" has in theology and biology. Heidegger concedes it as the usual rendering and denies it as the proper one: λόγος "does not properly mean science" (*besagt aber eigentlich nicht Wissenschaft*). The second to go is word and sound. λέγειν does not mean "making words and reciting words", and the *logos* as voice (φωνή) is determined from its sense, "on the contrary" (*umgekehrt*), not the other way round. What remains is a function, named three times: δηλοῦν, to make manifest; ἀποφαίνεσθαι, to let be seen; and the German compounds *aufzeigende, sehenlassende*, showing, letting see. The German *sehen lassen* (let see) renders the middle ἀποφαίνεσθαι by a verb of permitting, not of making. Discourse does not produce what it shows; it lets it be seen.

The preposition carries the rest. Heidegger isolates ἀπό with dashes, twice, and glosses it "from itself" (*von ihm selbst her*). In genuine discourse what is said must be "drawn" (*geschöpft*) from what is discoursed about. The *logos* is measured by the thing, not the thing by the *logos*. This is the same "from itself" that defined the phenomenon (row 12), and § 9b draws the consequence at once. Since *logos* as *apophainesthai* "has in itself a material relation to φαινόμενον", phenomenology is λέγειν τὰ φαινόμενα, "to let what is manifest in itself be seen from itself". The maxim "to the things themselves" "gives back nothing other than the name of phenomenology" (§9.5, GA 20 116–17). The two halves of the name do not join a subject-matter to a science. They repeat one structure, self-showing taken up by a letting-be-seen. "Phenomenology" is therefore a methodological title: it names the how, not the what (§9.5).

The paragraph's end, after the passage, sets a limit that the course will later revise. Every *logos* is *semantikos*, meaningful, but not every one is *apophantikos*: a request, a wish, a prayer means something without letting something be seen as it is. The *logos* of "-logy", and so of "phenomeno-logy", is the apophantic one, "the *logos* as θεωρεῖν, discourse in the sense of matter-grasping and *only* matter-grasping communication" (GA 20 116). Phenomenology's own *logos* is thus defined from the theoretical. This sits uneasily with the warning in § 5 that Husserl's noesis read the practical from the theoretical (row 7). In 1925 Heidegger accepts the restriction for his method while suspecting it in Husserl's description of acts.

The Main Part undoes the restriction by founding the apophantic *logos* in interpretation. In §28c the primary form of all interpretation is "addressing something *as* something", which "need not express itself in the linguistic form of a sentence"; the grammatical sentence is only one expression of this "primary and proper 'sentence'". Language exists "only insofar as" there is such addressing, which exists only insofar as there is interpretation, understanding and the uncoveredness of being-in-the-world (GA 20 360, :4492; §28.4). The *logos* that § 9 restricted to "only matter-grasping" communication is now a derivative of a pre-theoretical letting-be-seen in which the world is already articulated. *Sein und Zeit* gives this the form of a distinction between two "as"-structures. The assertion and "its structure, the apophantic 'as'" are "founded in interpretation and its structure, the hermeneutical 'as'" (SZ 223). History bears on this too: "the phenomenon of the apophantic 'as'" came into view early in the knowledge of the *logos*, and the way it was first seen "has had its effect on the subsequent history of logic" (SZ 158). The definition of *logos* as letting-be-seen survives intact from GA 20 § 9 to SZ § 7B (SZ 32–33). What changes between them is the rank of the theoretical *logos*. In § 9 it gives phenomenology its name; by 1927 it is a founded mode of something more original.

## §11, GA 20 144–45: consciousness as absolute in the order of constitution

Reads [§11.4](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), fourth paragraph under § 11.

The third of Husserl's four determinations calls consciousness "absolute" because it "needs no thing in order to exist" (*nulla re indiget ad existendum*). Heidegger reads the word through its function. Consciousness is absolute because everything real constitutes itself in it, so it is "the earlier, the a priori in the Cartesian and Kantian sense". The understanding the passage carries is therefore a priority of order. Consciousness comes first in the order of constitution, not in the order of being, and the determination says nothing about what consciousness is. It is the point, Heidegger says, at which idealism breaks into phenomenology.

> Consciousness, the immanently, absolutely given being, is that in which every possible other being constitutes itself, in which it properly *is* what it is. Absolute is the constituting being. Every other being, as reality, is only in relation to consciousness, that is, relative to it. "Thus the common sense of the talk of being is reversed. The being that is first for us is in itself the second, that is, it is what it is only in 'relation' to the first." This first, which must be presupposed, which must already be there in order that the real can announce itself, this first being has the privilege of not needing reality; on the contrary, reality needs the first being. …
>
> Absolute being therefore means: not being dependent on another, and that with regard to constitution; the first, that is, what must already be there in order that anything meant can be at all. … Consciousness is the earlier, the a priori in the Cartesian and Kantian sense.
>
> Consciousness in the meaning of the absolute says: priority of subjectivity over all objectivity. This third determination, absolute being, is again not one that determines the being itself in its being, but one that grasps the region of consciousness within the order of constitution and attributes to it, within this order, a formal being-earlier before everything objective. This determination and conception of consciousness is at the same time the place where idealism and the idealist way of asking, more precisely idealism in the sense of neo-Kantianism, breaks into phenomenology.

German:

> Das Bewußtsein, das immanent, absolut gegebene Sein, ist das, in dem jedes mögliche andere Seiende sich konstituiert, in dem es eigentlich *ist*, was es ist. Absolut ist das konstituierende Sein. Jedes andere Sein als Realität ist nur in Beziehung zum Bewußtsein, d. h. relativ auf es. »So kehrt sich der gemeine Sinn der Seinsrede um. Das Sein, das für uns das Erste ist, ist an sich das Zweite, d. h. es ist, was es ist, nur in >Beziehung< zum Ersten.« Dieses Erste, das vorausgesetzt werden muß, das schon da sein muß, damit sich Reales bekunden kann, dieses erste Sein hat den Vorzug, daß es der Realität nicht bedarf, sondern umgekehrt diese des ersten Seins. …
>
> Absolutes Sein besagt daher: Nicht-Angewiesensein auf anderes und zwar im Hinblick auf die Konstitution, das Erste, d. h. das, was schon da sein muß, damit Vermeintes überhaupt soll sein können. … Das Bewußtsein ist das Frühere, das Apriori im *Descartes’schen* und *Kantischen* Sinne.
>
> Das Bewußtsein in der Bedeutung des Absoluten besagt: Vorrang der Subjektivität vor jeder Objektivität. Diese dritte Bestimmung — absolutes Sein — ist wiederum keine, die das Seiende selbst in seinem Sein bestimmt, sondern eine, die die Bewußtseinsregion innerhalb der Ordnung der Konstitution faßt und ihr in dieser Ordnung ein formales Frühersein vor jedem Objektiven zuspricht. Diese Bestimmung und Fassung des Bewußtseins ist zugleich die Stelle, wo der Idealismus und die idealistische Fragestellung, genauer der Idealismus im Sinne des Neukantianismus, in die Phänomenologie hereinbricht.

*Note on the text.* The section title in the file reads "absolutes Gegebensein" (absolute givenness), where the table of contents and the argument have "absolute being"; the reading follows the argument. A stray line consisting of a single full stop interrupts the paragraph before this one (:2173). Husserl's own sentence, which supplies the Latin, is in the corpus only in the unreviewed conversion of *Ideen I* (§49, `husserl-hua-3-1-…-recheck.md`, :2357). There it reads that immanent being is absolute in the sense that it "in principle *nulla 're' indiget ad existendum*". The English cross-check file is too garbled at §49 to confirm it.

The passage builds its understanding from a vocabulary of need and order. Consciousness is "the first" (*das Erste*), "what must already be there" (*was schon da sein muß*), and has "the privilege" (*Vorzug*) of "not needing" (*nicht bedarf*) reality, while reality "needs" it. The verb *bedürfen* translates the Latin *indiget*: Husserl's "needs no thing" becomes Heidegger's "is not dependent on another" (*Nicht-Angewiesensein auf anderes*). Heidegger then adds the qualification that empties the predicate: "and that with regard to constitution" (*im Hinblick auf die Konstitution*). Need is measured in one respect only, the order in which things announce themselves to a consciousness. Husserl's own sentence, which Heidegger quotes, shows the operation in miniature: "the common sense of the talk of being is reversed (*kehrt sich … um*)". What is first for us is second in itself. A reversal of order is offered as a determination of being.

Heidegger's gloss names what has happened, and the name is a term from § 7. Consciousness "is the earlier, the a priori in the Cartesian and Kantian sense" (*das Frühere, das Apriori*). In § 7 phenomenology had shown that the a priori "is not a title of comportment but a title of being", "indifferent to subjectivity" (§7.3, row 11). Here Husserl's absolute consciousness takes the a priori back into the subject. The "being-earlier" (*Frühersein*) attributed to it is "formal", an earlier in the order of constitution, the very sense of the a priori that phenomenology was supposed to have overcome. The closing sentence uses a verb of violent entry: idealism "breaks into" (*hereinbricht*) phenomenology. The image is of a breach, not a development. The determination comes from outside the matter, from "neo-Kantianism", whose return to consciousness § 4 had described (§4.5).

The Latin phrase has a history in the course. In §22 Heidegger reads Descartes' definition of substance, "a thing that so exists that it needs no other thing in order to exist". He concludes that, strictly, only one being satisfies it: God, the being "unneeding of production and preservation" (*Unbedürftigkeit*, GA 20 233–34, :3366–:3368, §22.2). In Descartes, needing nothing is the mark of the *ens perfectissimum*. In Husserl's §49, as Heidegger quotes it in his title, the same formula marks pure consciousness. The position of the being that needs nothing passes from God to consciousness, and § 11 shows that the transfer settles nothing about the being of what is now absolute. Need and need-lessness are relations of dependence among beings in the order of constitution, and the question of what consciousness is stays unasked.

The passage is the third step of a demonstration whose point is made after all four. The four determinations, immanent, absolutely given, absolute in constitution and pure, are won "with a view to" consciousness as "grasped, given, constituting, ideated as essence". None of them is drawn "from the being itself". They are suited to "bar" the question of its being (§11.6). Heidegger names their source: "the idea of an *absolute science*", which "has occupied modern philosophy since Descartes" (§11.6). The third determination is the most consequential of the four because it ranks the regions of being. It makes consciousness the measure of the reality of everything else, and §13 will show that this ranking claims to be "the most radical of all distinctions of being" without asking what "being" means in it (row 16).

The thread that runs from this passage is the replacement of an order of constitution by an order of being. In GA 20 that replacement is the analytic of Dasein. Its first fundamental character, being in each case mine and having "to be" (§18.2, row 22), determines a being by its way of being, not by its rank in an order of givenness. When the analytic reaches the question of the external world, the inversion Husserl performed is itself inverted. Not consciousness first and reality second, but "*sum cogito*": I am in a world, and therefore I can think at all (§24.2, row 27). What comes first is no longer a constituting consciousness but a being that is already alongside a world.

## §12, GA 20 151: the reduction gives the ground out of hand

Reads [§12.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 12.

The phenomenological reduction, Heidegger argues, is by its method a "looking away from" (*Absehen-von*): it disregards the reality of consciousness in order to win pure consciousness. A method that looks away from the reality of the intentional cannot then determine its being. The reduction "gives out of hand" the only ground on which the being of the intentional could be asked about. The understanding the passage carries is the ground (*Boden*) the reduction surrenders: the factual human being, experienced as it is. The analytic of the Main Part begins on exactly that ground.

> Let us call to mind the sense and methodological task of the *phenomenological reduction*: from the factual, real consciousness given in the natural attitude, pure consciousness is to be won. This is accomplished in looking away from what is posited as real, in going back from every real positing itself. In the reduction, precisely the reality of consciousness, given in the natural attitude in the factual human being, is disregarded. The real experience is switched off as real, in order to win the pure, absolute (*ἐποχή*). The sense of the reduction is precisely to make no use of the reality of the intentional; it is not posited and experienced as real. … Accordingly, the reduction, by its methodological sense as looking-away-from, is in principle unsuited to determine positively the being of consciousness. In the sense of the reduction, precisely the ground is given out of hand on which alone one could ask about the being of the intentional (to be sure, with the intention of first determining the sense of this reality from the region now won). … Admittedly one must be careful here, insofar as Husserl would answer here: the sense of the reduction is precisely *first* to look away from reality, in order then to be able to consider it precisely as reality, as it announces itself in the pure consciousness that I win through the reduction.

German:

> Vergegenwärtigen wir uns den Sinn und die methodische Aufgabe der *phänomenologischen Reduktion*: Aus dem in der natürlichen Einstellung gegebenen faktischen, realen Bewußtsein ist das reine Bewußtsein zu gewinnen. Dies vollzieht sich in der Absehung von Realgesetztem, im Rückgang von jeder realen Setzung selbst. Von der Realität des Bewußtseins, gegeben in der natürlichen Einstellung am faktischen Menschen, wird gerade in der Reduktion abgesehen. Das reale Erlebnis wird als reales ausgeschaltet, um das reine, absolute zu gewinnen (*ἐποχή*). Der Sinn der Reduktion ist gerade, von der Realität des Intentionalen keinen Gebrauch zu machen; es wird nicht als Reales gesetzt und erfahren. … Sonach ist die Reduktion ihrem methodischen Sinne nach als Absehen-von grundsätzlich ungeeignet, positiv das Sein des Bewußtseins zu bestimmen. Im Sinne der Reduktion wird gerade der Boden aus der Hand gegeben, auf dem einzig nach dem Sein des Intentionalen gefragt werden könnte, (freilich in der Absicht, um von der nun gewonnenen Region her erst gerade den Sinn dieser Realität zu bestimmen). … Freilich muß man hier vorsichtig sein, sofern *Husserl* hier antworten würde: Der Sinn der Reduktion ist es gerade *zunächst* von der Realität abzusehen, um sie dann gerade als Realität betrachten zu können, so wie sie sich im reinen Bewußtsein, das ich durch die Reduktion gewinne, bekundet.

The argument rests on one verb and its noun. "Looking away" (*absehen*, *Absehung*) appears three times in the first paragraph before Heidegger hyphenates it into a definition: the reduction is "*Absehen-von*", looking-away-from. The hyphen turns the method into a structure with a direction, away from something, and the direction decides what the method can find. What it looks away from is said each time with care: "what is posited as real", "the reality of consciousness, given in the natural attitude in the factual human being", "the reality of the intentional". The reduction does not doubt or deny this reality; it makes "no use" of it (*keinen Gebrauch*). Heidegger's objection follows strictly from the verb. A gaze turned away from a being's reality is "in principle unsuited" (*grundsätzlich ungeeignet*) to determine that being's being.

The image that carries the conclusion is the one § 5 used against phenomenology's critics. In § 5 whoever refutes intentionality by criticizing Brentano "gives the theme out of hand" (row 8). Here "precisely the ground is given out of hand" (*gerade der Boden aus der Hand gegeben*) on which alone the being of the intentional could be asked about. A theme surrendered became a ground surrendered, and the agent changes: not the critics, but the method of phenomenology itself. *Boden* (ground, soil) is the word that matters. The reduction leaves the soil on which consciousness actually occurs, "the factual human being", for a region it has to win. Heidegger is scrupulous about Husserl's reply. In a parenthesis he grants the intention behind the reduction, to determine the sense of reality "from the region now won". He then gives Husserl's answer in the subjunctive (*würde antworten*) with the emphasis on "*first*" (*zunächst*): first look away, then consider reality as it announces itself. The fairness sharpens the objection. Reality returns in Husserl only as what "announces itself" in pure consciousness, as an object for a constituting regard. The being of the intentional is again determined as objectivity.

The rest of § 12 makes the same point about the starting place of the reduction. The "natural attitude" from which it starts is not natural. It is "a theoretical experience" that gives the human being as *zōon*, a natural object occurring in the world (§12.1, GA 20 155–56). The ground was surrendered twice: once when the reduction looked away from it, and earlier, when it was described in theoretical terms in the first place. This sets the task of the Main Part. If the being of the intentional can be asked about only on the ground the reduction leaves, the analytic must start there and describe it without the theoretical prejudice. §18 does so. Its field is "the everydayness of the everyday", factical Dasein in "its nearest everydayness", and it is not to be determined by "what it consists of" (§18.3–18.4). The ground of § 12 becomes the field of § 18.

Heidegger kept the word "reduction" and reversed its direction. In 1927 he called "phenomenological reduction" the leading back of the investigating gaze "from the naively grasped being to being". He did this "in its wording, not in its substance" (*dem Wortlaut, nicht aber der Sache nach*), and distinguished it explicitly from Husserl's reduction, the leading back from the natural attitude to transcendental consciousness (GA 24 29). He added at once that the reduction is "not the only, indeed not even the central, element" of method. Looking away from beings "needs" a positive leading toward being, since being "is not accessible as beings are" (GA 24 29). The structure of the 1925 objection survives in the 1927 method. A reduction that only looks away gets nothing, so what decides the method is where the gaze is led.

## §13, GA 20 158–59: the abyss of sense

Reads [§13.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 13.

Husserl wrote that between being as consciousness and being as reality "yawns a veritable abyss of sense". Heidegger takes the phrase at its word. A gap in *sense* between two kinds of being presupposes that one knows what "being" means in each, and that is exactly what Husserl never asked. The understanding the passage carries is the question the abyss conceals: what directs the distinction between the regions of being, the sense of being. The question of being is shown to be not an optional addition to phenomenology but "the most pressing question in phenomenology's ownmost sense".

> But we now see the remarkable thing: here it is claimed to win the most radical distinction of being without the being of the beings that enter into the distinction being properly asked about. More still: here there is a discussion of being, a distinction of regions of beings, that is, it is pretended that the distinction is made with regard to being. If we ask further what "being" means here, with regard to which absolute being is distinguished from reality,¹ we seek in vain for an answer, and still more for an explicit posing of this question itself. In winning this fundamental distinction of being, there is not even a question about the kind of being of the things distinguished, not about the kind of being of consciousness, and in principle not about what directs the whole distinguishing of the distinction of being: the sense of being. From this it becomes clear that the question of being is no arbitrary, merely possible question, but the most pressing question precisely in phenomenology's ownmost sense.
>
> ¹ In the fundamental division between being as consciousness and being as reality "yawns a veritable abyss of sense". Ibid., p. 93 (117).

German:

> Aber wir sehen jetzt das Merkwürdige, daß hier beansprucht wird, den radikalsten Seinsunterschied zu gewinnen, ohne daß nach dem Sein der Seienden, die in den Unterschied eingehen, eigentlich gefragt ist. Noch mehr: es ist hier eine Seinserörterung, eine Unterscheidung von seienden Regionen, das heißt, es wird vorgegeben, es sei unterschieden im Hinblick auf das Sein. Wenn wir weiter fragen, was hier Sein heißt, im Hinblick worauf das absolute Sein gegenüber der Realität unterschieden wird[^37], so suchen wir vergebens nach einer Antwort und noch mehr nach einer ausdrücklichen Stellung dieser Frage selbst. Bei der Gewinnung dieses fundamentalen Seinsunterschiedes ist nicht einmal nach der Seinsart der Unterschiedenen selbst gefragt, nach der Seinsart des Bewußtseins nicht und grundsätzlich nicht nach dem, was die ganze Unterscheidung des Seinsunterschiedes überhaupt dirigiert – nach dem Sinn von Sein. Daraus wird deutlich, daß die Seinsfrage keine beliebige, nur mögliche Frage, sondern die dringlichste Frage gerade im eigensten Sinne der Phänomenologie selbst ist
>
> [^37]: In der Fundamentalscheidung: Sein als Bewußtsein und Sein als Realität »gähnt ein wahrer Abgrund des Sinnes«. a.a.O., S. 93 (117).

*Note on the text.* Husserl's phrase stands in a footnote, attached to the question "what 'being' means here". The page reference is to *Ideen I* §49, the first edition's p. 93. The main text is on p. 158 (:2399–:2405), and the sentence continues past the quoted end with "— urgent in a far more radical sense with regard to the intentional".

Heidegger's sentences turn on the gap between claiming and asking. The distinction is "claimed" (*beansprucht*); being is "pretended" (*vorgegeben*) to be its respect; and the question that would give the claim a sense is sought "in vain" (*vergebens*). The vocabulary of the passage is a vocabulary of pretension, and it answers § 9, where semblance was "pretension to the manifest" (row 12). A distinction of being that never asks what being means is a semblance of an ontological distinction. Heidegger heightens the charge with "more still" (*Noch mehr*). Husserl does not merely neglect being; he presents his division of regions as made "with regard to being". The final clause names what is missing with a verb of command: the sense of being is what "directs" (*dirigiert*) the whole distinction. It governs the distinction whether or not anyone asks about it.

The footnote places Husserl's own words where the question is posed, and the placement is the argument. "Abyss of sense" (*Abgrund des Sinnes*) is Husserl's image for how far apart consciousness and reality are. Heidegger attaches it to the words "what 'being' means here". The phrase that measures the gap between the regions becomes evidence that the measure was never examined. An abyss of *sense* can only be asserted by someone who presupposes a sense on both sides. The word *Sinn* does the turning. In Husserl it means the meaning-content of a region of being, what consciousness and reality each are. In Heidegger's question it means the sense of being as such, what "is" means when said of both. Husserl's "abyss of sense" thus speaks of sense while leaving the sense of being, which the course is about, in the dark.

The passage opens § 13 and sets out its two neglects. Just before it, §12 has shown that the reduction surrenders the ground for asking about the being of the intentional (row 15). Here Heidegger anticipates the objection that asking about being is idle, that it suffices to determine the *what*. He answers it from Husserl's own aim. The exposition of pure consciousness "aims precisely at winning the fundamental distinction within beings", that is, "at bottom, at answering the question of being". Husserl is quoted to that effect: the doctrine of categories "must start" from "this most radical of all distinctions of being" (GA 20 157–58, :2387). The passage then draws the consequence. Phenomenology needs the question of being for its own enterprise, so the question is not imposed from outside. §13 proceeds to ask why it was nonetheless neglected, and finds the answer in tradition and in Dasein's falling (row 18).

The abyss returns at the centre of the analytic, moved from between two regions of being into Dasein itself. In anxiety Dasein finds itself before the bare fact "that I am", and this facticity is no presence-at-hand. "The ground is an *existential*, that is, a disclosed ground — and indeed an *abyss*" (*Abgrund*, GA 20 403, :4835). This is "the existential positivity of the nothing of anxiety". Husserl's abyss separated consciousness from reality and was asserted from above both. Heidegger's abyss is the groundlessness of the one who asks about being, disclosed from within, in a disposedness. The image of a gulf in the structure of what is gives way to a gulf in the ground of the questioner. In 1925 the course makes this move without comment. The two uses of the word lie nearly 250 pages apart, and the reading of the passage is complete only when they are read together.

## §13, GA 20 172–74: the human being as *animal rationale*

Reads [§13.6](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), sixth paragraph under § 13.

Behind every question about consciousness, the psychic, spirit and person in phenomenology stands the old definition of the human being, *animal rationale*. Heidegger reads it as a formula of composition: a present-at-hand world-thing, *animal*, that "has reason with it", *rationale*. Whoever takes the definition as a guiding thread will try to build the being of the human from layers (body, soul, spirit) and will never reach it, because the layering already treats the human as a thing. The understanding the passage carries is the definition as composition, and the analytic is defined against it.

> And even if the being of the acts and the unity of the stream of experience were determined in their being, there would still remain the question of the *being* of the full concrete human being. Can its being be, as it were, *built together* from the being of the material substratum, the lived body, the soul and the spirit? Is the being of the person the product of the ways of being of these strata of being? Or does it show itself precisely here that on this path of prior partition and subsequent combination one cannot get at the phenomena, that in this way precisely — for all the tendency toward the personal — the person is taken as a *multi-layered world-thing*, whose being is never reached by any determination of the reality of its directing-itself, however far it is pushed? …
>
> The consideration of the possibility of the personalistic attitude has led us to a correct insight: that in the background of all questions about the intentional, the psychic, about consciousness, experience, life, human being, reason, spirit, person, I, subject, stands the old definition of the human being — *animal rationale*. But is this definition drawn from experiences that aim at a primary experience of the being of the human being, or does it not spring from the experience of the human being as a present-at-hand world-thing — *animal* — that has reason with it — *rationale*?

German:

> Aber selbst wenn das Sein der Akte und die Einheit des Erlebnistromes seinsmäßig bestimmt wären, dann bliebe immer noch die Frage nach dem *Sein* des vollen konkreten Menschen. Läßt sich dessen Sein gleichsam aus dem Sein des materiellen Untergrundes, des Leibes, der Seele und dem Geist *zusammenbauen*? Ist das Sein der Person das Produkt der Seinsarten dieser Seinsschichten? Oder zeigt sich gerade hier, daß auf diesem Wege einer vorherigen Aufteilung und nachkommenden Zusammennahme nicht an die Phänomene heranzukommen ist, daß so gerade — bei aller Tendenz auf das Personale — die Person als ein *mehrschichtiges Weltding* genommen wird, dessen Sein durch eine auch noch so weit getriebene Bestimmung der Realität seines Sichrichtens nie erreicht wird? …
>
> Die Betrachtung der Möglichkeit der personalistischen Einstellung hat uns zu einer richtigen Einsicht geführt, daß im Hintergrund aller Fragen nach dem Intentionalen, Psychischen, nach Bewußtsein, Erlebnis, Leben, Mensch, Vernunft, Geist, Person, Ich, Subjekt die alte Definition des Menschen — *animal rationale* — steht. Ist diese Definition aber aus Erfahrungen geschöpft, die auf primäre Erfahrung des Seins des Menschen zielen, oder entspringt sie nicht der Erfahrung des Menschen als eines vorhandenen Weltdinges — *animal* —, das Vernunft bei sich hat — *rationale*?

*Note on the text.* The file has "Erlebnistromes" (one *s*) in the first sentence, quoted as it stands. The passage runs from p. 173 to p. 174; the omitted paragraph is the transition through Dilthey that the reading cites.

The passage builds its understanding from a vocabulary of construction. The being of the human being would have to be "built together" (*zusammenbauen*, italicized) from "the material substratum, the lived body, the soul and the spirit". It would be "the product" of the "ways of being of these strata" (*Seinsschichten*). The method that does this is "prior partition and subsequent combination" (*vorherige Aufteilung und nachkommende Zusammennahme*). The person so treated is "a *multi-layered world-thing*" (*ein mehrschichtiges Weltding*). Each phrase describes a building made of floors, with nature as the "fundamental stratum" (*Fundamentalschicht*, GA 20 172) on which the psychic and then the spiritual are set. The italicized compound says what that picture assumes. The person is a *thing* of the world, however many floors it has.

The second paragraph splits the definition itself with dashes. "A present-at-hand world-thing — *animal* — that has reason with it — *rationale*". The dashes isolate the two words of the formula and pair each with a gloss. *Animal* is rendered as "present-at-hand world-thing" (*vorhandenes Weltding*), the kind of being of nature. *Rationale* is rendered as something the thing "has with it" (*bei sich hat*), a possession or equipment. The question that frames them, "drawn from … or does it not spring from…", is phrased to expect the second answer. The definition comes not from an experience of the being of the human being but from the experience of a thing. The long list before it, "the intentional, the psychic, … consciousness, experience, life, human being, reason, spirit, person, I, subject", gathers every title phenomenology has used for its theme under the one formula. All the names for the bearer of intentionality, whose source row 8 found unasked, are traced to one origin.

The passage closes Heidegger's account of phenomenology's attempts to determine the being of acts from within. Husserl's personalistic attitude in *Ideen II* had seemed to promise more: the person as a "unity-formation" of a principally different kind from things (§13.5). Heidegger names three limits, of which this "is the most important". The personal is still approached through constitution, through reflection on acts (*inspectio sui*), and through the definition as guiding thread (GA 20 172). The passage shows why the third limit governs the other two: a person built from strata is a thing with reason added, and only reflection on its acts could find the addition. The result of § 13 extends the charge to Scheler, who takes over the specifically Christian definition and so becomes "some degrees more dogmatic" (§13.9, GA 20 180), and §13f names the definition among the forms in which tradition rules the questioner (row 18).

The analytic is defined against this formula from its first page. §18 places itself "in principle outside this horizon of experience and questioning that the definition … *homo animal rationale* … prescribes". It determines "not the look of this being" but "solely *its way to be*, not the what of what it consists of, but *the how of its being*" (GA 20 207, :3074–:3078, row 22). The composition of § 13 is answered by the unity of a way of being. §32 states the result. Dasein is "neither a coupling of comportments nor a composite of body, soul and spirit" (§32.1). The structures exhibited are not parts but "being-manners" of one being, whose wholeness is care. What was built together from layers in § 13 has been replaced by a whole that "lays itself" into each of its ways of being.

*Sein und Zeit* keeps both halves of the reading and sharpens each. In § 10 the definition ζῷον λόγον ἔχον, "interpreted as *animal rationale*, rational living being", is the first of two sources of the traditional anthropology. "The kind of being of the ζῷον is understood here in the sense of being present-at-hand and occurring", and the *logos* is "a higher endowment whose kind of being remains as dark as that of the being so composed" (SZ 48). GA 20's "has reason with it" has become "endowment" (*Ausstattung*), and the "composite" has become explicit. In § 34 the Greek formula is given back its phenomenal sense. The human being "shows itself as a being that speaks", that is, a being that is "in the way of uncovering the world and Dasein itself". The later Latin interpretation "is not 'false', but it covers over the phenomenal ground from which this definition of Dasein is taken" (SZ §34). Heidegger's critique of the formula ends by recovering, beneath it, the Greek experience it had buried.

## §13, GA 20 179–80: the neglect grounded in Dasein's falling

Reads [§13.9](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), ninth paragraph under § 13.

The neglect of the question of being, Heidegger concludes, is not a mistake of the philosophers but a feature of the one who asks. "In these neglects the history of our Dasein itself reveals itself", and Dasein is a being that falls (*verfällt*) into the interpretation it has been handed. Falling is not escaped. Dasein "first comes to its being" by rearing up against it. The understanding the passage carries is falling as the ground of the neglect, and with it the way out: not a better theory but a counter-movement within Dasein. This is the passage in which the course turns from critique to the analytic.

> The two neglects, first the neglect of the question of being as such and second the neglect of the question of the being of the intentional, are not accidental negligences of the philosophers. In these neglects the *history of our Dasein itself* reveals itself, history understood not as the totality of public events, but history as the *manner of happening of Dasein itself*. This means: Dasein, in its kind of being of falling, which it does not escape, first comes to its being precisely in rearing up against it. The rule of the *ontological* and *anthropological* and with it also of the *logical tradition* will assert itself in philosophy the more easily and self-evidently, the more philosophy itself, in projecting its tasks and questions and in the ways and means of answering them, places itself back into the tradition.

German:

> Die beiden Versäumnisse: Erstens das Versäumnis der Frage nach dem Sein als solchem und zweitens das Versäumnis der Frage nach dem Sein des Intentionalen sind nicht zufällige Nachlässigkeiten der Philosophen, sondern in diesen Versäumnissen offenbart sich die *Geschichte unseres Daseins selbst* — Geschichte nicht als die Gesamtheit der öffentlichen Ereignisse verstanden, sondern Geschichte als die *Geschehensart des Daseins selbst*. Das bedeutet: Dasein in seiner Seinsart des Verfallens, der es selbst nicht entrinnt, kommt gerade darin erst zu seinem Sein, wenn es sich dagegen aufbäumt. Die Herrschaft der *ontologischen* und *anthropologischen* und damit auch der *logischen Tradition* wird sich in der Philosophie um so leichter und selbstverständlicher behaupten, je mehr diese selbst im Entwurf ihrer Aufgaben und Fragen, in den Wegen und Mitteln ihrer Beantwortung sich wieder in die Tradition hineinstellt …

The passage's sentence on falling is built on a paradox. Dasein "does not escape" (*nicht entrinnt*) its falling, and yet it "first comes to its being" (*kommt … erst zu seinem Sein*) "precisely in" (*gerade darin*) rearing up against it. The verb *sich aufbäumen* is a horse's rearing against the rein, a resistance that does not get free of what it resists. Two claims are held together. Falling is constitutive, not an accident one could avoid. And Dasein's own being is reached only in a counter-movement that falling makes necessary. "Precisely in" and "first" place the coming-to-oneself inside the struggle, not after it. The rest of the paragraph describes the alternative: philosophy "places itself back" (*sich wieder … hineinstellt*) into the tradition, and the tradition then rules "the more easily and self-evidently". Falling is the default; rearing up is the exception that has to be achieved.

The passage rules out two explanations of the neglect and replaces them. The neglects "are not accidental negligences" (*nicht zufällige Nachlässigkeiten*), and the paragraph before called the more fundamental neglect "no mere carelessness, no mere overlooking (*Übersehen*) of a question to be posed". The word is the one that, in § 4, described Husserl's failure to "survey" his own work (row 5). The neglects are not failures of attention. They "reveal" (*offenbart sich*) something, the "history of our Dasein itself", and Heidegger at once excludes the public sense of "history". History is "the manner of happening of Dasein itself" (*Geschehensart*). That is why falling can explain a history of philosophy: the history of the question of being is a case of the way Dasein happens. The decline of § 2, "grounded in the being of Dasein" (row 2), here receives its ground. The tradition that rules is threefold, "*ontological* and *anthropological* and with it also *logical*": the Greek categories of being, the definition of the human being (row 17), and the logic of the assertion (row 13).

The passage concludes the Preparatory Part and opens the Main Part. Before it, §13f has said that phenomenology is "*unphenomenological*! — that is, *supposedly* phenomenological!" (GA 20 178, :2677). Wherever being is handled without being asked about, the categories of Plato and Aristotle are "in effect", and the question Plato posed in the *Sophist* "has fallen silent since Aristotle, and so fallen silent that one no longer knows that it has" (GA 20 179). The passage gives that silence its ground in Dasein. After it, the course draws the consequence in the opening of the Main Part. The question of being must be posed by working out the being of the questioner, because the questioner's way of being is what keeps it unposed (§14–§17). A better theory of consciousness would not help, since the neglect is not a theoretical error. What helps is an analytic of the being that falls.

Falling returns in the Main Part as a structure of everydayness. Idle talk, curiosity and ambiguity are its modes, and its movement is "tempting, tranquilizing, alienating" (§29). The word that had named the ground of a historical neglect becomes the name of "a constitutive being-structure of Dasein", in which Dasein "at first constantly has its being" (§29.1, row 32). The rearing up of § 13 also returns, as the movement out of *das Man*. "Running ahead to death at every moment of Dasein means the fetching back of Dasein out of the anyone, in the sense of self-choosing" (§34.8). The course thus begins and ends with the same figure. Dasein reaches its being only by turning against a falling it cannot escape. In § 13 that turning is philosophy's against its tradition; in § 34 it is Dasein's against the anyone.

*Sein und Zeit* opens where this passage ends. Its epigraph is the question from Plato's *Sophist* that GA 20 §13f quotes, and its first section repeats the diagnosis in almost the same words. "The question named is today forgotten." It held Plato and Aristotle in suspense, "only to fall silent (*verstummen*) from then on, as a thematic question of actual investigation" (SZ 2). Within a few pages, as in GA 20, the forgetting is grounded in Dasein. Ontology "falls prey to the tradition" (*der Tradition verfällt*) (SZ 21), and the destruction of the history of ontology is the task that answers it. In 1927, as in 1925, the forgetting of being is a falling of Dasein, and the response is a counter-movement within the history Dasein is.

## §16, GA 20 194–95: the structure of the question of being

Reads [§16.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 16.

Every question, Heidegger argues, has three moments: what it is out to obtain, what it asks about, and what it addresses with its asking. In the question of being these are the sense of being, the being of beings, and beings themselves. The understanding the passage carries is this structure, and its use. Taken seriously as a structure, it shows that the question cannot be posed without first determining a being in its being: the being that asks. The triad is not a classification of questions. It is the formal device by which the course passes from the question of being to the analytic of Dasein.

> The question asks after what being means. *The sense of being* is what is *asked-for* in the question. That is, what the question as such has to win, what is to be brought out in the answer, is the sense of being. What, looked at more closely, is *asked about* with this asked-for? If being is asked for in this way, that means: asking after the basic character of beings, what determines beings as beings. What determines beings as beings is *their being*. In the sense of being lies the *asked-about*: the *being of beings*. In the asked-for lies the asked-about, what is to be reached in the question. If beings are to be determined in their being, they must be questioned down toward this their being. The asked-about, the being of beings, and with it the exhibiting determination of the sense of being, becomes exhibitable only if beings themselves as beings are *interrogated* toward their being, that is, beings must be accessible in themselves in their being. The *interrogated* is beings themselves. In the asked-about lies the interrogated; in the sense of being of a being lies the being itself. So we have first drawn out a threefold in the structure of the question and of questioning, quite formally: 1. the *asked-for*: the sense of being; 2. the *asked-about*: the being of beings; 3. the *interrogated*: beings themselves.

German:

> Die Frage fragt nach dem, was Sein besagt. *Der Sinn von Sein* ist das in der Frage *Erfragte*. Das heißt, das, was die Frage als solche zu gewinnen hat, was in der Antwort herauszustellen ist, ist der Sinn von Sein. Was ist genauer besehen mit diesem Erfragten *gefragt*? Wenn das Sein so erfragt wird, so heißt das: Fragen nach dem Grundcharakter des Seienden, was Seiendes als Seiendes bestimmt. Was das Seiende als Seiendes bestimmt, ist *sein Sein*. Im Sinn von Sein liegt: das *Gefragte* — das *Sein des Seienden*. Im Erfragten liegt das Gefragte, was in der Frage erreicht werden soll. Soll das Seiende in seinem Sein bestimmt werden, so muß es auf dieses sein Sein hin abgefragt werden. Das Gefragte — das Sein des Seienden — und damit die ausweisende Bestimmung des Sinnes von Sein wird nur aufweisbar, wenn das Seiende selbst als Seiendes auf sein Sein hin *befragt* ist, d. h. das Seiende muß an ihm selbst in seinem Sein zugänglich sein. Das *Befragte* ist das Seiende selbst. Im Gefragten liegt das Befragte, im Sinn von Sein eines Seienden liegt das Seiende selbst. So haben wir in der Struktur der Frage und des Fragens zunächst ein Dreifaches herausgeholt, ganz formal: 1. Das *Erfragte*: Der Sinn von Sein. 2. Das *Gefragte*: Das Sein von Seiendem. 3. Das *Befragte*: Das Seiende selbst.

*Note on the text.* The file prints the running head "§ 16. Fragestruktur der Seinsfrage" inside the word "Er-fragten" (:2877); the running head is left out and the word joined. The passage sits at the start of § 16 (GA 20 194–95).

The structure is built from one verb. German *fragen* (to ask) takes three prefixes, and each prefix names a moment of the question. *Er-fragen* is to ask so as to obtain: the *Erfragte* is what the question is out to get, its answer, the sense of being. *Fragen nach* is to ask about: the *Gefragte* is what the question is about, the being of beings. *Be-fragen* is to interrogate, to put questions to someone: the *Befragte* is what is addressed, beings themselves. Two further compounds fill out the picture. Beings must be "questioned down toward" their being (*abgefragt*), and later in the section they are "asked at" (*angefragt*) with regard to it. The English triad hides that the whole structure is the morphology of a single verb. Heidegger reads the anatomy of questioning off the grammar of German, and the reading works because the prefixes really do distinguish the goal, the topic and the addressee of a question.

The three moments are joined by a repeated verb of containment, "lies in" (*liegt*). "In the asked-for lies the asked-about"; "in the asked-about lies the interrogated"; "in the sense of being of a being lies the being itself". The nesting runs from the most abstract moment to the most concrete. To ask about the sense of being is already to ask about the being of beings, and that is already to address beings. The direction of the nesting is the argument. The question of being, however formal its goal, has an addressee, and the addressee is a being that must be "accessible in itself in its being". A question about sense becomes, by its structure, a question to a being.

The passage sets up the turn that makes § 16 the hinge of the course. Before it, § 15 has shown that the question arises from a "vague pre-understanding" of being, which "is *still an understanding*" (§15.1, GA 20 193). After it, Heidegger works through the moments in reverse order and comes to the decisive question. What is to be determined is "the access to, the experience of, the looking at, the addressing as, the grasping and understanding as". "Are not such ways of access and experience themselves already beings?" The question of being "will become the more transparent, the more properly we have made visible this being, namely the being of the questioning of the questioner itself" (§16.5, GA 20 197). The interrogated, on reflection, includes the interrogator. The triad was introduced to describe the question; its last moment discloses the being that asks it, which §17 names Dasein (row 21). The circle this produces is the subject of row 20.

The triad recurs at the end of the analytic in a new vocabulary. In §32 Heidegger states the method of "interpretation of beings toward their being". It places a being into its "fore-having" (*Vorhabe*). It interrogates the being toward its being, and the "looking-toward" is placed into the "fore-sight" (*Vorsicht*). The concept of what is sought must be worked out (§32.2). The interrogated being, the respect of asking and the sought concept of § 16 have become the fore-structure of interpretation, which the new §31d derives from care (§31.6). The formal anatomy of a question has been grounded in the being of the questioner.

*Sein und Zeit* §2 keeps the triad almost word for word. "Questioning, as questioning *about* …, has its *asked-about*." Every questioning also has its interrogated and its asked-for. In the question of being "being makes up the asked-about"; "the interrogated of the question of being" is "beings themselves", which are "as it were questioned down toward their being" (*auf sein Sein hin abgefragt*, SZ 5–7). The compound *abgefragt* is the same. What changes is the frame. In 1927 the analysis begins from the claim that "every questioning is a seeking" and receives "its prior direction from what is sought" (SZ 5). The structure of the question is grounded in the structure of seeking before it is applied.

## §16, GA 20 197–98: the circle of being

Reads [§16.6](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), sixth paragraph under § 16.

To answer the question of being one must first work out a being in its being, the questioning being. That looks like a circle: being must be presupposed in order to be determined. Heidegger's answer is that this is not a circle in a proof but a circularity of a being, which he calls a "circle of being" (*Seinszirkel*). The objection of circularity, he claims, itself springs from that circularity. The understanding the passage carries is the circle as a structure of the questioner, not a fault of the argument, and the course returns to it until it becomes the circle of understanding.

> Is that not an obvious circle? We let this objection stand, to dispose of it later. For now let only this be stressed: the objection that there is a circle has sense only where it is a matter of deriving and grounding propositions from others, when propositions C and D, which are to be derived from propositions A and B, are themselves already set to work for the grounding of A and B, for the grounding of that from which they themselves are to be grounded. Here, however, it is not a matter of a deduction of propositions and sequences of propositions from one another, but of the working-out of the access to the matters from which propositions are first to be drawn. … If the question is to be posed, then here least of all is there any bargaining; then it must be worked out as a transparent question, then the being of the character of access, experience and so on must itself be illuminated in its being, at the risk that it is a circle: a circle of investigating, of the course and of being, a "circle of being", a circularity of a being that is to be understood, and from which circularity the favourite and traditional objection of a circle in grounding first springs. A circle of grounding, in any case, there is not.

German:

> Ist das nicht ein offenbarer Zirkel? Wir lassen diesen Einwand stehen, um ihn später zu erledigen. Jetzt sei nur das betont: Der Einwand, es liege ein Zirkel vor, hat nur Sinn, wo es sich um Ableitung und Begründung von Sätzen aus anderen handelt, wenn Sätze C und D, die aus Sätzen A und B abgeleitet werden sollen, selbst schon für die Begründung von A und B, für die Begründung dessen, woraus sie selbst begründet werden sollen, in Funktion gesetzt werden. Hier geht es aber nicht um eine Deduktion von Sätzen und Satzfolgen auseinander, sondern um die Ausarbeitung des Zuganges zu den Sachen, aus denen erst Sätze geschöpft werden sollen. … Soll aber die Frage gestellt werden, dann gibt es hier am allerwenigsten ein Paktieren, dann muß sie als durchsichtige Frage selbst ausgearbeitet werden, dann ist das Seiende vom Charakter des Zuganges, der Erfahrung usf. selbst in seinem Sein aufzuhellen auf die Gefahr hin, daß es ein Zirkel ist — ein Zirkel des Untersuchens, des Ganges und des Seins, ein ›Seinszirkel‹ — eine Zirkelhaftigkeit eines Seienden, die es zu verstehen gilt, und aus welcher Zirkelhaftigkeit erst der beliebte und traditionelle Einwand des Zirkels in der Begründung entspringt. Ein Zirkel der Begründung liegt jedenfalls nicht vor.

*Note on the text.* The omitted sentences call formal objections "sterile" at the start of fundamental investigations and set out the alternative: either the question of being is posed, or it is left unposed and the answer in the dark (GA 20 198).

The understanding is reached through a series of genitives that move the circle from logic to being. The objection speaks of "a circle in grounding" (*Zirkel in der Begründung*), propositions C and D presupposed by the A and B they are to be derived from. Heidegger grants that such a circle exists only "where it is a matter of deriving and grounding propositions". He then names the circle that is really here, in three genitives: "a circle of investigating, of the course and of being". The list climbs from the activity (investigating) through its path (the course) to what the activity is (being). The coinage that follows sums up the climb: "a 'circle of being'" (*ein ›Seinszirkel‹*). The distancing marks show that the word is new and meant strictly. It is then glossed as "a circularity of a being" (*Zirkelhaftigkeit eines Seienden*). The circle is not in the propositions but in the being that asks.

The passage makes two claims of different strength. The first is negative and holds: there is no circle in the proof, because nothing is being deduced. What is being worked out is "the access to the matters from which propositions are first to be drawn" (*erst … geschöpft*), and access is not a premise. The second is a claim of origin, and it is not argued here: the "favourite and traditional" objection of a circle "springs from" (*entspringt*) the circularity of a being. The objection is said to be a misreading of a real structure, one that sees a vicious circle where there is a circularity proper to the questioner. The claim is promissory. Heidegger "lets the objection stand, to dispose of it later" (*um ihn später zu erledigen*), and the passage says so in its second sentence. The rhetoric around the claim fills the gap. Formal objections are "sterile". There is "least of all any bargaining" (*Paktieren*) once the question is to be posed. The alternative is to leave the answer "in the dark". The insistence covers what is not yet shown, namely why a being should be circular at all.

The passage closes the formal analysis of § 16 and opens § 17. The preceding paragraph found that the "ways of access and experience" through which the question is to be worked out "are themselves already beings" (§16.5). The present passage concedes the circle that follows and refuses its logical reading. §17 then names the being: the questioning, which "is co-affected by what is asked for" (row 21). The circularity of a being is the circularity of a being whose being is at stake in its own questioning. §17.4, in the commentary's gloss, calls the circle "not vicious but constitutive". Dasein is the being that already understands being and can therefore be interrogated about it.

The promise to "dispose of it later" is kept in §28b, and the circle there has a new name. Understanding always extends to world, Dasein and co-Dasein together. "In this is grounded what, considered externally, is called the so-called *circle in understanding*" (*Zirkel im Verstehen*). "One finds in understanding merely a circle if one does not see" that understanding of world and of Dasein belong to every understanding (GA 20 357, :4465; §28.3). The hedges, "considered externally" and "merely", carry the 1925 argument. The objection sees from outside what is, from inside, the wholeness of understanding. The historical sciences' complaint about "the historian's personal standpoint" is answered the same way. The ideal is not freedom from the circle but the right entry into it (§28.3).

*Sein und Zeit* takes the circle twice, in the same places. In §2 the objection of "a 'circle in the proof'" is dismissed in terms close to GA 20: "there is no circle at all" in the formal sense (SZ 7–8). In §32 it receives the formula for which it is best known. "What is decisive is not to get out of the circle, but to come into it in the right way." This circle "is not an orbit in which any random kind of knowledge moves", but "the expression of the existential *fore-structure* of Dasein itself" (SZ 153). The "circularity of a being, which is to be understood" of 1925 has become the fore-structure of understanding. The coinage *Seinszirkel*, however, did not survive.

## §17, GA 20 199: the questioning being, Dasein

Reads [§17.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 17.

Dasein enters the course as the being that the questioning itself is. Its first characteristic is that the questioning is "co-affected" by what it asks about. The question of being turns back on the one who asks it, because the one who asks is a being and so falls under the question. The understanding the passage carries is this co-affectedness (*Betroffenheit*). It explains why the question of being must become a phenomenology of Dasein, and it is the first form of the later formula that Dasein is a being for which its being is at issue.

> This gives a quite distinctive questioning, insofar as in the content of the question, in the asked-for, the asked-for is itself what the questioning itself is. The asked-for in it, the sense of being, is given in all indeterminacy, as indeterminate as anything sought can be. If the questioning is genuine, it must as far as possible be adequate to its asked-for, that is, the questioning must rightly understand what it asks about, namely being. The asked-for here strikes back, as this asked-for, upon the questioning itself, insofar as this is a being. … As what is this being given beforehand, of which we say: questioning, looking at, addressing as, relating? It is *the being* that we ourselves are; this being that I in each case myself am we call *Dasein*. To work out the posing of the question of the sense of being means: to lay free the questioning as a being, that is, Dasein itself; for only so does what is sought become something sought in its ownmost sense. The questioning is here itself co-affected by its asked-for, because the questioning stands under the question about beings, and questioning is itself a being. This co-affectedness of the questioning being by the asked-for belongs to the ownmost sense of the question of being itself.

German:

> Damit haben wir ein ganz ausgezeichnetes Fragen, sofern im Fragegehalt, im Erfragten, selbst das Erfragte ist, was das Fragen selbst ist. Das Erfragte in ihm, der Sinn von Sein, ist dabei in aller Unbestimmtheit gegeben, so unbestimmt wie Gesuchtes nur sein kann. Wenn das Fragen echtes ist, dann hat es möglichst seinem Erfragten angemessen zu sein, d. h. das Fragen muß recht verstehen, was es fragt, nach dem Sein nämlich. Das Erfragte schlägt hier als dieses Erfragte auf das Fragen selbst zurück, sofern dieses ein Seiendes ist. … Als was ist dieses Seiende, von dem wir sagen: fragen, hinsehen auf, ansprechen als, beziehen — vorgegeben? Es ist *das Seiende*, das wir selbst sind; dieses Seiende, das ich je selbst bin, nennen wir das *Dasein*. Die Fragestellung nach dem Sinn des Seins auszuarbeiten, besagt: das Fragen als ein Seiendes, d. h. das Dasein selbst freilegen; denn nur so wird das Gesuchte ein seinem eigensten Sinne nach Gesuchtes. Das Fragen ist hier von seinem Erfragten her selbst mitbetroffen, weil das Fragen nach dem Seienden steht, und Fragen selbst ein Seiendes ist. Diese Betroffenheit des fragenden Seienden vom Erfragten her gehört zum eigensten Sinn der Seinsfrage selbst.

The passage works by giving an activity the status of a being. Its subject is not "the questioner" (*der Fragende*) but "the questioning" (*das Fragen*), the nominalized infinitive with the article, and it is that which "is itself a being". The list that prepares the name is a list of activities in the same form: "questioning, looking at, addressing as, relating" (*fragen, hinsehen auf, ansprechen als, beziehen*). Each is a way of comporting oneself, each ends on a preposition or particle that points away (*auf*, *als*), and each is an instance of the intentionality § 5 described. The question "as what is this being given beforehand?" is answered with a name, not a definition: "this being that I in each case myself am we call *Dasein*". The phrase "in each case myself" (*je selbst*) attaches the being to a first person before anything else is said of it. Its name says, as §18 will explain, how it is, not what it is (row 22).

The relation between the question and its questioner is carried by two verbs of impact. The asked-for "strikes back" (*schlägt … zurück*) upon the questioning, and the questioning is "co-affected" (*mitbetroffen*) by it. The prefix *mit-* makes the questioner a co-target of the question, one of the beings the question of being asks about. Two consequences are stated in the passage's own terms. The questioning, if it is genuine, must "rightly understand what it asks about", so a question about being requires a questioner that understands being. And laying the questioning free as a being is the only way "what is sought becomes something sought in its ownmost sense". The co-affectedness is not an obstacle to the inquiry but the condition of its being genuine. The next sentence adds the twist that the analytic will spend itself on. This being is given with the question, yet "in the course of the questioning first and above all overlooked" (*zuerst und vor allem übersehen*, GA 20 199). Being nearest makes it easiest to miss.

The passage completes the turn of § 16. The formal triad ended in a being that must be interrogated, and the ways of interrogating proved to be beings themselves (row 19). The circle this produced was a circularity of a being (row 20). Here the being is named. Heidegger anchors the step at once in the history of the question. In Parmenides the questioning and the questioned are already "identified": τὸ γὰρ αὐτὸ νοεῖν ἐστίν τε καὶ εἶναι, "being is the same as the perceiving of beings in their being". In Plato and Aristotle the *logos* and the seeing of the *eidos* are treated "accessorily", alongside the question of *ousia* (GA 20 200, §17.3). The ancients knew the co-affectedness without making it thematic. And §17.4 refuses the anthropological reading in advance: the analysis is driven "solely" by the sense of the question, not by an interest in the psychology of the human being.

The co-affectedness becomes, by the end of the analytic, the formal definition of care. Anxiety discloses that "Dasein is the being to which, in its being, … its own being is at issue" (§30.4). Care is "the term for the being of Dasein simply", with the formal structure "a being to which, in its being-in-the-world, this being itself is at issue" (§31.1). The "it is at issue" (*es geht um*) is the matured form of being "co-affected" by the question of being. In § 17 the questioner was implicated in the question as one of the beings asked about. In §31 Dasein is the being whose own being is always already in question for it, whether or not it asks the philosophical question.

*Sein und Zeit* §2 introduces Dasein in nearly the same words and with one telling change. The questioning of this question "is, as a *mode of being* of a being, itself essentially determined by what is asked about in it, by being". The phrase is the 1925 co-affectedness in a more formal voice. Then the name: "this being which we ourselves in each case are and which *among other things* has the possibility of being of questioning, we term *Dasein*" (SZ 7). In 1925 Dasein was "the questioning". In 1927 questioning is one of its possibilities "among other things" (*unter anderem*). The distance between the two definitions is the analytic of GA 20 itself. Having begun with the questioner, the course found a being whose questioning is one way among many in which its being is at issue.

## §18, GA 20 205–206: the name "Dasein" says no what

Reads [§18.2](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 18.

"Dasein" is a name that says how a being is, not what it is. Most nouns name beings by their content, a chair as against a house, and leave their being unsaid because it is taken for granted. "Dasein" does the reverse. It is "a quite specific expression of being", and what it names is a being whose way of being is "to be" it, in each case as mine. The understanding the passage carries is this kind of name and the kind of being it fits. Every later structure of the analytic is to be read as a way of this "to-be", and the analysis is barred from asking what the being consists of.

> The fundamental determination of the being under consideration has already been indicated: Dasein is the being that *I in each case myself am*, in whose being I as a being am "involved"; a being that *is*, in each case in my way, *to be it*. This determination shows the distinctive relation of being that we "have" to this being: *to be it itself*, not, as with beings of the character of nature, merely to grasp it or have it available in some sense. The phenomenal motive for designating this being that we ourselves are is therefore *Dasein*. …
>
> This naming "Dasein" for the distinctive being named means *no what*; it does not distinguish this being by its what, as a chair is distinguished from a house, but this designation expresses in its way *the way to be*. It is a quite specific expression of being, chosen here for a being, while we at first always name beings from their what-content and leave their specific being undetermined because we take it to be self-evident.
>
> This relation of being to the being that I myself am characterizes this *"to-be"* as the *"in each case mine"*. The kind of being, *to be it*, is essentially *in each case mine to be it*, whether I know it explicitly or not, whether I have lost myself in my being (cf. the *anyone*) or not.

German:

> Die Fundamentalbestimmung des in Betrachtung stehenden Seienden wurde schon angedeutet: Dasein ist das Seiende, das *ich je selbst bin*, an dessen Sein ich als Seiendes ›beteiligt‹ bin; ein Seiendes, das *ist*, je in meiner Weise es *zu sein*. Diese Bestimmung zeigt das ausgezeichnete Seinsverhältnis an, das wir zu diesem Seienden ›haben‹: *es selbst zu sein*, nicht etwa wie Seiendes vom Charakter der Natur, es lediglich zu erfassen, in irgendeinem Sinne verfügbar zu haben. Das phänomenale Motiv für die Bezeichnung dieses Seienden, das wir selbst sind, ist deswegen *Dasein*. …
>
> Diese Benennung ›Dasein‹ für das genannte ausgezeichnete Seiende bedeutet *kein Was*; es unterscheidet dieses Seiende nicht nach seinem Was, wie einen Stuhl gegenüber einem Haus, sondern diese Bezeichnung drückt in ihrer Weise *die Weise zu sein* aus. Sie ist ein ganz spezifischer Seinsausdruck, der hier für ein Seiendes gewählt ist, während wir Seiendes zunächst immer aus seinem Was-Gehalt benennen und sein spezifisches Sein unbestimmt lassen, weil wir es für selbstverständlich halten.
>
> Dieses Seinsverhältnis zu dem Seienden, das ich selbst bin, charakterisiert dieses *›Zu-sein‹* als das *›je meine‹*. Die Seinsart — *es zu sein* — ist wesenhaft *je meine es zu sein*, ob ich ausdrücklich darum weiß oder nicht, ob ich mich in meinem Sein verloren habe (vgl. das *Man*) oder nicht.

The passage builds the understanding from the infinitive. Dasein is a being that "*is*, in each case in my way, *to be it*" (*je in meiner Weise es zu sein*). The relation we "have" to it is "*to be it itself*" (*es selbst zu sein*), not to grasp it or have it available. The italicized infinitive phrases replace a predicate with a task: Dasein is not described as being something but as having to be. The distancing marks then take the ordinary verbs of possession and participation out of their usual sense. I am "involved" (›beteiligt‹) in its being, and we "have" (›haben‹) a relation to it. These are words for a relation between two things, and the marks show that no such relation is meant. The name follows "therefore" (*deswegen*). The "phenomenal motive" for calling this being "Dasein" (being-there) is that it is the being whose being is to be.

The second paragraph makes the point about names explicit, and its example is chosen to contrast with it. Most designations distinguish beings by their "what", "as a chair is distinguished from a house". "Dasein" means "*no what*" (*kein Was*) and expresses "*the way to be*" (*die Weise zu sein*). The negation is italicized, and so is the alternative. The name is "a quite specific expression of being" (*Seinsausdruck*), the only one in the course chosen for a being instead of for its being. Ordinary naming leaves being "undetermined because we take it to be self-evident". The name "Dasein" refuses that self-evidence for one being by making its being its name.

The third paragraph joins the "to-be" to the first person and gives the joint a technical form. The to-be is "in each case mine" (›je meine‹). The kind of being is "essentially *in each case mine to be it*", and the hyphenated string *Jeweilig-es-zu-sein* (in-each-case-to-be-it), introduced a sentence later, condenses the whole. The qualification that follows matters for the rest of the analytic. The "mine" holds "whether I know it explicitly or not, whether I have lost myself in my being (cf. the *anyone*) or not". Mineness is not self-awareness and not authenticity. It holds of the everyday self lost in *das Man* (the anyone) as much as of a self that has chosen itself. Heidegger adds at once that this character is "uncancellable" (*undurchstreichbar*) and that Dasein, as "being-possible", can modify itself "toward the anyone and back" (GA 20 206).

The passage turns the questioning being of §17 into the theme of an analytic. In §17 Dasein was "the questioning" (row 21). Here it is the being whose being is a "to-be" in each case mine, and the next paragraph draws the methodological consequence. Every phenomenon of Dasein is to be understood "as a mode of its to-be". Dasein must not be interrogated "by its look", by what it consists of, and the analysis sets itself "outside" the horizon prescribed by *homo animal rationale* (§18.3, GA 20 207, row 17). The name's refusal of a "what" is the positive side of that exclusion. §26 returns to the point from the other side. To speak of "the entity that has the being of Dasein" is, Heidegger says there, "at bottom mistaken", because it suggests a thing with a what that then also has a way of being. The proper question about Dasein is therefore "*who*" and not "*what*" (§26.1, GA 20 323).

Mineness carries the analytic to its end. In §33 it is the reason Dasein's wholeness cannot be experienced through the death of others: "*death is in each case mine*" (§33.3), and no one can take another's dying from them. In §34 the "to-be" of § 18 becomes "being-possible", and the utmost possibility of that being is death, which "I myself am" (§34.3, row 38). The formula of § 18, a being whose being is in each case mine to be, is the formula from which the course derives death as a phenomenon of Dasein.

*Sein und Zeit* §9 keeps both characters and states them as the two theses of the analytic. "The 'essence' of this being lies in its to-be (*Zu-sein*)", and its what-being "must be conceived from its being (*existentia*)". And "the being which this being is concerned about in its being is in each case mine" (SZ 42). The same paragraph names the second character *Jemeinigkeit*, "mineness", replacing GA 20's *Jeweiligkeit*, "in-each-case-ness" (SZ 42–43). The change of word is a change of emphasis. *Jeweiligkeit* stresses the particular occasion, "this one, each time"; *Jemeinigkeit* stresses the first person. The 1925 passage already contains both, "in each case" (*je*) and "mine" (*meine*), and SZ fuses them into a single noun.

## §19, GA 20 213: in-being as dwelling

Reads [§19.3](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 19.

"In" in "being-in-the-world" does not mean spatial containment, as water is in a glass. It means dwelling: being familiar with, tending, residing alongside. Heidegger supports the claim with an etymology: "in" from *innan*, "to dwell", and "I am" (*bin*) connected with "alongside" (*bei*). In-being is "being-alongside" the world as what is familiar. The understanding the passage carries is in-being as familiarity, and the course later shows it from its other side, when anxiety makes being-in-the-world a "not-at-home".

> "In-being" means such a spatial in-one-another as little as "in" originally means a spatial relation of the kind named at all. "In" derives from *innan*, and *innan* means to dwell, *habitare*, and "ann" means: I am accustomed, I am familiar with, I tend something — the Latin *colo* in the sense of *habito* and *diligo*. Dwelling also taken here as tending something in a familiar way, being-alongside. This same being that we characterize as in-being we also determine, as I said, as the being that I am, and the expression "am" is connected with "alongside"; "I am" means as much as: I dwell, I reside alongside the world as what is familiar. Being as in-being and "I am" means dwelling alongside …, and "in" means primarily nothing spatial at all, but says primarily: *being familiar with*.

German:

> Das ›In-Sein‹ meint ein solches räumliches Ineinander sowenig, als ›in‹ ursprünglich gar nicht eine räumliche Beziehung der genannten Art bedeutet. >In< stammt her von *innan*, und *innan* heißt wohnen, habitare, und ›ann‹ bedeutet: ich bin gewohnt, ich bin vertraut mit, ich pflege etwas — das lateinische *colo* im Sinne von *habito* und *diligo*. Wohnen auch hier genommen als vertrauterweise etwas pflegen, Sein-bei. Dieses selbe Seiende, das wir als In-Sein charakterisieren, bestimmen wir auch, wie ich schon sagte, als das Seiende, das ich bin, und der Ausdruck ›bin‹ hängt zusammen mit ›bei‹; ›ich bin‹ heißt soviel wie: ich wohne, halte mich auf bei der Welt als dem Vertrauten. Sein als In-Sein und ›Ich bin‹ heißt wohnen bei . . ., und ›in‹ bedeutet primär gar nichts Räumliches, sondern besagt primär: *vertraut sein mit*.

*Note on the text.* Heidegger's source for the etymology is given in a footnote: J. Grimm, *Kleinere Schriften*, vol. VII, p. 247 (:3177). Grimm is not in the corpus, so the etymology cannot be checked here. The reading treats it as Heidegger uses it, as support for a claim about the phenomenon.

The passage stacks three languages to fix one sense. Old High German *innan* means "to dwell" (*wohnen*). Its gloss passes into Latin, *habitare*, and then into a Latin verb with a range of senses, *colo*, "in the sense of *habito* and *diligo*": to inhabit and to cherish. The German glosses on *ann* fill in the second of these: "I am accustomed, I am familiar with, I tend something" (*ich bin gewohnt, ich bin vertraut mit, ich pflege etwas*). *Gewohnt* (accustomed) shares its stem with *wohnen* (to dwell), so the German itself joins dwelling and habit. The definition that follows gathers the senses into a hyphenated term: dwelling is "tending something in a familiar way, being-alongside" (*Sein-bei*). The second etymology attaches the same sense to the first person. "Am" (*bin*) "is connected with" "alongside" (*bei*), so "I am" means "I dwell, I reside alongside the world as what is familiar". The paragraph ends by stating the primary meaning of "in" in italics: "*being familiar with*" (*vertraut sein mit*).

The argument is weaker than its etymologies, and Heidegger's own sentences say so. The etymology is offered as what "in" means "originally" (*ursprünglich*). The claim that matters is about the phenomenon: in-being is "primarily" (*primär*) not spatial. An etymology cannot establish that, and the next paragraph concedes it. "Stating the genuine meaning of in-being does not yet guarantee seeing the phenomenon it expresses"; it is "more than a word-explanation" only in that it fixes "the direction of sight, above all prohibitively", indicating where not to look (GA 20 213–14). The etymology is a guide away from the picture of a body in a container, and the evidence for the phenomenon comes from the analyses that follow. The list of everyday modes of in-being that ends the section, handling, producing, tending, using, keeping, losing, asking, considering, all have "the character of *concern*" (GA 20 213).

The passage grounds the course's account of the world. The paragraph before it has shown the spatial "in" at work: the water in the glass, the bench in the hall, the hall in Marburg, Marburg in Europe, all one relation of present-at-hand things (§19.2). The present passage sets dwelling against it. The next paragraph gives the consequence: in-being is "no property" Dasein could have or lack, but "*the* constitution of being" in which every way of being of Dasein is grounded (§19.4). The sense of "familiar" then becomes a structural term. In §23 the environing world is "a closed reference-totality" distinguished by "a specific *familiarity*" (*Vertrautheit*). Its things "do not step out" of the references, and this is "the self-evidence and inconspicuousness" of the world's reality (§23.2). Familiarity, which the etymology made the primary meaning of "in", is now the mode in which the world is present.

The understanding is confirmed by its negation. In anxiety, "in the midst of the most familiar environing world", "it becomes uncanny" (*unheimlich*). "One is no longer at home in the nearest familiar environing world", and "being-in-the-world becomes in anxiety a complete 'not-at-home' simply" (*ein völliges ›Nicht-zu-Hause‹ schlechthin*, GA 20 400–401, §30.2). Being-in as dwelling and the uncanny as not-at-home are two sides of one structure. Only a being whose "in" means being at home can find itself not at home in the world as such. *Sein und Zeit* makes the connection explicit. In § 40 it recalls that "in-being was determined as dwelling alongside …, being familiar with …", and it reads the uncanny as "*not-being-at-home*" (*Nicht-zuhause-sein*), the state into which anxiety brings Dasein back from its falling absorption in the world (SZ 189). That recollection points back to §12, which repeats the 1925 etymology almost word for word and cites the same page of Grimm. "'In' stems from *innan-*, to dwell, *habitare*, to reside"; "'an' means: I am accustomed, familiar with, I tend something"; and "'am' is connected with 'alongside'", so that "'I am' means in turn: I dwell, I reside alongside … the world, as what is familiar in such and such a way" (SZ 54). What changes is where the etymology leads. GA 20 ends the paragraph on the primary meaning of "in", *being familiar with*. *Sein und Zeit* ends it on a definition: "*in-being is accordingly the formal existential expression of the being of Dasein, which has the essential constitution of being-in-the-world*" (SZ 54). The etymology now supports a term of art, an existential.

## §22, GA 20 245: true being as certain being

Reads [§22.3](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 22.

Descartes determines the being of the world from one way of knowing it: mathematics. What can be known mathematically, extension, is what truly is, "because for Descartes *verum ens* equals *certum ens*", true being is certain being. The understanding the passage carries is this inversion of access and being. A mode of apprehension prescribes what the world is, and so the world as it is encountered, its worldhood, is "overleaped". The pattern is the one the analytic of world is written against.

> In what way is the being of the world determined here? From a quite determinate kind of knowing of objects, the mathematical. The being of the world is nothing other than the *objectivity* of calculating, measuring apprehension of nature. Physics is now, as against all ancient and medieval knowledge of nature, *mathematical physics*. Only what is mathematically determinable in the world is knowable in it in the proper sense, and only what is mathematically known in this way is *true* being. This known true being, because for *Descartes verum ens* is equal to *certum ens*, is the proper being of the world. From a determinate and, indeed, possible kind of knowing of the world as nature, the proper being of the world is determined a priori.

German:

> Auf welchem Wege ist hier das Sein der Welt bestimmt? — aus einer ganz bestimmten Erkenntnisart von Gegenständen, der mathematischen. Das Sein der Welt ist nichts anderes als die *Objektivität* der rechnend messenden Naturerfassung. Physik ist jetzt gegenüber aller antiken und mittelalterlichen Erkenntnis der Natur *mathematische Physik*. Nur was mathematisch an der Welt bestimmbar ist, ist an ihr im eigentlichen Sinn erkennbar, und nur das so mathematisch Erkannte ist das *wahre* Sein. Dieses erkannte wahre Sein, weil für *Descartes verum ens* gleich *certum ens* ist, ist das eigentliche Sein der Welt. Aus einer bestimmten und zwar möglichen Erkenntnisart der Welt als Natur wird das eigentliche Sein der Welt a priori bestimmt.

The passage is built as a question and a chain of narrowings. "In what way is the being of the world determined here?" The answer is given in a dash before any argument: "from a quite determinate kind of knowing … the mathematical". Then each sentence narrows the field. The being of the world is "nothing other than" the objectivity of calculating apprehension. Physics is now "*mathematical* physics". "Only what is mathematically determinable" is knowable "in the proper sense", and "only what is so known" is "*true* being". The two "only"s (*Nur*, *nur*) carry the inference from a method to an ontology, and the italics mark where it lands: *true* being. The Latin then gives the hidden premise. "True being" is the world's proper being "because" (*weil*) for Descartes *verum ens* "is equal to" (*gleich*) *certum ens*. The equation of truth with certainty turns a criterion of knowledge into a measure of being.

The last sentence names the result in a phrase that recalls the critique of the a priori. The proper being of the world is determined "a priori" (*a priori bestimmt*) from a possible kind of knowing. This is the a priori in the sense that § 7 rejected, the a priori of a subject's knowing, imposed in advance on what is to be known (row 11). Heidegger concedes that the mathematical is "a determinate and, indeed, possible" (*bestimmte und zwar mögliche*) way of knowing nature. The objection is not that mathematics is false. It is that a possible way of access has been made to prescribe the being of what it accesses.

The paragraphs before the passage show the inversion at work on a single case, and their wording is as telling as their argument. Descartes argues that hardness, weight and colour can be "thought away" from a body without altering its nature. Hardness, he says, is given in the resistance of a body to the hand. If bodies receded at the speed of the approaching hand, nothing would ever be felt as hard, and the body would still be a body (GA 20 242–43). Heidegger's comment names the operation with a verb the file prints in a garbled form ("umininterpretiert", :3460): touch, "a quite primary" way of access to the world, is reinterpreted "from the outset" as "a process of nature", the motion of a thing called a hand toward another thing that runs away from it. Descartes "suppresses" (*unterschlägt*) the phenomenal content of touching. An experience of something becomes a relation of motions. The passage then generalizes the case: what is true of hardness is true of the world.

This is the "overleaping" (*Überspringen*) of § 22's title, and the passage supplies its mechanism. The world is first experienced in concern and is "not thematically experienced in its worldhood". It becomes explicit only when it is grasped theoretically, so the theoretical grasp "intrudes" as if it were the primary access (§22.4). The analytic of §23 starts from the other end: from the work-world of concern, in which the hammer "goes up in" its reference and is not looked at (§23.6). And §24 turns Descartes' own example against his successors. Dilthey and Scheler take reality to be given in resistance, the correlate of drive and will, and Heidegger answers that resistance "presupposes world" (§24.6). Descartes treated hardness as resistance in order to discard it, Dilthey and Scheler in order to found reality on it, and both read touch as an encounter between two present-at-hand things.

*Sein und Zeit* §21 repeats the hardness example nearly sentence for sentence. Hardness "is experienced in touching"; the parts of the hard thing "resist" the movement of the hand; if bodies receded at the speed of the hand, "it would never come to touching, hardness would not be experienced and so would never *be*" (SZ 91). It draws the same conclusion about access. "The only and genuine access to this being is knowing, the *intellectio*, in the sense of mathematical-physical knowledge." What satisfies the being accessible to it "*is* in the proper sense" (SZ 95). The 1927 text adds the name for the being so determined. It is constant remaining (*ständiger Verbleib*, SZ 92), what "always is what it is". The formula *verum ens = certum ens* is gone, but its consequence is stated as an ontology: being as permanent presence-at-hand.

## §23, GA 20 263–65: readiness-to-hand as founded presence

Reads [§23.8](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), eighth paragraph under § 23.

Readiness-to-hand (*Zuhandenheit*), in this passage, is not the original way the world is present. It is a "founded presence", grounded in the presence of what concern has put into care. Bodily presence (*Leibhaftigkeit*), which Husserl took as the mark of reality, is founded "all the more". The understanding the passage carries is an order of founding among kinds of presence. What concern is out for comes first. The ready-to-hand is present only in relation to it, and the present-at-hand and the merely perceived lie further off. The order is temporal in a sense the passage names but cannot yet explain.

> If we keep our distance from the inverted direction of interpretation, that is, do not explain the encountering of the world from apprehending, but understand apprehending as grounded in that encountering, then it becomes clear that the presence of concernedness first brings to appearance what, oriented by the theory of apprehension, we call the first given. The genuine first given is here again not the perceived, but what is present in concernful dealings, what is ready-to-hand within grasp and reach. Such presence of the environmental, which we call *readiness-to-hand*, is a *founded presence*. It is not something original, but is grounded in the presence of what is put into care. If even this nearest ready-to-hand in concern is a founded presence, then this holds all the more of the character of *reality* that we came to know earlier, and that Husserl claims for the proper being-present of the world: so-called *bodily presence*.

German:

> Wenn wir von der verkehrten Interpretationsrichtung Abstand nehmen, d. h. das Begegnen der Welt nicht aus dem Erfassen erklären, vielmehr dieses als in jenem gründend verstehen, dann wird deutlich, daß die *Besorgtheiträsenz* allererst das zum Vorschein bringt, was wir erfassungstheoretisch orientiert als das zunächst Gegebene bezeichnen. Das echte zunächst Gegebene ist dabei auch wiederum nicht das Wahrgenommene, sondern das im besorgenden Umgang Anwesende, das in Greif- und Reichweite *Zuhandene*. Solche Anwesenheit von Umweltlichem, die wir *Zuhandenheit* nennen, ist eine *fundierte Präsenz*. Sie ist nicht etwas Ursprüngliches, sondern gründet in der Präsenz dessen, was in die Sorge gestellt ist. Wenn schon dieses Nächst-Zuhandene im Besorgen eine fundierte Präsenz ist, dann gilt das noch viel mehr von dem Charakter der *Realität*, den wir früher schon kennengelernt haben, und den *Husserl* für das eigentliche Anwesendsein der Welt in Anspruch nimmt: die sogenannte *Leibhaftigkeit*.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "*Besorgtheiträsenz*", a dropped "P" for *Besorgtheitspräsenz* (presence of concernedness). Nearby it has "apprallsentiert" for *appräsentiert* (:3650). Both are quoted as they stand. The reading depends on the compound's second half, which is not in doubt. The passage straddles pp. 263–65 in a stretch where several pages carry OCR errors.

The passage builds its order from two nouns for presence. *Anwesenheit* is the German word: "presence of the environmental" (*Anwesenheit von Umweltlichem*), "being-present" (*Anwesendsein*). *Präsenz* is the Latin loan, and Heidegger uses it for the terms of the order. Readiness-to-hand is "a *founded presence*" (*fundierte Präsenz*). It is grounded "in the presence (*Präsenz*) of what is put into care". The first link is the "presence of concernedness" (*Besorgtheitspräsenz*). The Latin word makes presence a technical term that can be ranked: one presence is founded in another. The prepositions do the ranking. Readiness-to-hand "is not something original, but is grounded in" (*gründet in*) concernedness. Bodily presence is founded "all the more" (*noch viel mehr*). The order runs from what concern is out for, through what lies in reach, to what is merely perceived.

The first sentence states the methodological reversal that makes the order visible. The "inverted direction of interpretation" (*verkehrte Interpretationsrichtung*) explains the world's encounter from apprehending. Heidegger's direction understands apprehending as "grounded in" the encounter. Measured that way, what the theory of apprehension calls "the first given" (*das zunächst Gegebene*) is first brought to appearance by the presence of concernedness. The genuine first given is "not the perceived, but … what is ready-to-hand within grasp and reach" (*in Greif- und Reichweite*). The hyphenated pair measures nearness by the reach of a hand, not by distance from an eye. The sentence then goes one step further than the commentary's paraphrase does. The ready-to-hand is itself "not something original". The first in the order is not a kind of thing at all but the presence of what concern "has put into care".

The passage is the turning point of § 23. Before it, the analysis of the work-world has shown how the work to be produced refers to users, to a public world and to nature as the always already present (§23.7). The passage gathers these into two "reality-characters": readiness-to-hand, "the nearest available", and presence-at-hand, "the always-already-there" (GA 20 263). It then founds both in concern. The next page draws the consequence for perception. Bodily presence "is no primary character at all" but "an encounter-character of world-things insofar as the world encounters only a pure apprehending", a "*merely-still-looking*" in which the primary world is "in a certain way shut out" (GA 20 265). §24 states the corollary as one of its theses: reality is "not primarily to be understood from the bodily presence of the perceived" (§24.5). The perception-centred phenomenology of § 5, in which the chair was given in bodily presence (row 7), is here placed at the far end of an order of founding.

The passage promises its own completion in a sentence just before it. What "putting into care" and "standing in reference" mean "can be clarified only later, and only from the phenomenon of *time*" (GA 20 264). The word *Präsenz* already holds the promise, since presence is also the present. Two years later the promise is kept. In *The Basic Problems of Phenomenology* readiness-to-hand has "a presential sense" (*präsentischen Sinn*), and Heidegger introduces *Praesenz* as "the horizonal schema of the ecstasy of the present" (GA 24 431–33). The presence that founds readiness-to-hand in 1925 has become a temporal horizon in 1927.

*Sein und Zeit*, between them, takes a different line on the rank of readiness-to-hand, and the difference sharpens what GA 20 claims. "Readiness-to-hand is the ontological-categorial determination of beings as they are 'in themselves'" (SZ 71). In SZ the ready-to-hand is primary against the present-at-hand, and the being of the thing as it is "in itself" is identified with its readiness. GA 20 makes readiness-to-hand founded and places the original presence in concern, and so in care. Both texts reject the priority of the present-at-hand. GA 20 adds that the ready-to-hand is itself derivative of the care that lets it be encountered.

## §23, GA 20 274–75: significance

Reads [§23.9](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), ninth paragraph under § 23.

Significance (*Bedeutsamkeit*) is the name for the structure in which the world is encountered: the references in which things are what they are, taken as a signifying (*bedeuten*). Heidegger defines the term twice over. Against value, significance says nothing about the rank or worth of things added to natural things. Toward language, it is connected with the meaning of words. The understanding the passage carries is significance as a meaning-structure of the world prior to words. Heidegger admits that the term is "not the best", and that the connection of the world with discourse is "still quite dark". The course clears up that darkness three sections later.

> The encounter-structure thus determined in references as *signifying* we call "significance". … Significance, as we use the title, says negatively first of all nothing about meaning in the *sense* of value and rank. Meaning says then in another sense as much as *meaning of a word*, meaning as something that combinations of words can have. This sense of *meaning*, too, is in a certain way connected with what we call significance, and indeed much more properly than the first-named sense of meaning and significance in the sense of value. That such demarcations, which we carry out here quite formally on the bare words, become necessary already points to a certain embarrassment in the choice of the right expression for the complex phenomenon that we want to name with significance; and I confess openly, this expression is not the best, but I have found no other since, for years, above all none that gives expression to an essential connection of the phenomenon with what we designate as *meaning in the sense of word-meaning*, insofar as the phenomenon stands precisely in inner connection with word-meaning, discourse. This connection between *discourse* and *world* will perhaps still be quite dark now.

German:

> Die so bestimmte Begegnisstruktur in Verweisungen als *bedeuten* nennen wir die ›Bedeutsamkeit‹. … Bedeutsamkeit, so wie wir den Titel gebrauchen, besagt negativ zunächst nichts über Bedeutung im *Sinne* von Wert und Rang. Bedeutung besagt dann in einem anderen Sinn soviel wie *Bedeutung eines Wortes*, Bedeutung als etwas, was Wortverbindungen haben können. Auch dieser Sinn von *Bedeutung* hängt in gewisser Weise mit dem, was wir Bedeutsamkeit nennen, zusammen und zwar viel eigentlicher als der erstgenannte Sinn von Bedeutung und Bedeutsamkeit im Sinne von Wert. Schon daß solche Abgrenzungen, wie wir sie hier ganz formal an den bloßen Worten vollziehen, notwendig werden, deutet auf eine gewisse Verlegenheit in der Wahl des rechten Ausdrucks für das komplexe Phänomen, das wir eben mit Bedeutsamkeit benennen wollen; und ich gestehe offen, dieser Ausdruck ist nicht der beste, aber ich habe seither, seit Jahren, keinen anderen gefunden, vor allem keinen solchen, der einen wesentlichen Zusammenhang des Phänomens mit dem, was wir als *Bedeutung im Sinne der Wortbedeutung* bezeichnen, Ausdruck gibt, sofern gerade das Phänomen in innerer Zusammenhang mit Wortbedeutung, Rede steht. Dieser Zusammenhang zwischen *Rede* und *Welt* wird vielleicht jetzt noch ganz dunkel sein.

The term is built on a verb that the passage first takes from ordinary speech and then fixes. The reference that structures the world's encounter "we now designate more exactly as 'signifying'" (*bedeuten*, in distancing marks), and the structure of references as signifying "we call 'significance'" (GA 20 274). The suffix *-sam* makes an adjective of tendency or capacity (as in *wirksam*, effective), and *-keit* makes it a noun. *Bedeutsamkeit* is the condition of being such as to signify. The passage then fixes the sense of the noun against the two senses that *Bedeutung* (meaning) has in German. The emphasis falls on the first contrast: significance says nothing about meaning "in the *sense* of value and rank". The italics on *Sinne* (sense) mark a sense excluded, not one adopted. The second contrast is a qualified agreement. Word-meaning is connected with significance "in a certain way" (*in gewisser Weise*) and "much more properly" (*viel eigentlicher*) than value is.

The exclusion of value has an argument behind it, given just before the passage. To call things meaningful in the sense of valuable presupposes that "things are first natural things and then have something like value", so that value is "ontologically taken in a specific back-reference to natural thinghood" (GA 20 275). Value is the product of a "specific ontology of nature". The verb Heidegger chose excludes that ontology. To say that the world "signifies" is to say that things are encountered in references from the start, not that a neutral thing acquires a predicate. The commentary's paragraph on the ready-to-hand makes the same point: it is "not the present-at-hand plus a value-colouring" (§23.8).

The confession is part of the understanding. "I confess openly, this expression is not the best, but I have found no other since, for years" (*seither, seit Jahren*). The "years" are literal. The 1923 course on facticity already analyses "significance" as "the as-what and how of being-encountered" (GA 63 §§21–23, van Buren's translation). What the confession says the term lacks is precisely the connection it is chosen to suggest, with "word-meaning, discourse". Heidegger keeps the term because it points toward a connection he cannot yet show, and he marks the connection as unproven: it "will perhaps still be quite dark now" (*vielleicht jetzt noch ganz dunkel*). The admission keeps the term open to the later analysis.

The passage is the conclusion of the analysis of worldhood, and its place shows what it settles. §23 began with the world's "encounter-characters", reference, reference-totality, familiarity (§23.2), and worked through the work-world and the two reality-characters, readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand (§23.8). Here all of these are gathered into one structure: "the references and reference-connections are at bottom *connections of meaning*" (GA 20 278). After the passage, Heidegger warns against the opposite error, treating a formal thread like "sign" or "symbol" as a key to all beings, as in Leibniz's *characteristica universalis*, Spengler and Cassirer (GA 20 278). Significance is not a universal semiotics. It is the structure of a world in which Dasein is.

The darkness is cleared in §28c, in the very phrase that names the connection. Interpretation addresses something as something, and in this the "meaning of significance is, as it were, made explicit, *it comes to word*" (*sie kommt zum Wort*). "As meaning so lifted out it can now itself receive its word", and only then is it possible "to distinguish the lifted meaning as word-meaning from the meant thing" (GA 20 360, :4490). Word-meaning is founded in significance, not significance in word-meaning. The connection the term was chosen for turns out to run from world to word. Language "is" only insofar as there is such addressing, and addressing only insofar as there is understanding and being-in-the-world (§28.4).

*Sein und Zeit* takes over the definition and the order of founding in nearly the same words. Its §18 hyphenates the verb, "*be-deuten*", and names "the relational whole of this signifying *significance*" (SZ 87). Significance is "the ontic condition of the possibility" of discovering a totality of involvements and grounds the possible being "of word and language" (SZ 87). In §34 the phrase of GA 20 §28c returns as the definition of discourse: "the totality-of-meanings of intelligibility *comes to word*. Words accrue to meanings" (*Den Bedeutungen wachsen Worte zu*, SZ 161). The term Heidegger called "not the best" in 1925 kept its place, and the connection he called "dark" became, two years later, the founding relation of discourse.

## §24, GA 20 296: "sum cogito"

Reads [§24.2](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 24.

Descartes' formula, read in its own order, puts thinking first and being second: *cogito sum*, I think, I am. Heidegger reverses it: *sum cogito*. Even so the *sum* must be understood not as the presence-at-hand of a thinking thing but as the basic constitution of my being, "I-am-in-a-world", which is why I can think the world at all. The understanding the passage carries is this reinterpreted *sum*. It dissolves the question whether the external world is real by showing that the questioner already is in a world, and it becomes the course's model for answering Descartes with his own word.

> I "know" about the being-real of the world solely insofar as I am. Not "*cogito sum*" is the formulation for a primary finding, but "*sum cogito*", and this "*sum*" taken not in the ontological indifference it has in Descartes and all his successors, as the presence-at-hand of a thinking thing, but "*sum*" as the assertion of the basic constitution of my being: I-am-in-a-world, and therefore I am able to think it at all. But one has taken this Cartesian sentence in the reverse way, and rightly, because Descartes himself wanted it so understood, namely so that one does not ask about the "*sum*" at all, but believes consciousness to be given as the inner, absolutely, qua absolute starting-point, from which then all the riddles of "inside" and "outside" arise.

German:

> Ich ›weiß‹ um das Realsein der Welt, einzig sofern ich bin. Nicht ›*cogito sum*‹ ist die Formulierung für einen primären Befund, sondern ›*sum cogito*‹, und dieses ›*sum*‹ dabei nicht in dieser ontologischen Indifferenz wie bei *Descartes* und allen Nachfolgern genommen — als Vorhandensein eines Denkdinges —, sondern ›*sum*‹ als Aussage der Grundverfassung meines Seins: ich-bin-in-einer-Welt und deshalb vermag ich sie überhaupt zu denken. Man hat aber diesen *cartesianischen* Satz in der umgekehrten Weise angenommen und mit Recht, weil *Descartes* ihn selbst so verstanden wissen wollte und zwar so, daß man gerade nach dem ›*sum*‹ überhaupt nicht fragt, sondern das Bewußtsein als das Innere absolut qua absolutem Ausgang für gegeben glaubt, wodurch dann alle Rätsel des ›Innen‹ und ›Außen‹ entstehen.

The understanding is built by word order and by a hyphenated gloss. Swapping the two words of the formula reverses what grounds what: in *sum cogito* being comes first, and thinking is one way of being. But swapping is not enough, and the next clause says why. The *sum* must not be taken "in the ontological indifference" in which Descartes left it, "as the presence-at-hand of a thinking thing". The positive sense is given in a string of hyphens: "*ich-bin-in-einer-Welt*" (I-am-in-a-world). The hyphens make one word of a sentence, as "being-in-the-world" did, and they attach the first person to the world in a single structure. "And therefore" (*und deshalb*) then states the dependence that the original formula hid. Thinking the world is possible "at all" only for a being that is already in one.

The first sentence prepares the reversal with a distancing mark. I "know" (›weiß‹) about the reality of the world "solely insofar as I am". The marks take "know" out of epistemology. What is meant is not a knowledge that could be proved or doubted but the understanding that belongs to being in a world. The paragraphs before the passage have argued that this reality "is exempt from all proof and belief", and that the "faith" in the external world that Dilthey sought to justify is a phenomenon Heidegger "has not been able to find" (GA 20 294–95). The world is "there" "*before* all belief". The reinterpreted *sum* names the being for whom this is so.

The last sentence grants the opposing reading a strange right. One has taken the sentence "in the reverse way, and rightly, because Descartes himself wanted it so understood". The concession is a charge. Descartes' own intention was "that one does not ask about the '*sum*' at all" and takes consciousness as an "absolute starting-point". From that intention "all the riddles of 'inside' and 'outside' arise". Heidegger does not claim to have found the true meaning of Descartes. He claims that Descartes' meaning is the source of the problem, and that the formula, read against its author, contains its solution. The passage practises the rule of reading stated in § 4, reading a text's achievement against its self-interpretation (row 5), on Descartes.

The passage gives § 24 its first and governing thesis, that the reality of the external world is "exempt from every proof and belief in it". It also states the limit of the thesis at once. It is not enough to call the question "obviously senseless"; one must "let its countersense run up against the phenomenon", seen positively. The self-evidence of the world's presence is "given with the being of Dasein" ontically, "but ontologically it is riddling" (GA 20 297). The remaining theses of § 24 work out that riddle without the inside/outside picture. Reality is not determined from being-object (§24.3), not through the "in-itself" (§24.4), not from bodily presence (§24.5), not from resistance (§24.6).

The *sum* returns at the end of the course, again set against the *cogito* and again given a new predicate. The certainty that I myself am dying "is the basic certainty of Dasein itself", while "the *cogito sum* is only the semblance of such"; the adequate formula "must read: *sum moribundus*", and "the *moribundus* first gives the *sum* its sense" (§34.7, row 39). In § 24 the *sum* meant "I-am-in-a-world". In § 34 it means "I am dying". In both, Descartes' word is kept and its sense is supplied by the analytic. *Sein und Zeit* states the general programme in §6. Descartes "leaves the *sum* entirely undiscussed, although it is posited just as originally as the cogito"; "the analytic poses the ontological question of the being of the *sum*" (SZ 45). In § 43 the question of proof gets its famous formulation. "The 'scandal of philosophy'" is "not that this proof is still lacking, but that such proofs are *expected and attempted again and again*" (SZ 205). GA 20 had said the same of Dilthey's attempt to justify belief in the external world.

## §25, GA 20 313–14: de-severance as an existential

Reads [§25.6](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), sixth paragraph under § 25.

De-severing (*Ent-fernen*) is Heidegger's coinage for the way Dasein is spatial. The ordinary German word for distance, *Entfernung*, is split so that its prefix reads as a removal: *ent-fernen*, to remove the farness, to bring near. Dasein "is" de-severing. Its spatiality is a constant bringing-near, and the geometrical interval (*Abstand*) is a deficient form of it. The understanding the passage carries is the difference between de-severance as a character of Dasein's being and distance as a character of things, which Heidegger names here for the first time in the course: "*de-severance as an existential of Dasein and distance as a category*".

> I use the expression "de-severance" here, as it were, in a transitive, active sense: *de-severing* then means *making the farness disappear*, nearing as bringing close, or bringing oneself to, bringing close in such a way that the bringing oneself to becomes available at any time and easily, on average. That is a violation of natural linguistic usage, but here demanded by the phenomenon itself.
>
> *Dasein itself is de-severing*: the constant overcoming of farness as the presentifying of the present-at-hand. With Dasein as *being-in-the-world*, de-severing itself is given. The world itself is discovered as remote, or near. De-severance as well as resistance, for example, are *hermeneutic concepts*, that is, they express something that is intelligible in its sense as a structure of Dasein, a structure of being in which I myself reside as one who is there, as a being of the character of Dasein. With de-severance, accordingly, a way of being is expressed that I myself can be and constantly am. …
>
> … *Interval is a deficient de-severance*. To this peculiar transformation of the environmental de-severance — *de-severance as an existential of Dasein and distance as a category* — it comes precisely through the process, often named already, of *de-worlding*, which we cannot follow more exactly here in its structure, if only because for that we need the phenomenon of *time*.

German:

> Ich gebrauche dabey den Ausdruck ›Entfernung‹ gewissermaßen in einem transitiven, aktivischen Sinne: das *Ent-fernen* heißt dann *Verschwindenmachen der Ferne*, Näherung als Beibringen, bzw. sich hinzubringen, so beibringen, daß das Sich-hinzubringen jederzeit und leicht, durchschnittlich verfügbar wird. Das ist eine Vergewaltigung des natürlichen Sprachgebrauchs, aber hier für das Phänomen selbst gefordert.
>
> Das *Dasein selbst ist ent-fernendes*: die ständige Überwindung der Ferne als Gegenwärtigung des Vorhandenen. Mit dem Dasein als *In-der-Welt-sein* ist das Ent-fernen selbst gegeben. Welt selbst ist als entfernte, bzw. nahe, entdeckt. Entfernung sowohl wie Widerstand z. B. sind *hermeneutische Begriffe*, d. h. sie drücken etwas aus, was seinem Sinne nach als Daseinsstruktur verständlich ist, eine Seinsstruktur, in der ich mich selbst als Daseiendes, als Seiendes vom Charakter des Daseins aufhalte. Mit Ent-fernung ist sonach eine Seinsweise ausgedrückt, die ich selbst sein kann und ständig bin. …
>
> … *Abstand ist eine defiziente Entfernung*. Zu dieser eigentümlichen Umbildung der umweltlichen Entfernung — *Ent-fernung als Existenzial des Daseins und Entfernung als Kategorie* — kommt es eben durch den schon öfters genannten Prozeß der *Entweltlichung*,

*Note on the text.* The file reads "dabey" for *dabei* (:4059), quoted as it stands. The page markers here are irregular: the paragraph under "b)" carries a running head for p. 315 that should be p. 313, and p. 315 is marked again two paragraphs later. The passage is cited as GA 20 313–14. The last quoted sentence continues on the next line of the file ("den wir hier … nicht genauer verfolgen können, weil wir dazu das Phänomen der *Zeit* brauchen", :4066–:4068); the English renders the whole sentence.

The coinage is made by a hyphen, and Heidegger explains it before he uses it. The ordinary noun *Entfernung* means "distance". Its verb *entfernen* means "to remove", and its prefix *ent-* commonly marks separation or removal, as in *entkleiden* (to undress). Taken "in a transitive, active sense", *ent-fernen* reads as "to remove the *Ferne*": "*making the farness disappear*" (*Verschwindenmachen der Ferne*). The hyphen separates prefix and stem so that the word says the opposite of what it ordinarily says. Distance becomes bringing-near. Heidegger names the cost in one sentence: "That is a violation (*Vergewaltigung*) of natural linguistic usage, but here demanded by the phenomenon itself." The justification is the phenomenon, not etymology. Everyday Dasein is constantly bringing things within reach, and ordinary German has no word for this that does not already mean an interval.

The second paragraph turns the coinage into a statement about being. "*Dasein itself is de-severing*", with the adjective in italics and the copula in the strong sense. With being-in-the-world, de-severing "is given", and the world is "discovered as remote, or near". Then Heidegger classifies the concept, and the classification is the passage's main contribution. De-severance, "as well as resistance", is a "*hermeneutic concept*" (*hermeneutischer Begriff*): it expresses "a structure of being in which I myself reside". The paragraph ends with the formula that holds for every term of the analytic: "a way of being … that I myself can be and constantly am". A hermeneutic concept is one whose sense is understood from within, by the being it describes.

The third paragraph names the contrast that the classification implies, and gives it its terms. Things de-worlded "down to two geometrical points" lose de-severance and keep only an interval, "a quantum, a how-much". "*Interval is a deficient de-severance*", and the italicized pair states the difference: "*de-severance as an existential of Dasein and distance as a category*" (*Ent-fernung als Existenzial des Daseins und Entfernung als Kategorie*). The hyphen does the distinguishing in the formula itself. The same word, with and without a hyphen, names the two sides. This is the first place in the course where the noun "existential" (*Existenzial*) is set against "category". The adjective had appeared once, in a compound, in §20 (:3199). The distinction that *Sein und Zeit* makes programmatic is won here on a single phenomenon, and the transformation that produces the category is named: "*de-worlding*" (*Entweltlichung*).

The context shows why space, of all phenomena, yields the distinction. § 25 began by refusing Descartes' priority of extension and by insisting against Kant that "Dasein itself is spatial" (§25.1). Without a distinction between Dasein's spatiality and the spatiality of things, that insistence would make Dasein a body among bodies. The coinage keeps the two apart while letting them share a word. The analysis that follows works it out. The nearest is "not what has the least interval from me" but what lies within reach and sight; a wearer's spectacles are further off than the table. And I cannot "cross" my de-severance, "because I am it", as I cannot jump over my shadow (§25.8). The passage also points past § 25. De-severing is "presentifying" (*Gegenwärtigung*), and the full account of de-worlding "needs the phenomenon of *time*". §25.5 had already called the craving for nearness that drives modern speed "the flight of time from itself" into the present.

*Sein und Zeit* §23 keeps the coinage, its gloss and its admission. "De-severing means making the farness vanish"; "in Dasein there lies an essential tendency toward nearness"; with the radio Dasein carries out "a de-severance of the 'world' whose sense for Dasein cannot yet be surveyed" (SZ 105). What it drops is the 1925 explanation of the tendency as time's flight from itself. The distinction the passage names becomes in §9 a methodological rule. The characters of Dasein's being "we call *existentials*", and "they are to be sharply separated from the determinations of being of beings not of the character of Dasein, which we call *categories*" (SZ §9). What § 25 of GA 20 found in one hyphen, SZ states as the division of the whole analytic.

## §26, GA 20 331–34: being-with, not "with-world"

Reads [§26.5](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), fifth paragraph under § 26.

Others are encountered from the world, in the things we deal with, but they are never part of the world. They never have the kind of being of world. Heidegger therefore retracts the terms he had used in earlier lectures, "with-world" (*Mitwelt*) for others and "self-world" (*Selbstwelt*) for oneself, as "fundamentally false". "With" belongs to the being of Dasein, not to the world: Dasein is being-with (*Mitsein*), others are co-Dasein (*Mitdasein*), and the world is never "co-there". The understanding the passage carries is where the "with" belongs, and the passage shows Heidegger correcting his own vocabulary in front of his audience.

> This must be held fast: the worldhood of the world appresents not only world-things, the environing world in the narrower sense, but also, though not as worldly being, *the co-Dasein of others and one's own self*. But this says that the worldly encounter of something does not by itself decide about the kind of being of what is encountered. This can be appresented as ready-to-hand and present-at-hand, co-Dasein or self-Dasein. … On the ground of this worldly encounter of others, one could mark them off, in distinction from world-things in their presence-at-hand and readiness-to-hand in the environing world, as "with-world", and grasp one's own Dasein, insofar as it is encountered environmentally, as the "self-world". In my earlier lectures I saw things this way and took the terms in this sense. But the matter is fundamentally false. The terminology shows that the phenomena are not adequately grasped in this way, that precisely the others, though they are encountered worldly, do not have the kind of being and never the kind of being of the world. Therefore the others may not be designated as "with-world" either. … I have therefore now from the start used the expression "being-with". The world itself, by contrast, is never co-there, never co-Dasein, but is that in which Dasein in each case is as concern.

German:

> Festzuhalten ist dieses: Die Weltlichkeit der Welt appräsentiert nicht nur Weltdinge – die Umwelt im engeren Sinne –, sondern auch, obzwar nicht als weltliches Sein, *das Mitdasein Anderer und das eigene Selbst*. Das besagt aber, weltliches Begegnen von etwas entscheidet für sich noch nicht über die Seinsart des Begegnenden. Dieses kann als Zuhanden- und Vorhandensein, Mitdasein oder Selbstdasein appräsentiert werden. … Aufgrund dieses weltlichen Begegnens der Anderen könnte man diese im Unterschied zu den Weltdingen in ihrem Vor- und Zuhandensein in der Umwelt als »Mitwelt« abgrenzen, und das eigene Dasein, sofern es umweltlich begegnet, als die »Selbstwelt« fassen. In meinen früheren Vorlesungen habe ich die Dinge so gesehen und die Termini in diesem Sinne gefaßt. Die Sache ist aber grundfalsch. Die Terminologie zeigt, daß die Phänomene in dieser Weise nicht hinreichend gefaßt sind, daß eben die Anderen, ob sie zwar weltlich begegnen, nicht die Seinsart und nie die Seinsart der Welt haben. Dehalb dürfen die Anderen auch nicht als »Mitwelt« bezeichnet werden. … Ich habe deshalb jetzt von vornherein den Ausdruck >Mitsein< gebraucht. Die Welt selbst dagegen ist nie mit da, nie Mitdasein, sondern sie ist das, worin jeweils Dasein als Besorgen ist.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "Dehalb" for *Deshalb*, quoted as it stands. The passage runs across pp. 333–34; the page break falls inside the sentence on "being-with" (:4273–:4275). The omitted sentence before it says that worldly encounterability, though constitutive of being-in-the-world, never makes Dasein something worldly.

The passage turns on a compound and on where its first element is attached. "With" (*mit*) can be joined to "world" (*Mitwelt*), to "Dasein" (*Mitdasein*), or to "being" (*Mitsein*). Each compound says something different about what is shared. *Mitwelt* makes others a part of the world, a region alongside the environing world (*Umwelt*) and the self-world. *Mitdasein* says the others are Dasein too, there with me. *Mitsein* says that being-with is a character of my being. The passage keeps the second and third and rejects the first. The reason is given in one clause about kinds of being: the others "do not have the kind of being and never the kind of being of the world" (*nicht die Seinsart und nie die Seinsart der Welt*). The repetition with "never" (*nie*) turns a factual negation into a principled one. The last sentence draws the consequence for the world. It is "never co-there, never co-Dasein" (*nie mit da, nie Mitdasein*), but "that in which" Dasein is.

The retraction is presented as a lesson about terminology. "In my earlier lectures I saw things this way and took the terms in this sense. But the matter is fundamentally false (*grundfalsch*). The terminology shows that the phenomena are not adequately grasped." The order of the sentences matters. Heidegger does not say the terms were badly chosen for a phenomenon he had seen correctly. He says the terms reveal that the phenomenon was not seen: a vocabulary is evidence of how far a description has got. The first sentence of the passage gives the positive basis. Worldly encounter "does not by itself decide about the kind of being of what is encountered". Others, my own self, the ready-to-hand and the present-at-hand are all encountered from the world, and the analysis must ask in each case what kind of being the encountered has. "With-world" decided that question by the place of encounter.

The passage belongs to § 26's account of the "who". Just before it, Heidegger has warned against deriving being-with from a concept of the human as a "social being". It shows itself in the phenomenal content of everydayness, where "remarkably 'one oneself' (*man selbst*) is there in what one everydayly concerns" (GA 20 331). Just after it, being-with is shown to be a mutual understanding that moves "in a changing familiarity and intelligibility" (GA 20 334). §26b then answers the "who" with *das Man*, the anyone. The correction of vocabulary is the step between the two: it places the others in Dasein's being, not in its world, and so makes it possible to say that everyday Dasein is lived by the others it is with.

The rejected terms are in the corpus. The 1923 course on facticity analyses the disclosedness of the everyday world under two headings, "availability in advance" and "the advance appearance of the with-world" (GA 63 §23, van Buren's translation). The world appears there with its others as "with-world", the usage GA 20 calls fundamentally false. *Sein und Zeit* then does something the passage seems to forbid. "The world of Dasein is *with-world*" (*Die Welt des Daseins ist Mitwelt*, SZ 118). But it sets the word in a new frame. "'With' and 'also' are to be understood *existentially* and not categorially." "On the ground of this *with-like* being-in-the-world, the world is always already the one that I share with the others" (SZ 118). The term comes back with the "with" understood as GA 20 required. "With" says nothing about the kind of being of the others, who are co-Dasein. It says that the world is shared, because Dasein's being is being-with. The 1925 correction did not abolish the word; it fixed the place from which the word may be said.

## §28, GA 20 348–49: being the "there"

Reads [§28.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 28.

"Dasein", in its strict terminological sense, does not mean occurring "here" or "there". It means *being* the "there" itself. A material thing is never a "there"; it is encountered *into* one. The "there" is the being of a being that has the structure of uncoveredness: the world is disclosed to it, and it is uncovered to itself in being in that world. The understanding the passage carries is the "there" as a way of being, with the name of the being read back from it. The passage also shows the course's vocabulary for disclosure still in motion, with the two key terms assigned the reverse way from *Sein und Zeit*.

> These two phenomena, the *disclosedness* of the world itself as well as the fact that being-in-the-world is for its part co-uncovered, determine the unitary phenomenon that we characterize as *uncoveredness*. … "Here" and "there" are only possible insofar as there is something like a "there" at all, which "there" is our being toward being-with-one-another. … A thing occurring in the world, a material thing, is never itself a "there", but is encountered into one. Therefore we designate the being that we also call human being as the one that *is its "there" itself*. Only with this do we come to the strict formulation of the meaning of the term "Dasein". "Dasein" means, in our terminological use, according to the phenomena, not so much occurring "here" and "there", but *being the "there" itself*. … Such a being, which Dasein is, *brings its there along from the outset*, so that world can be uncovered at all. Dasein brings its there along from the outset, not in the sense of a dead property, but as what to be, namely to be its there, is precisely the proper sense of the being of Dasein.

German:

> Diese beiden Phänomene, die *Erschlossenheit* der Welt selbst sowohl wie der Tatbestand, daß das In-der-Welt-sein seinerseits mitentdeckt ist, bestimmen das einheitliche Phänomen, --das wir als *Entdecktheit* kennzeichnen. … ›Hier‹ und ›dort‹ ist nur möglich, sofern es so etwas wie ›Da‹ überhaupt gibt, welches ›Da‹ unser Sein zum Miteinandersein ist, sofern überhaupt die Möglichkeit einer Bewandtnisganzheit besteht. Ein in der Welt vorkommendes, ein materielles Ding ist nie selbst ein ›Da‹, sondern begegnet in ein solches. Deshalb bezeichnen wir das Seiende, das wir auch Mensch nennen, als dasjenige, das *sein ›Da‹ selbst ist*. Damit kommen wir erst zur strengen Formulierung der Bedeutung des Terminus ›Dasein‹. ›Dasein‹ heißt in unserer terminologischen Verwendung gemäß den Phänomenen nicht so viel wie ›da‹ und ›dort‹ vorkommen, sondern *das ›Da‹ selbst sein*. … Solches Seiendes, das Dasein ist, *bringt sein Da von Hause aus mit*, so daß Welt überhaupt erst entdeckt sein kann. Das Dasein bringt sein Da von Hause aus mit, nicht im Sinne einer toten Eigenschaft, sondern als das, was zu sein, nämlich sein Da zu sein, gerade der eigentliche Seinssinn des Daseins ist.

*Note on the text.* The file has a stray double hyphen before "das wir" in the first sentence, quoted as it stands; the footnote marker after *Entdecktheit* (n. 92, the editor's note) is omitted. The passage is at the opening of §28a, GA 20 348–49 (:4400–:4412).

The passage makes its terminological point with distancing marks around a single syllable. "There" (›Da‹) appears again and again in single quotation marks, and so do its neighbours "here" (›Hier‹) and "there, yonder" (›dort‹). The marks hold the word away from its ordinary use as a deictic adverb, the word that points to a place. The argument turns on an order of possibility. "'Here' and 'there' are only possible insofar as there is something like a 'there' at all." Pointing to places presupposes a being that is open to a world in which there are places. The "there" in this sense is not a place but "our being toward being-with-one-another". It is then denied to things: a material thing "is never itself a 'there', but is encountered into one" (*begegnet in ein solches*). The preposition *in* with the accusative makes the "there" the space into which things come to meet us.

From this the name is read back. "Therefore we designate the being that we also call human being as the one that *is its 'there' itself*." "Only with this" (*Damit … erst*) does the term "Dasein" receive its "strict formulation". Its meaning is "not so much occurring 'here' and 'there'", the ordinary sense of *dasein* as being present, "but *being the 'there' itself*". The italicized infinitive repeats the move of §18, where the name said no *what* but a way "to be" (row 22). Here the way to be is specified: to be the open "there" in which a world can be uncovered. The phrase that follows comes from § 5. Dasein "*brings its there along from the outset*" (*bringt sein Da von Hause aus mit*), "not in the sense of a dead property". In § 5 perception was intentional "from the outset" (row 6). Here Dasein is its "there" from the outset, and the phrase marks the analytic's arrival at the being whose directedness § 5 described.

The vocabulary of disclosure in the first sentence is assigned the reverse way from *Sein und Zeit*. The world is "disclosed" (*erschlossen*), Dasein is "co-uncovered" (*mitentdeckt*), and the unitary phenomenon is "uncoveredness" (*Entdecktheit*). The editor's note at this point says so directly. The terms "are here not yet as firmly delimited in their meanings" as in SZ; "rather the reverse tendency is noticeable, that 'uncoveredness' is ascribed to Dasein as an existential and 'disclosedness' is a determination of the being of world" (GA 20 349, n. 92, :4449). The afterword gives the evidence from the sources of the text. "In the manuscript Heidegger speaks, for example, of the 'uncoveredness of Dasein' and of the 'disclosedness of the world'. In the transcript of Simon Moser, Heidegger strikes 'uncoveredness' in favour of 'disclosedness' in the case of Dasein" (GA 20 445, :5206). The passage stands on the hinge of that correction. A reading that relies on which noun goes with Dasein has to note that the lecture text and its revision disagree.

The passage opens the chapter on in-being as such. Its first sentence gathers the analysis of the anyone: Dasein, "one oneself", is "co-uncovered" in the concerned world (§26). After the passage come the structures of this uncoveredness: disposedness (§28a, row 31), understanding (§28b) and interpretation (§28c). The "there" is the unitary phenomenon of which they are the articulation. The "there" also returns with its light. In §31c uncoveredness gives the concept for the old idea of a *lumen naturale*: Dasein is "lit in itself", while a stone has no light and is not even dark (GA 20 412).

*Sein und Zeit* §28 keeps the sentence and completes the correction. "The being that is essentially constituted by being-in-the-world *is* itself in each case its 'there'". The "here" of an "I-here" is understood from a "there" ready-to-hand (SZ 132). The *lumen naturale* is gathered into the same paragraph. Dasein is "'illuminated'", that is, "cleared in itself as being-in-the-world, not through another being, but such that it *is* itself the clearing (*Lichtung*)". Then comes the sentence from GA 20, word for word: "Dasein brings its there along from the outset", followed by the conclusion the 1925 vocabulary could not yet draw: "*Dasein is its disclosedness*" (*Das Dasein ist seine Erschlossenheit*, SZ 133). The word GA 20 gave to the world has passed to Dasein, as the Moser transcript had already marked.

## §28, GA 20 352–53: disposedness, finding oneself

Reads [§28.2](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 28.

Disposedness (*Befindlichkeit*) is the way Dasein is uncovered to itself in being affected by the world. In every dealing it "finds itself" (*befindet sich*) in some mood. The finding is not reflection: the "self" that is found need not be a known "I", and in everyday life it is the anyone. Heidegger chooses the word for this reason, and the understanding the passage carries is in the reflexive verb. Dasein is found by its own being, in a mood, before any knowledge of itself. A stone never finds itself; the simplest living thing already does.

> Dasein is in all its dealings with the world always somehow *addressed* … In such an elevation, or in an opposite depression, the same phenomenon shows itself again and again as a constitution of Dasein: that in all its dealings with the world itself it always *finds itself* so or so. It finds itself so or so, in this or that *mood*. When we say it *finds itself*, then with this "self" there is not at first explicitly meant a formed and thematically known "I" itself; rather, precisely in everyday absorption in the anyone, it can be this anyone itself in its indeterminacy, and it is. This co-uncoveredness of being-in-the-world in being addressed by the world is possible only because Dasein from the outset always *finds itself* in each of its ways of being, because Dasein itself is uncovered for itself. This basic form of the primary co-uncoveredness of Dasein we designate as *disposedness*. A stone never finds itself, but is merely present-at-hand; a quite primitive one-celled living thing, by contrast, will already find itself, where this disposedness can be the greatest possible and darkest dullness, but in this it is essentially different, in its structure of being, from something like the mere presence-at-hand of a thing.

German:

> In einem solchen Gehobensein bzw. in einem gegenteiligen Herabgedrücktsein zeigt sich immer wieder dasselbe Phänomen als Verfassung des Daseins, daß sich in allem seinem Umgang mit der Welt selbst immer so oder so *befindet*. Es befindet sich so oder so, in dieser oder jener *Stimmung*. Wenn wir sagen, es *befindet sich*, so ist mit diesem ›Sich‹ zunächst gar nicht ausdrücklich ein ausgeformtes und thematisch gewußtes ›Ich‹ selbst gemeint, sondern es kann gerade im alltäglichen Aufgehen im Man dieses Man selbst in seiner Unbestimmtheit sein und ist es auch. Diese Mitentdecktheit des *In-der-Welt-seins* im Angegangenwerden von der Welt ist nur deshalb möglich, weil Dasein von Hause aus *sich* immer in jeder seiner Seinsweisen *befindet*, weil Dasein selbst für es selbst entdeckt ist. Diese Grundform der primären Mitentdecktheit des Daseins bezeichnen wir als *Befindlichkeit*. Ein Stein befindet sich nie, sondern ist lediglich vorhanden; ganz primitives einzelliges Lebewesen dagegen wird sich schon befinden, wobei diese Befindlichkeit die größtmögliche und dunkelste Dumpfheit sein kann, aber bei dem ist es wesentlich der Seinsstruktur nach von so etwas wie einem bloßen Vorhandensein eines Dinges, einer Sache, verschieden.

*Note on the text.* The English adds the opening clause "Dasein is in all its dealings with the world always somehow *addressed*" from the preceding sentence for orientation; the German quotation begins with the sentence after it. The first sentence of the German lacks its subject in the file ("daß sich … befindet"; the context requires "daß es sich"), quoted as it stands.

The term is built from an everyday reflexive. *Sich befinden* means both "to be located" (the book is on the table) and "to be, to feel" in a state (how are you? *wie befinden Sie sich?*). Heidegger uses the second sense and reads the reflexive literally: Dasein *finds itself*. The italics fall on the verb each time, *befindet*, and once on the pronoun, *sich*. He then guards the pronoun at once. "With this 'self'" (›Sich‹, in distancing marks) no "formed and thematically known 'I'" is meant. In everyday life the self that finds itself in a mood "can be this anyone itself in its indeterminacy, and it is". The finding precedes any knowledge of who finds. The term is defined from this: "this basic form of the primary co-uncoveredness of Dasein we designate as *disposedness*". The suffix *-keit* makes the reflexive verb a structure.

The vocabulary of the surrounding sentences describes disposedness as being addressed, not as an inner state. The world "addresses itself" to concern; concernful being-in "is approached" (*angegangen*) by the world; being-in-the-world is "as it were constantly called upon" (*aufgerufen*) by the world's threateningness and non-threateningness (GA 20 351). Moods are named by their direction: "an elevation", "an opposite depression" (*Gehobensein*, *Herabgedrücktsein*). The same paragraph rejects the subjective reading outright. That caring Dasein experiences the world as threatening does not mean that it "'subjectively' takes the world so", which would be "a complete inversion of the facts"; rather, "caring in-being uncovers the world in its significance" (GA 20 352). Disposedness is a way of uncovering, and mood belongs to the world's showing itself.

The comparison that ends the passage fixes the term's range. "A stone never finds itself, but is merely present-at-hand." A one-celled organism "will already find itself", even if its disposedness is "the greatest possible and darkest dullness". The line is drawn between the present-at-hand and the living, not between the human and the animal. Heidegger concedes the organism a disposedness while denying that it is a mere thing; the passage does not say how its finding-itself differs from Dasein's, and the course does not return to the question. The point here is ontological. Finding oneself is a way of being that a thing cannot have.

The passage sets the ground for the course's decisive disposedness. Disposedness is "the a priori for uncoveredness and disclosedness" and stands "equiprimordially beside understanding" (§28.2). In §30 anxiety is "the disposedness in uncanniness", in which Dasein finds itself before "the fact that I am" (§30.2–30.3, row 35), and the same section names "death-anxiety", which is not fear of death but anxiety as the disposedness of naked being-in-the-world, the theme §34 takes up (§30.3). The everyday finding-oneself of § 28 is the base from which anxiety isolates Dasein.

*Sein und Zeit* keeps the term and the stone, and adds a concept GA 20 does not have. "What we indicate ontologically with the title disposedness is ontically the most familiar and everyday: mood, being attuned" (SZ 134). Then: "this 'that it is'" of Dasein, "veiled in its whence and whither", "we call the *thrownness* of this being into its there" (*Geworfenheit*, SZ 135). The word "thrownness" does not occur in GA 20. In 1925 disposedness discloses Dasein as addressed by the world; the bare "that I am" appears only in anxiety (§30.3). In 1927 every mood discloses the "that it is", and thrownness becomes, with understanding as projection, one of the two moments of disclosedness. The difference shows how much SZ's analysis of mood owes to what GA 20 reserved for anxiety.

## §29, GA 20 376–77: falling as Dasein's pushing itself away from itself

Reads [§29.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 29.

Falling (*Verfallen*) is the way everyday Dasein pushes itself away from itself (*Abdrängung*) into the anyone and its idle talk. It is a "movedness of being" (*Seinsbewegtheit*), not a fault to be lamented and one day removed. The understanding the passage carries is falling as a movement constitutive of Dasein. It is reached by an inference from idle talk: talk covers up even where nobody intends it to. A covering that sets in against every intention must belong to the being of the one who talks.

> Since this covering-up sets in against all explicit intention, there reveals itself in it and in the tendency to it a structure of being of Dasein that is given with Dasein itself. In the covering-up that Dasein temporalizes out of itself, there announces itself the peculiar kind of being that we designate as the *pushing-away* of Dasein from itself — pushing-away from its proper original disposedness and disclosedness. That Dasein, in its being as *everydayness*, pushes itself away from itself — this kind of being shall be designated as *falling*. The term "falling" designates a *movedness of being of the happening of Dasein*, and may again not be taken as an evaluation, as if this term marked something like a temporarily occurring bad property of Dasein that would be lamented and perhaps remedied in advanced times of human culture. Falling means, as do uncoveredness, being-with and in-being, a constitutive structure of being of Dasein, and indeed a specific phenomenon of in-being, in which Dasein at first constantly has its being.

German:

> Da sich diese Verdeckung wider alle ausdrückliche Absicht einstellt, offenbart sich in ihr und in der Tendenz zu ihr eine Seinsstruktur des Daseins, die mit diesem selbst gegeben ist. In der Verdeckung, die das Dasein aus sich selbst heraus zeitigt, bekundet sich die eigentümliche Seinsart, die wir als die *Abdrängung* des Daseins von ihm selbst bezeichnen. — Abdrängung von seiner eigentlichen ursprünglichen Befindlichkeit und Erschlossenheit. Daß Dasein in seinem Sein als *Alltäglichkeit* sich von ihm selbst abdrängt, diese Seinsart sei als das *Verfallen* bezeichnet. Der Terminus ›Verfallen‹ bezeichnet eine *Seinsbewegtheit des Daseinsgeschehens* und darf wiederum nicht als eine Bewertung genommen werden, als markierte dieser Terminus so etwas wie eine zeitweilig auftretende schlechte Eigenschaft des Daseins, die zu beklagen und in fortgeschrittenen Zeiten der Menschheitsskultur vielleicht zu beheben wäre. Verfallen meint ebenso wie Entdecktheit, Mitsein und In-Sein eine konstitutive Seinsstruktur des Daseins und zwar ein spezifisches Phänomen des In-Seins, worin das Dasein sein Sein zunächst ständig hat.

*Note on the text.* The file has "Menschheitsskultur" with a doubled *s*, quoted as it stands. The passage closes §29a (GA 20 377, :4617–:4619).

The passage defines falling in two steps, and each step has its own word. The first word is the noun of a movement: "pushing-away" (*Abdrängung*), and its reflexive verb, Dasein "pushes itself away from itself" (*sich von ihm selbst abdrängt*). The prefix *ab-* gives the direction, away, and the reflexive makes Dasein both the one who pushes and the one pushed. The appositive names what it is pushed away from: "its proper original disposedness and disclosedness". The second word is the term itself, introduced in the subjunctive of a stipulation: "this kind of being *shall be* designated as *falling*" (*sei … bezeichnet*). Then comes its classification: a "*movedness of being of the happening of Dasein*" (*Seinsbewegtheit des Daseinsgeschehens*). The compound of three nouns places falling in the class of movements, and the movement in the class of happenings of Dasein. Falling is not a state into which Dasein has fallen but the way it moves.

The inference that justifies the term is carried by the connective "since" (*Da*) at the head of the passage. Covering-up "sets in against all explicit intention" (*wider alle ausdrückliche Absicht*). It needs, as the paragraph before says, no "explicit intent to deceive"; "the groundless being-said and being-passed-on suffices" (GA 20 377). From the involuntariness of the covering, Heidegger infers that it "reveals" a structure "given with Dasein itself". The step is strong. An involuntary tendency is not yet a structure of being. What licenses the step is the claim, made in the same breath, that Dasein "temporalizes" the covering "out of itself" (*aus sich selbst heraus zeitigt*). The covering is not caused from outside. If it comes from Dasein against Dasein's own intentions, it shows what Dasein is beneath them.

The refusal of evaluation is spelled out, and its wording carries the point. The term may "not be taken as an evaluation", "as if" (*als markierte*, a subjunctive) it marked "a temporarily occurring bad property … that would be lamented and perhaps remedied in advanced times of human culture". The irony of the last phrase is aimed at the idea of progress. Falling cannot be abolished by improving culture, because it is "a constitutive structure of being", listed alongside uncoveredness, being-with and in-being. The polemic just before the passage, against congresses where nothing need be said "for one has, after all, spoken one's mind" and where "an understanding finally springs from the accumulation of non-understanding", is meant in the same spirit. It is "no moral sermon", and "ancient sophistry was in its essential structure nothing else" (§29.1, GA 20 376).

The passage gives a name to what §13f had first shown at work in the history of philosophy. There the neglect of the question of being revealed "Dasein in its kind of being of falling, which it does not escape" (row 18). Here falling is defined in the everyday, from the phenomena of discourse. The rest of § 29 works out its modes: curiosity (row 33), ambiguity, and the characters of its movedness, "tempting, tranquilizing, alienating", in which Dasein "entangles itself" (§29.4). §29e traces falling back to a "hang" and an "urge" and calls their structure "proneness" (row 34). In §30 the movement acquires a direction: falling is "the flight of Dasein from itself", and that flight is what anxiety discloses (§30.1).

Heidegger had described this movement four years earlier under another name. The 1921–22 course on Aristotle analyses the "movedness" of factical life as "ruinance" (*Ruinanz*). Its headings speak of a movedness that "is" life "in itself, as itself, for itself, out of itself: i.e., against itself". They add that the "presupposition of ruinance" is "a counter-movedness" (GA 61 98–99, Rojcewicz's translation, table of contents). The 1925 term keeps the structure of a movement of Dasein against itself and drops the Latin coinage. *Sein und Zeit* keeps the 1925 term and its disclaimer: "the title, which expresses no negative evaluation", means that Dasein is "at first and mostly *alongside* the concerned 'world'" (SZ 175). It then gives the movement a sharper name: "we call this 'movedness' of Dasein in its own being the *plunge*" (*Absturz*, SZ 178). Between 1921 and 1927 the vocabulary changes three times, ruinance, pushing-away, plunge. The understanding stays fixed: a movement of Dasein away from itself, which it does not escape and which presupposes a counter-movement.

## §29, GA 20 379–81: curiosity as the care of seeing

Reads [§29.2](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 29.

"Seeing" (*Sehen*) is the course's name for every way in which concern uncovers and appresents its world, not only for what the eyes do. Curiosity is this seeing set free from the work it guided, a care that now only wants to see. The understanding the passage carries is seeing in this wider sense, and it is reached through two witnesses. Augustine noticed that language lends the word "see" to all the senses when they are used to know. Aristotle opened his *Metaphysics* by placing the care of seeing in the being of the human being. Heidegger takes both as evidence and declines both explanations.

> This sight of circumspection is the formed possibility of concernful uncovering, *seeing*. Seeing is here neither restricted to seeing with the eyes, nor is the term in this use primarily related to sensory perceiving at all; rather, seeing is used here in the wider sense of concernful and caring appresentation. Augustine already became attentive to this remarkable priority of seeing over other ways of perceiving, without being able to clarify the phenomenon in the end. So he says in his *Confessions*, where he speaks of the *concupiscentia oculorum*, the so-called lust of the eyes … It becomes clear here that seeing has a priority within perceiving, and that therefore the sense of seeing is not tailored solely to perceiving with the eyes; rather, seeing, as was already constantly the case among the Greeks, is identified with grasping something. … In the being of the human being there lies essentially the care of seeing. … For him curiosity becomes nothing less than an original comportment, from which theoretical comportment … is motivated. That is, to be sure, a one-sided interpretation, though motivated by the Greek way of considering things.

German:

> Diese Sicht der Umsicht ist die ausgebildete Möglichkeit des besorgenden Entdeckens, Sehens. Sehen ist dabei weder auf das Sehen mit den Augen eingeschränkt, noch ist überhaupt der Terminus in diesem Gebrauche primär auf sinnliches Vernehmen bezogen, sondern Sehen ist hier in dem weiteren Sinn der besorgenden und sorgenden Appräsentation gebraucht. … Deutlich wird hier, daß das Sehen einen Vorrang hat innerhalb des Vernehmens, und daß deshalb der Sinn von Sehen nicht etwa einzig auf das Vernehmen mit den Augen zugeschnitten ist, sondern das Sehen, wie das ja auch schon bei den Griechen ständig der Fall war, wird identifiziert mit Erfassen von etwas. … Im Sein des Menschen liegt wesenhaft die Sorge des Sehens. … Für ihn wird nun geradezu die Neugier zu einem ursprünglichen Verhalten, aus dem sich das theoretische Verhalten … motiviert. Das ist freilich eine einseitige, aber aus der griechischen Betrachtungsart her motivierte Interpretation.

*Note on the text.* The Augustine passage between the first and second ellipses (*Confessions* X, 35, n. 96), Latin with Heidegger's German, is clean in the file and is summarized below rather than quoted. The Greek of Aristotle's first sentence (*Metaphysics* A 1, 980a21, n. 97) is OCR garble at :4641 and is not reproduced from this file; the clean Greek is quoted from SZ below. The word *theōrein* after "theoretical comportment" is also garbled and is omitted at the third ellipsis. A second note numbered 96 (:4667) records a misprint, "appralltresentiert" for *appräsentiert*, in the paragraph after the passage.

The passage turns on one word that it takes out of the senses. "Sight" (*Sicht*) first appears as the sight of "circumspection" (*Umsicht*), the looking-around that guides concern and gives all grasping, providing and performing "the path of its accomplishment, the means of execution, the right occasion and the suitable time" (GA 20 379). Then the noun becomes the verbal noun: this sight is the formed possibility of concernful uncovering, "*seeing*" (*Sehens*). The next sentence removes the eyes in two steps. Seeing is "neither restricted to seeing with the eyes", nor is the term "primarily related to sensory perceiving at all". What is left is a definition by function: seeing "in the wider sense of concernful and caring appresentation". Seeing names whatever makes something present for concern. The course has been using the word this way since circumspection appeared in § 23; the passage only makes the usage explicit, at the point where it is about to describe a seeing cut loose from concern.

Augustine is brought in as a witness to a fact of language, not to a theory. Heidegger quotes *Confessions* X, 35 in Latin with his own German alongside. "Seeing belongs properly to the eyes. But we use this word for the other senses too, when we take them in their work of knowing." "We do not say: hear how that glimmers, or smell how that shines, or taste how that gleams, or feel how that glitters; we say in all of this: *see*." But we do say "see how that sounds, see how that smells, see how that tastes, see how hard that is" (GA 20 379–80). This is why the experience of the senses in general is called "lust of the eyes" (*concupiscentia oculorum*). Heidegger's comment draws the conclusion his definition needed: the sense of seeing "is not tailored solely to perceiving with the eyes", and seeing "is identified with grasping something". He also marks a limit. Augustine noticed "this remarkable priority of seeing" "without being able to clarify the phenomenon in the end", and did not treat "the significance for being of this priority" (GA 20 380).

The second witness receives the same double verdict. The first treatise in Aristotle's writings on ontology opens with the sentence that all human beings by nature reach out for seeing (*eidenai*), which Heidegger renders in one line: "In the being of the human being there lies essentially the care of seeing" (GA 20 381). The rendering puts his own term "care" into Aristotle's verb of desire. He then adds that *eidenai* "may not be translated with knowing". For Aristotle "curiosity becomes nothing less than an original comportment", from which theoretical comportment is motivated. That is "a one-sided interpretation", though "motivated by the Greek way of considering things". The one-sidedness is the passage's point against the tradition. The Greeks saw that the care of seeing belongs to human being, but they took a free-floating seeing as the origin of knowledge. In Heidegger's analysis that freed seeing is curiosity, a mode of falling.

The paragraphs that follow explain how seeing becomes free. Concern can come to rest; in rest care does not vanish but "lays itself into the freed bare seeing and perceiving of the world" (GA 20 381). "Seeing, however, is a perceiving of the far." Freed seeing therefore leaps away from what is nearest to what is new, for the sake of having seen it. Its marks are "not-staying" (*Unverweilen*) and "distraction" (*Zerstreuung*), and together they form a "never-dwelling-anywhere" (*Aufenthaltslosigkeit*) in which Dasein "is everywhere and nowhere" and "organizes a *flight from itself*" (GA 20 381–83). The definition of seeing as appresentation is what lets curiosity count as a mode of being-in rather than a vice of the eyes. The "perceiving of the far" draws on §25, where seeing and hearing are "senses of distance" "because in them Dasein, as de-severing, primarily dwells" (GA 20 318). Curiosity is de-severance that has lost its work and brings near only appearances.

Heidegger had read the same chapter of the *Confessions* closely four years earlier. The 1921 lectures on Augustine give a section to "The Second Form of *tentatio*: *concupiscentia oculorum*". It is headed by Augustine's phrase "the superfluous curiosity of knowing" (*curiositas supervacanea cognoscendi*) and reads the chapter's sentence that "the eyes are the principal senses for knowing" (GA 60 §14). In 1921 the passage illustrates a form of temptation in factical life; in 1925 it is evidence for a structure of being.

*Sein und Zeit* §36 keeps the passage and changes its order and its verdict. The quotation from Augustine appears again almost word for word (SZ 171). Aristotle now comes first, with the Greek intact: "πάντες ἄνθρωποι τοῦ εἰδέναι ὀρέγονται φύσει. In the being of the human being there lies essentially the care of seeing." The 1925 judgment "one-sided" is gone. In its place: "this Greek interpretation of the existential genesis of science is not accidental", and it makes explicit "what is prefigured in the sentence of Parmenides", that being is what shows itself in pure perceiving (SZ 171). The book also separates curiosity from wonder (*thaumazein*): curiosity "has nothing to do with" admiring contemplation, and seeks knowledge "only in order to have known" (SZ 173). The analysis of seeing is the same; the reading of the Greeks has become a thesis about the history of ontology.

## §29, GA 20 390–91: authenticity read literally, and proneness

Reads [§29.5](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), fifth paragraph under § 29.

Falling is Dasein falling away from what is its own. A tendency to fall is possible only on the ground of an inclination (*Hang*), and the structure of this inclination is what Heidegger calls *Verhängnis*, here rendered "proneness". Two word-formations carry the understanding. "Authenticity" (*Eigentlichkeit*) is to be read literally, as having oneself as one's own. "Proneness" is not a fate but a structure, formed from *Hang* as *Begegnis* (encounter) is formed from *begegnen* and *Erkenntnis* (cognition) from *erkennen*. The passage gathers the analysis of falling into a single term and points it toward care.

> The levelling and disappearing of Dasein in the anyone is, through the publicness and everydayness of the anyone, a covered-up decay of Dasein. This decay temporalizes itself as the *falling-away of Dasein from its ownness* into the falling characterized above. *Authenticity* must here be understood in the literal sense as *having-oneself-as-one's-own-with-oneself*. The falling-away is a falling constitutive for Dasein itself, insofar as it is a being with the character of being-in-the-world and Dasein is in each case mine. Such a *tendency of being* toward falling is possible a priori only on the ground of an *inclination to it*. This inclination, to which the analysis of falling phenomenally comes back, makes up a basic structure of Dasein that we designate as *proneness*. We use the expression "proneness" here not in the sense of a fact, but in the meaning of "encounter" and "cognition", so that "proneness" here does not mean a definite state but an *existential* structure. Proneness is nothing other than the *flight of Dasein from itself*, flight from itself into the world uncovered by it. An inclination is in its being nothing original, but points back in itself to a possible *urge*.

German:

> Die Einebnung und das Verschwinden des Daseins im Man ist durch die Öffentlichkeit und die Alltäglichkeit des Man verdeckter Zerfall des Daseins. Dieser Zerfall zeitigt sich als *Abfallen des Daseins von seiner Eigentümlichkeit* in das charakterisierte Verfallen. *Eigentlichkeit* muß dabei im wörtlichen Sinne verstanden werden als *Bei-sich-selbst-sich-zu-eigen-haben*. Der Abfall ist ein für das Dasein selbst konstitutives Fallen, sofern es Seiendes vom Charakter des In-der-Welt-seins ist und Dasein je meines ist. Eine solche *Seinstendenz* des Fallens ist a priori nur möglich auf dem Grunde eines *Hanges dazu*. Dieser Hang, auf den die Analyse des Verfallens phänomenal zurückkommt, macht eine Grundstruktur des Daseins aus, die wir als das *Verhängnis* bezeichnen. Wir gebrauchen hier den Ausdruck ›Verhängnis‹ nicht im Sinne eines Faktums, sondern in der Bedeutung wie ›Begegnis‹ und ›Erkenntnis‹, so daß also ›Verhängnis‹ hier nicht einen bestimmten Zustand, sondern eine *existenziale* Struktur meint. Das Verhängnis ist nichts anderes als die *Flucht des Daseins vor ihm selbst*, Flucht vor ihm selbst in die von ihm entdeckte Welt. Ein Hang ist seinsmäßig nichts Ursprüngliches, sondern weist in sich selbst zurück auf einen möglichen *Drang*.

*Note on the text.* The passage is in §29e, GA 20 390; its last sentence runs onto p. 391 (:4717–:4721). "Proneness" is a working rendering chosen to keep the link to "inclination" (*Hang*); the ordinary sense of *Verhängnis*, "fate" or "doom", is the sense Heidegger sets aside.

The paragraph is built from the family of the verb "to fall" (*fallen*). In three sentences Heidegger uses "decay" (*Zerfall*), "falling-away" (*Abfallen*), "falling" (*Verfallen*), "the falling-away" (*Abfall*) and "falling" (*Fallen*). The prefixes do the work. *Zer-* is coming apart; *ab-* is away from something; *ver-* is the movement into something and getting lost in it. The decay of Dasein in the anyone "temporalizes itself as the *falling-away of Dasein from its ownness*" (*Eigentümlichkeit*) "into" falling. The sentence names both directions of the movement: away from what is its own, into the world. This makes visible what falling is a falling *from*, which §29a had left as Dasein's "proper original disposedness and disclosedness" (row 32).

The gloss on authenticity is the first of the two word-formation claims. "*Authenticity* must here be understood in the literal sense": *eigentlich* is read through *eigen*, "own", and spelled out as a hyphenated phrase, "*having-oneself-as-one's-own-with-oneself*" (*Bei-sich-selbst-sich-zu-eigen-haben*). The literal reading strips the word of its everyday sense of "real" or "genuine" and leaves a relation of Dasein to itself. Authenticity is not a higher grade of being but a way of being one's own. The same clause grounds falling in mineness: the falling-away is constitutive "insofar as … Dasein is in each case mine". Only a being that is its own can fall away from its ownness. The pair *eigen*/*Eigentlichkeit* thus ties the analysis of falling back to the "in-each-case-mine" of §18 (row 22).

The second claim concerns *Verhängnis*. The ordinary word means "fate", the doom that befalls someone. Heidegger forms it anew from *Hang*, "inclination" or "slope", the way one leans. He says how he uses it: "not in the sense of a fact, but in the meaning of 'encounter' and 'cognition'". *Begegnis* is the event of encountering and *Erkenntnis* the act or structure of knowing; both are formed from verbs with the suffix *-nis*. *Verhängnis* in this sense is the structure of being inclined, "not a definite state but an *existential* structure". The quotation marks around the three words (›Verhängnis‹, ›Begegnis‹, ›Erkenntnis‹) show that he is reasoning about word-formation. The fatalistic sense is excluded, but the echo is kept: the inclination is what Dasein cannot help.

The argument runs backward through three grounds. The tendency to fall is "possible a priori only on the ground of an *inclination*". The inclination "is in its being nothing original" but "points back … to a possible *urge*" (*Drang*). Urge and inclination in turn "are to be determined still more fundamentally in the phenomenon that we designate as *care*" (GA 20 391). The paragraph after the passage rules out a reading of this structure as a doctrine of the corruption of human nature or "any theory of original sin". It is "a pure consideration of structure that lies *before* all such considerations", and "no hidden theology is being presented" (GA 20 391). §31b takes up urge and inclination as modes of care (row 36). Between the two places, proneness gives falling the direction that §30 sets out to explain: it is "the *flight of Dasein from itself*". §30a starts from this phrase and asks what Dasein flees from (row 35).

*Sein und Zeit* keeps the literal reading of authenticity and drops proneness. Of authenticity and inauthenticity it says that "these expressions are chosen terminologically in the strict sense of the word", grounded in the fact that Dasein is determined by mineness (SZ 43). The word *Verhängnis* does not occur in the book. Its place in the list of falling's characters is taken by a near-homophone: "temptation, tranquillization, alienation and entangling-itself (*das Verfängnis*)" characterize falling, and its movedness is "the *plunge*" (SZ 178). The *-nis* formation survives, now from *sich verfangen*, to get caught, instead of from *Hang*. Urge and inclination go to §41, which denies that care can be derived from them, "because they themselves are founded in it" (SZ 182; SZ 194–95). The disclaimer about original sin returns as well: the existential interpretation makes "no ontic statement about the 'corruption of human nature'", "because its problematic lies *before*" any such statement (SZ 180).

## §30, GA 20 400–402: the nothing of anxiety as the world itself

Reads [§30.2](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 30.

The "nothing" before which anxiety is anxious is not nothing at all. It is nothing worldly, no thing within the world, and precisely as such it is "the *world in its worldliness itself*". The understanding the passage carries is this nothing as the world, and Heidegger reaches it by taking an everyday phrase at its word: when anxiety has passed, we say "it was really nothing". From there the analysis shows that what anxiety is anxious before and what it is anxious about coincide. Both are being-in-the-world, disclosed in its not-being-at-home.

> When anxiety has subsided, we say: "it was really nothing", and this talk hits the facts completely. It was *nothing*; *the before-which of anxiety is the nothing*, that is, nothing occurring in the world, nothing definite, nothing worldly; but insofar as it can nonetheless be there, encountering in an obtrusiveness, it is much more than a threateningness for fear, namely the *world in its worldliness itself*. The indeterminacy of the before-which, that is, this nothing as nothing worldly, is phenomenally completely determinate. It is the world in its worldliness, which to be sure does *not* give itself like a thing of the world. … The *before-which* anxiety is anxious is the *wherein* of being-in-the-world, and what one is anxious *about* in anxiety is this same being-in-the-world itself, and indeed in its primary uncoveredness of "not-at-home". In anxiety, then, the before-which of anxiety and the about-which of being anxious are not only worldly indeterminate; they coincide, or more precisely, in anxiety it does not yet come to such a separation; the before-which and the about-which is Dasein.

German:

> Wenn die Angst sich gelöst hat, dann sagen wir: »es war eigentlich nichts«, und diese Rede trifft völlig den Tatbestand. Es war *nichts*; *das Wovor der Angst ist das Nichts*, d. h. nichts in der Welt Vorkommendes, Bestimmtes, nichts Weltliches, sondern, sofern es doch begegnend in einer Aufdringlichkeit da sein kann, ist es viel mehr als eine Bedrohlichkeit für die Furcht, nämlich die *Welt in ihrer Weltlichkeit selbst*. Die Unbestimmtheit des Wovor, d. h. dieses Nichts als nichts Weltliches, ist phänomenal völlig bestimmt. Es ist die Welt in ihrer Weltlichkeit, die sich freilich *nicht* wie ein Weltding gibt. … *Wovor* die Angst sich ängstet, ist das *Worin* des In-der-Welt-seins und das, *worum* man sich ängstet in der Angst, ist dieses selbe In-der-Welt-sein selbst und zwar in seiner primären Entdecktheit des >Unzuhause<. In der Angst sind also das Wovor der Angst und das Worum des Sichängstens nicht nur weltlich unbestimmt, sondern sie fallen zusammen, genauer gesprochen, in der Angst kommt es noch nicht zu solcher Trennung; das Wovor und das Worum ist das Dasein.

*Note on the text.* The page markers in this stretch of the file are out of order (401, 400, 403, 402 at :4806–:4828), and the table of contents puts the start of §30b on p. 400. The page numbers given here, 400–402, are therefore approximate. The file also opens one sentence with a missing capital ("as Sichängsten vor", :4818), not quoted.

The passage begins with a piece of idle talk that turns out to be true. "When anxiety has subsided, we say: 'it was really nothing', and this talk hits the facts completely." The everyday phrase is meant as a dismissal; Heidegger reads it as a description. The italics then shift from the adverb to the noun. "It was *nothing*" becomes "*the before-which of anxiety is the nothing*" (*das Nichts*). The article turns the dismissal into a phenomenon with a structure. The next clause says what the noun excludes: "nothing occurring in the world, nothing definite, nothing worldly". What remains when every worldly thing is excluded is not emptiness but "the *world in its worldliness itself*". The sentence moves from "nothing" to "world" by way of what anxiety does not find.

The second sentence turns the negation into a determination. "The indeterminacy of the before-which … is phenomenally completely determinate." This is a paradox only on the assumption that whatever is determinate must be a thing. Fear has a definite before-which, something within the world approaching from a definite region. Anxiety has none, and this lack is its precise character: the world "does *not* give itself like a thing of the world". Before the passage, Heidegger describes the threat as "so near and yet not present as this or that", and the anxious person says "*it becomes uncanny for one*" (*es wird einem unheimlich*, GA 20 400). The German word holds the analysis: *unheimlich* is "un-homely", and Heidegger glosses it at once: one "is no longer at home" in the most familiar surroundings, and being-in-the-world becomes "a complete 'not-at-home' as such". He returns to it after the passage as "not-at-home" (›Unzuhause‹).

The second half of the passage closes the structure. Fear was analysed in §30a by its moments: a before-which (the threatening thing) and an about-which (the self that is threatened). In anxiety both are taken from the same place. The before-which is "the *wherein* of being-in-the-world", the world; the about-which is "this same being-in-the-world itself". So the two "coincide", "or more precisely, in anxiety it does not yet come to such a separation". The correction matters: the coincidence is not a fusion of two things, but a state prior to the division that fear presupposes. The next paragraph names what is disclosed: "the fact that I am", "in the sense of naked *being-in-the-world*", Dasein's facticity; and it calls anxiety "the *disposedness in uncanniness*" (GA 20 402). The ground that anxiety discloses is "an *existential*, that is, a disclosed ground — and indeed an *abyss*". Heidegger calls this "the existential positivity of the nothing of anxiety" (GA 20 402–403).

The passage answers the question that §29 left open. Falling was "the flight of Dasein from itself" (row 34), and §30 began by asking what the flight flees from (GA 20 391). The answer is now in: Dasein flees from its own being-in-the-world, disclosed as not-at-home, into the familiar world of the anyone. Anxiety is not a mode of fear; "*all fear is grounded in anxiety*" (GA 20 392). §30c then draws the consequence of the coincidence. Anxiety is anxiety before being-in-the-world and about it, and this means that Dasein is a being for which, in its being, "*its being itself is at issue*" (GA 20 405). That is the formula with which §31 defines care (row 36). The same paragraph points ahead. Anxiety can arise "in a distinctive sense in *death*", as "death-anxiety", "not fear of death, but anxiety as disposedness of naked being-in-the-world itself" (GA 20 403). The passage also corrects a remark from the reading of Descartes: there is, after all, an "affection by being as such", and anxiety is "nothing other than the sheer experience of being in the sense of being-in-the-world" (GA 20 403).

*Sein und Zeit* §40 keeps the phrase and adds a qualification that the 1925 text did not need to make. "When anxiety has subsided, everyday talk is accustomed to say: 'it was really nothing'. This talk in fact hits ontically *what* it was." Then: "this nothing of the ready-to-hand, which alone everyday circumspective talk understands, is no total nothing. The nothing of readiness-to-hand is grounded in the most original 'something', in the world" (SZ 187). The book says "ontically" and "no total nothing" where GA 20 says the talk "hits the facts completely" and calls the nothing "phenomenally completely determinate". The uncanny is kept and sharpened into the hyphenated "*not-at-home*" (*Un-zuhause*), and "falling flight *into* the at-home of publicness is flight *from* the not-at-home, that is, from uncanniness" (SZ 189). The question of the nothing was taken further in Heidegger's 1929 inaugural lecture, which is not in the corpus.

## §31, GA 20 407–409: care as being-ahead-of-itself in already-being-alongside

Reads [§31.2](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), second paragraph under § 31.

Care is the being of a being for which, in its being, that very being is at issue. Heidegger derives its articulated structure from a single idiom, "it is about" (*es geht um*). To be about something is to be out for it (*aus sein auf*), and to be out for one's own being is to be ahead of oneself. Since Dasein is always already in a world, it is ahead of itself in already-being-alongside something. The understanding the passage carries is this formula, and its two adverbs, "ahead" and "already", are words of time. The passage itself says that the structure will later be traced back to time.

> This "*it is about its own being for Dasein*" presupposes first that in this Dasein there lies something like a *being-out-for something*; Dasein is out for its own being, for its being itself, in order "*to be*" its being. *Care is as such being-about this being-out-for being, which this being-out-for itself is.* This must be understood in such a way that in it Dasein as it were *anticipates* itself. If the being of Dasein is what care is about, then Dasein has always already *held* its own being *ahead* of itself, even if not in the sense of a thematic consciousness of it. The innermost structure of this caring of Dasein about its being can be grasped *formally* as: *being-ahead-of-itself of Dasein itself*. … Thus we gain the whole structure of care in the formal sense: *the being-ahead-of-itself of Dasein in its always already being alongside something*. … This being-ahead-of-itself says just that care, or Dasein in care, has its own being *thrown ahead* as *existential facticity*. The being-out-for its own being, which it is about, is enacted *always already* in a *being-alongside* something, out of an *always-already-being-in-the-world-alongside*.

German:

> Dieses »*es geht dem Dasein um sein eigenes Sein*« setzt zunächst voraus, daß in diesem Dasein so etwas wie ein *Aussein auf etwas* liegt; das Dasein ist auf sein eigenes Sein aus, auf sein Sein selbst, um sein Sein »*zu sein*«. *Sorge ist als solches Sein-um dieses Aussein auf das Sein, das dieses Aussein selbst ist*. Das muß so verstanden werden, daß darin das Dasein sich gleichsam *vorwegnimmt*. Ist das Sein des Daseins das, worum es der Sorge geht, so hat das Dasein immer schon sein eigenes Sein sich *vorweggehalten*, wenn auch nicht im Sinne eines thematischen Bewußtseins davon. Die innerste Struktur dieses Sorgens des Daseins um sein Sein kann *formal* gefaßt werden als: *Sich-vorweg-sein des Daseins selbst*. … So gewinnen wir die Gesamtstruktur der Sorge im formalen Sinne: *das Sich-vorweg-sein des Daseins in seinem immer schon Sein bei etwas*. … Dieses Sich-vorweg-sein besagt eben, daß die Sorge oder das Dasein in der Sorge sein eigenes Sein sich *vorweggeworfen* als *existenziale Faktizität* hat. Das Aussein auf das eigene Sein, worum es ihm geht, vollzieht sich *immer schon* in einem *Sein-bei* etwas, aus einem *Immer-schon-in-der-Welt-sein-bei*.

*Note on the text.* The passage runs from GA 20 408 to 409 (:4882–:4886). Two slips in the sentences after the first ellipsis ("formalale" for *formale*; "Verppelung", apparently for *Verkoppelung*, "coupling") are outside the quotation. The sentence in §31a that defines care (GA 20 406) has the formal structure "being for which, in its being-in-the-world, this being itself is at issue".

The derivation unpacks an idiom. *Es geht um* is ordinary German for "it is a matter of", "what is at stake is". Heidegger has used it to define care: "*care is the term for the being of Dasein as such*", with the formal structure of a "being for which, in its being-in-the-world, this being itself is at issue" (*um dieses Sein selbst geht*, GA 20 406). The passage now asks what the idiom "presupposes". Its answer comes from the preposition *um*, "about", and its neighbour *aus … auf*, "out for". A being that is "about" its being is "*out for*" it (*Aussein auf etwas*). It is out for its being "in order '*to be*'" it, and the phrase in quotation marks again turns being into a task, as "to be" did in §18 (row 22). Then the definition circles back on itself. "Care is as such being-about this being-out-for being, which this being-out-for itself is." The sentence is hard because what it describes is reflexive: the being that care is about is the being-out-for in which care consists.

From "out for" Heidegger moves to "ahead", in three verbs with the same prefix. Dasein "*anticipates*" itself (*vorwegnimmt*, literally takes itself ahead); it has "*held*" its being "*ahead*" of itself (*vorweggehalten*); and it has its being "*thrown ahead*" (*vorweggeworfen*) "as *existential facticity*". The three verbs vary the action, taking, holding and throwing, around a constant adverb, *vorweg*, "ahead". From the verbs comes the noun phrase, "*being-ahead-of-itself*" (*Sich-vorweg-sein*). The qualifications guard it against a psychological reading. It is held ahead "even if not in the sense of a thematic consciousness", and it is "not some psychological process or a property of the subject" (GA 20 408). The third verb, "thrown ahead", is the only place in the passage where something like SZ's "thrownness" appears, and it is attached to facticity: Dasein has its being ahead of it as the factical being it has to be.

The second adverb, "already", completes the formula. Being-ahead must be understood together with the structures already shown: Dasein is "*always already alongside something*", namely the world (GA 20 408). The formula joins the two in one hyphenated phrase, which the next page repeats as "being-ahead-of-oneself-in-already-being-alongside" (GA 20 409). The parenthesis that follows gives it its range: "in-being is therefore constitutive for every kind of being of Dasein — even for the authentic!" The being-ahead never leaves the world behind. Even the most authentic projection is enacted out of an "always-already-being-in-the-world-alongside".

The formula gathers everything the course has analysed so far. Concern is now "only a mode of being of care" (GA 20 407); falling, as proneness, is one of its modes (row 34). The rest of § 31 tests the formula on what came before: on urge and inclination (§31b), on uncoveredness and the "I can" (§31c), on the fore-structure of interpretation (§31d), on the Cura fable (§31e), and on intentionality, the "mere directing-itself-toward", which "must rather be moved back into the unitary basic structure of being-ahead-of-itself-in-being-alongside" (§31f, GA 20 420). The course began with intentionality as the discovery of phenomenology (row 6); it ends its analysis of Dasein by placing intentionality inside care. The formula also explains a remark from the course's opening argument. In §7 the a priori was traced to "the earlier" and said to carry a faded temporal sense (row 11). Here the "already" and the "ahead" are found in the being of Dasein. The paragraph after the passage says what the passage only implies: "this basic structure of care will in the later interpretation be led back to the constitution of being that we will then learn to understand as *time*" (GA 20 409). §31b opens by naming the "riddling character" of the two moments "being-ahead-of-itself" and "already-being-alongside", "which is nothing other than *time*" (GA 20 409).

*Sein und Zeit* §41 gives the formula a third member and a fixed form. "The being of Dasein means: ahead-of-itself-already-being-in-(the-world) as being-alongside (entities encountered within the world)" (*Sich-vorweg-schon-sein-in-(der-Welt-) als Sein-bei (innerweltlich begegnendem Seienden)*), and "this being fulfils the meaning of the title *care*", used "purely ontologically-existentially" (SZ 192). The book restates the formula when it looks back on it (SZ 249, 317). The 1925 pair, ahead and already-alongside, has become a triad, ahead, already-in and alongside. The sentence before the formula says what the third member brings with it: "in ahead-of-itself-already-being-in-a-world there lies essentially co-included the falling being alongside the concerned ready-to-hand within the world" (SZ 192). Falling, which GA 20 §29e traced to proneness and left to be grounded in care (row 34), is written into the formula itself. §65 then carries out the reduction to time that GA 20 announces here. "*The original unity of the care-structure lies in temporality.* The ahead-of-itself is grounded in the future. The already-being-in… makes known in itself having-been. The being-alongside… is made possible in making-present" (SZ 327). GA 20 does not reach that step. Heidegger's manuscript ends with the Cura fable (§31e), and the Second Section is taken from the transcript Heidegger authorized (afterword, GA 20 446); its last section, §36, closes with the thesis that Dasein temporalizes its being as time (row 41) without assigning the moments of care to the dimensions of time.

## §34, GA 20 431–32: death as what impends, not what is outstanding

Reads [§34.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 34.

Death is not something "outstanding" (*aussteht*) in Dasein, a missing remainder that would complete it as the last piece completes a sum. It is what "impends" (*bevorsteht*) for Dasein in its being, and impends constantly for as long as Dasein is. The understanding the passage carries is death as impending, and it is carried by two verbs built on the same root, *stehen*, "to stand", with different prefixes: *aus-*, out, and *bevor-*, before. The exchange of one verb for the other turns death from an event at the end of a sequence into a character of the whole.

> Death, however, is neither a constituent of wholeness for a whole in the sense of a composite, nor … ; rather — if death is otherwise a character of the being of Dasein — it cannot in this sense of being be grasped primarily from the presence-at-hand and non-presence-at-hand of world-things. Dasein as care is rather being toward something. Death is not something that is still outstanding in Dasein, but what *impends* for Dasein in its being, and indeed constantly impends, as long as it is Dasein. As always already impending, death belongs to Dasein itself even when it is not yet whole and not yet finished, that is, not dying. Death is not a missing part for a whole as a composite; rather it *constitutes the wholeness of Dasein from the outset*, so that only from this wholeness does Dasein have the being of its respective parts, that is, of possible ways to be.

German:

> *Tod* aber ist weder Ganzheitskonstitutivum für ein Ganzes im Sinne des Kompositums, … sondern — wenn anders der Tod ein Seinscharakter des Daseins ist — kann er in diesem Seinsinne nicht primär aus dem Vorhandensein und Nichtvorhandensein von Weltdingen begriffen werden. Dasein vielmehr als Sorge ist Sein zu etwas. Der Tod ist nicht etwas, was am Dasein noch aussteht, sondern was dem Dasein in seinem Sein *bevorsteht* und zwar ständig bevorsteht, solange es Dasein ist. Als immer schon bevorstehend, gehört der Tod zum Dasein selbst auch dann, wenn es noch nicht ganz und noch nicht fertig ist, also nicht im Sterben ist. Der Tod ist nicht fehlender Teil für ein Ganzes als Kompositum, sondern er *konstituiert die Ganzheit des Daseins von vornherein*, so daß es erst aus dieser Ganzheit das Sein jeweiliger Teile, d. h. möglicher Weisen zu sein, hat.

*Note on the text.* The passage is at GA 20 432 (:5071), just after the opening of § 34 on p. 431. The clause omitted at the ellipsis reads "nor yet a constituent of wholeness for a whole as a composite such that it would no longer be with being-finished". "Seinsinne" (for *Seinssinne*) is as in the file. The second section of the main part, which includes this passage, survives only in the transcript Heidegger authorized; his manuscript ends with the Cura fable (afterword, GA 20 446).

The passage defines death by the contrast of two verbs. *Ausstehen*, said of a payment or a delivery, means to be still owing, not yet in. *Bevorstehen* means to lie ahead, to be imminent. Both verbs build on "to stand", and both describe something that is not yet. The prefixes put the "not yet" in different places. What is outstanding stands *out*, outside the whole it will complete; what impends stands *before* the one it concerns, already related to it. The first verb belongs to the vocabulary of the composite. §33 had used it for the difficulty that drives the problem of wholeness: as long as Dasein is, something in it "is still outstanding" (*steht noch aus*), and when nothing is outstanding it is no longer there (GA 20 426–27, 430). The second verb answers that difficulty by denying its premise.

The sentence that precedes the contrast gives the ground for the exchange: "Dasein as care is rather being toward something." Care was defined in §31 as being-ahead-of-itself (row 36). A being that is always ahead of itself does not wait for its end as a sum waits for its last term. Its end is something it is already toward. The italics fall on *bevorsteht*, and the addition "constantly" (*ständig*) and the clause "as long as it is Dasein" make the impending a feature of every moment, not of the last. Death "belongs to Dasein itself even when it is not yet whole and not yet finished, that is, not dying". The final sentence reverses the order of whole and part. Death "*constitutes the wholeness of Dasein from the outset*"; only from that wholeness does Dasein have its "parts", which are glossed as "possible ways to be". The whole comes first, as it did for the referential whole of the world in § 23.

The paragraph that follows shows that the new verb is necessary but not sufficient. "Many things" in Dasein "impend"; death is not yet distinguished by impending (GA 20 432). What impends could still be taken as an event that "strikes me as something alien from the world". It is grasped as a character of Dasein only when it is understood "from the structure of being of Dasein, from care, from being-ahead-of-oneself" (GA 20 432). The next page completes the step. With death "*my* ownmost *being*, my being-able at every moment, impends"; "this possibility, death as my death, I am myself. There is no death in general" (GA 20 433). The passage on the verbs is the hinge between §33, which seemed to prove that Dasein can never be whole, and §34. §33 closes by announcing that a pure phenomenological interpretation of death will show that impossibility to be "mere semblance" (GA 20 431).

The understanding depends on what §33 had established. There the contrast was between two senses of being "finished" (*fertig*). A table or a book is finished when it is present-at-hand and available; for Dasein, being finished means "no longer being" (GA 20 430). The ontology of the present-at-hand understands wholeness as a composite whose last piece arrives. "Outstanding" is its verb. §34 sets "impending" against it as the verb of care. The same section also refuses the substitute of taking another's death as the theme: "no one can take his dying from the other", and "death is in each case mine" (GA 20 429). Mineness and impending belong together: what impends for me is my own being.

The 1924 treatise *The Concept of Time* named death with a third word. It speaks of Dasein's "pastness" (*Vorbei*), "one's ownmost pastness", which "is constantly, at every moment, indeterminate", and of the "When of pastness" (*Wann des Vorbei*) that everyday Dasein tries to reckon (GA 64, ch. 3, Farin's translation). "Pastness" names death from its outcome, the having-gone-by of Dasein. The 1925 course replaces it with the verb of what lies ahead, and the replacement is kept. *Sein und Zeit* §50 turns the verb into a noun: death "is not something not yet present-at-hand, not the last outstanding item reduced to a minimum, but rather an *impending*" (*Bevorstand*). It adds at once that "much can impend for Dasein as being-in-the-world", so that impending does not yet mark death out, and death is then "an *eminent* impending" (*ausgezeichneter Bevorstand*, SZ 250–51). The two moves of GA 20 §34, the exchange of verbs and the qualification, become the two steps of SZ §50.

## §34, GA 20 433: "Dasein is thus essentially its death"

Reads [§34.3](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 34.

Dasein *is* its death. The sentence does not say that Dasein will die, or that it is mortal. It says that the extreme possibility of no longer being is the possibility that Dasein already is, because Dasein as care is being-ahead-of-itself and the "I can". The understanding the passage carries is death as Dasein's own possibility, and the consequence follows at once: as possible being Dasein is always already whole. The problem of wholeness that §33 declared insoluble is solved by the grammar of the copula.

> Care as being-ahead-of-itself is as such at the same time possibility. "I can", more precisely, I am this "I can" in a distinctive sense. I am, namely, this "I can die at any moment". This possibility is a possibility of being in which I always already am. It is a distinctive one, for *I am myself* this constant, *extreme* possibility *of my*self, namely of no longer being. Care, which is essentially care about the being of Dasein, is at its innermost nothing other than this being-ahead-of-itself in the extreme possibility of its own being-able. Dasein is thus essentially its death. With it, nothing worldly impends; rather it itself impends for itself, not in an arbitrary possibility of being but in that of its no-longer-being-there. Insofar as Dasein *qua* being-possible already is essentially its death, it is as Dasein always already whole.

German:

> Sorge als Sich-selbst-vorweg-sein ist als solche zugleich Möglichkeit. ›Ich kann‹, genauer gesprochen, ich bin dieses ›Ich kann‹ in einem ausgezeichneten Sinne. Ich bin nämlich dieses ›Ich kann jeden Augenblick sterben‹. Diese Möglichkeit ist eine Seinsmöglichkeit, in der ich immer schon bin. Sie ist eine ausgezeichnete, denn *ich bin selbst* diese ständige, *äußerste* Möglichkeit *meiner* selbst, nämlich nicht mehr zu sein. Sorge, die wesenhaft Sorge um das Sein des Daseins ist, ist im innersten nichts anderes als dieses Sich-selbst-vorweg-sein in der äußersten Möglichkeit des eigenen Seinkönnens. Dasein ist so wesensmäßig sein Tod. Mit ihm steht nicht etwas Weltliches bevor, sondern es selbst steht sich selbst bevor, nicht in einer beliebigen Seinsmöglichkeit, sondern in der seines Nicht-mehr-da-seins. Sofern Dasein *qua* Möglichsein wesensmäßig sein Tod schon ist, ist es als Dasein immer schon Ganzes.

*Note on the text.* The passage is at GA 20 433 (:5079).

The passage reaches its thesis by a chain of copulas, each of which replaces "have" with "be". Care "is as such at the same time possibility". The "I can" is not something I have: "I am this 'I can' in a distinctive sense". The phrase in quotation marks then receives its extreme content: "I am, namely, this 'I can die at any moment'." The quotation marks hold the sentence as something Dasein could say of itself, and the verb "am" places Dasein inside it. The next sentence repeats the move with emphasis on the pronoun: "*I am myself* this constant, *extreme* possibility *of my*self". The italics fall on "I am", on "extreme", and on "my". When the thesis arrives, "Dasein is thus essentially its death", it is the conclusion of a series of sentences in which possibility has been made the predicate of "am".

The thesis draws on §31c, where "understanding" in the sense of "being able" led to the formula "*I am, that is, I can*" (GA 20 412). Dasein "does not merely have possibilities" it could take up and drop; "Dasein itself, insofar as it is, is nothing other than *being-possible*" (*Möglichkeit-sein*). The passage on death takes that formula to its limit. Among the possibilities Dasein is, one is "distinctive" (*ausgezeichnet*) and "extreme" (*äußerst*): the possibility of not being. Because it is the possibility of not being at all, it is not one possibility among others that could be exchanged for another. It is the edge of the whole range. Care "at its innermost" is "being-ahead-of-itself in the extreme possibility of its own being-able". The innermost of care is its outermost possibility; the passage joins the two superlatives.

The verb of row 37 returns in reflexive form: "nothing worldly impends; rather it itself impends for itself" (*es selbst steht sich selbst bevor*). What lies ahead of Dasein is Dasein, "not in an arbitrary possibility of being but in that of its no-longer-being-there". The consequence follows from the copula: "Insofar as Dasein *qua* being-possible already is essentially its death, it is as Dasein always already whole." The wholeness that seemed attainable only at the moment when Dasein ceases to be is present in every moment, because the end is not a future event but a possibility Dasein is now. The word "already" (*schon*), from the formula of care, carries the solution.

The paragraph continues with a qualification that sets the task of the rest of the section. The whole of Dasein "will become phenomenally graspable in its structure" when "the way of being in which Dasein can *authentically* be this its extreme possibility" is worked out (GA 20 433). That Dasein *is* its death does not yet say *how* it is it. The phenomenological interpretation has to show the way of being in which Dasein is its extreme possibility, first in everydayness, then authentically. It also has limits it states at once. It gives no description of the causes and manners of dying, and it decides nothing about what may come after death. It moves in "the most radical this-sidedness", which prejudges so little about immortality that it first gives "the sense and the ground" for that question (GA 20 433–34). §34a then shows everyday Dasein covering up the possibility it is (rows 38–39), and §34b shows the authentic relation as running ahead (GA 20 439–40).

In the 1924 treatise the solution has the same shape, without the copula of identity. Running ahead to the extreme possibility is the mode of being through which "Dasein is authentically its ultimate possibility", and "as it runs ahead, Dasein is the future" (GA 64, ch. 3, Farin's translation). There the identity is reserved for authentic running ahead. GA 20 states it of Dasein as such and then asks how it can be lived authentically. *Sein und Zeit* §§46–53 keep the structure and change the formulation. Death is "Dasein's ownmost possibility", and "*death is, insofar as it 'is', essentially in each case mine*" (SZ 240). It is defined as the "*ownmost, non-relational, unsurpassable possibility*" (SZ 250–51). The sentence "Dasein is … its death" does not reappear; the book speaks of a "being toward death". The change keeps the distance that GA 20's copula had closed: Dasein is not its death but comports itself toward it as a possibility.

## §34, GA 20 437–38: "sum moribundus"

Reads [§34.7](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), seventh paragraph under § 34.

The certainty of death is "the basic certainty of Dasein itself". Against Descartes, Heidegger proposes that the statement adequate to Dasein's being is not *cogito sum*, "I think, I am", but *sum moribundus*, "I am dying" or "I am one who is to die". The understanding the passage carries is the certainty of being as the certainty of one's own dying, and it is set out in Latin. The reading turns on two words: the participle *moribundus*, which says "about to die" and "dying", and the order of the terms, which puts the *sum* first and lets the *moribundus* give it its sense.

> The inconspicuous concern of not-thinking of death covers up a basic character of it, namely its *certainty*. With the ambiguity of "one dies some day, after all", this certainty is reinterpreted into uncertainty. … This certainty, that I myself am it in my coming-to-die, is *the basic certainty of Dasein itself*, and is a genuine statement of Dasein, whereas the *cogito sum* is only the semblance of one. If such pointed formulas say anything at all, the adequate statement, the one that concerns Dasein in its being, would have to read: *sum moribundus*, and indeed not *moribundus* as one gravely ill or wounded, but insofar as I am, I am *moribundus*; the *moribundus* first gives the *sum* its sense.

German:

> Das unauffällige Besorgen des Nichtdenkens an den Tod verdeckt einen Grundcharakter an ihm, nämlich seine *Gewißheit*. Mit der Zweideutigkeit des ›Man stirbt ja einmal‹ ist diese Gewißheit zur Ungewißheit uminterpretiert. … Diese Gewißheit, daß ich es selbst bin in meinem Sterbenwerden, ist *die Grundgewißheit des Daseins selbst* und ist eine echte Daseinsaussage, während das *cogito sum* nur der Schein einer solchen ist. Wenn solche zugespitzten Formeln überhaupt etwas besagen, müßte die angemessene und das Dasein in seinem Sein betreffende Aussage lauten: *sum moribundus*, und zwar nicht moribundus als Schwerkranker oder Verwundeter, sondern sofern ich bin, bin ich *moribundus* — das *moribundus* gibt dem *sum* allererst seinen Sinn.

*Note on the text.* The passage is in §34a, GA 20 438 (:5108), at the turn from p. 437. The omitted sentence at the ellipsis gives the everyday saying "everyone will have to believe in it some day", a statement about death "addressed, as it were, to no one".

The passage sets two kinds of certainty against each other. The everyday saying "one dies some day, after all" admits death and in the same breath makes it uncertain. It is said by "one", and so of no one in particular, and it postpones death to "some day". This "reinterprets" certainty into uncertainty. Heidegger answers with a certainty of a different kind, "that I myself am it in my coming-to-die" (*in meinem Sterbenwerden*). The noun he coins is a verbal noun of becoming, "coming-to-die", not "dying" as an event. The certainty is not knowledge that an event will occur. It is the certainty that I am the one who is dying. He calls it "*the basic certainty of Dasein itself*", and contrasts it with the *cogito sum*, which is "only the semblance" of a statement about Dasein.

The contrast with Descartes is set out in Descartes' own language. If "such pointed formulas say anything at all", the adequate statement would be "*sum moribundus*". The Latin participle *moribundus* means "about to die", "dying", and is used for the gravely ill. Heidegger excludes that sense: "not *moribundus* as one gravely ill or wounded". He keeps the participle's form, the *-bundus* of something on the way to happening. "Insofar as I am, I am *moribundus*." The final clause states the order of sense: "the *moribundus* first gives the *sum* its sense". In Descartes' formula the *sum* follows from the *cogito*: thinking secures being. In Heidegger's, the *sum* comes first in word order but receives its meaning from what follows it. "I am" means "I am one who is dying". The certainty of my being is the certainty of my death.

The passage depends on the course's earlier reading of Descartes. In §22 Descartes' equation of true being with certain being, *verum ens* with *certum ens*, let one way of knowing decide what the world is (row 24). §24 had already answered the *cogito sum* with an inversion of its order: the first statement about Dasein is not "I think, I am" but "*sum cogito*", I am, and as being-in-the-world I think (row 27). The sentence on death completes the inversion. It names what the *sum* means, which §24 had only placed first. The *sum* in which every *cogito* is grounded is the being of a being whose ownmost possibility is its end.

The surrounding paragraph explains why certainty and indeterminacy belong together. The everyday calculation that death "is in no case to be expected now" overlooks that the "*indeterminacy*", that death can come at any moment, "belongs essentially to its certainty". The indeterminacy "gives it its sting" (*Stachel*). Death is "*the extreme, though indeterminate, yet certain possibility*" (GA 20 438). §34b asks how this possibility can be held in its character as possibility: not by realizing it, as in suicide, which turns it into an actuality, but by "running ahead" (*vorlaufen*) to it, letting it stand as possibility (GA 20 439). Running ahead brings Dasein back from the anyone, and "only in dying can I in a certain sense say absolutely 'I am'" (GA 20 440). The last clause returns to the *sum*: the "I am" said absolutely is said in view of death.

The formula *sum moribundus* does not occur in *Sein und Zeit*. The book keeps the analysis of certainty. It asks "how is it with the 'certainty of death'?", and it shows that the merely empirical certainty of death as an event "does not decide about the certainty of death" (SZ 256–58). It also keeps the critique of the *cogito sum*, which the introduction names as the second station in the destruction of the history of ontology: Descartes left "the sense of being of the 'sum'" undetermined (SZ 24). It does not bring the two together in a Latin counter-formula. The 1925 phrase, in which the critique of Descartes and the analysis of death meet in a single participle, remains peculiar to the course.

## §35, GA 20 440–41: the one who acts is always conscienceless

Reads [§35.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 35.

Running ahead to death is the choice of oneself, and what is chosen in it is "wanting-to-have-conscience" (*Gewissenhabenwollen*). Heidegger takes up a saying of Goethe's, "the one who acts is always conscienceless", and turns it into a thesis about this choice. The one who acts necessarily becomes guilty in being with others, and only one who has chosen to have conscience can be conscienceless in this sense. The understanding the passage carries is conscience as something chosen, with guilt as its correlate. The reading turns on the word *gewissenlos*, which in ordinary German means "unscrupulous" and in the passage comes to mean "having taken on guilt".

> Dasein can, in this running ahead to its death, make itself responsible in an absolute sense. It "can" choose the *presupposition of being* of itself, namely itself. In this choice nothing other is chosen than *wanting-to-have-conscience*. … *Running ahead is the choice of wanting-to-have-conscience*. The one who acts, however, is, as *Goethe* already said, always conscienceless. I can really be conscienceless only if I have chosen wanting-to-have-conscience. The one who acts is conscienceless, that is, he necessarily becomes "guilty" in being-with-one-another, not in the sense that he commits this or that misstep, but as acting being-with with others, and as such Dasein is *eo ipso* guilty, even if — and precisely then — it does not know that it harms another or destroys him in his Dasein. With the choice of wanting-to-have-conscience I have at the same time chosen *having-become-guilty*.

German:

> Dasein kann in diesem Vorlaufen zu seinem Tode sich selbst verantwortlich … in einem absoluten Sinne machen. Es ›kann‹ die *Seins-Voraussetzung* seiner selbst, nämlich sich selbst wählen. In dieser Wahl ist nichts anderes gewählt als das *Gewissenhabenwollen*. … *Das Vorlaufen ist die Wahl des Gewissenhabenwollens*. Der Handelnde aber ist, wie *Goethe* schon sagte, immer gewissenlos. Gewissenlos kann ich eigentlich nur sein, wenn ich das Gewissenhabenwollen gewählt habe. Der Handelnde ist gewissenlos, d. h. er wird im Miteinandersein notwendig ›schuldig‹, nicht in dem Sinne, daß er diesen oder jenen Fehltritt begeht, sondern als handelndes Mitsein mit Anderen und als solches ist das Dasein *eo ipso* schuldig, auch wenn es — und gerade dann — wenn es nicht weiß, daß es einen Anderen schädigt oder in seinem Dasein zerstört. Mit der Wahl des Gewissenhabenwollens habe ich zugleich das *Schuldiggewordensein* gewählt.

*Note on the text.* The ellipsis in the first sentence of the German marks a page break (:5160–:5164) at which the file has two running heads out of order, "442" and then "441"; the sentence runs on without loss. The table of contents puts §35 on p. 440 and §36 on p. 441, so the passage is GA 20 440–41. The editor gives no reference for Goethe's remark.

The section opens by connecting choice to running ahead. "With the choosing of myself as my possibility I choose my being" (GA 20 440). The possibility chosen in running ahead is certain and indeterminate, as §34 showed (row 39). To take up its indeterminacy is to understand it as possible at every moment, which means being "absolutely *resolved*" (*entschlossen*) in "*having chosen*" oneself. To take up its certainty is to put every other ability-to-be behind it, so that particular actions first spring from this resoluteness. From this Heidegger infers that Dasein "can … make itself responsible in an absolute sense". The verb "can" in quotation marks (›kann‹) marks the ability as Dasein's being, as in "I am, that is, I can" (row 38). What Dasein can choose is "the *presupposition of being* of itself, namely itself".

The next sentence names what is chosen. "In this choice nothing other is chosen than *wanting-to-have-conscience*." The compound is built from a verb phrase, "to want to have conscience" (*Gewissen haben wollen*), made into a noun. Conscience is not something that can be chosen directly; one has it or not. What can be chosen is the wanting to have it. The italic thesis "*Running ahead is the choice of wanting-to-have-conscience*" identifies the authentic relation to death with this wanting. The paragraph adds that the choice need not occur only in running ahead, but that only running ahead chooses Dasein "not for the next two days but in its being itself" (GA 20 441).

Goethe's remark then enters as a provocation. "The one who acts, however, is, as *Goethe* already said, always conscienceless." In ordinary use *gewissenlos* is a reproach: unscrupulous, without conscience. Heidegger reverses it. "I can really be conscienceless only if I have chosen wanting-to-have-conscience." The adverb "really" (*eigentlich*) moves the word from the moral to the existential register, and the sentence that follows says what it means there. The one who acts "necessarily becomes 'guilty' in being-with-one-another". The quotation marks around *schuldig* mark the same shift. It is not "this or that misstep" but the condition of "acting being-with with others", in which Dasein is guilty "*eo ipso*", "even if — and precisely then — it does not know that it harms another or destroys him in his Dasein". The interjected "and precisely then" makes ignorance an aggravation, not an excuse. Guilt belongs to action as such, because acting with others always affects them in ways the agent cannot oversee.

The last sentence joins the two terms. "With the choice of wanting-to-have-conscience I have at the same time chosen *having-become-guilty*." Choosing to have conscience means choosing to accept the guilt that action brings. The paragraph then sums up the result in a single parenthetical formula: the genuine way of being of Dasein toward its extreme possibility is running ahead as wanting-to-have-conscience, which is the choice of "the essential being-guilty of Dasein itself, insofar as it is" (GA 20 441). The conscience is not developed as a call, and guilt is not analysed further. §36 takes the two results, running ahead and being-guilty, as the future and the past of Dasein (row 41).

The passage is a sketch of what *Sein und Zeit* §§54–60 work out at length, and the comparison shows what the sketch lacks. The book analyses conscience as a "call", the call's understanding as "*wanting-to-have-conscience*" (*Gewissen-haben-wollen*), and the guilt it discloses as "being the ground of a nullity" (SZ 269–88). It keeps Goethe's remark without his name: "every action is factically necessarily 'conscienceless'", "not only because it does not avoid factical moral indebtedness, but because on the null ground of its null projecting it has always already become guilty toward others in being-with with them" (SZ 288). The 1925 course reaches the conclusion without the call and without the nullity. Guilt follows from acting with others, and conscience from the choice that running ahead makes. The book adds the ground that GA 20 does not yet have.

## §36, GA 20 441: "Dasein temporalizes qua time its being"

Reads [§36.1](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), first paragraph under § 36.

Time is not something that *is*, a frame outside or a flow inside. Dasein "temporalizes" (*zeitigt*) its being as time. The understanding the passage carries is time as the being in which Dasein can be its whole, and the course's last page reaches it in three steps: running ahead is Dasein's being its future, being-guilty its having-been, and the being in which both are one is time. The page then turns the result against the two ordinary conceptions of time and against the everyday, in which time is the time of the anyone. The grammar of its central sentence carries the thesis: the subject of the sentence moves from "time" to "Dasein".

> Running ahead into the ownmost possibility of being is nothing other than the being of my ownmost *coming-to-be*. The being-guilty, which is co-posited in it, is the being of the ownmost *having*-been. The being of having-been is past, in such a way that in such a being I myself am nothing other than the *future* of Dasein *and with it its past*. The being in which Dasein can authentically be its whole as being-ahead-of-itself is *time*. Not: *time is*, but: *Dasein temporalizes qua time its being*. Time is nothing that occurs outside somewhere as a frame for world-events; time is just as little something that whirs away inside in consciousness; rather it is what makes possible the being-ahead-of-itself-in-already-being-alongside, that is, what makes possible the being of care. … The movements of nature, which we determine spatio-temporally, do not run off "in time" as "in" a hinge; they are as such completely *time-free*; they encounter only "into" time, insofar as their being is uncovered as pure nature. They encounter "into" the time which we ourselves are.

German:

> Das Vorlaufen aber in die eigenste Seinsmöglichkeit ist nichts anderes als das Sein meines eigensten *Seinwerdens*. Das Schuldigsein, das darin mitgesetzt ist, ist das Sein des eigensten *Ge*wesenseins. Das Sein des Gewesenseins ist Vergangenheit, so zwar, daß ich in einem solchen Sein selbst und nichts anderes als die *Zukunft* des Daseins bin *und damit seine Vergangenheit*. Das Sein, in dem Dasein seine Gänze eigentlich sein kann als Sich-vorweg-sein, ist die *Zeit*. Nicht: *Zeit ist, sondern: Dasein zeitigt qua Zeit sein Sein*. Zeit ist nichts, was draußen irgendwo vorkommt als Rahmen für Weltergebnisse; Zeit ist ebensowenig etwas, was drinnen im Bewußtsein irgendwo abschnurrt, sondern sie ist das, was das Sich-vorweg-sein-im-schon-sein-bei, d. h. was das Sein der Sorge möglich macht. … Die Bewegungen der Natur, die wir räumlich-zeitlich bestimmen, diese Bewegungen laufen nicht »in der Zeit« ab wie »in« einem Scharnier, sie sind als solche vollständig *zeitfrei*; sie begegnen nur »in« die Zeit, sofern ihr Sein als reine Natur entdeckt ist. Sie begegnen »in« die Zeit, die wir selbst sind.

*Note on the text.* §36 is the course's last section, GA 20 441 (:5170–:5176), taken from the transcript; the table of contents puts the afterword on p. 443, and the file's running heads are out of order at the end of §35. The omitted paragraph at the ellipsis identifies everyday time with the anyone (see below). "Weltergebnisse" may be a slip for *Weltereignisse*, "world-events"; the English follows that sense and the German is quoted as it stands. The image of the "hinge" (*Scharnier*) is as in the file.

The first three sentences assign the moments of the preceding sections to the dimensions of time by word-formation. Running ahead is "the being of my ownmost *coming-to-be*" (*Seinwerdens*), a coined noun from "to become" in which being is something still to come. Being-guilty is "the being of the ownmost *having*-been" (*Ge*wesenseins). The italics fall on the prefix alone, *Ge-*, the mark of the past participle in German: *gewesen*, "been". Guilt is what Dasein has already become; §35 called it "*having-become-guilty*" (*Schuldiggewordensein*, row 40). The next sentence draws the consequence. The being of having-been "is past", but in such a way that "I myself am nothing other than the *future* of Dasein *and with it its past*". The italic "and with it" binds the two: the future Dasein is in running ahead carries its past along. Then the conclusion: "The being in which Dasein can authentically be its whole as being-ahead-of-itself is *time*." The problem of wholeness, opened in §33 and answered in principle in §34 (row 38), receives its name.

The central sentence is a correction of grammar. "Not: *time is*, but: *Dasein temporalizes qua time its being*." The first formula makes time the subject and gives it the verb "to be", as if it were a being. The second makes Dasein the subject and gives it a transitive verb, *zeitigen*, whose object is "its being". *Zeitigen* in ordinary German means to bring to ripeness, to produce results; the course has used it throughout for the way Dasein brings about its modes of being (the covering that Dasein "temporalizes out of itself", row 32; the decay that "temporalizes itself" as falling-away, row 34). "Qua time" names the manner: Dasein brings its being about as time. The sentence after it excludes the two conceptions that the first formula invites. Time is not "outside somewhere as a frame for world-events", the time of physics. It is not "inside in consciousness", whirring away, the time of lived experiences. It is "what makes possible the being-ahead-of-itself-in-already-being-alongside, that is, … the being of care". The formula of row 36 returns, and time is named as its condition.

The two short paragraphs after the passage extend the thesis in two directions. Everyday time, "the time we know and reckon with", "is, seen more precisely, nothing other than the *anyone* to which Dasein in its everydayness has fallen"; being in the anyone is "a definite kind of *temporality*" (GA 20 441). And the movements of nature, determined "spatio-temporally", are "completely *time-free*"; they "encounter only 'into' time". The preposition with the accusative, »in« die Zeit, repeats the construction of row 30, where a thing "is encountered into" the there (*begegnet in ein solches*). Things do not run off "in time" as in a container. They come to meet us in the time that "we ourselves are". The last words of the course place time where §28 placed the "there".

The page is the end of the course as delivered, not the end of its plan. §3 had announced three parts: an analysis of the phenomenon of time and the gaining of its concept, a disclosure of the history of the concept of time, and, on that basis, the working out of the horizon for the question of being as such and of history and nature in particular (GA 20 11). The course ends inside the first part, with a page of theses. The afterword records that the second section of the main part comes from the Moser transcript, Heidegger's manuscript breaking off with the Cura fable (GA 20 446). The temporal interpretation of care that §31 announced (row 36) is therefore present only as an outline. Being-guilty is assigned to the past, running ahead to the future; the present, and the being-alongside it would ground, are not assigned.

The 1924 treatise had already said that "each Dasein is itself 'time'" (*Das jeweilige Dasein selbst ist (die) Zeit*). It had also warned that it "would constitute a fundamental misunderstanding" of the investigation to sum up its findings in the "categorical proposition: 'Dasein is in each case time'" (GA 64, ch. 3, Farin's translation). The GA 20 sentence answers that worry by removing the copula from both sides: neither "time is" nor "Dasein is time", but "Dasein temporalizes". *Sein und Zeit* keeps the verb and transfers it to temporality: "Temporality 'is' not a *being* at all. It is not, but *temporalizes* itself" (SZ 328). Its theses on time state what GA 20's last page lacks: "Temporality temporalizes itself primarily out of the future", and "original time is finite" (SZ 331). The ecstases are assigned in full, including the present that GA 20 leaves out: "having-been arises in a certain way out of the future" (SZ 326), and the being-alongside "is made possible in making-present" (SZ 327, row 36).

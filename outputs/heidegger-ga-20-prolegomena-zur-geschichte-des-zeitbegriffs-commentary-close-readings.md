# Heidegger, *Prolegomena zur Geschichte des Zeitbegriffs*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md) maps the argument of the whole course at equal depth. Each reading here instead follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Heidegger means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds that meaning, what work the passage does between what precedes and what follows it, and where the understanding leads in the rest of the course and in his other works.

The text is the German of GA 20, the Marburg lecture course of summer 1925, edited by Petra Jaeger (Klostermann). Passages are cited by § and by the printed page, "GA 20 n". Unit references such as "§2.3" name the third paragraph of the main commentary under § 2. All English renderings are my working translations, close enough to show the structure under discussion. They are not Theodore Kisiel's published translation (*History of the Concept of Time*), which is cited by name where it differs in a way that matters. Other works are cited by their Gesamtausgabe volume and page, *Sein und Zeit* by the Niemeyer page ("SZ n"), and works held in the corpus only in English by the translation and its German page where the file gives one.

The German comes from a converted file that is not wholly reliable. Page numbers are taken from running heads and can be one page out. Ten pages (27, 46, 47, 85, 87, 130, 137, 301, 417, 419) were recovered from the PDF's text layer and have lost their italics, so no reading argues from the absence of emphasis on them. Line-end hyphenation in the file is joined silently. Any other damage is noted where it occurs, and none of it is repaired.

## §1, GA 20 2–3: productive logic

Reads [§1.3](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 1.

For Heidegger, "productive logic" is logic that goes ahead of a science and discloses the field it will study. It lays open, in its constitution of being, the field a science is to work in, before the science has cut its objects out of that field, and it hands the science the basic structure of those objects. Logic in this sense is not a theory of correct thinking, and not a theory of science that studies how an existing science has built its concepts. It is the disclosure of the matter itself, carried out for the sake of a possible science. The first paragraphs of the course work toward this definition, and the rest of the course carries it out, though not on the objects for which it was announced.

> Because reality, nature as well as history, can be reached only, as it were, by leaping over the sciences, this prescientific, properly philosophical disclosing of these realities becomes what I call *productive logic*: an anticipatory disclosure and conceptual penetration of possible object-domains for the sciences. Unlike traditional theory of science, it does not run along behind a determinate fact, a contingent, historically given science, and examine that science for its structure. It is a logic that leaps ahead into the primary subject-field of a possible science and, by disclosing the constitution of being of this subject-field, first makes available the basic structure of the possible object of this science. That is the process of *original* logic, as Plato and Aristotle brought it out, though only within very narrow limits. Since then the concept of logic has been buried and no longer understood.

German:

> Weil zur Wirklichkeit — Natur sowohl wie Geschichte — nur vorgedrungen werden kann gewissermaßen im Überspringen der Wissenschaften, wird dieses vorwissenschaftliche, eigentliche philosophische Erschließen dieser Wirklichkeiten zu dem, was ich als *produktive Logik* bezeichne, vorausgehende Erschließung und begriffliche Durchdringung möglicher Gegenstandsgebiete für die Wissenschaften, die nicht, wie traditionelle Wissenschaftstheorie, hinter einem bestimmten Faktum einer zufälligen, historisch vorgegebenen Wissenschaft herläuft und sie auf ihre Struktur untersucht, sondern eine Logik, die vorausspringt in das primäre Sachfeld einer möglichen Wissenschaft und durch Erschließung der Seinsverfassung dieses Sachfeldes die Grundstruktur des möglichen Gegenstandes dieser Wissenschaft erst bereitstellt. Das ist der Prozeß der *ursprünglichen* Logik, wie sie von *Plato* und *Aristoteles* herausgestellt wurde — allerdings nur in ganz engen Grenzen. Seither ist der Begriff der Logik verschüttet und nicht mehr verstanden worden.

The definition rests on a pair of terms that § 1 set up in its opening paragraph: the object-domain (*Gegenstandsgebiet*) and the subject-field (*Sachfeld*). A science's object-domain is what it has made thematic. The subject-field is the whole of the matter from which that domain was "cut out" (*herausgeschnitten*, GA 20 2). Heidegger begins with a doubt: "it is not settled" (*es ist nicht ausgemacht*) whether an object-domain gives the subject-field at all, and whether historical knowledge "ever sees history in its historicity" (*die Geschichte in ihrer Geschichtlichkeit*, GA 20 2, :361). Productive logic is the answer to that doubt. It works at the level of the *Sachfeld*, and its result, the basic structure of possible objects, is handed down to the level of the *Gegenstandsgebiet*. The phrase "possible object-domains" (*möglicher Gegenstandsgebiete*) makes the order exact: the domain is still only possible, because the field has not yet been disclosed.

Heidegger's verbs then give this order a direction. The noun he chooses for the work, disclosing (*Erschließen*, *Erschließung*), occurs three times in the passage. Its counterpart in the opening paragraph is what stays "closed off" (*verschlossen*) to a science, and the unity the split between nature and history keeps "covered" (*verdeckt*). Disclosure is what a science cannot do for itself, and the verbs of motion place it accordingly. Reality is reached by pressing forward (*vordringen*). Productive logic "leaps ahead" (*vorausspringt*) into the field. Theory of science "runs along behind" (*hinter … herläuft*) a science that already exists. The two disciplines face opposite ways on one line, and the line is the factual science. What theory of science trails is "a determinate fact" (*Faktum*), the word of the neo-Kantians whom § 4 describes as having "for its proper object the fact of science" (*das Faktum der Wissenschaft*, GA 20 21, :608). Heidegger qualifies the fact as "contingent" and "historically given", and that is enough to take away its authority. The decisive verb comes last: productive logic "first makes available" (*erst bereitstellt*) the basic structure of the object. The "first" (*erst*) states the priority. The science does not have its object until the logic has disclosed the field.

The grammar of the definition shows that this is a redefinition of logic, not a discovery within logic. The philosophical disclosing "becomes what I call productive logic" (*wird … zu dem, was ich als produktive Logik bezeichne*). Heidegger does not argue that logic was always such a disclosing. He names the disclosing "logic" and then defends the name with a history: this was the "original" logic of Plato and Aristotle, since "buried" (*verschüttet*). In §9 the same word names the most dangerous kind of covering-up, something once discovered that has fallen back into concealment and survives only as semblance (GA 20 119). §9 also explains why the name fits. *Logos* means letting something be seen from itself (*apophainesthai*), and "-logy" in "phenomenology" is taken in that sense (§9.4). Logic in the original sense is already disclosure, and productive logic is that sense recovered.

The surrounding paragraphs show why § 1 needs the concept. Directly after the passage, Heidegger reads the "crisis of the sciences" as the sciences themselves calling for it. The real crisis is that "the basic relation of the individual sciences to the matters they question has become questionable" (GA 20 3–4). In that crisis, "scientific research gains a philosophical tendency"; the sciences "say that they need an original interpretation which they cannot provide themselves" (GA 20 4, :412). The survey that follows, of mathematics, physics, biology, history and theology, repeats one verb for each discipline: each is "pressing forward" (*vordringen*) to its matters, and "everywhere we see the pressing forward to an original understanding of the matters themselves" (GA 20 6, :429). This is the passage's own verb. The sciences press toward their subject-fields and cannot reach them, and productive logic is the name for reaching them. Phenomenology, the passage concludes, "has the task of making the subject-fields intelligible *before* scientific treatment" (GA 20 3). The concept thus turns the course's subtitle, "prolegomena to a phenomenology of history and nature", into a programme: the prolegomena are what productive logic must settle before it can disclose history and nature themselves.

The course never reaches history and nature. Its first section turns to Dasein, and § 1 already says why. The sciences "are nothing other than concrete possibilities of human Dasein itself, of expressing itself about its world and about itself" (GA 20 6, :429). A logic that discloses the field of a science must therefore first disclose the being whose possibilities the sciences are. When the analytic reaches its result in §32, it states the method in terms that repeat the definition: phenomenological research is "interpretation of beings toward their being", and its aim is "the concrete foundation for every possible research into being" (§32.2). Productive logic, announced for nature and history, is carried out on Dasein.

The concept runs through Heidegger's work of these years. In the 1923 course *Ontology: The Hermeneutics of Facticity* a note already pairs "the productive logic of the sciences" with the "concrete sojourns and abodes in life" that they develop (GA 63, appendix, van Buren's translation). The course of the following winter, *Logic: The Question of Truth* (1925–26), opens by setting a "philosophizing logic" against school logic. School logic "began *as* a decline". It comes from a time "when philosophy had already lost its character as a productive force", and it keeps "the content of an original philosophical questioning that was alive in Plato and Aristotle" (GA 21 13, Sheehan's translation). The same lecture says a science advances only through "a revision of basic concepts", with Einstein as the example (GA 21 17). That is the crisis of GA 20 § 1 seen from inside. *Sein und Zeit* gives the definition its final form. Research into basic concepts "must run ahead of the positive sciences, and it *can*"; it is "productive logic in the sense that it as it were leaps forward (*vorspringt*) into a determinate region of being" (SZ 10). Then comes the example that shows what the term had meant all along: Kant's transcendental logic is "an a priori logic of the subject-matter of the region of being 'nature'" (*apriorische Sachlogik des Seinsgebietes Natur*, SZ 11). The *Sach-* of *Sachlogik* is the *Sach-* of *Sachfeld*. A productive logic is a logic of the matter, not of thought about the matter. Read this way, Kant's *Critique* becomes the model case, and the Kant interpretations of 1927–29 (GA 25, GA 3) follow from that reading. Between 1925 and 1927 one claim also hardens. GA 20 grants Plato and Aristotle the original logic "only within very narrow limits". In *Sein und Zeit* their work is "proof" (*Beweis*, SZ 10) that such research is possible.

## §2, GA 20 8: the history of the question of being as decline

Reads [§2.3](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), third paragraph under § 2.

The understanding this passage carries is decline (*Verfall*). The history of the question of being is not a neutral sequence of answers. It is a decline, and the decline is not an accident of intellectual history but follows from the way the questioner is. In a single sentence the passage moves the word from the history of a concept to "the being of Dasein". The course then spends thirteen sections earning that move.

> But then the history of the concept of time is the history of the discovery of time and the history of its conceptual interpretation; that is, this history is the history of the question of the being of beings, the history of the attempts to uncover beings in their being, carried by the understanding of time prevailing at each stage, by the stage reached in the conceptual working-out of the phenomenon of time. So the history of the concept of time is, more exactly, in the end *the history of the decline and the history of the mutilation of the ground-question* of scientific research into the being of beings: a history of the incapacity to pose the question of being radically anew and to work it out anew in its first foundations, an incapacity grounded in the being of Dasein.

German:

> Die Geschichte des Zeitbegriffs ist dann aber die Geschichte der Entdeckung der Zeit und die Geschichte ihrer begrifflichen Interpretation; d. h. diese Geschichte ist die Geschichte der Frage nach dem Sein des Seienden, die Geschichte der Versuche, das Seiende in seinem Sein zu entdecken, getragen von dem jeweiligen Verständnis der Zeit, von der jeweiligen Stufe der begrifflichen Ausarbeitung des Zeitphänomens. So ist die Geschichte des Zeitbegriffs genauer am Ende *die Geschichte des Verfalls und die Geschichte der Verstümmelung der Grundfrage* wissenschaftlicher Forschung nach dem Sein des Seienden: Geschichte des Unvermögens, die Seinsfrage radikal neu zu stellen und in ihren ersten Fundamenten neu auszuarbeiten — ein Unvermögen, das im Sein des Daseins gegründet ist.

The sentence works by repeating "the history of" (*die Geschichte*) and changing what follows it. The history of a concept becomes the history of a discovery. Through "that is" (*d. h.*) it becomes the history of the question of being. With "so" (*so*) it becomes the history of a decline. The repetition keeps the subject constant while its content is replaced, and the reader is carried from a scholarly topic, how time has been conceived, to a philosophical thesis about why being has not been understood. Each "is" is an identification rather than an inference. Heidegger says as much in the next sentence, where he calls the account a "wholly external characterization" (*ganz äußerliche Kennzeichnung*, GA 20 8).

Three nouns give "decline" its content. *Verfall* is a falling-away from a height. *Verstümmelung* (mutilation) is damage to a body that goes on living, so the question of being is not lost but survives maimed. *Unvermögen* (incapacity) is the inability to pose the question "anew", which means the question was once posed and cannot now be posed again. Together they describe a question that is still present in the inherited concepts and can no longer function as a question. Kisiel's "distortion" for *Verstümmelung* misses this. A distortion misrepresents something; a mutilation leaves it present and incapable, and incapacity is exactly what the colon goes on to name. The two qualifiers, "more exactly" (*genauer*) and "in the end" (*am Ende*), add that the verdict is read backward from the present. The decline shows only to a questioner who stands at its end.

The final clause carries the weight: the incapacity is "grounded in the being of Dasein". Dasein has not been introduced. It arrives as the questioning being only in §17. The clause promises that the decline will turn out to be a feature of the questioner, not of the texts, and the noun *Verfall* is the vehicle of that promise. In §13f the course comes back to it with the verb. The two neglects of Husserlian phenomenology, of the being of the intentional and of the sense of being, "are not accidental negligences of the philosophers"; in them "the history of our own Dasein reveals itself", and "Dasein, in its way of being of falling (*Verfallen*), which it does not itself escape, first comes to its being precisely in rising up against it" (GA 20 179, :2704). The question "has fallen silent since Aristotle, and so fallen silent that one no longer knows that it has fallen silent" (GA 20 179, :2700). That is the mutilation of § 2 described from inside: the question is handled in inherited concepts and no longer heard as a question. When the Main Part opens, Heidegger recalls his own sentence and hedges it. The history of the attempts to determine beings in their being "would perhaps be" (*sei vielleicht*) the history of the decline (§14, GA 20 191, :2815). The external characterization has served its purpose; what is to replace it is the analysis.

Decline also shapes the plan of the course. The history of the concept of time was to run backward from Bergson through Kant and Newton to Aristotle, and § 3 explains the order. Each station is only a "relative" transformation, because "at bottom the concept of time as Aristotle grasped it is retained" (GA 20 11). A decline is a continuity of the same concept, ever further from its source, so its history has to be read toward the source. The Main Part gives the decline its existential name, falling (§29), and the newly written block on the "fore-" structure states the consequence for historical research. Great historians reach more original conceptions because they carry out "the destruction of what was once uncovered in a certain way, but has fallen again" (§31.7, GA 20 418).

*Sein und Zeit* §6 assembles these pieces into a method. Dasein "is its past" and grows up into a traditional interpretation of itself (SZ 20); ontology "falls prey to the tradition" (*der Tradition verfällt*) that makes its concepts self-evident; and a "destruction" of the traditional content of ancient ontology is required, carried out "on the guiding thread of the question of being" (SZ 22). What GA 20 § 2 calls the history of decline, SZ calls the tradition to be destroyed. The verb in "der Tradition verfällt" is the verb of § 2's noun. The course of the following winter applies the same figure to logic, which "began *as* a decline" (GA 21 13). Between 1925 and 1927 "decline" stops being a judgment on history and becomes the name for how the question of being is handed down. It thereby becomes the reason a destruction is needed at all.

## §2, GA 20 9–10: phenomenological research before the division into historical and systematic

Reads [§2.4](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), fourth paragraph under § 2.

Here "phenomenological" names a sphere of research, not a method set beside history and system. The sphere lies "before" the academic division of philosophy into historical and systematic work. The passage claims that the question of being forces research into this sphere, because the question has a history that belongs to it. To ask the question is already to stand in that history. The passage is phrased as a condition, and the rest of the course is meant to show that the condition holds.

> This opinion remains plausible as long as one believes that a systematic philosophical discussion is possible in a radical sense without being, in its innermost ground, historical. But should that be the case, should it turn out that precisely the ground-question of philosophical research, the question of the being of beings, compels us to press forward into an original sphere of research that lies *before* the traditional division of philosophical work into historical and systematic knowing, then the *prolegomena* to the investigation of beings in their being are to be won only by way of *history*; that is, the manner of research is *neither historical nor systematic, but phenomenological*.

German:

> Diese Meinung bleibt so lange einleuchtend, als man glaubt, eine systematische philosophische Erörterung sei in einem radikalen Sinne möglich, ohne daß sie im innersten Grunde historisch sei. Sollte das aber der Fall sein, sollte es sich ergeben, daß gerade die Grundfrage der philosophischen Forschung, die Frage nach dem Sein des Seienden, dazu zwingt, in eine ursprüngliche Forschungsphäre vorzudringen, die *vor* der traditionellen Aufteilung der philosophischen Arbeit in historisches und systematisches Erkennen liegt, dann sind die *Prolegomena* zur Erforschung des Seienden in seinem Sein nur auf dem Wege über die *Geschichte* zu gewinnen, d. h. die Forschungsart ist *weder historisch noch systematisch, sondern phänomenologisch*.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "zu." at the foot of p. 9 and resumes "gewinnen" on p. 10 (:458). The conditional "Sollte … dann sind … zu gewinnen" needs the continuation, so the full stop is a conversion artifact and is left out of the quotation. The italics fall on pp. 9 and 10, neither of them a text-layer page.

The understanding is carried by one word set against another, and the passage's surface contradiction shows where they part. The prolegomena are to be won "only by way of *history*" (*Geschichte*, italicized), and yet the research is "*neither historical*" (*historisch*). The "that is" (*d. h.*) presents the second clause as a restatement of the first. The way runs through *Geschichte*, history as what has happened, here the history of the question of being itself. What the research is not is *historisch* in the sense the preceding paragraph gave the word: the doxographical "historiography" (*Historie*) of the concept of time, a "collection of opinions" that "remains blind" until what it asks about has been clarified (GA 20 9). One kind of history is the way; the other is a discipline the way does not belong to. The italicized "before" (*vor*) names the place where the two meet: a sphere prior to the division of labour in which historiography and system become separate subjects. "Phenomenological" is the name for that sphere.

§ 1 has already given the vocabulary for this distinction. Heidegger doubted there whether "historical knowledge of historical reality is ever able to see history in its historicity" (*ob die historische Erkenntnis der geschichtlichen Wirklichkeit die Geschichte in ihrer Geschichtlichkeit je zu sehen vermag*, GA 20 2, :361). That sentence holds *historisch* and *geschichtlich* apart and names *Geschichtlichkeit* (historicity). Measured against it, the first sentence of the present passage is loose. It says a systematic discussion must be "in its innermost ground historical" (*historisch*), where § 1's usage would require *geschichtlich*. The looseness is local. The passage relies on the distinction § 1 drew, and its own claim depends on keeping the two apart. Kisiel renders *historisch* throughout as "historiological", which is right for the second and third occurrences. In the first, it makes Heidegger say that systematic philosophy must be historiographical at its innermost ground, which is not his point.

The syntax keeps the claim in suspense. "Should that be the case, should it turn out that…" (*Sollte … sollte es sich ergeben*) governs everything up to the colon, so the phenomenological character of the research depends on what the question of being will "compel" (*zwingt*). The verb repeats the pressing forward (*vordringen*) of § 1. In § 1 the sciences pressed forward toward their subject-fields; here the question of being presses philosophy forward into its own. The next paragraph makes it an aim of the course to show the necessity "from the thematic content of the matters to be investigated and not from some arbitrarily invented idea of philosophy or a so-called philosophical standpoint" (GA 20 10). It closes by calling the separation of the historical clarification from the analysis of the phenomenon of time "only didactic" (*nur didaktisch getrennt*, :462).

The course redeems the conditional in its own construction. The Preparatory Part is a history, of phenomenology's breakthrough from Brentano to Husserl, and at the same time a systematic argument. The editor's afterword warns against reading it as "a merely historical sketch" (GA 20 445, :5210). The immanent critique of §§10–13 is the turning point. Phenomenology fails to ask about the being of its own field because the tradition, Descartes and the definition of the human as *animal rationale*, decides that being in advance. A systematic question, what consciousness is, turns out to be answerable only through a history, how the question of being came to be decided without being asked. §13f names the history it means: the neglects reveal "the history of our Dasein itself", history understood "not as the totality of public events" but as "the manner of happening of Dasein itself" (*die Geschehensart des Daseins selbst*, GA 20 179, :2704). This is *Geschichte* in the sense the passage needs. §9 adds the formal reason. "Phenomenology" is a "methodological" title that names the *how* of research, not its subject-matter (§9.5), so it can name a manner of research prior to the split between historical and systematic subjects. The fore-structure described in §31d supplies the hermeneutic reason: every interpretation, historical interpretation included, stands in a fore-having and fore-sight it must make its own, so historiography is never finished, and the history that philosophy goes through is its own way of being (§31.6–7).

*Sein und Zeit* states the distinction the passage depends on and draws the methodological consequence. Historicity (*Geschichtlichkeit*) "means the constitution of being of the 'happening' of Dasein as such", and historiography (*Historie*) is possible "only because" Dasein is "determined by historicity in the ground of its being" (SZ 20). Since the question of being is itself "characterized by historicity", working it out requires the "destruction" of the tradition (SZ 22). The "manner of research" that GA 20 calls "neither historical nor systematic" becomes, in 1927, the joining of the existential analytic to a destruction of the history of ontology, the two halves of the plan of *Sein und Zeit*. Kisiel's note at this point in his translation makes the same connection, from the GA 20 distinction to that plan.

## §4, GA 20 26: Brentano's intentional in-existence as in-dwelling

Reads [§4.11](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), eleventh paragraph under § 4.

Heidegger first presents intentionality as Brentano's "intentional in-existence", and he hears in it in-dwelling (*Innewohnen*): something objective dwells within the psychic experience. Intentionality, on this account, is a relation inside the psyche, the mark that sets psychic phenomena apart from physical ones. The course takes over the structure and spends the rest of its length turning it round, until the dwelling is no longer the object's in the psyche but Dasein's in the world.

> So the question arises: what is the nature of psychic phenomena as against physical ones? Brentano poses this question in the first book of the *Psychology*. He says that psychic phenomena are distinguished from all physical ones by nothing so much as by this: that something objective dwells within them. Should there, therefore, be differences within the field of psychic phenomena, they must be differences with respect to this basic structure of dwelling-within, differences in the way in which something is objective to the experiences. These differences, in which something is objective to the various experiences (in representing the represented, in judging the judged, in willing the willed), form, according to these differences, the chief class-differences within psychic phenomena. This basic structure of the psychic, that something objective dwells within every experience, Brentano designates as *intentional in-existence*.
>
> *Intentio* is an expression of scholasticism and means as much as: *directing oneself toward*.

German:

> So entsteht die Frage: Welches ist die Natur der psychischen Phänomene gegenüber den physischen? Diese Frage stellt *Brentano* im ersten Buch der Psychologie. Er sagt, die psychischen Phänomene unterscheiden sich von allen physischen durch nichts so sehr als dadurch, daß ihnen etwas Gegenständliches innewohnt. Sollte es deshalb Unterschiede innerhalb des Feldes der psychischen Phänomene geben, so müssen es Unterschiede sein hinsichtlich dieser ihrer Grundstruktur des Innewohnens, Unterschiede in der Weise, in welcher den Erlebnissen etwas gegenständlich ist. Diese Unterschiede, in denen den verschiedenen Erlebnissen etwas gegenständlich ist, im Vorstellen das Vorgestellte, im Urteilen das Geurteilte, im Wollen das Gewoll, je nach diesen Unterschieden bilden sich die vorzüglichsten Klassenunterschiede innerhalb der psychischen Phänome. Diese Grundstruktur des Psychischen, daß jedem Erleben etwas Gegenständliches innewohnt, bezeichnet *Brentano* als *intentionale Inexistenz*.
>
> *Intentio* ist ein Ausdruck der Scholastik und besagt soviel wie: *sich richten auf*.

*Note on the text.* This is p. 26, which audits as partial, and the file drops letters here: "Gewoll" for *Gewollte* and "Phänome" for *Phänomene*. They are quoted as the file has them and do not bear on the reading. The next page, p. 27, comes from the text layer; the italics above fall on p. 26 and are not affected.

The paraphrase builds the understanding by translation. Brentano's Latinate term, "intentional in-existence", appears only in the last sentence. Before it, Heidegger has said the same thing three times in plain German: something objective "dwells within" (*innewohnt*); the basic structure is "dwelling-within" (*Innewohnen*); something objective dwells within "every experience". Whether *innewohnen* is Brentano's own word cannot be checked, because the *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* is not in the corpus. As Heidegger presents it, the technical term arrives already interpreted. "In-existence" means that the object is *in* the experience, and the "in" is that of dwelling.

What dwells within is "something objective" (*etwas Gegenständliches*), a nominalized adjective, not "an object". The phrasing leaves open whether what is in the experience is the thing itself or the thing as meant, and the ambiguity is Brentano's. In §5 Heidegger says that Brentano "wavered" over his "intentional object", between the being itself and the how of its being-grasped (§5.10). The grammar of the passage already holds the place of that wavering. The argument then turns on a "therefore" (*deshalb*). Because in-dwelling divides the psychic from the physical, any differences *within* the psychic "must" be differences in the manner of in-dwelling. Nothing makes that necessary. The step follows only from Brentano's maxim, stated just before, that a classification must take its principle from the "nature" of the matter. Heidegger glosses "nature" here as "what a thing is, seen from itself" (*von ihm selbst her*, GA 20 26), which is the formula §9 will use for the phenomenon. So the maxim is read as phenomenological before the word has been explained. In-dwelling thus becomes a single structure that both marks the psychic off and articulates it from within, and the three classes, representation, judgment and interest, are its modes.

The context shows what Heidegger values in this and what he will refuse. Before the passage, Brentano is the one who freed psychology from natural-scientific method: "'empirical' means here not inductive in the sense of the natural sciences, but as much as factual, non-constructive" (GA 20 25, :629). After it, Brentano's founding thesis follows, that every psychic phenomenon is a representation or rests on one (§4.11), and then the line to Husserl. In §5 the structure seen here is called a phenomenological *discovery*, because "crude acquaintance" with it differs from understanding its sense (§5.1). Brentano had the in-dwelling. What he lacked, according to §5.10, is the *intentum*, the being in the how of its being-intended, and above all any question about what intentionality is the structure *of*. He identified it with the psychic, which "he took in the traditional Cartesian sense". The plain gloss on *intentio*, "an expression of scholasticism", answers in advance the charge §5 rejects: that phenomenology inherited a "dark, metaphysical, scholastic concept" (§5.1). Heidegger grants the provenance and reduces it to a word-meaning, "directing oneself toward", which §5 repeats as "literally" (*dem Wortsinne nach*, GA 20 37).

In-dwelling returns at the centre of the analytic with its direction reversed. In §19 Heidegger derives "in" from *innan*, "to dwell, *habitare*", and "I am" from "alongside" (*bei*). In-being means dwelling-alongside the world as what is familiar, and it is "nothing spatial" (GA 20 213, §19.3). In Brentano the object dwells in the experience. In the analytic, Dasein dwells in the world. The figure survives the critique; what changes is who dwells and where. The newly written close of §31 completes the reversal: seen from care, "what was grasped as intentionality in phenomenology … is fragmentary, a phenomenon seen only from outside". The bare directing-toward must be set back into care's structure of being-ahead-of-itself-in-being-alongside (§31.9, GA 20 420). The passage's in-dwelling and §19's dwelling-alongside meet in that structure.

Heidegger's later summary confirms the arc and names its stake. In 1928 he writes that for Brentano intentionality "remains an internal affair of psychology and gains no fundamental significance". Husserl widened it to the essence of consciousness and reason, but "the insight into intentionality does not go so far as to see at the same time that grasping this structure as an essential structure of Dasein must revolutionize the whole concept of the human" (GA 26 167). That revolution is what GA 20 carries out between § 4 and § 31, and Brentano's in-dwelling is where it starts.

## §4, GA 20 31: a work's self-interpretation and what it in fact achieves

Reads [§4.13](../commentaries/heidegger/heidegger-ga-20-prolegomena-zur-geschichte-des-zeitbegriffs-commentary.md), thirteenth paragraph under § 4.

The passage rests on one understanding: a work's self-interpretation (*Selbstauslegung*) can fall short of what the work in fact achieves, and the interpreter must read the achievement, not the self-interpretation. Heidegger states this as a verdict on one sentence of Husserl's. It is also the rule by which the whole course reads phenomenology, and by which Heidegger will later read Kant.

> To blame for this misunderstanding is, in a certain way, the self-interpretation that Husserl for his part gave of his work in the introduction to this volume: "Phenomenology is descriptive psychology." This self-explication of his own work is wholly inappropriate to what is worked out in it. In other words, when Husserl wrote the introduction to these investigations, he was not able properly to survey what he had in fact laid down in this volume. Only two years later did he himself set this misinterpretation right, in the *Archiv für systematische Philosophie*, 1903.

German:

> Schuld an diesem Mißverständnis ist in gewisser Weise die Selbstinterpretation, die *Husserl* seinerseits in der Einleitung zu diesem Bande von seiner Arbeit gegeben hat: »Phänomenologie ist deskriptive Psychologie«. Diese Selbstauslegung des eigenen Werks ist dem, was darin erarbeitet wird, ganz unangemessen. Mit anderen Worten, *Husserl* war, als er die Einleitung zu diesen Untersuchungen schrieb, nicht imstande, eigentlich das zu übersehen, was er faktisch in diesem Bande vorgelegt hatte. Erst zwei Jahre später hat er diese Fehlinterpretation selbst richtig gestellt, im »Archiv für systematische Philosophie«, 1903.

The vocabulary divides into two columns. On one side is what Husserl said about his work: a "self-interpretation" (*Selbstinterpretation*), then a "self-explication" (*Selbstauslegung*), finally a "misinterpretation" (*Fehlinterpretation*). On the other is what he did: "what is worked out in it" (*was darin erarbeitet wird*) and what he "in fact laid down" (*faktisch … vorgelegt*). The verdict measures the first against the second, and the measure is fitness: the self-interpretation is "wholly inappropriate" (*ganz unangemessen*) to the work. The pivot of the explanation is *übersehen*. In this sentence it means "to survey": Husserl was "not able properly to survey" what he had done. The word also means "to overlook". Heidegger does not play on it here, but the structure he describes is overlooking. A researcher who cannot survey the content of his own research has, in that respect, overlooked it.

Two qualifiers keep the verdict from being a dismissal. The self-interpretation is to blame only "in a certain way" (*in gewisser Weise*), for the readers share the blame: just before, the Munich circle had taken the *Investigations* for "improved descriptive psychology", and Natorp had read the second volume as a relapse into psychologism (GA 20 30–31). And Husserl corrected the formula "himself", two years later. The self-interpretation is therefore not the author's last word, and the gap between saying and doing can close. The paragraph that follows turns the verdict into a rule of reading. The *Investigations* demand "step by step, explicitly intuitive presentation and controlling exhibition" of their matters; one cannot "simply take out results" without perverting the sense of the investigations (GA 20 32). A work of this kind is understood by repeating what it does, and that is why Heidegger declines to summarize it and works out its discoveries instead (§4.13). The verdict on the formula and the refusal to summarize express the same understanding.

The rule then governs the whole Preparatory Part, and the course states it in the vocabulary of this passage. §8b is headed "The self-understanding of phenomenology" (*Das Selbstverständnis der Phänomenologie*, GA 20 109). It rejects once more the reading of phenomenology as "descriptive psychology" (:1679). It holds that the definition just won must be understood from "what lies in it as task", not from what is said about it, just as one would not interpret physics from astrology (§8.3). The immanent critique applies the same measure to Husserl's later work. Phenomenology, in determining its own field, is "*unphenomenological*! — that is, *supposedly* phenomenological!" (*vermeintlich*, GA 20 178, :2677). Here the self-interpretation is no longer a sentence in an introduction but the self-understanding of the research as such, and the gap between saying and doing has become the gap between "phenomenological" and "supposedly phenomenological". The 1903 correction did not close that gap. It changed the label and left in place the traditional determination of the being of consciousness that §§10–13 expose.

In the Main Part, interpretation (*Auslegung*) becomes a structure of Dasein (§28c), and the newly written §31d gives the rule of this passage its ground. Every interpretation stands in a fore-having, fore-sight and fore-conception, and must ask "whether what it has taken … was explicitly appropriated or only picked up by chance" (§31.6). A self-interpretation can fall short of its work because interpreting oneself is itself an interpretation, with a fore-structure that may have been taken over from the tradition and never made one's own. Husserl's formula is a case: he interpreted his discovery through the inherited horizon of "the psychic".

Heidegger later states the rule as a principle of his own interpretations, and he borrows it from Kant. The Kant book of 1929 quotes Kant's complaint about historians who, "over the word-research into what [earlier philosophers] have said", cannot see "what they wanted to say". Heidegger then adds: "in order to wrest from what the words say what they want to say, every interpretation must necessarily use violence" (GA 3 202). The 1925 passage is the mild form of the same principle, applied to a living author who had corrected himself. In 1929 it has become a declared method. The violence is the hard form of the claim that a work's self-interpretation does not have the last word on its achievement.

Heidegger gives the formula in guillemets as a quotation but cites no page, and he names the 1903 correction only by journal and year. The corpus holds the *Logical Investigations* in German only for volume I (Hua XVIII). The English volumes translate the second edition of 1913, in which Husserl revised the passages on the nature of phenomenology, so they do not show the first-edition wording. Heidegger's report therefore cannot be checked against Husserl here, and nothing in the reading depends on its exact wording.

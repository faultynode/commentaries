# Jean-Luc Marion, *Réduction et donation*, read against Heidegger, *Sein und Zeit*: a comparative commentary

## Foreword

Marion (R) is cited by division, section, paragraph and note (R I.7 ¶1, R II n. 50), since the edition used has no page numbers. Heidegger's *Sein und Zeit* (P) is cited by section and by Niemeyer page (SZ 38). R cites the tenth Niemeyer edition (1963) by page and line, and its pages are those of the corpus text, the unchanged eleventh edition of 1967. R's French for SZ is Emmanuel Martineau's privately printed translation of 1985, which the corpus holds, so every French rendering in R can be checked against the translation R names. Departures from Martineau are counted as Marion's own. Five traps govern this pair. First, *Sein und Zeit* never uses the word "reduction": Heidegger's name for leading the gaze back from entities to being as a "phenomenological reduction" belongs to the summer course of 1927 (GA 24 §5), which ties into Husserl's term "in wording, not in substance". Second, SZ treats givenness as a trap. It warns against starting from "an initially given I and subject" (§10, SZ 46) and names the phenomenon by self-showing, not by givenness (§7, SZ 28). Third, Marion reads SZ through the lecture courses published between 1975 and 1983 and through the Zähringen seminar of 1973; a doctrine of a course is SZ's only where SZ states it. Fourth, the marginal notes from Heidegger's own copy, which carry much of chapter IV, are cited from GA 2, are later than 1927, and cannot be checked in this corpus. Fifth, SZ's "ontological difference" (§12, SZ 56) separates Dasein's kind of being from that of other entities, and is not the difference between being and entities. Marion's chapter IV joins the two under one name. "Presence" needs one more caution: SZ's *Anwesenheit* is its reading of ancient ontology's οὐσία from the present (§6, SZ 25), not a name for metaphysics as a whole.

The Foreword's single sentence puts givenness "at the centre of the reduction, and therefore of phenomenology", and aims at what, "without being it, comes after the subject" (R Foreword ¶1). Neither term is SZ's. Its method has no reduction at its centre, and its successor to the subject is defined by being. SZ itself is the most rigorous attempt in R's field to say what comes after the subject: §10 sets aside "subject, soul, consciousness, spirit, person" because every idea of a subject carries the ontology of the *subjectum* along with it (SZ 46), and it names Dasein by the fact that "in its being, this being is at issue for it" (§4, SZ 12). Marion's formula has two senses, "without being the subject" and "without being at all". The first is SZ's own programme. The second is aimed at SZ: what comes after the subject is not to be determined by its relation to being. The Foreword thus announces, as a programme rather than as exegesis, a contestation of SZ's answer to the very question SZ was the first to pose in these terms. The heading of the last section of chapter VI, "'There' outside being", states it without the first sense.

The acknowledgements thank Courtine and Franck for a practice of phenomenology "older and better" than Marion's own, the Leuven Husserl Archive, and Michel Henry for his friendship and "example of philosophical probity" (R Foreword ¶2). For this pair the names matter because two of them are readers of SZ in the corpus. Franck's *Heidegger and the Problem of Space* has an inheritance record pairing it with SZ, and Henry's *L'essence de la manifestation* reads SZ through its whole length (runs/henry-on-sz.md). Neither debt is specified. Whether Marion's SZ owes anything to them, in particular the charge that Dasein remains bound to the I (chapter III) and the charge that SZ subordinates appearing to being (chapters IV and VI), is to be watched where those chapters make the charges. The date, 24 February 1989, falls after the publication of GA 24 (1975), GA 20 (1979) and GA 2 with its marginal notes. Marion's SZ is the SZ of a reader who has the courses and the notes, and he writes, as he says in the next division, from "the recent publication" of the courses.

## Bibliographical note

Husserl is cited from the Husserliana, except for the *Logical Investigations*, cited from the second edition of 1913 (R Bibliographical note ¶1). The exception puts Marion's LU on the same text as SZ's. Heidegger cites "Log. Unters. 2. A." (second edition) in §44 (SZ 218 n.) and in his notes on sign and signification (SZ 77 n., 166 n.). When chapter I sets the breakthrough of 1900–1901 against Heidegger's reading of it, the text under both readings is the same edition. The companion run treats the note's consequences for Husserl (runs/marion-rd-on-hua3.md).

Heidegger is cited from the Gesamtausgabe, with one exception: SZ, cited from its tenth edition (Niemeyer, 1963), "page and lines", and quoted in Martineau's translation, "modifying it where needed" (R Bibliographical note ¶2). The exception has three consequences for this run. First, Marion's page numbers resolve directly in the corpus text, since the ninth edition (1960) fixed the pagination and the tenth and eleventh reprint it unchanged. Second, the exception is incomplete. When Marion quotes Heidegger's notes from his own copy he cites GA 2 ("GA, 2, p. 47", R II n. 50; "p. 275", chapter IV). These notes exist only in that edition. The note keeps SZ apart from its marginal notes in the citations, and chapter IV joins them in the argument. A reader who checks only the Niemeyer text will not find the words on which chapter IV most depends. Third, Martineau's translation, begun in July 1984 and printed in 1985 for the translator's friends, is the French in which the book argues: Dasein left untranslated, "under-the-hand" (*sous-la-main*) for present-at-hand (*vorhanden*), "being-at-the-world" (*être-au-monde*) for being-in-the-world, and "the questioned", "the interrogated", "the asked-for" (*le questionné*, *l'interrogé*, *le demandé*) for the three terms of §2. Since the corpus holds this translation, R's quotations of SZ can be read against the French Marion names as well as against the German. Where Marion's French departs from Martineau, the departure is his own.

The note lists the first versions: chapter I in *Philosophie* (1984); chapter III as "L'*ego* et le *Dasein*. Heidegger et la 'destruction' de Descartes dans *Sein und Zeit*" (*Revue de Métaphysique et de Morale*, 1987); chapter IV as "Différence ontologique ou question de l'être. Un indécidé de *Sein und Zeit*" (*Tijdschrift voor Filosofie*, 1987) (R Bibliographical note ¶3). The two chapters that engage SZ most densely were written as articles on SZ in the same year. The subtitle of the second gives the verdict before the chapter argues it: on ontological difference, SZ is "undecided". The book's reading of SZ thus took shape in 1987, after GA 24 and GA 2 were available.

## Phenomenology as Such

The epigraph is Heidegger's, from the course of 1919/20 (GA 58, 5): what does "given", "givenness" mean, "this magic word of phenomenology and the stumbling block for the others"? The course is not in the corpus. Against SZ the epigraph is a selection. It takes from Heidegger the question SZ does not pursue. In 1919/20 givenness is a word to be questioned. In SZ it is a word to be avoided: the existential analytic would "walk into a trap" if it began from "the givenness of the I" (§25, SZ 115), and the evidence of "an immediate givenness of experiences, of the I and of consciousness" falls short of the certainty that belongs to anticipation (§53, SZ 265). SZ defines the phenomenon by showing, "that which shows itself in itself" (§7, SZ 28), not by giving. Marion's Heidegger therefore begins before SZ, and the book will argue that SZ let fall the question its author had raised. That argument is announced here by the choice of a pre-SZ text to stand over a book about the reduction.

Phenomenology takes over the role of philosophy after Nietzsche has completed the possibilities of metaphysics, and undertakes "a new beginning" (R Phenomenology as such ¶1). The frame is Heidegger's, but not that of SZ. SZ begins with a retrieval of a question that "has today been forgotten" (§1, SZ 2). It names its task the destruction of the history of ontology, not the end of metaphysics (§6, SZ 19), and its Nietzsche is the author of the second *Untimely Meditation* on history (§76, SZ 396–397) and of a remark about growing "too old for one's victories" (§53, SZ 264). Metaphysics completed in Nietzsche and a new beginning after it belong to the Nietzsche lectures and the texts of the 1930s. This is re-inscription: SZ's retrieval of a forgotten question is placed in a history of being that SZ does not yet tell.

The question that joins Nietzsche's end and Husserl's beginning is whether the conditions of presence can be stretched until every being reaches presence, "without any condition or reserve" (R Phenomenology as such ¶2). Here "presence" first enters the book, and the word covers two things SZ keeps apart. SZ's *Anwesenheit* is a temporal determination: ancient ontology understands being as παρουσία or οὐσία, which "means, ontologically and temporally, presence", so that entities are grasped "with regard to a definite mode of time, the present" (§6, SZ 25). (The corpus text garbles the Greek at this point.) Marion's "presence" is the condition under which a thing is given, a matter of limits and reserves. SZ's remedy for its *Anwesenheit* is time as the horizon of being. Marion's remedy for his presence will be an unconditioned givenness. On SZ's terms, a presence without condition or reserve would be the extreme of the privilege of the present that §6 sets out to destroy, not an escape from it. Marion places phenomenology on SZ's line of diagnosis and does not follow SZ's line of cure.

The breakthrough of 1900–1901 extends presence beyond all limits and thereby reproduces presence's metaphysical determination, objectivity (R Phenomenology as such ¶3). SZ's own name for what the modern ideal of knowledge privileges is presence-at-hand (*Vorhandenheit*), and its account of objectivity is not a charge against phenomenology. "Thematization objectifies" (§69b, SZ 363): science frees entities so that they can be questioned and determined "objectively", and this is a mode of being-in-the-world, with its own right. Knowing the world is a founded mode of being-in (§13). The charge that a phenomenology "consecrates" objectivity is GA 20's against Husserl (§§10–13 of that course), not SZ's. In SZ the objectivity of the object is derivative but not illegitimate. Marion takes the charge from the courses and gives it the force of a verdict on phenomenology as such, which SZ does not.

"The objective of phenomenology does not coincide with objectivity: so runs Heidegger's starting point", and the courses published "recently" establish that being became the stake of phenomenology for Heidegger first in a critique of Husserl's ideal of objectification (R Phenomenology as such ¶4). Marion says where his evidence lies, and it is not SZ. SZ names Husserl rarely and almost always to credit him (SZ 38 and n.; the notes on sign, signification and truth). It criticizes him by name once, in §10, where Husserl's and Scheler's interpretations of the person "no longer ask the question of the being of the person" (SZ 47 and n.). The sustained immanent critique of Husserl is GA 20 §§10–13. This is mediation, stated by R itself: the Heidegger who starts from a critique of objectivity is the Heidegger of the Marburg courses. The paragraph's second question, whether what performs "the re(con)duction" (reduction as leading back) acts "as transcendental I or as Dasein", puts to SZ a term SZ does not use. SZ's Dasein is not the agent of a reduction. It is the entity interrogated (§2) and the place where being is understood (§4). The alternative "I or Dasein" fixes the book's comparison on one axis, the agent of the method, and SZ refuses that axis when it calls the starting point of a given I and subject a fundamental miss (SZ 46).

Heidegger failed in what he attempted: Dasein "remains haunted by the I", the "phenomenon of being", even in the weakened figure of ontological difference, is never exposed, and the "phenomenology of the inapparent" stays a contradictory programme, as Heidegger "was the first to concede, as early as 1927" (R Phenomenology as such ¶5, n. 3: SZ §83, 437, 37–41). Three texts are joined in one verdict. The phenomenology of the inapparent is the Zähringen formula of 1973. The phenomenon of being is SZ's: in the phenomenological sense the phenomenon is what "for the most part precisely does *not* show itself", what is "hidden" yet belongs to what shows itself as its sense and ground, and this is "the *being* of entities" (§7, SZ 35). The concession of 1927 is not at R's locus. SZ 437, lines 37–41, ends the book with questions: "Does a way lead from original time to the sense of being? Does time itself reveal itself as the horizon of being?" They are the questions Division Three was to answer, and the paragraph before them calls the whole book a preparation for a strife "not yet even kindled" (SZ 437). The concession comes in the Preliminary Remark of the later editions, which the corpus text prints, written, by its own account, a quarter of a century after 1927: the second half "can no longer be added without the first being presented anew". Marion reads the questions of 1927 with the later verdict. That is a retroactive reading, and it makes SZ concede what it only asked. The charge that Dasein is haunted by the I is asserted here and argued in chapter III. It will have to meet §10 and §25, where SZ itself makes the given I the trap.

The method of the book: since "in phenomenology, as against metaphysics, possibility surpasses actuality in truth", the principle must be pushed to its end, even against "phenomenology already carried out", because a genuine thought is surpassed by being repeated, "and then even failure succeeds" (R Phenomenology as such ¶6, n. 4: SZ §7, 38, 29–31). Martineau has "higher than actuality stands *possibility*" (Plus haut que l'effectivité se tient la *possibilité*), and Marion's "possibility surpasses actuality in truth" (la possibilité surpasse en vérité l'effectivité) is his own. He adds "as against metaphysics" and "in truth". The context of the sentence matters more than its wording. It closes the paragraph in which Heidegger says that the investigations "became possible only on the ground laid by E. Husserl, with whose *Logical Investigations* phenomenology achieved its breakthrough", and the actuality it subordinates is phenomenology's being actual "as a philosophical 'school'" (SZ 38). Heidegger's sentence frees phenomenology from its school in order to keep its method. Marion's frees it from every phenomenology already carried out, Heidegger's included. A tribute to Husserl becomes a licence to repeat Heidegger against himself. The repetition Marion invokes also has an SZ form he does not cite. In §74 repetition "replies" to the possibility of existence that has been, and the reply is at once a "revocation" of what works in the present as "past" (SZ 386). Marion's "surpass by repeating, even by borrowing the means to think with it beyond it" is close to that structure: a reply to a possibility, not a refutation of a position. SZ supplies the method by which R will be measured against SZ.

## I. The Breakthrough and the Enlargement

### 1 - Two Interpretations and an Enlargement

Of the two readings strong enough to place the breakthrough of the *Logical Investigations* in the history of metaphysics, one is Heidegger's: the Sixth Investigation reaches the categorial intuition of being and opens onto a givenness of being, and Marion quotes Zähringen (1973) for it, "through this *tour de force* … I finally had a ground" (R I.1 ¶1, n. 4). From this follows "the direct filiation, which Heidegger never formulates explicitly but never ceases to suggest", between the *Investigations* and SZ. SZ formulates it explicitly, in the sentence before the one Marion's method paragraph quotes: the investigations of SZ "became possible only on the ground (*Boden*) laid by E. Husserl, with whose *Logical Investigations* phenomenology achieved its breakthrough (*Durchbruch*)" (SZ 38), which Martineau renders as the *Investigations* having "assured the breakthrough of phenomenology" (ont assuré la percée de la phénoménologie), with R's own word for it. The two words on which chapter I is built, ground and breakthrough, are SZ's own words for its debt to the LU. What SZ does not say is that the debt runs through categorial intuition. That specification is Zähringen's, and Marion's "never explicitly" holds only of it. The topology Marion builds next, in which the breakthrough anticipates the destruction of ontology, is his construction: SZ's §6 destroys the ontology of Kant, Descartes and Aristotle and does not count Husserl in that history.

Read from the Sixth Investigation, the LU make it possible to pass to ontological difference "by accomplishing the 'destruction of the history of ontology'". Read from the First, they lead to *différance* (R I.1 ¶2). The quoted phrase is the title of SZ §6 (SZ 19). The link Marion draws between destruction and ontological difference is not in §6, which neither uses the phrase "ontological difference" nor names Husserl. §6 ties the destruction to time: ancient ontology understood being from the present, and the destruction is to expose that temporal determination (SZ 25–26). Marion's sentence makes the destruction a passage to ontological difference, which is chapter IV's thesis, given here in advance as though it were Heidegger's. On this point Marion's Heidegger is the Heidegger of the essay of 1929 and the course of 1927, both of which he will cite in chapter IV.

The return to "the things themselves" leads concepts back to intuition, and the 1913 draft preface calls the LU method "radically intuitive" in an enlarged sense (R I.1 ¶3). The maxim is SZ's too: "To the things themselves!" stands at the head of the preliminary concept of phenomenology, against free-floating constructions and borrowed concepts (§7, SZ 27–28). SZ keeps the maxim and drops the intuitionism. "'Intuition' and 'thinking' are both already remote derivatives of understanding. Even the phenomenological 'seeing of essences' is grounded in existential understanding" (§31, SZ 147). This sentence is the decisive SZ locus for chapter I, and R does not cite it. It is where SZ departs from the breakthrough Marion has just defined: the return to the things themselves no longer leads back to intuition, because intuition is itself led back to understanding. What R I.7 will present as Heidegger's preference for givenness over categorial intuition, SZ states as the derivation of all intuition from understanding.

The principle of all principles of 1913 does not redirect phenomenology, Marion argues: the draft preface says the LU "radically profess" it, and the LU anticipate it in the Fifth Investigation and in the principle of presuppositionlessness (R I.1 ¶4). SZ does not cite the principle. Its view of it follows from SZ 147: intuition as the source of right is precisely what SZ ranks as derivative, and "about this kind of seeing" SZ defers judgement until "the explicit concepts of being and the structure of being have been won". Marion's unit is Husserl's, and its comparative weight lies in what it prepares. It makes intuition the one principle of the LU, so that any thinker who demotes intuition seems to break with the breakthrough itself. SZ has demoted it, and still claims the LU as its ground (SZ 38).

Enlarged intuition is the condition of the breakthrough. The Sixth Investigation carries out the enlargement in §45, and its order of exposition is a "zigzag" (R I.1 ¶5). SZ's one reference to that part of the Sixth Investigation takes something else from it. In the note to §44 Heidegger refers, "for the idea of verification as 'identification'", to the Sixth Investigation, to "Evidence and Truth" (§§36–39), and, in passing, to the section "On sensuous and categorial intuitions" as the source of Lask's *Logic of Philosophy* (SZ 218 n.). What SZ selects from LU VI is truth as identification and confirmation, not intuition's enlargement. The two readers of the same edition choose different chapters from it. Marion's breakthrough is the enlargement of intuition (§45). SZ's debt is to the doctrine of evidence and truth (§§36–39), which SZ rereads as uncovering.

### 2 - The Domains of Intuition

Enlarged intuition gives "something objective which is, without being in the manner of the real", and "objectness" (*Gegenständlichkeit*) replaces "object" to cover states of affairs and categorial forms (R I.2 ¶1). SZ has a counterpart, but not in the vocabulary of objects. Its widening goes beyond reality, not beyond the real object. "Reality has no priority among the modes of being of innerworldly entities", and it cannot characterize world or Dasein (§43c, SZ 211). Where Husserl, on Marion's reading, widens the field of what can be an object, SZ widens the field of modes of being, and treats the object as one mode among them. The difference is the one R's chapter II will make central: a widening of objectness against a widening of being. Here it is present only by presupposition.

Against Kant's restriction of intuition to the senses, Marion finds three degrees of the categorial: sensuous intuition fulfilling a form, ideation, and categorial intuition proper. He glosses ideation with Heidegger's 1925 example: "I see first, universally, this: here is a house" (R I.2 ¶2, n. 37 / GA 20, 90). The quotation checks against GA 20: acts of ideation "give" the idea, εἶδος, "the look of something" (*Aussehen von etwas*). This is mediation, and SZ revises the example it draws on. In SZ, plain seeing of the things nearest to hand "carries the structure of interpretation in itself so originally" that a grasp of something free of the "as" requires a reorientation (§32, SZ 149). The "as" of the house is the hermeneutic "as" of understanding, articulated before any assertion, not the gift of an act of ideation. Marion's "I see *the* house, *as* house, before (and in order to) see *a* house" is GA 20's categorial reading. SZ has already moved the same seeing from intuition to interpretation. A reader who took R's gloss for SZ's doctrine would attribute to SZ the categorial intuition that SZ 147 derives from understanding.

Categorial intuition proper answers to the "is" of the statement, and "intuition results from givenness without exception" (R I.2 ¶3). SZ's treatment of the "is" goes the other way. The ontological sense of the "is", "which an external theory of the proposition and judgement has deformed into a 'copula'", is to be delimited from the temporality of discourse (§68, SZ 349), and §33 makes the apophantic "as" of assertion a derivative of the hermeneutic "as". Where Marion has the "is" given to an intuition that follows givenness, SZ has it disclosed in understanding and deformed by the theory of judgement. Neither is cited by R, but R's chapter IV will need SZ's "is" when it reads being's "es gibt" in §44, and the unit fixes, before that, a reading of the "is" as given that SZ does not share.

The Kantian limit is transgressed in several ways "for a single motive: the phenomenological requirement that every phenomenon be given in presence, without remainder or reserve" (R I.2 ¶4). SZ's Kant is another Kant. In §6 Kant is the one thinker who went "a stretch of the way" toward the dimension of temporality, before the schematism failed for want of an ontology of the subject (SZ 23–24). Marion's Kant limits intuition, and the phenomenologist exceeds him by giving more. SZ's Kant limits nothing of that kind; he comes nearest to time and stops short. The unit is independent of SZ, and it sets up a measure, presence without remainder, by which SZ will later be judged without having adopted it.

### 3 - The Accomplishment of Presence

If metaphysics is the uninterrogated primacy of bringing entities into presence, and if intuition performs that bringing, metaphysics is never so accomplished as in the LU (R I.3 ¶1). The definition of metaphysics by presence is not SZ's. SZ does not use "metaphysics" for the whole of philosophy. It uses "ontology", and it determines ancient ontology, not metaphysics as such, by *Anwesenheit* (§6). The identification of metaphysics with the primacy of presence is later Heidegger, and the phrase "metaphysics of presence" is Derrida's (R I.4 ¶1 says as much). Marion's conditional premise, "if metaphysics is …", rests on a history that SZ only begins. The unit's verdict on the LU therefore rests on an extension of SZ §6 beyond what §6 claims.

Nietzsche's fragment "How I recognize my own kind" and Husserl's "secret longing" of all modern philosophy (Ideen I §62) are made to say the same thing (R I.3 ¶2). There is no SZ locus. SZ's Nietzsche, in §76, is the author of the three kinds of history, monumental, antiquarian and critical, read as a sign of historicity (SZ 396–397). The pairing of Nietzsche with the end of metaphysics is the Heidegger of the 1930s and 1940s. The Husserl side is treated in the companion run.

The answer to the first question of I.1 stands: the breakthrough accomplishes the metaphysics of presence, like Nietzsche's great Amen, by bringing all beings into presence (R I.3 ¶3). For this pair the conclusion matters through what it implies about Heidegger. If the LU complete metaphysics, then Heidegger's claim to stand on their ground (SZ 38) makes SZ the heir of a completed metaphysics or makes its breakthrough a misreading of the LU. R will choose neither and look for a third reading, a breakthrough of givenness, in I.6–I.7. SZ's own description of its debt, a ground and a breakthrough but no doctrine of presence, leaves room for that third reading. It does not supply it.

### 4 - A Misconstrual of Signification?

Determining metaphysics by the primacy of presence and identifying metaphysics as the unifying figure of all philosophy "refers to the thought of Heidegger, without which these notions would have remained impracticable", and Derrida uses this Heideggerian thesis against Heidegger's own reading of the LU without justifying the split (R I.4 ¶1, n. 51). Marion's remark is accurate as to provenance and imprecise as to text. The temporal reading of being as presence is SZ's (§6, SZ 25), but SZ reads it as a determination of ancient ontology to be destroyed by a temporal analytic, not as the essence of metaphysics. Marion's objection to Derrida applies in part to Marion: he too will play one Heideggerian thesis, presence as the mark of metaphysics, against another, the LU as ground (SZ 38), and resolve the conflict through a third term, givenness, which SZ treats with suspicion.

If signification signifies without intuition, and intuition, enlarged to a "total intuition", exhausts presence, what presence remains for signification? "Derrida makes the aporia unavoidable, while Heidegger seems not even to suspect it" (R I.4 ¶2). Heidegger suspected it, and SZ dissolves it by moving signification out of the field of presence. Significance (*Bedeutsamkeit*), the relational whole of the world, is the ontological condition for the possibility that understanding Dasein "can disclose such things as 'significations', which in turn found the possible being of word and language" (§18, SZ 87). "Words accrue to significations; word-things are not provided with significations" (§34, SZ 161). And the notes on sign and signification cite the First Investigation and Ideen I §§123 ff. as "the more radical version of the problem" (SZ 77 n., 166 n.). SZ places signification before both intuition and expression, in the world. Marion's aporia is real for a reader who keeps signification ideal and intuition present. It does not arise in SZ, which grants signification neither ideality nor dependence on intuition. Whether SZ's answer is adequate to what the LU demand of ideal meaning is a fair question, but "not even to suspect" is not supported by the text.

### 5 - Presence without Intuition

Signification is opposed to intuition by a "phenomenologically irreducible difference" that precedes all the distinctions within intuition (R I.5 ¶1). The unit is Husserl's (see the companion run). For this pair it fixes the term against which SZ 87 and 161 have just been read: Marion's signification is the ideal unit of the First Investigation, and SZ's is a moment of the world's significance. The two will not meet on the ground of chapter I, because they are not the same thing under one name.

An act of signification constitutes itself "without the least fulfilling intuition having to intervene", and mathematical idealities show that inadequacy is the rule: "π is transcendental" is seen with evidence to be identical whether anyone thinks it or not (R I.5 ¶2). SZ addresses the ideal content of judgement directly and puts its ontological status in question. In §44a the traditional concept of truth divides judgement into a real psychical process and an ideal content, and SZ asks whether the separation of the "real" and the "ideal" is ontologically warranted at all, and what the being of the relation between them is (SZ 216–217). The "validity" (*Geltung*) to which the ideal content is assigned is named in §2 among the being-words whose sense is to be clarified (SZ 7) and examined in §33 (SZ 155–156). Marion takes the ideal being of signification as a datum of the LU and asks what presence suits it. SZ takes it as a thesis whose being is unclarified. The unit supports Marion's reading of Husserl and leaves SZ's question unanswered.

Derrida's proof text, LU I §11, states natural consciousness's view, as the inserted "as one usually says" shows, and the sentences Derrida omits state Husserl's own (R I.5 ¶3). The unit is wholly within the Husserl–Derrida exchange. It bears on this pair only as method: Marion reads the clause Derrida cuts. The same reading to the end of the sentence, applied to SZ 437 in the preceding division and to SZ 38 here, finds questions where R found a concession and a tribute where R found a method.

Derrida's misreading has its motive in the essentially occasional expressions of LU I §26, "I" and "this", which take their signification from living speech; Marion answers that they are exceptions, and that the LU's I is a mere complex of experiences (R I.5 ¶4). SZ's treatment of the same words is the converse. In §25 the "I" may be understood only as a "non-committal formal indication" of something that may turn out to be its own "opposite" (SZ 116), and in §26 Humboldt's observation that some languages render "I" by "here" and "you" by "there" shows that the pronoun points to Dasein's spatiality (SZ 120). Where Marion makes the occasional expressions exceptions to signification, SZ makes the "I" a pointer to a being whose existence it does not yet determine. Marion does not engage this. It will matter in chapter III, where Dasein is said to remain bound to the I.

Signification gives itself evidently without depending on fulfilling intuition, and has an ideal content: "what mode of presence *sui generis* unfolds when signification presents itself by itself alone?" (R I.5 ¶5). The question has an answer in Ideen I, the noema (companion run). SZ answers that the question is wrongly posed, since signification is not a mode of presence at all but a moment of disclosedness: understanding discloses significance, and significations, then words, follow (SZ 87, 161). Marion keeps the question in the vocabulary of presence, which SZ §6 makes the vocabulary of the tradition to be destroyed. The unit therefore keeps open, against SZ, the possibility that presence has modes SZ does not consider. Chapter I's next step names the mode: givenness.

### 6 - The Evidence of Givenness

The Sixth Investigation confirms the First: signification can be thought without any correlative intuition, and countless significations never come to expression; since "phenomenologically, being reduces to evidence", independent significations are actual (R I.6 ¶1). The quoted equation of being and evidence is the point at which SZ's §44 begins its departure from the LU. SZ takes over the doctrine of evidence as verification of an assertion by the entity itself (SZ 218 and n.) and denies that this agreement is the primary phenomenon of truth. Truth is primarily the uncovering of entities and the disclosedness of Dasein (SZ 219–221). Marion reads "being reduces to evidence" as the LU's final word on being. SZ reads the same doctrine as a derivative form of truth that presupposes disclosedness. The unit is Husserl's. Its interest for this pair lies in the choice of starting point: chapter I's being is the being of evidence, and SZ will be measured, in chapters II and IV, against a being that it has already called derivative.

Signification is enlarged until it exists as an autonomous being, so that two surpluses collide, and the *Krisis* sharpens the conflict by saying that the LU made evidence a problem (R I.6 ¶2). There is no SZ locus. The *Krisis* (1936) postdates SZ, and the comparison it sets up, two sources of evidence, is not one SZ considers, since for SZ neither intuition nor signification is a source.

The way out is the *Krisis* note: the breakthrough of 1898 was "this universal correlational *a priori* of the object of experience and its modes of givenness", which Marion reads as "the correlation between appearing and what appears as such" (R I.6 ¶3, n. 80). SZ has its own version of the correlation, and it is not a correlation of givenness. Uncovering and uncoveredness belong together, so that "being-true as being-uncovering" is a way of being of Dasein, and the entity is uncovered "in the how of its uncoveredness" (§44, SZ 218–219). The correlation is grounded in being-in-the-world, not in a giving. Marion's formula "appearing and what appears" will reappear in chapter II as the content of SZ's §7. Here it is Husserl's, and its later transfer to Heidegger should be read with this difference in view: SZ's correlation has a third term, Dasein's disclosedness, that Marion's formula leaves out.

Presence is therefore givenness, and Derrida's conclusion stands while his argument falls. And if presence culminates in a givenness, "how can Heidegger have recognized his starting point in [the LU], in the Marburg courses as in his last seminar of 1973?" (R I.6 ¶4). Marion's list of the places where Heidegger recognized his starting point omits the one in SZ. SZ 38 is a public, printed acknowledgement in the book itself, and its terms (ground, breakthrough) are those R has adopted. The omission is not trivial. It lets R take the courses and Zähringen as the record of Heidegger's debt, and the courses specify the debt as categorial intuition, which SZ 38 does not. R's question is well posed for the courses. For SZ it is posed with the wrong evidence.

### 7 - Givenness as Question

Heidegger twice acknowledged the breakthrough of the LU as the necessary condition of SZ: in 1925 categorial intuition "gives a ground", and in 1973 Husserl's *tour de force* gave him "a ground". But what matters in both texts, Marion argues, is not the intuition of categorial being but givenness: categorial intuition is an analogy imposed by the givenness of being, and Heidegger, unlike Husserl, "will set himself to think givenness, and therefore will destroy categorial intuition; and in fact he will no longer mention it in *Sein und Zeit*, nor later" (R I.7 ¶1, nn. 90–92). Three points can be checked. First, GA 20 does say "gives the ground", but the ground is for "bringing out the structures of these ideal objects, that is, for working out the categories". What it calls decisive is that "there are acts in which ideal contents show themselves in themselves (*sich an ihnen selbst zeigen*), which are not products of these acts" (GA 20, 97–98). The decisive word is self-showing, which SZ §7 will make the formal concept of phenomenon (SZ 28). The 1925 text supports Marion's demotion of categorial intuition, but it supports self-showing, not givenness. Second, Marion's "twice" leaves out SZ 38 (see I.6 ¶4). Third, the closing claim is too strong on both counts. SZ mentions categorial intuition once, as the title of the section of LU VI from which Lask's *Logic* derives (SZ 218 n.). And Zähringen, which R has just quoted, discusses it at length. What R can claim is that SZ never uses categorial intuition. That claim holds, and SZ 147 says why: all intuition, the seeing of essences included, is a derivative of understanding. Marion's "destroy" is the right word for SZ's operation, but what SZ destroys is the priority of intuition in general, and it does so in favour of understanding, not of givenness.

The 1925 course confirms the reading: categorial intuition is intuition, the "plain grasp of what is given in bodily presence as it shows itself", and "objectivity in the widest sense is far richer than the reality of a thing"; the enlargement is "the demand for a freeing of the ground" (R I.7 ¶2, nn. 93–96). The widening survives into SZ with its name changed. SZ keeps the thesis that the thing's reality is one mode among others ("reality has no priority", §43c, SZ 211) and drops "objectivity" as the name of the wider field. The freeing of the ground (*Freilegung*) also becomes SZ's own method word: the analytic of Dasein is to free the horizon for the interpretation of the sense of being (§5). Marion reads the 1925 widening as the widening of presence understood as objectivity. SZ makes the same widening a widening of modes of being, and in doing so it gives up the word "objectivity" that R will hold against Husserl. This is selection on R's part: from GA 20 it takes the stage at which Heidegger still speaks Husserl's language of objectivity and givenness, and not the transposition into modes of being that SZ carries out.

Heidegger does justice to the Sixth Investigation on two points: Husserl never says that being is intuited, since in fulfilment "being in the sense of truth" is realized and the little word "is" is "itself given, or at least presumed given"; and Husserl always infers categorial intuition by analogy (R I.7 ¶3, nn. 97–98). The first point is the one SZ actually takes from LU VI. In §44 the assertion is verified when perception shows "nothing other than *that* it is the entity itself that was meant in the assertion", so that what comes to confirmation is the assertion's "being-uncovering" (SZ 218), with LU VI §§36–39 named in the note. Marion's reconstruction of what Heidegger owes to the Sixth Investigation is confirmed by SZ, on the point Marion makes: being in the sense of truth, not an intuition of being. SZ takes the doctrine one step further than R reports. The identity of meant and given is itself possible only because the assertion is a being toward the entity that uncovers it, and that uncovering rests on Dasein's disclosedness. What Marion calls the "anonymous" givenness of the "is", SZ assigns to an entity, Dasein, and to its way of being.

Husserl could not answer "what, then, gives itself?", because he never heard it as a question. Heidegger, seeing that the breakthrough is the enlargement of givenness, asks what such givenness means. Two prerequisites follow, a step back before givenness and the recognition that the being of what appears is at stake, and they make one demand: "does the reduction lead phenomenology to see being as a phenomenon?" (R I.7 ¶4). The last question is SZ's own, in SZ's words: what in the phenomenological sense must be called "phenomenon" is the being of entities, which "for the most part precisely does not show itself" (§7, SZ 35). In that respect R I.7 ¶4 reconstructs SZ faithfully. The surrounding claim does not. SZ does not ask what givenness means, and it does not reach being as phenomenon by a reduction. It reaches it by an interpretation of Dasein, whose method §7 calls hermeneutic (SZ 37). Marion's "Heidegger … bearing his whole interrogation on what such givenness means" is a re-inscription of SZ's question of the sense of being as a question about givenness. R I.7 thus attributes to Heidegger the question that the book itself will ask, and that SZ, by its own account of the givenness of the I (SZ 46, 115), declined to make central. Chapter II's task, to show that Heidegger's method is a reduction, starts from this attribution.

Across chapter I, Marion has made SZ load-bearing at two points without citing it. He uses SZ's terms for its debt to the LU, ground and breakthrough (SZ 38), as the terms of his own reading of the LU, while omitting the passage in which SZ states the debt, so that the courses and Zähringen become the record of what Heidegger took. And he makes SZ's question of being as phenomenon (SZ 35) the question to which the LU's givenness leads, while recasting it as a question about givenness, a word SZ uses mostly to name a trap (SZ 46, 115). Where R reconstructs SZ's actual use of LU VI, being in the sense of truth verified by the entity itself (SZ 218), its reading holds. Where it reports what Heidegger did not take from the LU, it overstates: SZ names categorial intuition once (SZ 218 n.), and Zähringen discusses it at length. Where it says Heidegger did not suspect the aporia of signification, it misses §§18 and 34, which remove signification from the field of presence (SZ 87, 161). The chapter's real point of contact with SZ is one it does not cite: SZ 147 derives intuition and the seeing of essences from understanding. That sentence is the place where the breakthrough as R defines it, the enlargement of intuition, is left behind by the book that claims to stand on its ground. Chapter I has prepared R's thesis that Heidegger thinks givenness by showing that he does not think intuition. SZ supports only the second half.

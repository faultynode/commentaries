# Jean-Luc Marion, *Réduction et donation*, read against Heidegger, *Sein und Zeit*: a comparative commentary

## Foreword

Marion (R) is cited by division, section, paragraph and note (R I.7 ¶1, R II n. 50), since the edition used has no page numbers. Heidegger's *Sein und Zeit* (P) is cited by section and by Niemeyer page (SZ 38). R cites the tenth Niemeyer edition (1963) by page and line, and its pages are those of the corpus text, the unchanged eleventh edition of 1967. R's French for SZ is Emmanuel Martineau's privately printed translation of 1985, which the corpus holds, so every French rendering in R can be checked against the translation R names. Departures from Martineau are counted as Marion's own. Five traps govern this pair. First, *Sein und Zeit* never uses the word "reduction": Heidegger's name for leading the gaze back from entities to being as a "phenomenological reduction" belongs to the summer course of 1927 (GA 24 §5), which ties into Husserl's term "in wording, not in substance". Second, SZ treats givenness as a trap. It warns against starting from "an initially given I and subject" (§10, SZ 46) and names the phenomenon by self-showing, not by givenness (§7, SZ 28). Third, Marion reads SZ through the lecture courses published between 1975 and 1983 and through the Zähringen seminar of 1973; a doctrine of a course is SZ's only where SZ states it. Fourth, the marginal notes from Heidegger's own copy, which carry much of chapter IV, are cited from GA 2, are later than 1927, and cannot be checked in this corpus. Fifth, SZ's "ontological difference" (§12, SZ 56) separates Dasein's kind of being from that of other entities, and is not the difference between being and entities. Marion's chapter IV joins the two under one name. "Presence" needs one more caution: SZ's *Anwesenheit* is its reading of ancient ontology's οὐσία from the present (§6, SZ 25), not a name for metaphysics as a whole.

The Foreword's single sentence puts givenness "at the centre of the reduction, and therefore of phenomenology", and aims at what, "without being it, comes after the subject" (R Foreword ¶1). Neither term is SZ's. Its method has no reduction at its centre, and its successor to the subject is defined by being. SZ itself is the most rigorous attempt in R's field to say what comes after the subject: §10 sets aside "subject, soul, consciousness, spirit, person" because every idea of a subject carries the ontology of the *subjectum* along with it (SZ 46), and it names Dasein by the fact that "in its being, this being is at issue for it" (§4, SZ 12). Marion's formula has two senses, "without being the subject" and "without being at all". The first is SZ's own programme. The second is aimed at SZ: what comes after the subject is not to be determined by its relation to being. The Foreword thus announces, as a programme rather than as exegesis, a contestation of SZ's answer to the very question SZ was the first to pose in these terms. The heading of the last section of chapter VI, "'There' outside being", states it without the first sense.

The acknowledgements thank Courtine and Franck for a practice of phenomenology "older and better" than Marion's own, the Leuven Husserl Archive, and Michel Henry for his friendship and "example of philosophical probity" (R Foreword ¶2). For this pair the names matter because two of them are readers of SZ in the corpus. Franck's *Heidegger and the Problem of Space* has an inheritance record pairing it with SZ, and Henry's *L'essence de la manifestation* reads SZ through its whole length (runs/henry-on-sz.md). Neither debt is specified. Whether Marion's SZ owes anything to them, in particular the charge that Dasein remains bound to the I (chapter III) and the charge that SZ subordinates appearing to being (chapters IV and VI), is to be watched where those chapters make the charges. The date, 24 February 1989, falls after the publication of GA 24 (1975), GA 20 (1979) and GA 2 with its marginal notes. Marion's SZ is the SZ of a reader who has the courses and the notes, and he writes, as he says in the next division, from "the recent publication" of the courses.

## Bibliographical note

Husserl is cited from the Husserliana, except for the *Logical Investigations*, cited from the second edition of 1913 (R Bibliographical note ¶1). The exception puts Marion's LU on the same text as SZ's. Heidegger cites "Log. Unters. 2. A." (second edition) in §44 (SZ 218 n.) and in his notes on sign and signification (SZ 77 n., 166 n.). When chapter I sets the breakthrough of 1900–1901 against Heidegger's reading of it, the text under both readings is the same edition. The companion run treats the note's consequences for Husserl (runs/marion-rd-on-hua3.md).

Heidegger is cited from the Gesamtausgabe, with one exception: SZ, cited from its tenth edition (Niemeyer, 1963), "page and lines", and quoted in Martineau's translation, "modifying it where needed" (R Bibliographical note ¶2). The exception has three consequences for this run. First, Marion's page numbers resolve directly in the corpus text, since the ninth edition (1960) fixed the pagination and the tenth and eleventh reprint it unchanged. Second, the exception is incomplete. When Marion quotes Heidegger's notes from his own copy he cites GA 2 ("GA, 2, p. 47", R II n. 50; "p. 275", chapter IV). These notes exist only in that edition. The note keeps SZ apart from its marginal notes in the citations, and chapter IV joins them in the argument. A reader who checks only the Niemeyer text will not find the words on which chapter IV most depends. Third, Martineau's translation, begun in July 1984 and printed in 1985 for the translator's friends, is the French in which the book argues: Dasein left untranslated, "under-the-hand" (*sous-la-main*) for present-at-hand (*vorhanden*), "being-at-the-world" (*être-au-monde*) for being-in-the-world, and "the questioned", "the interrogated", "the asked-for" (*le questionné*, *l'interrogé*, *le demandé*) for the three terms of §2. Since the corpus holds this translation, R's quotations of SZ can be read against the French Marion names as well as against the German. Where Marion's French departs from Martineau, the departure is his own.

The note lists the first versions: chapter I in *Philosophie* (1984); chapter III as "L'*ego* et le *Dasein*. Heidegger et la 'destruction' de Descartes dans *Sein und Zeit*" (*Revue de Métaphysique et de Morale*, 1987); chapter IV as "Différence ontologique ou question de l'être. Un indécidé de *Sein und Zeit*" (*Tijdschrift voor Filosofie*, 1987) (R Bibliographical note ¶3). The two chapters that engage SZ most densely were written as articles on SZ in the same year. The subtitle of the second gives the verdict before the chapter argues it: on ontological difference, SZ is "undecided". The book's reading of SZ thus took shape in 1987, after GA 24 and GA 2 were available.

## Phenomenology as Such

The epigraph is Heidegger's, from the course of 1919/20 (GA 58, 5): what does "given", "givenness" mean, "this magic word of phenomenology and the stumbling block for the others"? The course is not in the corpus. Against SZ the epigraph is a selection. It takes from Heidegger the question SZ does not pursue. In 1919/20 givenness is a word to be questioned. In SZ it is a word to be avoided: the existential analytic would "walk into a trap" if it began from "the givenness of the I" (§25, SZ 115), and the evidence of "an immediate givenness of experiences, of the I and of consciousness" falls short of the certainty that belongs to anticipation (§53, SZ 265). SZ defines the phenomenon by showing, "that which shows itself in itself" (§7, SZ 28), not by giving. Marion's Heidegger therefore begins before SZ, and the book will argue that SZ let fall the question its author had raised. That argument is announced here by the choice of a pre-SZ text to stand over a book about the reduction.

Phenomenology takes over the role of philosophy after Nietzsche has completed the possibilities of metaphysics, and undertakes "a new beginning" (R Phenomenology as such ¶1). The frame is Heidegger's, but not that of SZ. SZ begins with a retrieval of a question that "has today been forgotten" (§1, SZ 2). It names its task the destruction of the history of ontology, not the end of metaphysics (§6, SZ 19), and its Nietzsche is the author of the second *Untimely Meditation* on history (§76, SZ 396–397) and of a remark about growing "too old for one's victories" (§53, SZ 264). Metaphysics completed in Nietzsche and a new beginning after it belong to the Nietzsche lectures and the texts of the 1930s. This is re-inscription: SZ's retrieval of a forgotten question is placed in a history of being that SZ does not yet tell.

The question that joins Nietzsche's end and Husserl's beginning is whether the conditions of presence can be stretched until every being reaches presence, "without any condition or reserve" (R Phenomenology as such ¶2). Here "presence" first enters the book, and the word covers two things SZ keeps apart. SZ's *Anwesenheit* is a temporal determination: ancient ontology understands being as παρουσία or οὐσία, which "means, ontologically and temporally, presence", so that entities are grasped "with regard to a definite mode of time, the present" (§6, SZ 25). (The corpus text garbles the Greek at this point.) Marion's "presence" is the condition under which a thing is given, a matter of limits and reserves. SZ's remedy for its *Anwesenheit* is time as the horizon of being. Marion's remedy for his presence will be an unconditioned givenness. On SZ's terms, a presence without condition or reserve would be the extreme of the privilege of the present that §6 sets out to destroy, not an escape from it. Marion places phenomenology on SZ's line of diagnosis and does not follow SZ's line of cure.

The breakthrough of 1900–1901 extends presence beyond all limits and thereby reproduces presence's metaphysical determination, objectivity (R Phenomenology as such ¶3). SZ's own name for what the modern ideal of knowledge privileges is presence-at-hand (*Vorhandenheit*), and its account of objectivity is not a charge against phenomenology. "Thematization objectifies" (§69b, SZ 363): science frees entities so that they can be questioned and determined "objectively", and this is a mode of being-in-the-world, with its own right. Knowing the world is a founded mode of being-in (§13). The charge that a phenomenology "consecrates" objectivity is GA 20's against Husserl (§§10–13 of that course), not SZ's. In SZ the objectivity of the object is derivative but not illegitimate. Marion takes the charge from the courses and gives it the force of a verdict on phenomenology as such, which SZ does not.

"The objective of phenomenology does not coincide with objectivity: so runs Heidegger's starting point", and the courses published "recently" establish that being became the stake of phenomenology for Heidegger first in a critique of Husserl's ideal of objectification (R Phenomenology as such ¶4). Marion says where his evidence lies, and it is not SZ. SZ names Husserl rarely and almost always to credit him (SZ 38 and n.; the notes on sign, signification and truth). It criticizes him by name once, in §10, where Husserl's and Scheler's interpretations of the person "no longer ask the question of the being of the person" (SZ 47 and n.). The sustained immanent critique of Husserl is GA 20 §§10–13. This is mediation, stated by R itself: the Heidegger who starts from a critique of objectivity is the Heidegger of the Marburg courses. The paragraph's second question, whether what performs "the re(con)duction" (reduction as leading back) acts "as transcendental I or as Dasein", puts to SZ a term SZ does not use. SZ's Dasein is not the agent of a reduction. It is the entity interrogated (§2) and the place where being is understood (§4). The alternative "I or Dasein" fixes the book's comparison on one axis, the agent of the method, and SZ refuses that axis when it calls the starting point of a given I and subject a fundamental miss (SZ 46).

Heidegger failed in what he attempted: Dasein "remains haunted by the I", the "phenomenon of being", even in the weakened figure of ontological difference, is never exposed, and the "phenomenology of the inapparent" stays a contradictory programme, as Heidegger "was the first to concede, as early as 1927" (R Phenomenology as such ¶5, n. 3: SZ §83, 437, 37–41). Three texts are joined in one verdict. The phenomenology of the inapparent is the Zähringen formula of 1973. The phenomenon of being is SZ's: in the phenomenological sense the phenomenon is what "for the most part precisely does *not* show itself", what is "hidden" yet belongs to what shows itself as its sense and ground, and this is "the *being* of entities" (§7, SZ 35). The concession of 1927 is not at R's locus. SZ 437, lines 37–41, ends the book with questions: "Does a way lead from original time to the sense of being? Does time itself reveal itself as the horizon of being?" They are the questions Division Three was to answer, and the paragraph before them calls the whole book a preparation for a strife "not yet even kindled" (SZ 437). The concession comes in the Preliminary Remark of the later editions, which the corpus text prints, written, by its own account, a quarter of a century after 1927: the second half "can no longer be added without the first being presented anew". Marion reads the questions of 1927 with the later verdict. That is a retroactive reading, and it makes SZ concede what it only asked. The charge that Dasein is haunted by the I is asserted here and argued in chapter III. It will have to meet §10 and §25, where SZ itself makes the given I the trap.

The method of the book: since "in phenomenology, as against metaphysics, possibility surpasses actuality in truth", the principle must be pushed to its end, even against "phenomenology already carried out", because a genuine thought is surpassed by being repeated, "and then even failure succeeds" (R Phenomenology as such ¶6, n. 4: SZ §7, 38, 29–31). Martineau has "higher than actuality stands *possibility*" (Plus haut que l'effectivité se tient la *possibilité*), and Marion's "possibility surpasses actuality in truth" (la possibilité surpasse en vérité l'effectivité) is his own. He adds "as against metaphysics" and "in truth". The context of the sentence matters more than its wording. It closes the paragraph in which Heidegger says that the investigations "became possible only on the ground laid by E. Husserl, with whose *Logical Investigations* phenomenology achieved its breakthrough", and the actuality it subordinates is phenomenology's being actual "as a philosophical 'school'" (SZ 38). Heidegger's sentence frees phenomenology from its school in order to keep its method. Marion's frees it from every phenomenology already carried out, Heidegger's included. A tribute to Husserl becomes a licence to repeat Heidegger against himself. The repetition Marion invokes also has an SZ form he does not cite. In §74 repetition "replies" to the possibility of existence that has been, and the reply is at once a "revocation" of what works in the present as "past" (SZ 386). Marion's "surpass by repeating, even by borrowing the means to think with it beyond it" is close to that structure: a reply to a possibility, not a refutation of a position. SZ supplies the method by which R will be measured against SZ.

## I. The Breakthrough and the Enlargement

### 1 - Two Interpretations and an Enlargement

Of the two readings strong enough to place the breakthrough of the *Logical Investigations* in the history of metaphysics, one is Heidegger's: the Sixth Investigation reaches the categorial intuition of being and opens onto a givenness of being, and Marion quotes Zähringen (1973) for it, "through this *tour de force* … I finally had a ground" (R I.1 ¶1, n. 4). From this follows "the direct filiation, which Heidegger never formulates explicitly but never ceases to suggest", between the *Investigations* and SZ. SZ formulates it explicitly, in the sentence before the one Marion's method paragraph quotes: the investigations of SZ "became possible only on the ground (*Boden*) laid by E. Husserl, with whose *Logical Investigations* phenomenology achieved its breakthrough (*Durchbruch*)" (SZ 38), which Martineau renders as the *Investigations* having "assured the breakthrough of phenomenology" (ont assuré la percée de la phénoménologie), with R's own word for it. The two words on which chapter I is built, ground and breakthrough, are SZ's own words for its debt to the LU. What SZ does not say is that the debt runs through categorial intuition. That specification is Zähringen's, and Marion's "never explicitly" holds only of it. The topology Marion builds next, in which the breakthrough anticipates the destruction of ontology, is his construction: SZ's §6 destroys the ontology of Kant, Descartes and Aristotle and does not count Husserl in that history.

Read from the Sixth Investigation, the LU make it possible to pass to ontological difference "by accomplishing the 'destruction of the history of ontology'". Read from the First, they lead to *différance* (R I.1 ¶2). The quoted phrase is the title of SZ §6 (SZ 19). The link Marion draws between destruction and ontological difference is not in §6, which neither uses the phrase "ontological difference" nor names Husserl. §6 ties the destruction to time: ancient ontology understood being from the present, and the destruction is to expose that temporal determination (SZ 25–26). Marion's sentence makes the destruction a passage to ontological difference, which is chapter IV's thesis, given here in advance as though it were Heidegger's. On this point Marion's Heidegger is the Heidegger of the essay of 1929 and the course of 1927, both of which he will cite in chapter IV.

The return to "the things themselves" leads concepts back to intuition, and the 1913 draft preface calls the LU method "radically intuitive" in an enlarged sense (R I.1 ¶3). The maxim is SZ's too: "To the things themselves!" stands at the head of the preliminary concept of phenomenology, against free-floating constructions and borrowed concepts (§7, SZ 27–28). SZ keeps the maxim and drops the intuitionism. "'Intuition' and 'thinking' are both already remote derivatives of understanding. Even the phenomenological 'seeing of essences' is grounded in existential understanding" (§31, SZ 147). This sentence is the decisive SZ locus for chapter I, and R does not cite it. It is where SZ departs from the breakthrough Marion has just defined: the return to the things themselves no longer leads back to intuition, because intuition is itself led back to understanding. What R I.7 will present as Heidegger's preference for givenness over categorial intuition, SZ states as the derivation of all intuition from understanding.

The principle of all principles of 1913 does not redirect phenomenology, Marion argues: the draft preface says the LU "radically profess" it, and the LU anticipate it in the Fifth Investigation and in the principle of presuppositionlessness (R I.1 ¶4). SZ does not cite the principle. Its view of it follows from SZ 147: intuition as the source of right is precisely what SZ ranks as derivative, and "about this kind of seeing" SZ defers judgement until "the explicit concepts of being and the structure of being have been won". Marion's unit is Husserl's, and its comparative weight lies in what it prepares. It makes intuition the one principle of the LU, so that any thinker who demotes intuition seems to break with the breakthrough itself. SZ has demoted it, and still claims the LU as its ground (SZ 38).

Enlarged intuition is the condition of the breakthrough. The Sixth Investigation carries out the enlargement in §45, and its order of exposition is a "zigzag" (R I.1 ¶5). SZ's one reference to that part of the Sixth Investigation takes something else from it. In the note to §44 Heidegger refers, "for the idea of verification as 'identification'", to the Sixth Investigation, to "Evidence and Truth" (§§36–39), and, in passing, to the section "On sensuous and categorial intuitions" as the source of Lask's *Logic of Philosophy* (SZ 218 n.). What SZ selects from LU VI is truth as identification and confirmation, not intuition's enlargement. The two readers of the same edition choose different chapters from it. Marion's breakthrough is the enlargement of intuition (§45). SZ's debt is to the doctrine of evidence and truth (§§36–39), which SZ rereads as uncovering.

### 2 - The Domains of Intuition

Enlarged intuition gives "something objective which is, without being in the manner of the real", and "objectness" (*Gegenständlichkeit*) replaces "object" to cover states of affairs and categorial forms (R I.2 ¶1). SZ has a counterpart, but not in the vocabulary of objects. Its widening goes beyond reality, not beyond the real object. "Reality has no priority among the modes of being of innerworldly entities", and it cannot characterize world or Dasein (§43c, SZ 211). Where Husserl, on Marion's reading, widens the field of what can be an object, SZ widens the field of modes of being, and treats the object as one mode among them. The difference is the one R's chapter II will make central: a widening of objectness against a widening of being. Here it is present only by presupposition.

Against Kant's restriction of intuition to the senses, Marion finds three degrees of the categorial: sensuous intuition fulfilling a form, ideation, and categorial intuition proper. He glosses ideation with Heidegger's 1925 example: "I see first, universally, this: here is a house" (R I.2 ¶2, n. 37 / GA 20, 90). The quotation checks against GA 20: acts of ideation "give" the idea, εἶδος, "the look of something" (*Aussehen von etwas*). This is mediation, and SZ revises the example it draws on. In SZ, plain seeing of the things nearest to hand "carries the structure of interpretation in itself so originally" that a grasp of something free of the "as" requires a reorientation (§32, SZ 149). The "as" of the house is the hermeneutic "as" of understanding, articulated before any assertion, not the gift of an act of ideation. Marion's "I see *the* house, *as* house, before (and in order to) see *a* house" is GA 20's categorial reading. SZ has already moved the same seeing from intuition to interpretation. A reader who took R's gloss for SZ's doctrine would attribute to SZ the categorial intuition that SZ 147 derives from understanding.

Categorial intuition proper answers to the "is" of the statement, and "intuition results from givenness without exception" (R I.2 ¶3). SZ's treatment of the "is" goes the other way. The ontological sense of the "is", "which an external theory of the proposition and judgement has deformed into a 'copula'", is to be delimited from the temporality of discourse (§68, SZ 349), and §33 makes the apophantic "as" of assertion a derivative of the hermeneutic "as". Where Marion has the "is" given to an intuition that follows givenness, SZ has it disclosed in understanding and deformed by the theory of judgement. Neither is cited by R, but R's chapter IV will need SZ's "is" when it reads being's "es gibt" in §44, and the unit fixes, before that, a reading of the "is" as given that SZ does not share.

The Kantian limit is transgressed in several ways "for a single motive: the phenomenological requirement that every phenomenon be given in presence, without remainder or reserve" (R I.2 ¶4). SZ's Kant is another Kant. In §6 Kant is the one thinker who went "a stretch of the way" toward the dimension of temporality, before the schematism failed for want of an ontology of the subject (SZ 23–24). Marion's Kant limits intuition, and the phenomenologist exceeds him by giving more. SZ's Kant limits nothing of that kind; he comes nearest to time and stops short. The unit is independent of SZ, and it sets up a measure, presence without remainder, by which SZ will later be judged without having adopted it.

### 3 - The Accomplishment of Presence

If metaphysics is the uninterrogated primacy of bringing entities into presence, and if intuition performs that bringing, metaphysics is never so accomplished as in the LU (R I.3 ¶1). The definition of metaphysics by presence is not SZ's. SZ does not use "metaphysics" for the whole of philosophy. It uses "ontology", and it determines ancient ontology, not metaphysics as such, by *Anwesenheit* (§6). The identification of metaphysics with the primacy of presence is later Heidegger, and the phrase "metaphysics of presence" is Derrida's (R I.4 ¶1 says as much). Marion's conditional premise, "if metaphysics is …", rests on a history that SZ only begins. The unit's verdict on the LU therefore rests on an extension of SZ §6 beyond what §6 claims.

Nietzsche's fragment "How I recognize my own kind" and Husserl's "secret longing" of all modern philosophy (Ideen I §62) are made to say the same thing (R I.3 ¶2). There is no SZ locus. SZ's Nietzsche, in §76, is the author of the three kinds of history, monumental, antiquarian and critical, read as a sign of historicity (SZ 396–397). The pairing of Nietzsche with the end of metaphysics is the Heidegger of the 1930s and 1940s. The Husserl side is treated in the companion run.

The answer to the first question of I.1 stands: the breakthrough accomplishes the metaphysics of presence, like Nietzsche's great Amen, by bringing all beings into presence (R I.3 ¶3). For this pair the conclusion matters through what it implies about Heidegger. If the LU complete metaphysics, then Heidegger's claim to stand on their ground (SZ 38) makes SZ the heir of a completed metaphysics or makes its breakthrough a misreading of the LU. R will choose neither and look for a third reading, a breakthrough of givenness, in I.6–I.7. SZ's own description of its debt, a ground and a breakthrough but no doctrine of presence, leaves room for that third reading. It does not supply it.

### 4 - A Misconstrual of Signification?

Determining metaphysics by the primacy of presence and identifying metaphysics as the unifying figure of all philosophy "refers to the thought of Heidegger, without which these notions would have remained impracticable", and Derrida uses this Heideggerian thesis against Heidegger's own reading of the LU without justifying the split (R I.4 ¶1, n. 51). Marion's remark is accurate as to provenance and imprecise as to text. The temporal reading of being as presence is SZ's (§6, SZ 25), but SZ reads it as a determination of ancient ontology to be destroyed by a temporal analytic, not as the essence of metaphysics. Marion's objection to Derrida applies in part to Marion: he too will play one Heideggerian thesis, presence as the mark of metaphysics, against another, the LU as ground (SZ 38), and resolve the conflict through a third term, givenness, which SZ treats with suspicion.

If signification signifies without intuition, and intuition, enlarged to a "total intuition", exhausts presence, what presence remains for signification? "Derrida makes the aporia unavoidable, while Heidegger seems not even to suspect it" (R I.4 ¶2). Heidegger suspected it, and SZ dissolves it by moving signification out of the field of presence. Significance (*Bedeutsamkeit*), the relational whole of the world, is the ontological condition for the possibility that understanding Dasein "can disclose such things as 'significations', which in turn found the possible being of word and language" (§18, SZ 87). "Words accrue to significations; word-things are not provided with significations" (§34, SZ 161). And the notes on sign and signification cite the First Investigation and Ideen I §§123 ff. as "the more radical version of the problem" (SZ 77 n., 166 n.). SZ places signification before both intuition and expression, in the world. Marion's aporia is real for a reader who keeps signification ideal and intuition present. It does not arise in SZ, which grants signification neither ideality nor dependence on intuition. Whether SZ's answer is adequate to what the LU demand of ideal meaning is a fair question, but "not even to suspect" is not supported by the text.

### 5 - Presence without Intuition

Signification is opposed to intuition by a "phenomenologically irreducible difference" that precedes all the distinctions within intuition (R I.5 ¶1). The unit is Husserl's (see the companion run). For this pair it fixes the term against which SZ 87 and 161 have just been read: Marion's signification is the ideal unit of the First Investigation, and SZ's is a moment of the world's significance. The two will not meet on the ground of chapter I, because they are not the same thing under one name.

An act of signification constitutes itself "without the least fulfilling intuition having to intervene", and mathematical idealities show that inadequacy is the rule: "π is transcendental" is seen with evidence to be identical whether anyone thinks it or not (R I.5 ¶2). SZ addresses the ideal content of judgement directly and puts its ontological status in question. In §44a the traditional concept of truth divides judgement into a real psychical process and an ideal content, and SZ asks whether the separation of the "real" and the "ideal" is ontologically warranted at all, and what the being of the relation between them is (SZ 216–217). The "validity" (*Geltung*) to which the ideal content is assigned is named in §2 among the being-words whose sense is to be clarified (SZ 7) and examined in §33 (SZ 155–156). Marion takes the ideal being of signification as a datum of the LU and asks what presence suits it. SZ takes it as a thesis whose being is unclarified. The unit supports Marion's reading of Husserl and leaves SZ's question unanswered.

Derrida's proof text, LU I §11, states natural consciousness's view, as the inserted "as one usually says" shows, and the sentences Derrida omits state Husserl's own (R I.5 ¶3). The unit is wholly within the Husserl–Derrida exchange. It bears on this pair only as method: Marion reads the clause Derrida cuts. The same reading to the end of the sentence, applied to SZ 437 in the preceding division and to SZ 38 here, finds questions where R found a concession and a tribute where R found a method.

Derrida's misreading has its motive in the essentially occasional expressions of LU I §26, "I" and "this", which take their signification from living speech; Marion answers that they are exceptions, and that the LU's I is a mere complex of experiences (R I.5 ¶4). SZ's treatment of the same words is the converse. In §25 the "I" may be understood only as a "non-committal formal indication" of something that may turn out to be its own "opposite" (SZ 116), and in §26 Humboldt's observation that some languages render "I" by "here" and "you" by "there" shows that the pronoun points to Dasein's spatiality (SZ 120). Where Marion makes the occasional expressions exceptions to signification, SZ makes the "I" a pointer to a being whose existence it does not yet determine. Marion does not engage this. It will matter in chapter III, where Dasein is said to remain bound to the I.

Signification gives itself evidently without depending on fulfilling intuition, and has an ideal content: "what mode of presence *sui generis* unfolds when signification presents itself by itself alone?" (R I.5 ¶5). The question has an answer in Ideen I, the noema (companion run). SZ answers that the question is wrongly posed, since signification is not a mode of presence at all but a moment of disclosedness: understanding discloses significance, and significations, then words, follow (SZ 87, 161). Marion keeps the question in the vocabulary of presence, which SZ §6 makes the vocabulary of the tradition to be destroyed. The unit therefore keeps open, against SZ, the possibility that presence has modes SZ does not consider. Chapter I's next step names the mode: givenness.

### 6 - The Evidence of Givenness

The Sixth Investigation confirms the First: signification can be thought without any correlative intuition, and countless significations never come to expression; since "phenomenologically, being reduces to evidence", independent significations are actual (R I.6 ¶1). The quoted equation of being and evidence is the point at which SZ's §44 begins its departure from the LU. SZ takes over the doctrine of evidence as verification of an assertion by the entity itself (SZ 218 and n.) and denies that this agreement is the primary phenomenon of truth. Truth is primarily the uncovering of entities and the disclosedness of Dasein (SZ 219–221). Marion reads "being reduces to evidence" as the LU's final word on being. SZ reads the same doctrine as a derivative form of truth that presupposes disclosedness. The unit is Husserl's. Its interest for this pair lies in the choice of starting point: chapter I's being is the being of evidence, and SZ will be measured, in chapters II and IV, against a being that it has already called derivative.

Signification is enlarged until it exists as an autonomous being, so that two surpluses collide, and the *Krisis* sharpens the conflict by saying that the LU made evidence a problem (R I.6 ¶2). There is no SZ locus. The *Krisis* (1936) postdates SZ, and the comparison it sets up, two sources of evidence, is not one SZ considers, since for SZ neither intuition nor signification is a source.

The way out is the *Krisis* note: the breakthrough of 1898 was "this universal correlational *a priori* of the object of experience and its modes of givenness", which Marion reads as "the correlation between appearing and what appears as such" (R I.6 ¶3, n. 80). SZ has its own version of the correlation, and it is not a correlation of givenness. Uncovering and uncoveredness belong together, so that "being-true as being-uncovering" is a way of being of Dasein, and the entity is uncovered "in the how of its uncoveredness" (§44, SZ 218–219). The correlation is grounded in being-in-the-world, not in a giving. Marion's formula "appearing and what appears" will reappear in chapter II as the content of SZ's §7. Here it is Husserl's, and its later transfer to Heidegger should be read with this difference in view: SZ's correlation has a third term, Dasein's disclosedness, that Marion's formula leaves out.

Presence is therefore givenness, and Derrida's conclusion stands while his argument falls. And if presence culminates in a givenness, "how can Heidegger have recognized his starting point in [the LU], in the Marburg courses as in his last seminar of 1973?" (R I.6 ¶4). Marion's list of the places where Heidegger recognized his starting point omits the one in SZ. SZ 38 is a public, printed acknowledgement in the book itself, and its terms (ground, breakthrough) are those R has adopted. The omission is not trivial. It lets R take the courses and Zähringen as the record of Heidegger's debt, and the courses specify the debt as categorial intuition, which SZ 38 does not. R's question is well posed for the courses. For SZ it is posed with the wrong evidence.

### 7 - Givenness as Question

Heidegger twice acknowledged the breakthrough of the LU as the necessary condition of SZ: in 1925 categorial intuition "gives a ground", and in 1973 Husserl's *tour de force* gave him "a ground". But what matters in both texts, Marion argues, is not the intuition of categorial being but givenness: categorial intuition is an analogy imposed by the givenness of being, and Heidegger, unlike Husserl, "will set himself to think givenness, and therefore will destroy categorial intuition; and in fact he will no longer mention it in *Sein und Zeit*, nor later" (R I.7 ¶1, nn. 90–92). Three points can be checked. First, GA 20 does say "gives the ground", but the ground is for "bringing out the structures of these ideal objects, that is, for working out the categories". What it calls decisive is that "there are acts in which ideal contents show themselves in themselves (*sich an ihnen selbst zeigen*), which are not products of these acts" (GA 20, 97–98). The decisive word is self-showing, which SZ §7 will make the formal concept of phenomenon (SZ 28). The 1925 text supports Marion's demotion of categorial intuition, but it supports self-showing, not givenness. Second, Marion's "twice" leaves out SZ 38 (see I.6 ¶4). Third, the closing claim is too strong on both counts. SZ mentions categorial intuition once, as the title of the section of LU VI from which Lask's *Logic* derives (SZ 218 n.). And Zähringen, which R has just quoted, discusses it at length. What R can claim is that SZ never uses categorial intuition. That claim holds, and SZ 147 says why: all intuition, the seeing of essences included, is a derivative of understanding. Marion's "destroy" is the right word for SZ's operation, but what SZ destroys is the priority of intuition in general, and it does so in favour of understanding, not of givenness.

The 1925 course confirms the reading: categorial intuition is intuition, the "plain grasp of what is given in bodily presence as it shows itself", and "objectivity in the widest sense is far richer than the reality of a thing"; the enlargement is "the demand for a freeing of the ground" (R I.7 ¶2, nn. 93–96). The widening survives into SZ with its name changed. SZ keeps the thesis that the thing's reality is one mode among others ("reality has no priority", §43c, SZ 211) and drops "objectivity" as the name of the wider field. The freeing of the ground (*Freilegung*) also becomes SZ's own method word: the analytic of Dasein is to free the horizon for the interpretation of the sense of being (§5). Marion reads the 1925 widening as the widening of presence understood as objectivity. SZ makes the same widening a widening of modes of being, and in doing so it gives up the word "objectivity" that R will hold against Husserl. This is selection on R's part: from GA 20 it takes the stage at which Heidegger still speaks Husserl's language of objectivity and givenness, and not the transposition into modes of being that SZ carries out.

Heidegger does justice to the Sixth Investigation on two points: Husserl never says that being is intuited, since in fulfilment "being in the sense of truth" is realized and the little word "is" is "itself given, or at least presumed given"; and Husserl always infers categorial intuition by analogy (R I.7 ¶3, nn. 97–98). The first point is the one SZ actually takes from LU VI. In §44 the assertion is verified when perception shows "nothing other than *that* it is the entity itself that was meant in the assertion", so that what comes to confirmation is the assertion's "being-uncovering" (SZ 218), with LU VI §§36–39 named in the note. Marion's reconstruction of what Heidegger owes to the Sixth Investigation is confirmed by SZ, on the point Marion makes: being in the sense of truth, not an intuition of being. SZ takes the doctrine one step further than R reports. The identity of meant and given is itself possible only because the assertion is a being toward the entity that uncovers it, and that uncovering rests on Dasein's disclosedness. What Marion calls the "anonymous" givenness of the "is", SZ assigns to an entity, Dasein, and to its way of being.

Husserl could not answer "what, then, gives itself?", because he never heard it as a question. Heidegger, seeing that the breakthrough is the enlargement of givenness, asks what such givenness means. Two prerequisites follow, a step back before givenness and the recognition that the being of what appears is at stake, and they make one demand: "does the reduction lead phenomenology to see being as a phenomenon?" (R I.7 ¶4). The last question is SZ's own, in SZ's words: what in the phenomenological sense must be called "phenomenon" is the being of entities, which "for the most part precisely does not show itself" (§7, SZ 35). In that respect R I.7 ¶4 reconstructs SZ faithfully. The surrounding claim does not. SZ does not ask what givenness means, and it does not reach being as phenomenon by a reduction. It reaches it by an interpretation of Dasein, whose method §7 calls hermeneutic (SZ 37). Marion's "Heidegger … bearing his whole interrogation on what such givenness means" is a re-inscription of SZ's question of the sense of being as a question about givenness. R I.7 thus attributes to Heidegger the question that the book itself will ask, and that SZ, by its own account of the givenness of the I (SZ 46, 115), declined to make central. Chapter II's task, to show that Heidegger's method is a reduction, starts from this attribution.

Across chapter I, Marion has made SZ load-bearing at two points without citing it. He uses SZ's terms for its debt to the LU, ground and breakthrough (SZ 38), as the terms of his own reading of the LU, while omitting the passage in which SZ states the debt, so that the courses and Zähringen become the record of what Heidegger took. And he makes SZ's question of being as phenomenon (SZ 35) the question to which the LU's givenness leads, while recasting it as a question about givenness, a word SZ uses mostly to name a trap (SZ 46, 115). Where R reconstructs SZ's actual use of LU VI, being in the sense of truth verified by the entity itself (SZ 218), its reading holds. Where it reports what Heidegger did not take from the LU, it overstates: SZ names categorial intuition once (SZ 218 n.), and Zähringen discusses it at length. Where it says Heidegger did not suspect the aporia of signification, it misses §§18 and 34, which remove signification from the field of presence (SZ 87, 161). The chapter's real point of contact with SZ is one it does not cite: SZ 147 derives intuition and the seeing of essences from understanding. That sentence is the place where the breakthrough as R defines it, the enlargement of intuition, is left behind by the book that claims to stand on its ground. Chapter I has prepared R's thesis that Heidegger thinks givenness by showing that he does not think intuition. SZ supports only the second half.

## II. The Entity and the Phenomenon

### 1 - Putting Ontology Out of Play

The chapter opens on two declarations set against each other: Heidegger in 1925, that there is no ontology alongside phenomenology and that scientific ontology is nothing but phenomenology (GA 20, 98), and Husserl in 1912, that ontology in itself is not phenomenology (R II.1 ¶1, n. 1). The third definition of the chapter's programme, the entity, is where the two part, since for Heidegger the title "phenomenon" "says nothing about the being of the entities concerned, but designates only their *mode of encounter*" (n. 9 / GA 20, 117–118, checked). Marion's evidence for Heidegger is the 1925 course, and SZ restates it in the published form R cites in the next section: "Ontology is possible only as phenomenology" (§7, SZ 35), and the expression "phenomenology" "means primarily a *concept of method*. It characterizes not the material 'what' of the objects of philosophical research but their 'how'" (SZ 27). The unit is a reconstruction of the 1925 Heidegger, faithful to GA 20, and SZ confirms it. One shift should be marked at once. GA 20's "mode of encounter" becomes in SZ the defining term of the phenomenal: "'phenomenal' is what is *given* and explicable in the mode of encounter of the phenomenon" (SZ 37). The word Marion will make the axis of the book, "given", appears in SZ in exactly this subordinate place: what the phenomenon's way of being encountered allows to be explicated.

The Husserl side of the second paragraph is treated in the companion run. Its last move is aimed at SZ, as Commentary R notes: Husserl's demand for phenomenological intelligibility will "press all the harder on any later attempt to restore classical ontology, even by destroying it" (R II.1 ¶2). The words fit SZ only in part. SZ does not restore classical ontology. It calls for a destruction of its history (§6), and names its own ontology "universal phenomenological ontology", starting from "the hermeneutic of Dasein" (SZ 38), not from the categories of the tradition. What Marion anticipates is a real feature of SZ, its keeping of the title "ontology" after its critique of the tradition, but the phrase "restore … even by destroying it" makes destruction a means of restoration. That is R's reading. SZ's own word for its relation to the tradition is a retrieval of the *question* of being (§1), not of any ontology already given. Marion's charge is a contestation in advance, and its object, an ontology that comes back through destruction, is less SZ than the danger SZ itself names when it says the question "has today been forgotten" in spite of all the interest in metaphysics (SZ 2).

### 2 - The Method of Ontology

SZ §7 sets a reciprocal relation, ontology possible only as phenomenology and phenomenology "taken in its material content" as "the science of the being of entities — ontology" (SZ 35, 37, checked), and GA 24 names phenomenology the method of ontology (GA 24, 27, 16). Marion draws "two inversions": phenomenology loses the rank of autonomous science, and its demotion to "the ancillary status of method" serves ontology's restoration (R II.2 ¶1, nn. 11–13). The demotion is not in SZ. In the sentence Marion cites from SZ 38, "Ontology and phenomenology are not two different disciplines among others belonging to philosophy. The two titles characterize philosophy itself according to its object and its manner of treatment." The titles do not rank one above the other; they name one philosophy twice, by what and by how. And SZ blocks the reading of method as an instrument: the more genuinely a concept of method works, "the more originally it is rooted in the confrontation with the things themselves, and the farther it moves from what we call a technical device" (SZ 27). Marion quotes the same thought from GA 24, "scientific method is never a technique" (R II n. 65), two sections later, against his own "ancillary". Commentary R has followed R here without remark. The unit is re-inscription: Marion lays on SZ a hierarchy of science and method, his own frame for comparing Husserl's phenomenology as final science, and SZ 38 has none.

How does phenomenology take on an ontological charge? The 1925 course answers: "being is what is to be read off (*abgelesen*) the entity; what phenomenological interpretation places in its fore-sight is being" (R II.2 ¶2, nn. 15–16 / GA 20, 423). Marion then lists the 1925 theses: the a priori as a character of the being of entities, the criterion of the phenomenological as the mode of exhibition, and the "omission" of the question of the being of the intentional, which makes Husserl's phenomenology "non-phenomenological" (nn. 18–26, all GA 20). SZ carries only some of this. Its §2 asks "On *which* entity is the sense of being to be read off?" (SZ 7, checked; R n. 16), and its §7 fixes the criterion in the form Marion's n. 19 quotes: "'phenomenological' means everything that belongs to the manner of exhibition and explication" (SZ 37, checked). The immanent critique of Husserl, the omission of the being of the intentional and the verdict "non-phenomenological" are GA 20 §§10–13 and are absent from SZ. SZ names intentionality once, in a note that defers its grounding in ecstatic temporality to the unpublished division (§69b, SZ 363 n.). Marion's note 16 adds a marginal note from Heidegger's own copy to the question of SZ 7: "But it is not on this entity that the sense of being is read off". It cannot be checked here. If it is accurate, it retracts the very question that SZ uses to make Dasein exemplary, and Marion cites it without saying that it does. The unit is mediation throughout. Its Heidegger is the Heidegger of 1925, and the one sentence it takes from SZ (SZ 7) comes with a later note that unsettles it.

The turn from Husserl to Heidegger before 1927 has one index, the inversion of phenomenology's relation to ontology, which points to a displacement from knowledge of phenomena to knowledge of their mode of exhibition (R II.2 ¶3). SZ supports the displacement in its own terms. Phenomenology's "formal sense" is "to let that which shows itself be seen from itself, just as it shows itself from itself" (SZ 34), a description of a way of exhibiting, not of a domain. But SZ does not call the result "phenomenality". Its name for what phenomenology exhibits is "the being of entities, its sense, its modifications and derivatives" (SZ 35). Marion's pairing, phenomenon and phenomenality, which will be superposed on entity and being in II.5, is R's own vocabulary placed on SZ's formal concept.

### 3 - The "Reduced Phenomenon," Reduced to Present Objectivity

Heidegger repeats that the principle of phenomenology is the return to the things themselves; Husserl does not respect it, because in Ideen I it competes with the principle of all principles; Heidegger's 1962 comment makes that principle a methodological one, legitimated by transcendental subjectivity (R II.3 ¶1, nn. 27–29). Two of Marion's three loci for "Heidegger repeats" are SZ (§7, 27 and 34), the third is *Zur Sache des Denkens* (1969). SZ states the maxim, "To the things themselves!", against "free-floating constructions" and "pseudo-questions" (SZ 27–28), and gives it the formal sense of SZ 34. It never mentions the principle of all principles, and it does not set two Husserlian principles against each other. Marion grants that for Husserl there is no split between the principles and that it becomes visible "only from an already Heideggerian standpoint", and the standpoint is 1962, not 1927. This is mediation, and Marion is candid about it. What SZ contributes is negative: SZ 147's derivation of intuition, the seeing of essences included, from understanding (see chapter I) is the 1927 form of the objection Marion attributes to the 1962 text. A reader who wants SZ's version of "the principle of all principles concerns method, not content" will find it there, not at SZ 27.

Three remarks establish the domination of consciousness over phenomenality in Husserl: givenness in the present as bodily actuality, intuition as the tribunal of appearance, and the phenomenon defined from lived experience (R II.3 ¶2). Each has an SZ counterpart that R does not cite, and each counterpart is a critique made from time, not from appearing. The privilege of the present is SZ's *Anwesenheit* (§6) and the vulgar concept of time as a sequence of nows (§81). The tribunal of intuition is what SZ 147 demotes. And the equivocation of *Erscheinung*, appearance as the lived experience and as the appearing object, has its counterpart in §7A, which separates phenomenon from appearance (*Erscheinung*) as announcement of something that does not show itself (SZ 29–31). Marion's three remarks read Husserl from the standpoint of a phenomenon that "gives itself". SZ's corresponding critiques read the tradition, Husserl included, from the standpoint of a temporal analytic. The convergence is real; the ground is different, and R's later chapters will need SZ's ground (time) and will set it aside.

Consciousness reduces the phenomenon to permanent presence, and Husserl's consciousness is "brought back to the Cartesian definition of substance", since Ideen I §49 "deliberately cites and deliberately modifies" the *Principia* (R II.3 ¶3, n. 46). Marion's note says Heidegger "notes and criticizes this submission to the tradition" in GA 20 §11 (143–145). SZ has the Cartesian definition (*substantia* as what "needs no other entity in order to be", §20, SZ 92) but applies it to Descartes's world, not to Husserl's consciousness. In SZ Husserl does not appear in the Descartes chapters at all. The charge that Husserl's consciousness is Cartesian substance is GA 20's, and chapter III will carry it into SZ's destruction of Descartes. The point to hold is that SZ makes it only by silence: §§19–21 describe a Cartesian ontology of the world that a reader of Ideen I could apply to consciousness, and SZ leaves the application unmade. Marion's "flat phenomenon", with no remainder, is his coinage for the result, and it is the foil for the next section's reading of §7.

### 4 - From the Inapparent Phenomenon to the "Phenomenon of Being"

SZ §7 retains as the meaning of "phenomenon" "that which shows itself in itself, the manifest", but this is not Husserl's presence, Marion argues, for two reasons. (a) "The phenomenon *gives itself* of itself and from its own visibility, far from being reducible to presence for a consciousness", and Heidegger "does not mention anything like consciousness here". (b) Coveredness is the counter-concept to phenomenon, so that the phenomenon in the distinctive sense is what does not show itself at first, the being of entities (R II.4 ¶1, nn. 49–50 / SZ 28, 31, 35, 36). Point (b) is SZ's text, quoted correctly (SZ 35, 36, checked), and Marion's reading of it is a faithful reconstruction. Point (a) carries three moves of R's own. First, the formula. Martineau has "what shows itself in itself" (ce-qui-se-montre-en-lui-même) for SZ 28. Marion changes Martineau's *lui-même* to the reflexive *soi-même* (both "itself"; the French reflexive points the phrase back to the phenomenon's own initiative), then writes "showing itself of itself" (se-montrant-de-soi-même), and his n. 49 says he understands the phrase as "from itself", following Hyppolite. The "from itself" is in SZ, but at SZ 34, in the formal sense of phenomenology ("to let what shows itself be seen from itself"), not in the definition of the phenomenon at SZ 28. Second, "gives itself": SZ says "shows itself" throughout §7, and Marion's paraphrase changes the verb. This is the first place in the book where SZ's showing is restated as giving, and it is unmarked (see guard 2). Third, "nothing like consciousness": SZ 28 adds at once that entities "can show themselves from themselves in various ways, according to the mode of access to them" (Martineau: "suivant le mode d'accès à lui"). SZ's self-showing is relative to access, and §7C makes the access to the being of entities the interpretation of Dasein. The self-showing is not decided "upstream of all consciousness". It is decided in an access that SZ does not call consciousness and does not dispense with. Marion's marginal note from GA 2, that what remains hidden is "the truth of being", is unchecked and later than 1927.

Phenomena are not lying open, the ways to the things are not laid out, the phenomenon is "enigmatic", and "precisely because phenomena are proximally and for the most part *not* given, there is need of phenomenology" (R II.4 ¶2, nn. 51–52 / GA 20, 119, 188–189; GA 24, 446; SZ 36). The last sentence is SZ's (SZ 36, checked), and it uses the word Marion needs. "Given" appears in §7 exactly once in this sense, and negatively. Phenomena are at first not given; phenomenology exists because they are not. Marion quotes the sentence and reads it as part of a doctrine of "the play of the apparent with the inapparent". SZ's sentence supports that reading and puts a limit on R's later step. If phenomenology is needed because phenomena are not given, then the work of phenomenology is not to receive a givenness but to exhibit what is covered (*Aufweisung*, SZ 35). The "enigma" is GA 24's word (§21, 446), from a context on time, not SZ's.

The 1925 formula taken up in SZ, "as much semblance, so much being", connects the two cuts: what in the most proper sense remains hidden is "the being of entities", so that the return to the things themselves comes back to "the phenomenality of being itself"; Heidegger dares once to call being a phenomenon, "this phenomenon, 'being'" (R II.4 ¶3, nn. 53–55). SZ prints the formula with scare quotes around being: "as much semblance, so much 'being'" (Wieviel Schein jedoch, soviel »Sein«; SZ 36, checked). Marion's n. 53 quotes it without them. The quotation marks matter to Marion's use. In SZ the "being" that semblance attests is the being of what was once uncovered and is still visible "only as semblance", the uncoveredness of an entity, not being as such. Marion moves from it to "the being of entities" of SZ 35 as though they were one. The audacity of calling being a phenomenon is also SZ's, and in the published book, not only in GA 20, 423: what must be called phenomenon "in a distinctive sense" is "the *being* of entities" (SZ 35). On the main point R reconstructs SZ faithfully. On the formula, R removes the marks by which SZ kept its "being" provisional.

At Zähringen in 1973 this is called a phenomenology of the inapparent. Being is not perceivable, "the being of entities 'is' not itself an entity" (SZ 6), and inquiry into being must seek a goal other than uncovering, since uncoveredness of entities is distinguished from disclosedness of their being (R II.4 ¶4, nn. 56–60 / GA 24, 102, 461–462; SZ 6). The SZ loci are correct. "Being, as what is asked about, demands its own mode of exhibition, which differs essentially from the uncovering of entities" (SZ 6), and "being can never be 'explained' from entities" (SZ 196, 207). Two moves are R's. The first is "phenomenology of the inapparent", a 1973 formula placed over a 1927 text, which is retroactive in the strict sense, though Marion gives the date. The second is the identification of being with the nothing, which Marion says the 1929 lecture makes and SZ "already senses". SZ has the nothing in the analysis of anxiety (§40, see II.7), but it does not identify being with it. The distinction of uncoveredness and disclosedness is drawn by Marion from GA 24 §22. SZ draws it too (§44, SZ 220–221), with a difference that matters for chapter IV: in SZ disclosedness is first Dasein's (the "there"), and the disclosedness of being is grounded in it. In GA 24 it is "the disclosedness of its being", the entity's. Marion's question at the close, how to make phenomenal what can never become visible as a present entity, is put in SZ's terms and is SZ's question. Its answer, in SZ, is the interpretation of Dasein's temporality, which the closing questions of SZ 437 leave open.

### 5 - The Two Senses of the Reduction

The Heideggerian phenomenon has depth: being is to be "read off" (*ablesen*) the entity and placed in "fore-sight" (*Vor-sicht*), which Marion glosses as gleaning and as the attention that looks ahead toward a horizon of danger; the phenomenological gaze transgresses the entity toward its being, and that transcendence must be understood as Dasein's self-transcendence (R II.5 ¶1, nn. 61–64 / GA 20, 423, 186; SZ 38; GA 24, 23). Marion's glosses are drawn from ordinary French and German. SZ has a technical sense for the second word. In §32 interpretation is grounded in a fore-having, a fore-sight and a fore-conception: fore-sight "cuts" what is held in fore-having "toward a definite interpretability" (SZ 150, checked). It is the hermeneutic fore-structure, not a precaution, and SZ uses it to define sense: "Sense is the upon-which of a projection, structured by fore-having, fore-sight and fore-conception, from which something becomes intelligible as something" (SZ 151). Marion's gloss replaces SZ's fore-structure by an image of vigilance, and the replacement loses what SZ will need to answer R's question in II.6, since the sense of being is defined through that structure. On transcendence Marion cites SZ 38, "being is the *transcendens* pure and simple", and concludes (n. 64) that being's transcendence "must be understood as a transcendence of Dasein". SZ 38 distinguishes the two in the next sentence: "The transcendence of the being of Dasein is a distinctive one, insofar as in it lies the possibility and necessity of the most radical *individuation*." Dasein's transcendence is a distinctive case, not the meaning of being's. The identification comes from GA 24 (n. 64 quotes §20, on the ecstatic-horizonal character), and SZ keeps the two apart.

For the second sense of the reduction Marion quotes GA 24 §5 "in full, because it is the hinge of his whole book" (Commentary R): for Husserl, the reduction leads the gaze back from the natural attitude to transcendental conscious life; "for us [on the contrary]" it leads back "from the apprehension of the entity as always determined to the understanding of the being of that entity". "Heidegger therefore remains a phenomenologist, since he takes up the reduction." Husserl, who sees one reduction with two terms, misses Heidegger's three terms in two reductions, the Husserlian one from the thesis of the world to the phenomenon, and the Heideggerian one from the entity to the sense of being (R II.5 ¶2, nn. 65–67 / GA 24, 29). The quotation checks against GA 24, 29, and it is cut at both ends. The sentence before it defines the reduction as "leading the investigating gaze back from the *naively apprehended* entity to being", and adds: "In this we tie into a central term of Husserl's phenomenology *in wording, not in substance*." The paragraph after it says that the reduction "is not the only, not even the central element of the phenomenological method", because turning the gaze away from entities needs a positive leading toward being itself: being "must in each case be brought into view in a free projection" (GA 24, 29; the corpus text breaks off in mid-sentence after this and resumes at p. 36). Marion inserts "[on the contrary]" and drops the disclaimer that would have made it Heidegger's own. Within the quotation, Heidegger's phrase means "from the apprehension of the entity, *however* determined" (von der wie immer bestimmten Erfassung des Seienden). Marion's French has "as always determined" (comme toujours déterminé). Whether the rendering is his or the published translation's cannot be settled here, but his argument rests on what it hides. In Heidegger's sentence the apprehension of the entity may be naive or reduced; it does not matter, because the leading back starts from the entity as it is apprehended, however that may be. Marion's three-term structure needs Husserl's reduction as the first stage, and GA 24 starts "from the naively apprehended entity". The text R calls the hinge of the book thus says: that the reduction is Husserl's in name only; that it does not presuppose Husserl's; and that it is not central. Marion's conclusion, "Heidegger therefore remains a phenomenologist, since he takes up the reduction", is selection carried to the point of contradicting the cited text. SZ adds a further fact against the redoubling. Its analytic is to begin not from a reduced sphere but from Dasein "in its average everydayness", which "is *not nothing*, but a positive phenomenal character of this entity" (§9, SZ 43). Husserl's complaint that Heidegger stayed short of the reduction has a textual basis in SZ's starting point, even if it mistakes its sense. Marion's reply, that Heidegger went beyond the reduction rather than short of it, is defensible as an account of where SZ ends. As an account of where it begins it is not, and SZ, which never uses the word, cannot be said to "take up the reduction" at all.

### 6 - The Redoubled Reduction — Dasein

The analytic of Dasein escapes the suspicion of anthropology because it answers the injunction that the question of being must be "constructed"; the apparatus leads everything given back to being, so that "a certain manner of reduction is being practised" from the outset (R II.6 ¶1, nn. 68–69 / GA 20, 193; SZ 5). The SZ locus is "the question of the sense of being is to be *posed*" (SZ 5), with the working-out of the question that follows. Marion's word "construct" points to the positive element that GA 24 §5 sets beside the reduction in the paragraph he did not quote, the "free projection" in which being is brought into view (GA 24, 29). R thus uses the second element of the method while leaving out the sentence that ranks the reduction below it. The step from "the question must be worked out" to "a reduction is being practised" is R's. SZ's working-out of the question does not lead the given back to being; it fixes what the question asks about, what it interrogates and what it seeks to find out (SZ 5–6). Marion's gloss of "posed" (*gestellt*) by the enframing (*Gestell*), the later word for the essence of technology, is a pun across thirty years, and Marion flags it as a resonance. It does no harm, provided it is not read as SZ's meaning.

The question has three terms, not two, and the 1925 course, "more explicit here than *Sein und Zeit*", names them: what is to be found out, the sense of being; what is asked about, the being of entities; what is interrogated, the entity itself. Heidegger's reduction therefore "relaunches the entity twice": from the entity to its being, then beyond the entity to the sense of being, "being without regard for the entity", as *Zeit und Sein* will say (R II.6 ¶2, nn. 70–71 / GA 20, 195). The mapping checks against both texts. GA 20 numbers the three terms (Erfragtes, Gefragtes, Befragtes), and SZ names the same three in the same roles (SZ 5–6, checked). "More explicit" holds only of the numbering. The departure lies in what Marion makes of the third term. He insists that the sense of being is not the sense "of" being but "being directly as sense", and that the question leads "to being without regard for the entity". SZ answers both claims in §32. Sense is "the upon-which of a projection … from which something becomes intelligible as something" (SZ 151). And: "when we ask about the sense of being, the inquiry … asks about being itself insofar as it enters into the intelligibility of Dasein. The sense of being can never be set in opposition to entities, or to being as the supporting 'ground' of entities" (SZ 152, checked). The first half of the sentence supports Marion against a reading of "sense" as a third thing beside being. SZ does ask about being itself. The second half contradicts him. The sense of being is not reached by leaving the entity behind, and "being without regard for the entity" is a formula SZ excludes by name. Marion takes the phrase from *Zeit und Sein* (1962) and places it in the structure of SZ's question as its goal from the start. That is retroactive reading, and in this case SZ itself refutes it.

Which entity? Dasein, not by an anthropological bias but because it is ontically distinguished by being ontological; SZ is "more reticent" here, and the privilege must be said "phenomenologically", through intentionality as "the *ratio cognoscendi* of transcendence" (R II.6 ¶3, nn. 72–76 / GA 20, 199–200; SZ 12, 311; GA 24, 379, 91, 322, 444; GA 9, 134; GA 26, 20). SZ is not reticent on the privilege. §4 states a threefold priority, ontic, ontological and ontic-ontological as the condition of all ontologies (SZ 13, checked), and "the ontic distinction of Dasein lies in its being ontological" (SZ 12). What SZ withholds is the phenomenological ground Marion supplies, intentionality. It mentions intentionality once, to defer it: how "the intentionality of 'consciousness' is grounded in the ecstatic temporality of Dasein" will be shown in the division that was not published (SZ 363 n.). The analysis Marion uses is GA 24 §9, which is the analysis SZ §69b defers. So R's "phenomenological, not anthropological" privilege of Dasein is drawn from the course that stands in for the missing Division Three. That is legitimate, and it should be stated as such. As a reading of SZ the unit is an extension, carrying into SZ what SZ promised and did not print. One of Marion's loci gives the other side. "The entity that we ourselves are is ontologically the farthest" (SZ 311, checked; R n. 73), and SZ grounds this in care. Dasein's privilege does not make it a transparent witness. Marion's "chief witness" in the inquiry into the sense of being overlooks that SZ makes the witness the hardest entity to read.

### 7 - The Redoubled Reduction — the Nothing

The second tactic is the 1929 lecture *What Is Metaphysics?* In 1927 anxiety singularizes Dasein against the totality of entities; in 1929 it works on entities as a whole, from a result already obtained by boredom, so that the reduction passes, like physics from fission to fusion, from one entity to any entity; anxiety repels entities and refers to being (R II.7 ¶1, nn. 77–82 / GA 9, 106, 110–114; SZ §40, 184 ff.). The lecture is not in the corpus, and its text is checked only as R quotes it. The contrast with 1927 can be checked, and it is overdrawn. In SZ §40 anxiety already concerns entities in their totality. "Nothing of what is ready-to-hand and present-at-hand within the world functions as that in the face of which anxiety is anxious. The totality of involvements of the ready-to-hand and present-at-hand uncovered within the world is, as such, of no consequence at all. It sinks into itself" (SZ 186, checked). And the nothing is there: "if the nothing, that is, the world as such, emerges as that in the face of which anxiety is anxious, this means: that in the face of which anxiety is anxious is being-in-the-world itself" (SZ 187). The singularizing comes after this, as its consequence: "Anxiety individualizes Dasein to its ownmost being-in-the-world" (SZ 187). What changes in 1929, on Marion's own quotations, is the direction in which the analysis is read: from the nothing back to Dasein's being-in-the-world in 1927, from the nothing to the being of entities in 1929. Marion grants half of this when he says Dasein's field "already concerns entities in their totality by right from 1927". His fission and fusion image needs the widening to be new in 1929, and SZ 186 shows that it is not. Boredom (GA 29/30) is not in the corpus.

The 1929 reduction leads from entities as a whole to the being of entities, since "in the being of entities the nihilation of the nothing occurs"; its third term, what one wants to find out, is ontological difference, which Marion offers "as a simple hypothesis", supported by Heidegger's additions to the lecture of 1931 and 1949 and by *Vom Wesen des Grundes* (R II.7 ¶2, nn. 83–85 / GA 9, 115, 120, 134). The additions are later than the lecture, as Marion says, and are not in the corpus. *Vom Wesen des Grundes* is, and its p. 134 does name the difference: ontic and ontological truth belong together "on the ground of their relation to the *distinction of being and entities* (ontological difference)". The notes to that sentence in the corpus text are headed "1st edition 1929" and are later marginalia in Heidegger's copy. One of them calls the distinction ambiguous, a step toward overcoming the tradition "and yet a fateful binding back" to it. Another says the essence of truth is here grasped "from the 'distinction' as a fixed mark" instead of the distinction being overcome from the truth of being (GA 9, 134 nn. a and c). Marion's hypothesis equates SZ's sense of being with ontological difference. The equation drops the term SZ gives the sense of being, which is time: the analytic is to free "the horizon for the most original interpretation of being" (SZ 17), and the book ends asking whether "time itself reveals itself as the horizon of being" (SZ 437). Marion admits that SZ "does not always manage to decide the sense of being", and replaces the undecided answer, time, by a 1929 term that the 1929 text's own author later called a binding back. This is selection, and on a hypothesis Marion himself marks as such.

The two retrievals converge on receiving being "in pure givenness, in person and as phenomenon"; "being unfolds precisely as appearing"; and Heidegger, rejecting Husserl's reduction as non-phenomenological, can still call himself Husserl's disciple, since possibility stands higher than actuality (R II.7 ¶3, nn. 86–88 / GA 40, 108; SZ 38; GA 20, 184). Three texts of different dates are joined. "Being means appearing" is the 1935 *Introduction to Metaphysics*. The disciple is GA 20 and SZ 38 with its note of thanks. The possibility of thinking that "changes with the times" comes from a late text Marion quotes from *Zur Sache des Denkens*. Marion's n. 88 labels a quotation from GA 20 as "*Grundprobleme*, § 14, *GA*, 20", which mixes the two courses. "Pure givenness in person" describes Marion's goal, not either retrieval's. SZ's goal is the sense of being, and its word for the access to it is interpretation. SZ 36's "not given" is the reason phenomenology is needed, not the thing phenomenology delivers. The closing observation, that the word claim (*Anspruch*) "quietly anticipates" chapter VI, places a later word in Heidegger's late self-description. It belongs to R's own argument and does not claim to be SZ's.

Across chapter II, Marion makes SZ load-bearing at four places, and his reading holds at two. It holds where he reconstructs §7: ontology possible only as phenomenology, the phenomenon in the distinctive sense as the being of entities, coveredness as the counter-concept, being not an entity (SZ 6, 35–37). Those are SZ's texts, quoted correctly, and they give Marion's phenomenology of the inapparent a real anchor in 1927, even if the phrase is from 1973. It does not hold at the two places that carry the chapter's thesis. First, the "redoubled reduction": SZ never uses the word, and the text Marion calls the hinge of the book, GA 24 §5, takes up Husserl's term "in wording, not in substance", starts from the naively apprehended entity rather than from Husserl's reduced phenomenon, and declares the reduction not central. Marion's quotation omits all three points. Second, the goal of the redoubling, "being without regard for the entity": SZ 152 says that the sense of being can never be set in opposition to entities. Between these two, Marion's restatements move SZ's vocabulary toward his own. SZ's "shows itself in itself" becomes "gives itself of itself" (SZ 28); the scare quotes come off SZ's "being" (SZ 36); fore-sight loses its hermeneutic sense (SZ 150); Dasein's distinctive transcendence becomes the meaning of being's (SZ 38); and SZ's answer to the sense of being, time, gives way to ontological difference. The chapter's Heidegger is mostly GA 20 and GA 24, and Marion says so. Where SZ itself is the text, it supports a phenomenology in which being is the hidden phenomenon to be exhibited by interpretation, not a reduction that receives being in givenness.

## III. The Ego and Dasein

### 1 - The Figure of Descartes in Heidegger's Itinerary

Heidegger's relation to Descartes looks secondary beside his confrontations with Nietzsche, Hegel, Kant and Aristotle, Marion says, yet Descartes is present from 1921 to 1974 (R III.1 ¶1). For SZ the claim is easily confirmed, and it understates SZ's own plan. Descartes is one of the three stations of the destruction that SZ announces: the second division of the unpublished Part Two was to treat "the ontological foundation of Descartes' 'cogito sum' and the taking over of medieval ontology into the problematic of the 'res cogitans'" (§8, SZ 40, checked). SZ thus gives Descartes a place equal to Kant's and Aristotle's in its own design. What it does not give him is the published text: the division was not written. Marion's evidence for the relation, here and in the next three units, is therefore largely outside SZ, in the courses and seminars. Within SZ the Descartes material is §6 (SZ 22–25), §10 (SZ 45–46, 49), §14 (SZ 66) and §§19–21 (SZ 89–101), and the plan says that these are preliminary.

The course of 1921/22 already marks the limit: the *sum* is first for Descartes, but he does not stay with it, and takes it in an "indifferent, formally objective" sense, placing the weight of the question on the I rather than on the "am" (R III.1 ¶2, n. 1 / GA 61, 172–173). GA 61 is not in the corpus. The thesis Marion draws from it is SZ's thesis in its published form: Descartes "investigated the cogitare of the ego, within certain limits", but "leaves the sum wholly undiscussed, even though it is posited just as originally as the cogito" (§10, SZ 45–46). Marion's reconstruction of the early course thus reads it in the light of SZ's sentence, and the line from 1921 to 1927 he draws is supported by SZ. One difference of emphasis should be kept. SZ does not say that Descartes failed to "stay with" the *sum*; it says that he left it "undiscussed" while positing it originally. The *sum* is in SZ a neglected starting point, not one abandoned for the *ego*.

The Marburg years open and close with Descartes: the course of winter 1923/24 and the last Marburg course of summer 1928, which treats Descartes as prolonged in Leibniz, who also draws the fundamental concepts of metaphysics from the *ego cogito* without grasping the I in its specific mode of being (R III.1 ¶3, n. 2 / GA 9, 79, 89–90). The text Marion cites is "From the Last Marburg Lecture" in *Wegmarken*, which the corpus does not hold. Marion's conclusion, that Heidegger finds Descartes decisive not in the institution of the *ego* as principle but in the indeterminacy of the mode of being of that *ego*, which falls under the mode of being of objects, is again SZ's: "with the 'cogito sum' Descartes claims to provide philosophy with a new and secure ground. But what he leaves undetermined in this 'radical' beginning is the mode of being of the res cogitans, more exactly the sense of being of the 'sum'" (§6, SZ 24). Marion adds that the concealment calls for phenomenology "since phenomenology bears par excellence on what does not manifest itself". This is a bridge to chapter II's reading of §7, and it is R's. SZ's own reason for interrogating Descartes is the destruction of the history of ontology (§6), not the phenomenology of the inapparent.

The late texts confirm the constancy: Le Thor (1969), with Descartes between the Greeks and Hegel and Fichte's absolutizing of the *cogito*; Zähringen (1973), where subjectivity, as *fundamentum inconcussum* since Descartes, is the barrier to the question of being; and *Der Fehl heiliger Namen* (1974), on the treatises on method (R III.1 ¶4, nn. 3–5). None of these is SZ, and only Zähringen is in the corpus, in English. The thread that ties them to SZ is a phrase: "the entity posited by him as fundamentum inconcussum" (unshaken foundation; §6, SZ 25, checked). Marion will contest the phrase as a reading of Descartes (III.5). Here it shows that the late seminar's formula is SZ's, and that Marion's account of constancy rests on SZ more than his citations show. His own caution, that the abundance of texts establishes the encounter but not its reason, is the right one, and SZ supplies the reason he goes on to seek: the plan of §8.

### 2 - The Phenomenological Motive of the Original Confrontation

While Heidegger was criticizing Descartes at Marburg, Husserl was approving him at Freiburg, so that any discussion of Descartes was a discussion with Husserl; Heidegger takes the second of two possible directions, in which Husserl's phenomenology is not fully phenomenological because it remains captive to Cartesian decisions (R III.2 ¶1). The unit is R's thesis for the chapter, and it rests on a silence of SZ. SZ never names Husserl in the Descartes chapters. The one place where SZ sets Husserl next to Descartes is §10, which Marion does not use for this purpose. There the critique of the "subject" runs from Descartes's undiscussed *sum* (SZ 45–46) to Husserl's and Scheler's interpretations of the person, which "no longer ask the question of the being of the person" (SZ 47 and n.), and back to the *cogitationes*, which also "remain ontologically undetermined" (SZ 49). §10 gives Marion's thesis a textual hook in SZ itself. Marion takes the thesis instead from GA 20 (next unit), and the reader should know that the published book makes the connection only by juxtaposition, not by argument.

The 1925 course puts to Husserl's region of consciousness the question it had put in 1921 to Descartes's *ego*: what does being mean when consciousness is called a region of absolute being? Husserl names an "abyss of sense" between consciousness and reality but reasons within a pair, certainty and contingency, that belongs to the being of worldly reality, and he leans on Descartes's definition of substance to do so (R II.2 ¶2, nn. 8–12 / GA 20, 140–145). This is mediation throughout, and the Husserl side is treated in the companion run (runs/marion-rd-on-hua3.md, chapters II–III). For SZ the unit's interest is what it lacks. SZ has the Cartesian definition of substance, "an entity that is such that, in order to be, it needs no other entity" (§20, SZ 92, with *Principia* I §51), and applies it to Descartes's world. It does not apply it to Husserl's consciousness. The charge that Ideen I §49 borrows Descartes's definition for consciousness is GA 20's, and SZ leaves it out.

Five remarks on Husserl's citation of *Principia* I §51 in Ideen I §49: Husserl drops *alia* and puts *res* in quotation marks; the doctored formula proves that a Cartesian determination unsuited to consciousness is being used, since for Descartes substantiality covers extended things too and every finite substance depends on God's concurrence (R III.2 ¶3, nn. 10–11). The unit is Marion the Cartesian scholar on Husserl. Its note 11 makes a claim about SZ: the difference between finite and infinite substance is one "that Heidegger agrees with Husserl to pass over in silence". SZ does not pass over it. "Between the two entities there is an 'infinite' difference of their being, and yet we address the created as well as the creator *as entities*" (§20, SZ 92, checked), and the next page sets the problem of the analogy of being, with a note to Cajetan (SZ 93 and n.). Heidegger's point is that Descartes evades the ontological question the difference raises. He does not ignore the difference; he makes it the place where Descartes's indifference to the sense of being shows. Marion's own interpretation of Descartes (*Sur le prisme métaphysique de Descartes*, which has its own commentary in the corpus) makes the finite and infinite distinction central. His note credits that work with what SZ already marks, and denies SZ what it has.

Heidegger's 1925 course notes Husserl's borrowing, and here it is Heidegger who defends Cartesian orthodoxy against Husserl's adjustments; Husserl strays more radically by taking over the Cartesian idea of an absolute science, so that Descartes stands as the sole obstacle between Husserl and full phenomenology, which Heidegger must destroy (R III.2 ¶4, nn. 12–16 / GA 20, 145, 139). The "affinity" (*Verwandtschaft*) Marion quotes is GA 20's word (n. 16). SZ has neither the word nor the argument. Its account of how the modern ideal of knowledge rules Descartes's ontology of the world is that mathematical knowledge is taken as the access to the true being of entities because it grasps what "always remains" (§21, SZ 95–96). That account is close to Marion's "Cartesian ideal of *mathesis universalis*". It concerns the world and not consciousness, and SZ does not draw from it a verdict on Husserl. Marion's sentence that Descartes is "the sole obstacle and stumbling block" between Husserl and Heidegger is an extension of GA 20, and it changes the order of SZ, in which Descartes is one station among three and comes after Kant in the order of the destruction (§6, SZ 22–25).

Descartes occupies Heidegger because thinking him means destroying the *ego*, to make appear the mode of being of what must replace it; destroying the *ego* opens access to Dasein (R III.2 ¶5). SZ supports the formula and gives its terms. The destruction is a "return through the history of ontology" by which the ontological foundations of the *cogito sum* are brought out, and it shows "why" Descartes thought that the absolute "being-certain" of the *cogito* dispensed him from asking about its sense of being (§6, SZ 24, checked). Marion's "destroying the *ego* … undertakes to free its ontological dignity" goes beyond SZ in one word. SZ's destruction is to free the question, not the dignity of the *ego*. Chapter III ¶6 and ¶7 will need that dignity, and the word prepares them.

### 3 - The First Failure: The Indeterminacy of the "ego sum"

In SZ Descartes appears as the extreme counter-case of the ontology of the world, but the reproach comes first, in the Introduction, about the *cogito sum*; three consequences follow: the failure is first about the *ego*; the *ego* and the *res extensa* fail alike, since the *ego* borrows certainty from the domain of extension where method first tested it; and the two failures anticipate Husserl's two regions (R III.3 ¶1, nn. 17–22). The order of the reproach is correct and well observed. SZ criticizes the undetermined *sum* in §6 (SZ 24) and §10 (SZ 45–46) before the analysis of the *res extensa* (§§19–21), and calls Descartes's ontology of the world its "perhaps most extreme" execution (§18, SZ 89, checked). Consequence (b) departs from SZ. SZ's genealogy of the undetermined *sum* is not a transfer of certainty from extension to the *ego*. It is a transfer of medieval ontology: Descartes carries out his fundamental reflections "by way of a transference of medieval ontology to the entity posited by him as *fundamentum inconcussum*. The res cogitans is determined ontologically as ens, and the sense of being of ens is fixed for medieval ontology in the understanding of ens as ens creatum" (§6, SZ 25, checked). Marion's version, in which the *ego* takes its certainty from the method's object and so from the "grey ontology" of the *Regulae*, is the thesis of his own Descartes books placed in SZ's place. Both diagnoses yield an undetermined *sum*. They name different causes, created being in SZ and methodical certainty in R, and the difference returns in III.6, where Marion faults Heidegger for reading Cartesian finitude through createdness. Consequence (c) is GA 20's. Marion's note 22 adds a marginal note to §12 ("a retrospective interpretation", GA 2, 78), which is unchecked.

The *ego* is characterized only epistemically; Heidegger never contests the certainty of the *cogito*, but its certainty is ontologically indeterminate and indifferent to modes of being, and by its univocity it reinforces the indeterminacy of the *sum*; Descartes's first failure "is accomplished by failing itself" (R III.3 ¶2). Marion's observation that SZ does not enter the debate on the certainty of the *cogito* holds. SZ's interest is in why the "absolute 'being-certain'" of the *cogito* seemed to dispense Descartes from the question (SZ 24). The scare quotes are SZ's, and they make the point Marion makes: certainty is a mode of being that is not examined. The univocity of certainty and the comparison with the medieval univocity of being are R's. SZ's own term for the medieval background is the analogy of being, which it treats in §20 (SZ 93). Marion's "univocity" is a sharper and partly different charge, and it belongs to his reading of Descartes, not to SZ. The reply to Hobbes is Descartes's text and is outside this run.

If Descartes does not think his *sum*, he thinks it implicitly on the model of the intraworldly entity, by a back-radiation of the understanding of world onto the interpretation of Dasein; the *ego* "loses itself at the very moment it finds itself" (R III.3 ¶3, n. 22 / SZ 16, 21). The SZ term is quoted correctly: Dasein's tendency to understand itself from the world brings "the ontological back-radiation (*Rückstrahlung*) of the understanding of world onto the interpretation of Dasein" (§5, SZ 16, checked). In SZ this back-radiation is a structure of Dasein, not a fault of Descartes: Dasein "is ontically 'closest' to itself, ontologically farthest" (SZ 15–16), and every interpretation of it is exposed to the same pull. Marion applies a general structure to one case, which SZ licenses in §6, where Descartes's *res cogitans* is the historical instance. The closing formula is Marion's and fair to SZ.

### 4 - The Second Failure: The Permanence of the Intraworldly Entity

The interpretation of intraworldly entities misses the world and substitutes the presence-at-hand of what remains; presence-at-hand follows from readiness-to-hand by impoverishment; Descartes prescribes to the world its true being from an idea, being as constant presence-at-hand, neither legitimated nor unveiled in its origin (R III.4 ¶1, n. 24 / SZ 95–99). The unit is a close reconstruction of §21, and the quotations check. SZ: what has the character of "constant remaining, as remanens capax mutationum" (as what remains, capable of changes), properly is, and mathematics knows it (SZ 96, checked). The Latin formula is Heidegger's gloss, not a quotation from Descartes. Marion's summary of §§15–18 adds one word, "short-lived", which is a judgement on the later fate of the analysis of equipment. SZ does not call its analysis provisional, though it calls the whole analytic "preparatory".

Three remarks as a historian of philosophy. (a) Heidegger cites the remaining of the *res extensa* through every change, but Descartes attributes permanence first to the *ego*, before the wax. (b) Substance does not affect us; Descartes confuses its ontological and ontic senses; behind this "insignificant difference of signification" hides an inability to master "the fundamental problem of being", to which Heidegger's own copy adds the note "ontological difference". (c) The proof of the existence of the external world shows that the world has been reduced to a representation awaiting actuality (R III.4 ¶2, nn. 25–31). (a) is an extension. SZ's text is *Principia* II §4, on hardness, weight and colour taken away from a body while extension remains (§19, SZ 91 and n.). The wax of the Second Meditation is not cited in SZ. Marion's correction concerns Descartes's text, and it confirms SZ's thesis, as he says. (b) is a faithful reconstruction of §20: "*substantia* functions now in an ontological, now in an ontic, but mostly in a blurred ontico-ontological signification. Behind this insignificant difference of signification lies hidden the failure to master the fundamental problem of being" (SZ 94, checked). The marginal note "ontological difference" cannot be checked. It is what Marion makes "decisive", and it is later than 1927. SZ's own word is *Unterschied der Bedeutung*, a difference of signification, and its "fundamental problem of being" is the sense of being in its unity and modes, which §20 calls the problem of analogy (SZ 93). Marion's conclusion, that Descartes "failed to confront the difference between being and entities", is reached through the note, not through the text (guards 4 and 5). (c) is SZ's own argument, which Marion presents as turning Descartes's references "into arguments for *Sein und Zeit*". SZ says so in its words: "The 'scandal of philosophy' is not that this proof has yet to be given, but that such proofs are expected and attempted again and again" (§43a, SZ 205, checked).

Descartes's two failures make one, having grasped the being of Dasein in the same mode as that of the *res extensa*; and Kant follows him, since "consciousness of my *Dasein*" means for Kant consciousness of my presence-at-hand in Descartes's sense (R III.4 ¶3, n. 33 / SZ 203). The quotation checks: "'Consciousness of my Dasein' means for Kant: consciousness of my being-present-at-hand in Descartes's sense. The term 'Dasein' means the presence-at-hand of consciousness as well as the presence-at-hand of things" (SZ 203). Marion lets the point stand without comment, and it is worth one. SZ takes the word "Dasein" from the tradition in which it means presence-at-hand and gives it to the one entity that is not present-at-hand. The appropriation of the word is itself a destruction in the sense of §6, carried out on a single term. Marion's note 33 adds that §43a "follows Husserl" here (LU V Appendix, CM §§40–41). SZ cites neither, and the claim is R's.

### 5 - Dasein as "Destruction" of the "ego"

The two failures come from misconstruing ontological difference, "at least in its negative formulation: being is never explicable from entities" (with a marginal note, again, "ontological difference"); idealism that leaves the subject undetermined is "methodologically no less naive than the crudest realism"; the *Da-* of Dasein echoes the *ego* as "here itself", but where the *ego* gives the rest only as *cogitatum*, Dasein gives it being; the *res cogitans* coincides with Dasein "neither ontically nor ontologically" (R III.5 ¶1, nn. 35–37 / SZ 208, 320 n., 66). The SZ quotations check (SZ 208, SZ 66). The first move is the note: "being is never explicable from entities" is SZ's (SZ 196, 207–208), and what makes it "ontological difference" is the unchecked marginal note. The third move is Marion's reading of the word Dasein, and it runs against SZ's own account of the "there". "'Here' and 'there' are possible only in a 'there' (*Da*), that is, if there is an entity that, as the being of the 'there', has disclosed spatiality" (§28, SZ 132, checked); "Dasein is its disclosedness" (SZ 133). The *Da* is not a here, privileged or otherwise. It is the disclosedness in which here and there are possible. Marion's contrast, "the *ego* gives to be thought, Dasein gives being", is R's formula, and SZ would not accept that Dasein "gives being". In SZ being "is" only in the understanding of the entity to whose being something like an understanding of being belongs, while entities are "independent of the experience, acquaintance and grasp through which they are disclosed" (§43, SZ 183). The dependence of being on understanding is not a giving of being by Dasein, and SZ keeps it apart from any dependence of entities on Dasein. The unit's last sentence quotes SZ 66 correctly, and its thesis, that the whole Descartes interpretation can be stated in the opposition of the *ego cogito* to Dasein, is a reconstruction SZ supports.

The ontic opposition: the *ego* is a *res*, Dasein admits nothing real in itself; the *ego* is born of theory and doubt, Dasein relates to the world first in concern, and knowing presupposes a "deficiency" of concernful dealings; the *res cogitans* escapes space, Dasein is spatial by de-distancing; Heidegger "never sets this out explicitly" (R III.5 ¶2, nn. 38–40 / SZ 211–212, 230, 11, 61–62, 104, 111). The three lines are a reconstruction from scattered SZ texts, and each is supported: knowing as founded in a deficiency of concern (§13, SZ 61–62, checked), spatiality as de-distancing and directionality grounded in being-in-the-world, which "has nothing to do with a determinacy restricted in advance to a worldless subject" (§23, SZ 110). Marion's assembly is fair. His claim that SZ never sets the contrast out is literally true, but §14 states its principle ("the res cogitans, which coincides with Dasein neither ontically nor ontologically", SZ 66), and Marion has just quoted it. The note on Franck's book on space is a pointer, not an argument.

The ontological opposition. (a) Dasein's being is at issue for it; Heidegger is "textually wrong" to call the *ego* a *fundamentum inconcussum*, though Descartes does seek a firm foundation; the *ego* closes all play in self-certainty. (b) Dasein's existence is not the traditional *existentia*, which means presence-at-hand; Descartes takes existence as self-evident. (c) Dasein is in a world; Descartes reaches the *ego* on the hypothesis of its independence from every world, so Heidegger may speak of a "worldless I". The *ego* is the entity for which its being is not at issue; Dasein stands to it "in a relation of destruction" (R III.5 ¶3, nn. 41–48 / SZ 12, 24, 42, 13, 316, 388). Point (b) reconstructs §9 exactly: if we choose "existence" for the being of this entity, "this title does not and cannot have the ontological signification of the traditional term existentia", which means presence-at-hand (SZ 42, checked). Point (c) holds: "not too much but *too little* is 'presupposed' for the ontology of Dasein if one 'starts' from a worldless I" (SZ 315–316, checked). Point (a) contains a correction of Heidegger as a historian. SZ's phrase is "the entity posited by him as fundamentum inconcussum" (SZ 25). Marion says Descartes's own words are of a firm and unshaken foundation, not this formula. That is a question about Descartes's text, which this run does not check. In SZ the phrase names what Descartes's position amounts to in its function, and Marion grants the function. The contrast of "play" and certainty, with the image of wood that "plays", is Marion's own. It fits SZ's "at issue" (*es geht um*), though SZ does not speak of play.

### 6 - Dasein as Confirmation of the "ego"

A relation of destruction makes sense only if an ontology is already at work in the *ego*, and only if the *ego* keeps a reserve of being that has its place in the new beginning (R III.6 ¶1). The first condition is SZ's: destruction bears on the history of ontology and "is not meant to bury the past in nullity; it has a *positive* intent" (§6, SZ 22–23). The second is R's. SZ's destruction loosens the tradition to win back "the original experiences in which the first, and thereafter guiding, determinations of being were won" (SZ 22). It does not promise that what is destroyed will return in the new beginning. Marion's counter-movement, which §5 prepared, reads the positive intent of destruction as a promise of restitution. That is a re-inscription of §6, and the rest of the section depends on it.

(a) Dasein exists finitely, toward its death; the *ego cogito* is also finite, "since I am finite" (*cum sim finitus*), and its finitude provokes doubt and so institutes entities as *cogitata*; "the pertinence of the comparison stays masked to and by Heidegger, who envisages the *ego*'s finitude only within the horizon of ancient-Christian anthropology and reduces the relation between finite and infinite substance to efficient production" (R III.6 ¶2, n. 47 / SZ 48, 24, 92, 95). The SZ loci exist. §10 names "the ancient-Christian anthropology" as what blocks the question of the being of Dasein (SZ 48–49), and §6 traces the *res cogitans* to the *ens creatum* (SZ 25). But Marion's verb, "masked", misreports SZ. SZ does not overlook Cartesian finitude. It assigns it a cause, created being, and holds that this cause is precisely what keeps finitude from being thought existentially. SZ's own finitude is temporal: "Original time is finite" (§65, SZ 331, checked). Marion's comparison holds at the level of the word. At the level of the concept SZ has an answer to it, which Marion states and rejects without examining. This is contestation: the objection is stated; SZ's resource (createdness as the medieval ontology that §6 destroys) is named but not met.

(b) Dasein's being is each time mine, so that addressing Dasein must include the personal pronoun, "I am, you are"; Dasein can say *-sein* only by saying *ich bin*, hence *ego sum*; Descartes ties thinking and existence in the unsubstitutable first person. (c) Death is "the possibility of the pure and simple impossibility of Dasein", and Cartesian free will knows the possibility of the impossible before divine omnipotence (R III.6 ¶3, nn. 48–51 / SZ 12, 42, 250). Both SZ quotations check (SZ 42, SZ 250). The step from "ich bin" to "ego sum" omits SZ's qualification of the pronoun. Saying "I" is right ontically, SZ grants, but "the 'I' may be understood only in the sense of a non-committal *formal indication* of something that, in each case, in the phenomenal context of being, may reveal itself as its 'opposite'" (§25, SZ 116, checked), and Dasein is "proximally and for the most part" not itself but the "they". SZ's first person is a formal indication whose content is still open. Marion's *ego sum* fills it with the content SZ withholds. This is selection: Marion takes SZ 42 and leaves out SZ 116, which qualifies it. The parallel of death and Cartesian freedom in (c) is Marion's own, and it stands outside SZ.

(d) The *ego* is ontologically undetermined, but so is Dasein at first, and in decisive places SZ makes indeterminacy positive: in anxiety, whose "that in the face of which" is "wholly undetermined"; in death, whose certainty goes with "the indeterminacy of its when"; in conscience, whose caller's indeterminacy "is not nothing but a *positive* distinction"; in resoluteness, to which indeterminacy "necessarily belongs". The debate is between an ontological indeterminacy (the *ego*) and an ontic one (Dasein), and the *ego* may mime Dasein as the "they" mimes authentic Dasein (R III.6 ¶4, nn. 52–55 / SZ 116, 186, 258, 274–275, 298). Every quotation checks against SZ (SZ 186, 258, 274–275, 298). This is the most careful reconstruction in the chapter, and it uncovers a real pattern in SZ that the Descartes passages do not mention. Two points qualify it. First, Marion's first quotation is SZ 116, where the ontological horizon "for determining what is accessible in plain *giving* (*in schlichter Gebung*)" may remain undetermined. Commentary R renders this as "givenness". SZ's sentence is the one that warns against starting from "the givenness of the I" (SZ 115–116), so the indeterminacy it names is the danger of the Cartesian start, not a trait Dasein shares with the *ego*. Second, the final step is an extension with a change of category. The "they" is a mode of Dasein's own being, "an existential" (SZ 129). The *ego cogito* is an ontological interpretation of that being. Inauthentic Dasein can mime authentic Dasein because both are Dasein. A theory of the subject cannot mime Dasein in the same sense, though it can, on SZ's account, be the theoretical expression of the "they"'s self-interpretation (§25, SZ 116). Marion's mimicry needs the second reading, and he states the first.

*Ego* and Dasein meet along finitude, mineness, the possibility of the impossible and indeterminacy; their opposition along authenticity and inauthenticity belongs to Dasein's existence, so that it is not easy to decide that they are strangers (R III.6 ¶5). The summary stands on the units just treated: one point is SZ's (indeterminacy as positive), one is SZ's with its qualification cut (mineness), and two are Marion's comparisons outside SZ (finitude through doubt, impossibility through freedom). The last sentence turns the authenticity distinction into a bond, which SZ uses to separate the two modes. That is the premise of the next section.

### 7 - The Repetition of the "ego"

The destruction would not have been so urgent if Dasein had not recognized itself in the *ego*; Heidegger himself sketches the possibility: "if the 'cogito sum' is to serve as the starting point of the existential analytic of Dasein, then there is need not only of an inversion but of a new ontologico-phenomenal confirmation of its content. The first statement is then: 'sum', and in the sense: I-am-in-a-world"; the late seminars speak of thinking the Greek sense of *egō* (R III.7 ¶1, nn. 56–57 / SZ 211; Le Thor 1968; *Nietzsche II*). The quotation checks (§43c, SZ 211). Its grammar matters. "Should the 'cogito sum' serve as the starting point …" is a conditional. SZ states what such a start would require and then contrasts it with Descartes ("Descartes, by contrast, says: cogitationes are present-at-hand, and in them an ego is present-at-hand along with them as a worldless res cogitans"). It does not take the start. Marion calls it "stupefying" that Heidegger "still sketches the possibility of retranscribing the analytic of Dasein" in Cartesian terms. The sentence does sketch it, and Marion's attention to it is a real discovery. His reading turns a conditional into an intention, and the rest of the section depends on that turn. The seminars and the Nietzsche lectures are not SZ and are not checked here.

Must one recognize that in SZ the *ego*, during the destruction of its Cartesian sense, "is born for the first time to its authentic phenomenological figure"? (R III.7 ¶2). The question is R's thesis put as a question. SZ's term for what replaces the *ego* is the Self (*Selbst*), and its account of the I in §64 is that "I-saying" means the entity that I am as "I-am-in-a-world" (SZ 321, checked). Whether that is the birth of the *ego* or its replacement is the issue of the next unit. The word "born" already decides it in R's favour.

Dasein's mineness requires the personal pronoun; the I would have no legitimacy unless selfhood were conceived existentially; the Self disclosed in the reticence of resoluteness is the phenomenal ground of the question of the being of the I; "self-care" would be a tautology; the I appears either as the constancy of substance or as the Self, and "self-constancy" means nothing but anticipatory resoluteness; so the I can be destroyed or confirmed, and *ego cogito sum* is "a territory to occupy, a statement to reinterpret, a work to redo" (R III.7 ¶3, nn. 58–63 / SZ 42, 116–117, 193, 317–323; GA 2, 420). The SZ quotations check: self-care a tautology (SZ 193); "self-constancy signifies existentially nothing other than anticipatory resoluteness" (SZ 322). The marginal note (GA 2, 420) is unchecked. Marion's reconstruction of §64 is accurate, and his conclusion goes one step further than §64. SZ's I in §64 is a formal indication taken up into the Self, and its everyday form is the flight of falling: Dasein "*flees* in the face of itself into the 'they'", so that "in the 'I' the Self expresses itself that I proximally and for the most part am *not* authentically" (SZ 322, checked). The direction is from the I to the Self, and the I survives as what Dasein says, not as what it is. Marion's "territory to occupy" reverses the direction, so that the Self becomes the authentic form of the *ego*. SZ allows the reversal in one respect: it keeps the first person, and it speaks of "I-saying". It refuses it in another: it never calls the Self an *ego*, and it treats substantiality as "the sameness and constancy of something always already present-at-hand" (SZ 320). The unit is re-inscription. The Cartesian *ego* is given SZ's Self as its truth, which SZ does not grant it.

Is the I wholly determined by selfhood, and selfhood by care? Is the I exhausted as the I of a *sum*? Might there be at stake in the I another stake than being? "Is it even permitted, despite *Sein und Zeit*'s silence, to ask the question?" (R III.7 ¶4). The questions are R's, and they point to chapters V and VI. For this pair the last word counts. SZ is not silent on the question whether the I is devoted to being. It answers it at the start: Dasein is the entity for which "in its being this being is at issue" (SZ 12). What Marion calls a silence is a thesis. The question he asks "despite" SZ is a question against SZ's first definition of Dasein, and it should be read as a contestation, not as an extension into a field SZ left open.

Across chapter III, Marion has made SZ's destruction of Descartes load-bearing for two theses: that Heidegger's critique of Descartes is a critique of Husserl, and that Dasein repeats the *ego* it destroys. The reading holds where Marion reconstructs SZ's texts: the order of the reproach, *sum* before *res extensa* (§§6, 10); substance and its blurred significations (§20, SZ 94); the scandal of the proofs (§43a, SZ 205); Kant's Dasein as presence-at-hand (SZ 203); existence against *existentia* (SZ 42); the positive indeterminacies of anxiety, death, conscience and resoluteness (SZ 186, 258, 275, 298); the Self and self-constancy (SZ 322). Every one of these quotations checks. It does not hold at four points. The link to Husserl rests on GA 20; SZ makes it only by juxtaposition in §10, which Marion does not use. The cause of Descartes's undetermined *sum* is in SZ the transfer of medieval ontology, the *ens creatum* (SZ 25), not the univocity of methodical certainty, which is the thesis of Marion's own Descartes books. The finite and infinite difference that Marion says Heidegger "passes over in silence" is named in SZ 92. And the step from Dasein's "I am" to *ego sum* cuts SZ 116, where the I is only a "non-committal formal indication" that may reveal its opposite. The three marginal notes that carry the passage to ontological difference (GA 2, 78, 127, 275) cannot be checked, and SZ's own word there is a difference of signification. The chapter's main discovery stands: SZ 211's conditional, which sketches a repetition of the *cogito sum*. Marion reads it as an intention, and SZ wrote it as a condition that Descartes failed to meet.

## IV. The Question of Being or Ontological Difference

### 1 - The Breakthrough and the Difference: *Sein und Zeit*

Phenomenology's second breakthrough is Heidegger's: intentionality returned to the being of entities; ontological difference "defines Heidegger's breakthrough entirely", since it displaces phenomenology toward the thinking of being and alone permits the destruction of the history of ontology; any imprecision about where and when it appears weakens the breakthrough itself (R IV.1 ¶1, nn. 1–3). Marion's frame is *Identity and Difference* (1957), and his n. 1 quotes SZ §6 on the positive sense of destruction (SZ 22–23). The order of dependence he lays down, ontological difference first and destruction second, is not SZ's. §6 grounds the destruction in time: ancient ontology understood being from "a definite mode of time, the 'present'" (SZ 25), and the destruction is to be carried out "along the guideline of the problematic of Temporality" (SZ 24, checked, the Kant passage). SZ's breakthrough is the temporal interpretation of being, with the destruction as its historical side. Marion's demand that the breakthrough be measured by ontological difference sets a 1957 criterion over a 1927 book, and he states it as a hypothesis to be tested, not as a finding. This is a re-inscription of §6, and it determines everything that follows: if SZ must contain ontological difference in order to be a breakthrough, its absence will count as a failure, while on SZ's own terms what would count as failure is the absence of time.

Since the destruction must coincide with a breakthrough into ontological difference, and SZ §6 formulates the destruction explicitly, the difference should be formulated there too; "but does *Sein und Zeit* not precisely ignore ontological difference?" (R IV.1 ¶2, nn. 2–8). The inference holds only on the premise of the previous unit. §6 formulates the destruction and names its guideline, Temporality (*Temporalität*), not difference. On SZ's terms the question to put to §6 is whether the temporal interpretation of being is achieved, and SZ's answer is that it is reserved for the unpublished Division Three, "Time and Being" (§8, SZ 39). The question Marion puts is legitimate as his question. It is not a question SZ obliges, and Marion's doubt (a), whether a breakthrough can emerge gradually from within the unthought, applies to SZ only if SZ's project is described in the vocabulary of *Identity and Difference*.

### 2 - Emergence and Delay

On Heidegger's own word, in the foreword of 1949 to *Vom Wesen des Grundes*, the essay of 1928 "names the ontological difference" while *What Is Metaphysics?* meditates the nothing; the essay does name it, in deriving ontic and ontological truth from "the distinction of being and entities (ontological difference)"; so ontological difference seems to be named only in 1928/29, after SZ (R IV.2 ¶1, nn. 4–5 / GA 9, 123, 134). Both quotations check against the corpus text of the essay: "the latter [the lecture] thinks the nothing, the former [the essay] names the ontological difference" (Diese bedenkt das Nichts, jene nennt die ontologische Differenz; GA 9, 123), and "the distinction of being and entities (ontological difference)" (Unterschied von Sein und Seiendem (ontologische Differenz); GA 9, 134). The unit is reconstruction. It sets out the received view fairly, and the text supports it. One detail matters later. The 1928 essay itself writes *Unterschied*, distinction, and puts "ontological difference" in parentheses as its name: the word *Differenz* is introduced as a gloss on SZ's ordinary word.

The conclusion is fragile: naming is not thinking; the alethiological origin of the difference goes back to the summer course of 1927, which distinguishes "not only terminologically" the uncoveredness of an entity from the disclosedness of its being, and already traces both to ontological difference; and SZ itself says that being "demands its own mode of exhibition, which differs essentially from the uncovering of entities", with §44 as the culmination (R IV.2 ¶2, nn. 6–10 / GA 24, 102; SZ 6). The SZ sentence checks (SZ 6), and its verb is *unterscheidet*, "differs essentially". Marion's argument here is sound as an argument from the matter: SZ distinguishes being's exhibition from the uncovering of entities, and §44 distinguishes uncoveredness from disclosedness (SZ 220–221). If the 1928 difference is the ground of the two truths, SZ already has its ground. SZ also says, at the close of Division One, what it does with that distinction. Being "is", "although it is to be distinguished from all entities", and what that means "can be asked concretely only when the sense of being and the reach of the understanding of being in general have been clarified" (§44c, SZ 230, checked). In SZ the distinction of being from entities is not the guideline of the inquiry. It is a question deferred until the sense of being has been clarified. Marion will reach the same structure in IV.5, where the ternary question governs the binary difference, and SZ 230 is the text that states it.

Heidegger himself, "in a note of 1929 to his text", refers to the first public communication in the summer course of 1927, §22, adding that the whole course belongs to SZ, Part One, Division Three; there, the difference is latent in Dasein, and "we call the explicitly performed distinction of being and entities the *ontological difference*"; but the course breaks off after one chapter of its second part, and the difference is named "only so as to have an unanswered question recognized as an aporia"; and the course stands in continuity with SZ, since the explicit difference depends on Dasein's temporality (R IV.2 ¶3, nn. 11–15 / GA 9, 134 n. b; GA 24, 454; SZ 39). The GA 24 sentences check (GA 24, 454, and the latent difference just before it). The note does not date as R dates it. In the corpus text it is headed "1st edition 1929", and the corpus map identifies these headings as marking later marginalia in Heidegger's copy of the first edition, not text printed in 1929. The neighbouring notes on the same sentence of p. 134, under the same heading, call the distinction ambiguous, "a step toward overcoming" the tradition "and yet a fateful binding back" to it (n. a), and say that the essence of truth is here grasped "from the 'distinction' as a fixed mark" instead of the distinction being overcome from the truth of being (n. c). R cites n. b and not its neighbours. Marion's chronological argument, that Heidegger "himself" placed the first communication in 1927 at the time, rests on a misdated note, and the same layer of notes disowns the distinction he is dating. The substance of the unit survives the correction. GA 24 §22 does name the ontological difference, in 1927, and it grounds it in temporality, as Marion says: the section's first heading is "Temporality, Temporality [*Zeitlichkeit*, *Temporalität*] and ontological difference". That heading has a consequence for R II.7, which offered ontological difference as a replacement for time in the sense of being. The 1927 course does not set the two against each other. It derives the difference from time.

### 3 - The Occurrences and the Notes

Two answers are current: SZ does not name the difference (Vail), or it moves within it without naming it (Sallis, Granel, Beaufret, for whom SZ is "the book of the difference of being and entities" before the word comes forward); Marion refuses Beaufret's formula as an explanation that needs explaining, and separates the factual thesis (SZ never uses the phrase) from the theoretical one (SZ moves within ontological difference thought as such), to contest both (R IV.3 ¶1, nn. 16–19). The commentators are outside this run. Commentary R gives the second of them as "John Salus"; R's n. 17 has "John C. Sallis". Marion's separation of the two theses is clean, and the factual one can be tested in SZ, which the next unit does.

The phrase does occur: §12, "the ontological distinction (*den ontologischen Unterschied*) between being-in as an existential and the 'insideness' of present-at-hand entities among one another as a category" (SZ 56); a similar formula at §40; "a first ontological distinction (*Unterscheidung*) of existence and reality" at §63 (SZ 314); and the last page, the difference of the being of existing Dasein from the being of entities not of its kind, and the "difference" of "consciousness" and "thing" (SZ 437); translators balk at the name "from a scruple about acknowledging a fact whose status remains indeterminate" (R IV.3 ¶2, nn. 20–25). The SZ texts check (SZ 56, 314, 437), and the fact Marion asserts holds of §12: the adjective "ontological" is joined to *Unterschied*. Martineau renders it "ontological difference" (la différence ontologique), which Marion approves (n. 20). The other occurrences are weaker than R makes them. At §40 SZ says that the existential sense of being-in was clarified "as distinct from" (*im Unterschied von*) the categorial meaning of "insideness", with a note back to §12 (SZ 188–189, checked). It is a prepositional phrase, and Martineau's "par opposition à" (by opposition to), which Marion's n. 21 calls unjustified, renders it correctly. At §63 the word is *Unterscheidung*, the act of distinguishing. Marion's n. 22 rightly notes that Martineau renders it "différenciation" and, a few lines later, "distinction", which is inconsistent. The inconsistency does not make the word *Differenz*. All the occurrences Marion lists separate Dasein's kind of being from the kind of being of other entities, as his own n. 36 concedes. That is guard 5's point, and Marion will make it the centre of IV.5. So the factual thesis he contests, that SZ does not name *the* ontological difference, is true of the difference between being and entities. What SZ names is something else under the same adjective.

The phrase works in SZ in verbal form as well, at two decisive places. At §2 the construction of the question of being calls for three terms, and being "demands its own mode of exhibition, which differs essentially from the uncovering of entities": "a difference established by and for the question of being is exactly an ontological difference". At the end of Division One being "is given" only insofar as truth is, and "what it means that being 'is', where it is nevertheless to be distinguished from all entities", can be asked only once the sense of being is clarified; Heidegger's own copy adds "ontological difference"; Martineau's "si tant est qu'il doit être distingué" (if indeed it must be distinguished) wrongly introduces a reservation (R IV.3 ¶3–¶4, nn. 26–28 / SZ 6, 230; GA 2, 304). Both SZ sentences check (SZ 6, SZ 230). Marion's correction of Martineau at SZ 230 holds: the clause means "whereas it is after all supposed to be distinguished" (wo es doch … unterschieden sein soll), an adversative, not a conditional. The marginal note cannot be checked here. Two points qualify the reading. First, "an ontological difference" is the conclusion Marion draws from SZ 6 by a definition: a difference "established by and for the question of being" is called ontological. SZ uses the verb and not the noun, and the definition is R's. Second, the sentence at SZ 230 makes the distinction the object of a question that "can only be asked concretely" once the sense of being is clarified. In SZ the distinction comes after the sense of being, as something to be understood from it. Marion takes SZ 230 as evidence that SZ works with ontological difference. It is better evidence for the thesis of IV.5, that SZ subordinates the difference to the sense of being.

Four marginal notes from Heidegger's own copy gloss SZ with "ontological difference": at §7 (the sense of being in general, glossed "sense of the difference", GA 2, 50), at §20 (substance's blurred signification, GA 2, 127), at §39 (being "is" only in the understanding of being, "*qua* difference 'in' Da-sein", GA 2, 244), at §43 (being never explicable from entities, GA 2, 275); granting the caution about marginalia, "no forcing" could have introduced the difference "if *Sein und Zeit* did not already move of itself within the horizon opened by ontological difference" (R IV.3 ¶5, nn. 29–32). The four SZ sentences check: SZ 37 ("the cardinal problem, the question of the sense of being in general"), SZ 94 (see chapter III), SZ 183 ("entities *are* independently of the experience, acquaintance and grasp through which they are disclosed … but being 'is' only in the understanding of the entity to whose being something like an understanding of being belongs"), SZ 208. None of the notes can be checked in the corpus. Marion's inference, that Heidegger "did not have to over-interpret his own texts", proves the opposite of what he wants on one count. The notes show that Heidegger later read SZ through the difference. They do not show that SZ "already moves of itself" within it, since that is the question at issue, and the notes are the later self-interpretation that guard 4 holds apart. The 1929-headed notes to the 1928 essay (previous section) show how far that self-interpretation could go against the text it annotates. This is retroactive reading, and Marion grants the methodological objection before setting it aside.

### 4 - The Most Cardinal Unthought

Some ontological difference runs through SZ, but whether it is the later concept must be decided from SZ's own sense of it; Marion's hypothesis is that its guiding thread comes from Husserl (R IV.4 ¶1). The hypothesis is the chapter's wager, and its test lies in the next four units. For this pair the point to hold is the one R states: the sense of the SZ phrase is to be read from SZ and "not in advance from what the same locution will indicate after 1927". That is guard 5 stated by R, and the units that follow can be measured by it.

Husserl too uses the locution, in the second edition of the *Logical Investigations* (1913), Third Investigation §9, three times; §39 of SZ opposes entities, independent of experience, to being, which is only in the understanding of being, and the passage is glossed in Heidegger's copy in terms of difference; SZ §63's distinction of existence and reality recovers Ideen I §42's "principial difference of manners of being, the most cardinal there is", between consciousness and reality, with Dasein in place of consciousness; and SZ's last page uses Husserl's terms, the "difference" of "consciousness" and "thing" (R IV.4 ¶2, nn. 34–39 / SZ 183, 314, 437). The Husserl texts are treated in the companion run, which records that Marion uses Ideen I §43's "unbridgeable essential difference", between perception and image or sign, as though it were §42's difference of consciousness and reality (runs/marion-rd-on-hua3.md, guard 12). The SZ loci check. Two readings of SZ 437 must be held side by side. Marion reads the quotation marks around "consciousness" and "thing" as Heidegger quoting his teacher. Commentary P reads the paragraph as directed at the era's critical vocabulary of "reification", of which consciousness and thing are the common terms (SZ 437 asks "what does reification mean?"). The quotation marks support both. SZ names no author. Marion's reading of §63 as a repetition of Ideen I §42 "with one correction" is an extension. SZ gives the distinction's origin in its own analysis ("the delimitation of the structure of care gave the basis", SZ 314) and adds at once that the distinction "can be drawn only in the horizon" of an idea of being in general, "for both mean *being*" (SZ 314). The second sentence is what separates SZ's distinction from a distinction of regions, and it is the sentence Marion's argument needs and does not cite. Bernet's frame (sources/notes/bernet-interpretive-frame.md, §3) locates the Husserlian anticipation of ontological difference elsewhere, in categorial intuition, on Heidegger's own late account. R and Bernet name different Husserlian origins, and SZ names neither.

The rupture was played out on the interpretation of this difference: the 1925 course cites Ideen I §§49 and 76 and finds "the remarkable thing, that here the most radical distinction of being is claimed without the being of the entities that enter into the distinction being properly asked about"; Husserl names a difference of manners of being without thinking it, for lack of the sense of being (R IV.4 ¶3, nn. 40–41 / GA 20, 158–159). The quotation checks against GA 20 ("das Merkwürdige, daß hier beansprucht wird, den radikalsten Seinsunterschied zu gewinnen, ohne daß nach dem Sein der Seienden, die in den Unterschied eingehen, eigentlich gefragt ist", GA 20, 158). This is mediation, and faithful: GA 20 does make the Husserlian distinction the point of the critique, in SZ's own word, *Unterschied*. SZ keeps the critique and drops the name. Its counterpart is §10's charge that the "subject", "soul" and "consciousness" are left ontologically undetermined (SZ 46), with Husserl named once on the person (SZ 47). Marion's double failure, phenomenological and ontological, is GA 20's argument, and he reports it as GA 20's.

The letter to Husserl of October 1927 mentions difference twice: the mode of being of human Dasein is "totally different from that of all others", and the absolute ego's mode of being differs from that of the pure psychical; compare SZ §61, "Dasein is ontologically different in principle from everything present-at-hand and real" (R IV.4 ¶4, nn. 42–43 / Hua IX, 601–602; SZ 303). The SZ comparison checks (SZ 303, checked: *verschieden*, "different"). The letter is outside this pair's sources. The companion run records that it is dated 22 October 1927 in the Husserliana editor's introduction, where R has 27 October. The two passages Marion cites from the letter confirm what IV.3 found: Heidegger's difference in 1927 is between Dasein's kind of being and that of other entities. That is β of the next section, not the difference of being and entities.

Three conclusions: Husserl supplies the locution without the concept (LU, 1913) and the concept without its ontological determination (Ideen I); Heidegger interprets the difference of consciousness and thing as ontological from the sense of being that Husserl misses; the difference precedes and makes SZ possible (R IV.4 ¶5). The conclusions rest on the GA 20 critique (IV.4 ¶3) and on the reading of SZ §63 as a repetition of Ideen I §42 (IV.4 ¶2). The first is documented. The second is R's, and SZ 314's horizon clause qualifies it. Conclusion (c), "the difference precedes and makes *Sein und Zeit* possible", says more than SZ allows. On SZ's account the distinction of existence and reality is made possible by the idea of being in general, not the reverse (SZ 314). What precedes and makes possible is the question of the sense of being. Marion's conclusion restates the ordering of IV.1 (difference first) in a section whose texts show the other order.

### 5 - The Irreducibility of the "Question of Being" to "Ontological Difference"

At first sight SZ distinguishes being from entities with unambiguous force: being is not an entity, it cannot be explained from entities; but the formula lets one suppose being to be another term beside entities; the difference is required as ontological "precisely because ontically being is confused with entities"; so SZ does show "a working of ontological difference between being and entities" (R IV.5 ¶1, nn. 44–46 / SZ 4, 6, 9, 37, 183, 196, 207–208). The quotations check: "being is in each case the being of an entity" (SZ 9); "the phenomenon in the phenomenological sense is only ever what makes up being, but being is in each case being of entities" (SZ 37, checked); SZ 183 as above. This is reconstruction, and the best in the chapter. Marion reads SZ's distinction of being from entities as inseparable from their belonging together, and SZ's texts say exactly that. The phrase "ontological difference" is R's for what SZ states without it.

Two difficulties: the concept coincides with none of the occurrences of the locution, and the occurrences distinguish two entities, or their two manners of being, not being and entities; three figures are superimposed: (α) Husserl's ontic difference of consciousness and reality, (β) the difference of the manners of being of those two entities, which SZ calls ontological, and (γ) being and entities, the later difference; SZ breaks through with an analogy of proportionality, the thing is to presence-at-hand as Dasein is to existence, and the relation of entity to being is always doubled (R IV.5 ¶2, across the two diagrams). The topography is Marion's, and it is accurate to SZ's usage: every SZ occurrence of "ontological" with *Unterschied* or *Unterscheidung* is β (guard 5). This is the point where R and the commentators he contested in IV.3 agree: SZ does not name γ. The analogy of proportionality is R's apparatus. SZ knows the analogy of being as the scholastic answer to the equivocity of "being" said of God and creatures, and leaves it unsolved (§20, SZ 93). It does not use analogy to relate Dasein to things. What links β to γ in SZ is the sentence of SZ 314 that Marion does not cite here either: the distinction of existence and reality "can be drawn only in the horizon" of the idea of being in general, "for both mean *being*". SZ does not relate the two kinds of being to each other by proportion. It relates both to being in general, which is the third term of the next unit.

Dasein enters the topography because the question of being has three terms: what is interrogated (the entity), what is asked about (its being), what is to be found out (the sense of being); had §2 stopped at the first two, it would coincide with canonical ontological difference; the third term, "totally ignored by canonical ontological difference", makes the ternary question irreconcilable with the binary difference (R IV.5 ¶3, nn. 47–49 / SZ 5–6, 37). The mapping checks against §2 (SZ 5–6) and against GA 20's numbered triad. The thesis that the third term cannot be fitted into a binary difference is R's, and it is the right observation about SZ: SZ 230 (see IV.3 ¶3–¶4) makes the distinction of being from entities a question that awaits the sense of being. Marion arrives by analysis at the order that SZ 230 states.

Two gaps follow, entity to being of the entity, and being of the entity to the sense of being; the first is "ontically too limited", since it is the being of *this* entity; the second, which Heidegger's copy glosses "sense of the difference", fails too, since SZ did not reach the sense of being, since "must being be understood within the horizon of time? Not only will *Sein und Zeit* end by doubting it, but the abandonment of the horizon of time will continue", and since the being of the entity is not the entity of the difference; so the topography of the question "dispenses with" ontological difference (R IV.5 ¶4, n. 50 / SZ 7, 37; GA 2, 50). The argument is internal to R's topography and mostly sound in its terms. One step misreports SZ. SZ does not "end by doubting" that time is the horizon of being. It ends by asking whether it is (SZ 437), in questions that the book presents as the task of the unpublished division. The doubt belongs to the later Heidegger, to whom Marion's next clause passes ("the abandonment of the horizon of time will continue"). This is the retroactive reading flagged under "Phenomenology as Such" ¶5, applied to the same page. Marion's n. 50 cites SZ 7, "entities can be determined in their being without the explicit concept of the sense of being having to be available already", which checks. It supports his first gap: SZ itself allows the being of entities to be determined short of the sense of being.

The paradox is only apparent: SZ confronts ontological difference obliquely, multiplying it into two differences; Max Müller reports that Division Three was to have three differences, transcendental, transcendence-like and theological (R IV.5 ¶5, n. 52). Müller's report is outside the corpus. Marion reads its list as "hesitation rather than rigorous elaboration". The report is evidence about the plan of Division Three as Heidegger described it later, not about SZ's text. As evidence that the published SZ "doubles" the difference, it is a mediation through a reported conversation, and Marion uses it with a "could confirm".

### 6 - The Ontic Primacy of the Question of Being as Interrogation

From the standpoint of Dasein, the redoubling is an advance: the entity put in question must be Dasein, and SZ exercises ontological difference "only from a topography other than the dual one of difference" (R IV.6 ¶1). The unit restates IV.5 and prepares the confirmation. It is R's, and it fits SZ's construction of the question: the inquiring of this question "is, as a *mode of being* of an entity, itself essentially determined by what is asked about in it, by being" (§2, SZ 7, checked).

Dasein exercises ontological difference first as transcending: things do not transcend, Dasein does, and it transcends into "the dimension of being", since "being is the *transcendens* pure and simple"; the transcendence of world is made possible by being-in-the-world (R IV.6 ¶2, nn. 53–55 / GA 24, 425; GA 26, 20; SZ 38, 363, 208, 366, 419). The SZ quotations check (SZ 38; §69c, "The Temporal Problem of the Transcendence of the World", SZ 364–366). The same caution as in R II.5 applies. SZ 38 says that being is the *transcendens* pure and simple and that "the transcendence of the being of Dasein is a distinctive one". Marion's "transcendence of Dasein, but through being; transcendence of the being of Dasein" turns SZ's distinction into a relation of derivation, which is defensible as an interpretation and not stated by SZ. The full doctrine of Dasein's transcendence is GA 24 §20 and GA 26 §11, as Marion's notes show, and the corpus map names GA 26 as "the fullest statement of Heidegger's concept of transcendence". In SZ the doctrine is announced, and §69c points it forward to the unpublished division.

Dasein is itself the difference: "the ontic distinction of Dasein lies in its being ontological"; Dasein has the sense of being as a musician has the sense of melody; being resides solely in understanding; what is at issue for Dasein is "being", glossed in Heidegger's copy as "to be the *there* and so to sustain being in general" (R IV.6 ¶3, nn. 56–60 / SZ 12, 183, 42; GA 2, 56, 16). The SZ texts check (SZ 12, SZ 183). Marion's "being consists in nothing of an entity, since it resides solely in the understanding" compresses SZ 183, which keeps the two halves apart: entities are independent of understanding, and being "is" only in it. The compression is harmless here. The gloss Marion needs for "being in general", the notes to SZ 12 and SZ 42, cannot be checked. Without them SZ's "the being for which this entity is at issue" (SZ 12, 42) is Dasein's own being, as SZ 42 says ("the being which is at issue for this entity in its being is in each case mine"). Marion's reading of it as being in general rests on the later notes (guard 4).

In 1927 Heidegger identifies Dasein not with ontological difference but with the question of being, because the dual difference is reinterpreted within the ternary question, and because the question adds to the difference "no third term other than Dasein itself"; so ontological difference "had to efface itself behind the question of being, letting itself be covered over by the 'ontological difference' between the manner of being of Dasein alone and that of other entities" (R IV.6 ¶4, n. 61 / SZ 7; GA 20, 199). The first reason is IV.5's. The second maps the three terms of the question onto the two terms of the difference. To find only Dasein as the surplus, Marion folds what is asked about (the being of the entity) and what is to be found out (the sense of being, "which comes down to being in general") into a single "being". That fold undoes the two gaps on which IV.5 ¶4 rested its argument, where the being of the entity and the sense of being were kept apart. SZ keeps them apart too: entities can be determined in their being "without the explicit concept of the sense of being having to be available already" (SZ 7). And in SZ's triad Dasein is not an extra term. It is the entity in the place of what is interrogated, specified as the inquirer (SZ 7). What supports Marion's conclusion is the fact, observed in IV.3 and IV.5, that SZ's named "ontological" distinction always involves Dasein's kind of being. The covering of γ by β is R's interpretation of that fact, and a strong one. The fold of the triad into two terms is not needed for it, and it contradicts the preceding section.

### 7 - The Misconstrual of Ontological Difference by "Ontological Difference"

SZ knows an "ontological difference" and does not yet think ontological difference; the motive is the ternary question, and the third term enters with Dasein; since everything SZ publishes is devoted to Dasein, Marion asks as a hypothesis whether Dasein is "the obstacle" to ontological difference and to the sense of being, so that SZ's incompletion is bound to Dasein (R IV.7 ¶1). The hypothesis is clearly stated as one. The premise that SZ's being in general receives "strictly programmatic mentions" is accurate to the published text (§§5, 8, 83).

"The last two published pages of *Sein und Zeit*, those of §83, pose exactly this question; better, they answer it explicitly" (R IV.7 ¶2). The first half is supported in the next unit. The second is the unit's whole claim, and it does not hold of the pages. §83 ends with questions, and the last of them asks whether time reveals itself as the horizon of being (SZ 437). The answer Marion reports comes from the marginal notes he cites in IV.7 ¶5, not from §83.

(a) §83 recalls the results, Dasein's totality grounded in care and care's sense in temporality, and calls them "only a *way*"; the goal is the question of being in general. (b) Dasein is there marked twice by "ontological difference", the being of existing Dasein against entities not of its kind and the "difference" of "consciousness" and "thing", and that difference is "only the *point of departure* of the ontological problematic, but nothing on which philosophy can rest", so that one must ask whether it suffices for an original unfolding of the problematic (R IV.7 ¶3, nn. 62–67 / SZ 436–437). The SZ texts check (SZ 436–437). Commentary P renders the *Unterschied* as "distinction" and reads the paragraph as SZ's last challenge to the era's talk of "reification". Marion's reading, that SZ here criticizes its own β, is supported by the text: the distinction of Dasein's being from that of things is called a point of departure and nothing to rest on. It is the strongest support SZ gives Marion in the chapter, and it is SZ's own. What the page criticizes is that the distinction is not enough "so long as the question of the sense of being in general remains unposed and unclarified" (SZ 437). SZ's remedy is the sense of being. It is not a binary ontological difference.

The fundamental problem §83 calls "still enveloped" is whether "ontology can be grounded *ontologically*, or whether it needs an *ontic* foundation for this too, and *which* entity must take over the function of founding"; this aims back at §2's question, on which entity the sense of being is to be read off, and at §4's threefold primacy of Dasein; so SZ's last question puts Dasein's primacy in question (R IV.7 ¶4, nn. 68–71 / SZ 436, 7, 13). The SZ texts check (SZ 436, SZ 7, SZ 13). Two points of exactness. First, what SZ says must "not count as dogma" is "the thesis" just restated from §7, that philosophy is universal phenomenological ontology, "proceeding from the hermeneutic of Dasein" (SZ 436). Commentary R has "the analytic of Dasein must not be taken for a dogma". The difference is small, and the thesis does include the starting point in Dasein. Second, SZ asks the question in the form of an alternative and does not answer it. Marion's reading, that the question "puts Dasein's primacy in question", is a fair reading of what the question opens. His claim in ¶2, that the pages answer it, is not. SZ leaves the alternative open, as the book's last page does with time.

Heidegger's own copy confirms the self-criticism: at §2's question "on which entity is the sense of being to be read off" he notes two questions "misleadingly" joined, above all as regards the role of Dasein; at the exemplarity of Dasein, "misleading", with Dasein as the partner of the play of being (*Bei-spiel*); at §63, where falling denies ontology its adequate basis, "wrong! As if ontology could be read off from the authentic ontic"; so SZ "owes its incompletion to nothing but the concealment within it of ontological difference by 'ontological difference'" (R IV.7 ¶5, nn. 70–75 / SZ 7, 311; GA 2, 9, 412). The SZ sentences check: "the falling being … covers over ontically the authentic being of Dasein, so as to deny the ontology directed at this entity its adequate basis" (SZ 311, checked). None of the notes can be checked here. If they are as R gives them, they are the strongest evidence in the chapter, and they are Heidegger's later verdict on the book, not the book's. The conclusion turns that verdict into SZ's own diagnosis. It is retroactive in the strict sense: the cause SZ assigns to its own incompletion, in §83, is that the question of the sense of being "remains unposed and unclarified" (SZ 437), and the cause the notes assign is the primacy of Dasein. Marion is entitled to prefer the notes. He is not entitled to say that §83 "answers explicitly", since the answer is the notes'.

Across chapter IV Marion has made SZ load-bearing at every step, and his reading divides along a clear line. Where he reads SZ's usage, he is right: every SZ occurrence of "ontological" with *Unterschied* or *Unterscheidung* separates Dasein's kind of being from that of other entities (SZ 56, 314, 437), and he says so himself (n. 36, figure β). The distinction of being from entities is stated in SZ without the name (SZ 6, 9, 37, 183, 230). And §83 calls the first distinction a point of departure on which philosophy cannot rest (SZ 437). Those readings hold, and they are the chapter's lasting contribution. Where he reads SZ through 1928–1957, he is not. The measure he applies in IV.1, that a breakthrough must be a breakthrough into ontological difference, is *Identity and Difference*'s, not SZ's, whose guideline is time (§6). The chronology of IV.2 rests on a GA 9 note that R dates to 1929 and the corpus identifies as a later marginal note. Its neighbours in the same layer disown the distinction it dates. The extra occurrences of IV.3 at §40 and §63 are a prepositional phrase and an *Unterscheidung*, and Martineau's renderings of them are defensible. The four notes that make SZ "move within" the difference, and the three that make §83 "answer" its own question, are Heidegger's later reading, unverifiable here. Against these, one SZ sentence decides the chapter's structural thesis in Marion's favour, though he cites it for something else. At SZ 230 the meaning of being's distinction from all entities "can only be asked concretely" once the sense of being is clarified. SZ subordinates the binary distinction to the ternary question, as IV.5 argues. Read with SZ 314 ("both mean *being*") and SZ 437, the published book gives the order Marion reconstructs, sense of being first and distinction after. It gives the sense of being the name time, which Marion's chapter sets aside.

## V. Being and the Region

Chapter V has no Commentary R. Its units are reconstructed from R's text, and its Husserl side (Ideen I, *Ideen III*, *Formal and Transcendental Logic*, *First Philosophy*, the *Cartesian Meditations*) is treated in the companion run (runs/marion-rd-on-hua3.md, chapter V). The chapter cites SZ only in its first and last sections. Elsewhere this commentary asks what SZ doctrine R's argument about Husserl presupposes, confirms or omits.

### 1 - The Possibility of Ontology

Should the phenomenologist take the question of being into view, as one object among others or as a privileged one, and should phenomenology then take up "the old title, at once envied and despised, of ontology"? (R V.1 ¶1). The questions are posed in the vocabulary SZ makes available: being as a "quasi-object" that is no object, phenomenology as the claimant to the title of ontology. SZ answers all three in §7, and the next two units say so.

We can no longer put this question to Husserl directly, because a "third party" screens him from us, Heidegger, through whom we read Husserl's greatness and failures; the screen must first be made visible (R V.1 ¶2). The unit is R's reflection on its own chapters II–IV, which read Husserl through Heidegger, and it is accurate about them. For this pair it is a reversal of roles: chapter V will use Husserl to put the Heidegger of SZ in question. The screen Marion names is mostly the Heidegger of GA 20, as the next unit shows, and the reader should hold that the "Heidegger" being made visible here is not SZ alone.

Two theses: (a) "taken in its material content, phenomenology is the science of the being of entities, ontology", "ontology is possible only as phenomenology", and phenomenology alone can make visible "the being of entities, its sense, its modifications and derivatives"; (b) "from the standpoint of *Sein und Zeit*", Husserl's use of phenomenology misses its essence twice, by spreading it over every field of objects and by leaving the being of consciousness and of objectivity undetermined, so that it is "*non-phenomenological*! — that is, *phenomenological only in intention*!" (R V.1 ¶3, nn. 1–5 / SZ 35, 37; GA 24, 3; GA 20, 178). Thesis (a) is SZ's, quoted correctly (SZ 35, 37). Its gloss, that phenomenology "fixes an entity, in fact the sole privileged entity, Dasein", runs §7 together with §§2–4, which is fair as a summary of SZ's method. Thesis (b) is GA 20's. Its decisive sentence is quoted from GA 20 (n. 5), and SZ makes no such charge. SZ says once that Husserl and Scheler "no longer ask the question of the being of the person" (SZ 47). It does not say that Husserl's phenomenology is non-phenomenological. The phrase "from the standpoint of *Sein und Zeit*" is therefore mediation presented as SZ (guard 3). It matters here because the chapter goes on to refute thesis (b), and what it refutes is GA 20's charge, which SZ itself did not print.

### 2 - Phenomenology as the "True and Authentic Universal Ontology"

Husserl's late and earlier texts show that he held the ontological destination of phenomenology before Heidegger: the *Cartesian Meditations* (phenomenology "would be *eo ipso* the true and authentic universal ontology"), *Ideen III* (1912: phenomenology "swallows up all the ontologies"), *First Philosophy* (1923–24), the texts of Hua VIII ("the completely concrete ontology is *eo ipso* the authentic transcendental philosophy"); "this thesis, Husserl had held it before Heidegger himself" (R V.2 ¶1, nn. 6–9). The Husserl texts are the companion run's (guard 15 there records that "the true and authentic universal ontology" is CM §64, 1929, and that Ideen I's relation of phenomenology to the ontologies is dominion and clarification). For this pair the test is chronological and lexical. Of the texts cited, only the *Cartesian Meditations* (1929) were published in Husserl's lifetime, and they postdate SZ. *Ideen III*, *First Philosophy* and the texts of Hua VIII were published posthumously in the Husserliana. SZ could not answer them. It could know of them only through what Husserl showed Heidegger in manuscript, which SZ's note of thanks acknowledges in general ("unpublished investigations", SZ 38 n.). The word they share with SZ, "universal ontology", has different senses. SZ's "universal phenomenological ontology" (SZ 38) is the science of the being of entities, from the hermeneutic of Dasein, toward the sense of being. Husserl's "universal ontology" is "the *logos* of a possible world in general", whose last word, in the text Marion quotes, is that "the entity is the entity of knowledge" (n. 9). The thesis Marion credits to Husserl "before Heidegger" is the same in words and different in sense, which is the question the next unit concedes. This is the move the Bernet frame warns about, "Husserl already had it": the answer to "which Husserl?" is a Husserl SZ could not read.

That Husserl held the ontological destination first does not mean he thought it in SZ's sense; the question becomes in what sense each treated ontology by the phenomenological method, on the ground of phenomenology and of the question of being, "since, contrary to what Heidegger had claimed, Husserl already occupied it, and solidly" (R V.2 ¶2). The concession is the right correction of the previous unit, and the last clause withdraws it. SZ does not claim that Husserl did not occupy the field of ontology. SZ 77 n. cites Ideen I §10 ff. as Husserl's formal ontology (next section). What SZ claims is that ontologies, Husserl's included in its silence, leave the sense of being unasked (§3, SZ 11: every ontology "remains blind" if it has not first clarified the sense of being). Whether Husserl "occupied" the question of being in SZ's sense is what the rest of the chapter must decide, and Marion's own answer in V.5 will be no.

### 3 - The Anonymous Concept of an Ontology

Husserl traces the union of phenomenology and ontology back to the *Logical Investigations*, the text Heidegger privileges: *Formal and Transcendental Logic* §27 (1929) says that the idea of a formal ontology appears "for the first time" in LU I, though "not yet under the *name*" of formal ontology; the restoration of the word in 1929 may owe something to SZ (R V.3 ¶1, nn. 10–11). The chronological suspicion is Marion's and fair. SZ is the published text that made "ontology" a title phenomenology could claim, two years before FTL. What SZ shows, and Marion does not cite, is that Heidegger already knew Husserl's formal ontology and its source in LU I. In §17, where SZ asks whether the sign can be "formalized into a *universal kind of relation*", its note refers to "E. Husserl, Ideen I, § 10 ff.; furthermore already *Logical Investigations*, vol. I, ch. 11" (SZ 77 and n., checked). LU I ch. 11 is the chapter of the *Prolegomena* that sets out the idea of pure logic, the same place FTL §27 names.

Ideen I §10 (1913) says Husserl did not then dare restore the word "ontology", "offensive for historical reasons", and now holds it more correct to revive it; the draft preface of 1913 says that in the LU "the idea of ontology came back to life", only the word being avoided; the concept was formal ontology, the *Mathesis pura* of LU I and Investigations III–IV (R V.3 ¶2, nn. 12–13). Ideen I §10 is the passage SZ's note cites (SZ 77 n.). Heidegger read it, and his use of it in SZ is critical in one specific way. The formalization of the sign into a universal relation gives "an ontological guideline for a 'characteristic' of all entities in general", and SZ's reply is that signs are first of all equipment, and that relation as a formal character has its origin in reference (SZ 77–78). Husserl's formal ontology enters SZ as an example of formalization, which §18 says "levels the phenomena so far that the genuine phenomenal content is lost" (SZ 88, checked).

Two conclusions: Husserl gives phenomenology the charge of ontology from 1913 at the latest, not under the impact of SZ; and when he locates the meeting in the LU he refers to the *Prolegomena* and Investigations II–IV, "never, as Heidegger will always and exclusively do, to Investigation VI"; so the conflict is played out on the ground of ontology, which both occupied (R V.3 ¶3, n. 14). The second conclusion is too strong about Heidegger. SZ refers to the *Prolegomena* chapter on pure logic and to Ideen I §10 for formal ontology (SZ 77 n.), to LU I for the theory of signification (SZ 77 n., 166 n.), and to LU VI for truth (SZ 218 n.). Heidegger "always and exclusively" referring to LU VI is true of the courses and of Zähringen, where categorial intuition is the theme. It is not true of SZ, which cites LU I, IV, V and VI in its notes (SZ 166 n.: "LU II, 1st and 4th–6th Investigation"). In SZ Heidegger and Husserl point to the same texts of the LU for formal ontology, and SZ treats the result as formalization (SZ 88). The conflict is on the ground of ontology, as Marion says, and SZ had already placed it there.

### 4 - The Formality of Objectivity

Husserl does not ignore ontology or regionalize it, since he fixes a universal ontology as one of phenomenology's achievements; what universality, and in what sense an answer to the question of being? (R V.4 ¶1). The question is SZ's §3 put to Husserl: a "universal" ontology is to be measured by whether it clarifies the sense of being (SZ 11). Marion's next units answer it from Husserl's texts.

Ideen I §§8–10 order the sciences: every empirical science depends on the laws of "objectness (*Gegenständlichkeit*) in general", a "formal-ontological complex of disciplines", formal logic and *mathesis universalis*; regional ontologies depend on the empty form of objectness and the eidos of the region; the relation of formal ontology to objectness in general is not yet justified (R V.4 ¶2, nn. 15–16). Husserl's side is the companion run's. SZ's counterpart is the passage of §6 on what happens when the traditional categories are applied to the subject: "the categorial stock of traditional ontology is carried over to this entity with corresponding formalizations and merely negative restrictions" (SZ 22, checked). SZ's diagnosis of formalization is the same in 1927 as Marion's here. It is aimed at the tradition's handling of Dasein, not at Husserl's formal ontology by name.

Ideen I §10: categories refer to an "original objectness" (*Urgegenständlichkeit*); a region is determined by "the empty form of region in general"; "formal ontology contains at the same time in itself the forms of all possible ontologies in general"; to raise formal logic to an ontology Husserl must "extend" it to *mathesis universalis* and understand ontology as pure objectness (R V.4 ¶3, n. 17). This is the passage of Ideen I that SZ's note cites (SZ 77 n.). The unit is reconstruction of Husserl. For this pair it fixes the text Heidegger had before him when he wrote that formalizations level the phenomena (SZ 88).

Logic reaches objectness in general by doubling the categories: categories of signification (apophantic) and logical categories, "the purely logical fundamental concepts" by which "an object as such, an any-something-whatever", is determined; Husserl thinks the objectness of the object as the only original form (R V.4 ¶4). The "any something" (*irgend Etwas*) is the formal object of Ideen I. SZ's counterpart is its first prejudice about being: being as "the most universal concept" (§1, SZ 3). SZ answers that the universality of being "is not that of a genus" and "transcends" every generic universality (SZ 3). Husserl's empty something is a formal universality of the kind SZ sets aside at its first page. Marion does not cite §1. The unit's question, whether objectness can be raised to the first form, is SZ's question about universality in another form.

*Formal and Transcendental Logic* unites apophantic and formal mathematics in "the concept of the object in general", so that mathematics is "an *ontology* … but a *formal* ontology"; ontology is possible only on condition of absolute abstraction; "must one still speak of an ontology?" (R V.4 ¶5, nn. 18–19). SZ's answer to Marion's question is in §18: a formalization of the referential whole "in the sense of a system of relations" levels the phenomena (SZ 88). Marion's question is SZ's verdict, posed as a question.

Husserl needs to distinguish apophantic and ontological attitudes because the empty concept of something "occurs nowhere but in judgement"; their "separation is at the same time an equivalence", so that they "count as one and the same science" (R V.4 ¶6, nn. 20–22). FTL (1929) postdates SZ. The unit is Husserl's. Its interest for this pair is that SZ's own derivation of the apophantic (§33, the assertion as a derivative mode of interpretation) makes the judgement, not the object, the derived term. SZ would read Husserl's equivalence of judgement and formal ontology as confirmation that formal ontology stays within the apophantic "as", the "as" of assertion, which §33 grounds in the hermeneutic "as" of understanding (SZ 158). Marion does not draw on §33 here.

Four arguments distinguish the two attitudes (judging is directed to objects; syntactic variation refers to a last substrate; syntactic forms derive from the object; the one who judges is directed at the object through categorial forms, "which are therefore ontological forms"), all resting on intentionality, which cannot supply a conceptual discrimen since it never shows itself; the separation of logic and ontology is "an unconceptualizable transgression" (R V.4 ¶7, nn. 23–27). The argument is R's on Husserl. Its premise, that intentionality is "invisible, unformulable", is R's, and in the comparison with SZ it is worth noting that SZ withholds intentionality from its published analytic and defers its grounding in temporality (SZ 363 n.). Marion's charge that intentionality cannot discriminate the ontological from the logical is close to SZ's reason for deferring it. Intentionality is to be grounded, not to ground.

"One and the same objectness and, in principle but without real distinction, two sciences"; only in this sense does analytics have "an *ontic* and … an *ontological* direction" (R V.4 ¶8, n. 28). The quoted words "ontic" and "ontological" are Husserl's (FTL, 1929). They are the pair SZ made current (§§3–4), used here in a sense SZ would not grant, since Husserl's "ontological" is the formal a priori of objects and SZ's is the being of entities (SZ 11–13). The coincidence of vocabulary after 1927 supports Marion's suspicion in V.3 ¶1 that FTL answers SZ.

### 5 - The First Insufficiency: by Universality

Husserl built a formal ontology, authentic and universal; does it withstand Heidegger's objection? Husserl misses the question of being not by failing to define an ontology "but inversely because he succeeded only too perfectly in constructing it" (R V.5 ¶1). The thesis is R's, and it reverses GA 20's form of the charge (V.1 ¶3) while keeping its result. SZ's form of the charge is §3's: an ontology "remains at bottom blind" if it has not first clarified the sense of being, however rich its categories (SZ 11). That is closer to Marion's "succeeded too perfectly" than GA 20's "non-phenomenological".

(a) The logical status of the entity: formal ontology is equivalent to formal mathematics, and "the entity is for the one who judges an entity 'from now on'", as long as he holds the conviction; ontology never breaks through the logical space; "far from undertaking the destruction of the history of logic that Heidegger will carry out", Husserl holds to the equivalence of logic and ontology (R V.5 ¶2, nn. 29–31). The SZ counterpart to "the destruction of the history of logic" is in §1 and §33. The traditional "definition" of logic "has its foundations in ancient ontology" and does not apply to being (§1, SZ 4, checked), and §33 traces the assertion to interpretation. SZ announces the destruction of logic as part of the destruction of ontology. The phrase "will carry out" points beyond SZ, which announces the task and does not carry it out. Marion's charge on Husserl, that the entity is the substrate of judgement, is SZ §33's charge on the tradition. R makes the connection without the citation.

(b) The primacy of objectness: formal ontology needs nothing but objectness, a substrate of predication; can the entity be exhausted in objectness? Husserl does not envisage any other mode of being than the subsistence of the substrate, "in the sense in which Heidegger at the same time tries to break through to the handiness of the tool (*Zuhandenheit*) and above all to the existence of Dasein" (R V.5 ¶3, nn. 32–34). The SZ references are given in summary and are correct: readiness-to-hand (§§15–18) and existence (§9). The unit is SZ supporting R. One qualification: SZ does not say that the "subsistence of the substrate" is Husserl's. Its name for it is presence-at-hand, and its examples are the tradition's (§§6, 21). Marion's attribution to Husserl is his, and fair to FTL.

(c) The originarity of possibility: formal ontology admits the possible as the first figure of objectness; intuition founds possibility as possibility; the *Cartesian Meditations*' "new ontology" stays with possibility as the origin of entities "more decidedly than Leibniz and Wolff"; formal ontology "regionalizes itself before the question of being" (R V.5 ¶4, nn. 35–38). SZ has the distinction Marion needs and he does not cite it. "The being-possible which Dasein is existentially is as distinct from empty, logical possibility as from the contingency of something present-at-hand"; possibility as a modal category of the present-at-hand means what is not yet actual and never necessary, while possibility as an existential is "the most original and ultimate positive ontological determination of Dasein" (§31, SZ 143–144, checked). Husserl's formal possibility is what SZ calls empty logical possibility, and Marion's verdict is SZ's distinction applied to FTL. There is an irony that R does not mark. Its own method, "possibility surpasses actuality" (SZ 38), comes from the page that SZ's §31 explains, and the possibility of SZ 38 is the possibility of phenomenology as an existential possibility of research, not the logical possibility Marion here rejects in Husserl.

### 6 - The Second Insufficiency: by Reduction

Objectness is subordinated to the transcendental I: "subjectivity precedes objectivity in dignity of being"; the world has "a thoroughly relative being"; but if "being" is said of two regions separated by "an abyss of sense", and only according to "empty logical categories", what does "being" still mean, and does the "original category of being" belong to being? (R V.6 ¶1, nn. 39–45). The questions are SZ's §§10, 43 and 63 put to Husserl's texts. SZ asks whether "the 'difference' of 'consciousness' and 'thing'" suffices for the ontological problematic (SZ 437) and answers that existence and reality can be distinguished only in the horizon of being in general, "for both mean *being*" (SZ 314). Marion's question, whether "being" is said only by empty logical categories of the two regions, is SZ's question in Husserl's terms. Note 45's "region of absolute being … unworldly, unreal" is an autumn 1929 addition to Ideen I (companion run, guard 14). It postdates SZ and may answer it.

Husserl drew the consequence: "a first philosophy prior even to ontology", phenomenology turning the *a priori* into an *a posteriori*, an ontology of the real world "one-sided and not philosophical in the final sense", phenomenology "no ontology at all" (R V.6 ¶2, nn. 46–48). The English phrase "prior even to ontology" is Husserl's in conversation with Dorion Cairns, dated 19 May 1932 (n. 46), five years after SZ. "No ontology at all" comes from lectures of 1906–1907 (Hua XXIV) and from an appendix to *Ideen III* (n. 48). SZ's corresponding claim runs the other way: "ontology is possible only as phenomenology" (SZ 35), and the two titles name one philosophy (SZ 38). Husserl's "phenomenology is no ontology" and SZ's "not two disciplines" are the two positions of the dispute in their own words. Marion sets them side by side in V.1 and V.6 without quoting SZ 38 against Husserl here.

Two remarks: formal ontology is set out in Ideen I before the principle of principles and the reduction, so it has only conditional legitimacy and falls under the extended reduction (§§59–60); the reduction brackets not only ontology but "being or non-being of the world" (R V.6 ¶3, nn. 49–51). The Husserl side is the companion run's (guards 3 and 15 there). For this pair the unit establishes the premise of V.7: the reduction brackets the question of being. SZ, which has no reduction (guard 1), has no counterpart operation, and its silence on the reduction is the subject of V.7 ¶2.

Phenomenology brackets ontology and still claims to be its "mother soil" (*Mutterboden*); the phenomenologist works on "the same statements" as the ontologist; but "in the phenomenology of thing-consciousness the question is not how things in general *are* … but how the consciousness of things is made"; "a 'thing' as correlate is not a thing"; a "cardinal difference" separates the two attitudes (R V.6 ¶4, nn. 52–55). The texts are *Ideen III* (1912, published 1952). The unit is Husserl's. The phrase "cardinal difference" returns, and it is not the same as Ideen I §42's "most cardinal" difference of consciousness and reality, which chapter IV made the source of SZ's distinction. Here it separates two attitudes toward one material. Marion's next unit makes it a non-ontological difference.

Conclusions: (a) in the situation of reduction ontology is regionalized; the "cardinal difference" between thing and "thing", between what belongs to the entity and what frees itself from it, is not ontological: "ontological difference is not cardinal, the cardinal difference is not ontological"; so "one must finally do justice to Heidegger's critique": the question of the mode of being yields to the question of access; (b) the reduction of the thing to its correlate may betray the return to the things themselves; Heidegger's critique is legitimate here too; (c) if the I is excepted from the reduction and from all ontology, where *is* it? Either the I *is*, in a mode left undetermined, or the I, and the reduction with it, *is not* (R V.6 ¶5, nn. 56–57). Conclusions (a) and (b) concede to Heidegger what chapters II–IV contested, in SZ's own terms. "The question of the mode of being yields to the question of the mode of access" restates GA 20's "the title 'phenomenon' … designates only their mode of encounter" (II.1) in reverse: what Heidegger made the meaning of "phenomenological", Marion now finds Husserl substituting for ontology. Marion's chiasmus, "ontological difference is not cardinal, the cardinal difference is not ontological", uses "ontological difference" in chapter IV's figure β, the difference of kinds of being of two entities. The cardinal difference here, between the thing and its correlate, is not between two entities. The chiasmus holds, but it compares two differences that SZ would not set on the same plane. Conclusion (c) is the chapter's turn, and its second branch, that the I "is not", answers SZ's first definition of Dasein, for which "in its being this being is at issue" (SZ 12), with its negation.

### 7 - "I" outside Being

Two assumptions: (a) Husserl may have been the first to envisage that the I, which performs the reduction of all ontology, transcends ontology, "outside being"; (b) Heidegger's critique can be turned round: if the being of entities is not the ultimate *Sache selbst*, Husserl did not fail but leapt toward a horizon not determined by being, crossing "the domain of the question of being, where Heidegger tried to lodge — and to block — phenomenology"; the slogan that possibility surpasses actuality "could be turned against the enterprise of *Sein und Zeit*" (R V.7 ¶1). This is the third use of SZ 38 in R (Phenomenology as Such ¶6, II.7 ¶3), and it now turns the sentence explicitly against SZ. Assumption (b) is a contestation of SZ's founding decision, §1–§2's thesis that the question of being is the question, and it is stated as an assumption. SZ's own defence is §1: the question of being is not one question among others, because the understanding of being is presupposed in every comportment toward entities (SZ 4). Marion's leap "outside being" must meet that argument, and the unit does not yet meet it.

Husserl did not thematize this horizon, from "the intoxication of constitutions" and the inability to recognize ontological difference; but why should the question of being be excepted from the reduction, when "all ontologies fall under the reduction"? Heidegger "claims, at least in *Sein und Zeit*, to take up the phenomenological method without ever doing justice to the reduction" (n. 59: "at least explicitly; see chap. II, §§5–7"); this "massive silence" marks "an evasion or a fear"; "Heidegger presupposes that the question of being can reduce the reduction: he never demonstrates it" (R V.7 ¶2, nn. 58–59). Here R states, in its own words, what this commentary set as its first guard: SZ never uses the reduction. The statement stands against chapter II, where Marion concluded that "Heidegger therefore remains a phenomenologist, since he takes up the reduction" (R II.5 ¶2), and calls that reading back only by note 59's "at least explicitly". The two chapters together give the accurate account: the reduction in Heidegger is a term of the 1927 course (GA 24 §5), taken up "in wording, not in substance", and absent from SZ. Marion's psychological reading of the silence ("a fear") is R's. SZ gives a reason of its own for beginning without a reduction: the analytic must start from Dasein's average everydayness (SZ 43), and its access to being is interpretation (SZ 37). That is a methodological choice SZ states. It does not "demonstrate" that the question of being withstands the reduction, which is Marion's fair point, because it does not recognize the reduction as the court before which it must appear.

The crisis crystallizes in two statements: Heidegger's Postscript to *What Is Metaphysics?*, that man alone experiences "the wonder of all wonders: *that* entities are"; and Husserl's *Ideen III* (1912): "the wonder of all wonders is the pure I and pure consciousness", but the wonder "disappears" under the light of phenomenology (R V.7 ¶3, nn. 60–61). The Postscript (1943) is not in the corpus. Marion's note 60 compares SZ §57, where the caller of conscience is Dasein in its uncanniness, "the naked 'that' in the nothing of the world" (SZ 276–277, checked). SZ has a closer parallel to the Postscript in §29: in the most indifferent everydayness "the being of Dasein can break forth as the naked 'that it is and has to be'" (SZ 134). In SZ the "that" belongs to Dasein's thrownness, its own being, not to entities as a whole. The Postscript's "that entities are" is the later widening, and SZ's "that" is closer to Husserl's than Marion's opposition allows: in both the wonder is first of all the self's.

The two wonders oppose the two phenomenologists: Husserl's pure I against Heidegger's Dasein determined by the fact of entities; but Husserl lets the wonder "disappear" into a science, refusing any wonder that would escape the reduction, since "all ontologies fall under the reduction"; no phenomenon par excellence will dazzle the phenomenological gaze, "now without idol" (R V.7 ¶4, n. 62). Marion's reading of Husserl's "disappears" is careful and turns the 1912 text against both its author and Heidegger. For SZ it fixes an opposition SZ does not hold: SZ's Dasein is not "determined by the entity taken in its fact" but by the understanding of being, and its "that" (SZ 134, 276) is its own. The phrase "now without idol" anticipates Marion's later work and is R's.

A phenomenology with no exception to the reduction: (a) the reduction exceeds all ontologies and so cannot be conceived ontologically; Husserl never determined its realm "beyond and otherwise than being"; metaphysics' reductions always started from an identified instance (the place of the forms, *topos eidōn*; ontology; a privileged entity: *ego*, transcendental I, "even *Dasein*"); the reduction "reducing all ontology keeps no ontological ground"; "from where does the reduction reduce?" (R V.7 ¶5). The list puts Dasein among the privileged entities from which metaphysics reduces. In SZ's terms that is a category mistake. Dasein is not an instance from which entities are reduced to *cogitata*, and SZ grounds the understanding of entities in being-in-the-world, not in a reduction (§§12–13). Marion's list repeats chapter III's assimilation of Dasein to the *ego* in a single word. The question "from where does the reduction reduce?" is R's own and stands.

(b) The I's conceptual recovery does not dissolve it into objectness; the "cardinal difference" may be irreducible to ontological difference "by excess", anticipating a non-ontological determination of the I: "indescribable in itself and by itself: pure I and nothing else"; "put outside being", as a building is "put out of water", the I may offer itself to other transcendences (R V.7 ¶6, nn. 63–64). The unit is R's programme, and its note 64 names its heirs (Merleau-Ponty, Levinas, Henry, Derrida), all of whom "construct a doctrine without the being of the I", "with the massive exception of Heidegger". The exception is SZ: the being of the I as care (§64). SZ's account of the I as "a non-committal formal indication" (SZ 116) is the nearest SZ comes to Husserl's "indescribable in itself", and it leads the other way, to the being of the Self (SZ 322). Marion's "outside being" is the contestation of SZ §64 that chapter III's last question announced.

"The greatness of phenomenology is that in it possibility always surpasses actuality"; its present possibility is neither to restore objectness nor to surpass it toward the *Seinsfrage*, but to ask how far the I that reduces even the ontologies "does not first have to be"; Husserl glimpsed it (R V.7 ¶7). SZ 38 returns a fourth time, unquoted, to close the chapter. The sentence is now used against both its author and its object. On SZ's terms the I that "does not first have to be" contradicts the definition of Dasein as the entity that "has to be" its being (*zu sein*, SZ 12, 42). Marion knows this, and the chapter's last word ("a nourishing ground") is aimed at it.

Across chapter V, SZ is load-bearing in a different way from chapters II–IV. Marion uses Husserl to contest Heidegger, and the Heidegger he contests is first GA 20's (the "non-phenomenological" charge, V.1 ¶3), then SZ's founding decision (V.7). Two findings concern SZ directly. First, SZ already contains much of the critique Marion makes of Husserl's formal ontology, and R cites none of it. Formalization "levels the phenomena so far that the genuine phenomenal content is lost" (SZ 88). Existential possibility is distinct from "empty, logical possibility" (SZ 143). Logic has its foundations in ancient ontology (SZ 4). And SZ cites Ideen I §10 and the *Prolegomena* for formal ontology (SZ 77 n.), against R's claim that Heidegger refers "always and exclusively" to LU VI. Marion's critique of Husserl in V.4–V.5 is a critique SZ had made in general and that R carries out in particular. Second, R V.7 ¶2 says outright that SZ "takes up the phenomenological method without ever doing justice to the reduction". That confirms this commentary's first guard, and it withdraws the conclusion of R II.5 ¶2 behind a note ("at least explicitly"). The chapter's thesis, that the I may be "outside being", is a contestation of SZ §§1–4 and §64, announced and not yet argued. Its Husserlian ground lies in texts SZ could not read (*Ideen III*, *First Philosophy*, the 1929 additions to Ideen I).

## VI. The Nothing and the Claim

Chapter VI has no Commentary R, and its principal text, *What Is Metaphysics?* (1929) with its Postscript (1943), its Introduction (1949) and Heidegger's later notes, is not in the corpus. Nor are GA 29/30, GA 40, GA 51 or the "Letter on Humanism". Quotations from these texts are reported as R gives them and are unchecked. SZ is engaged at two places, the analysis of anxiety (§40) and the call of conscience (§§54–60), and in the definition of Dasein (§4). These are checked.

### 1 - Access to the "Phenomenon of Being"

If the enigma of SZ is the concealment of ontological difference by an "ontological difference" limited to Dasein, its aporia is the impossibility of direct access to the "phenomenon of being": the question of being "is always confused, in principle, with the question of the being of Dasein alone", as SZ itself asks, "Is there a way of winning *this being* [that of Dasein] phenomenally on the ground of the present starting point of the existential analytic?"; but SZ also intends to construct the question of being in general, and the principle of all principles demands "unconditionally justified givenness" (R V.1 ¶1, n. 2 / SZ 181). The SZ sentence checks (§39, SZ 181), and R's bracket is right: "this being" is Dasein's being as a structural whole. The use R makes of it is selection. §39 asks how the whole of Dasein's being can be won phenomenally, and answers with anxiety and care. It is not SZ's statement of the question of being in general, which SZ distinguishes from it throughout (SZ 7, 13, 436). The principle of all principles is Ideen I §24, and "unconditionally justified" drops its clause of limits (companion run, guard 16). Two premises of the chapter are thus in place from its first paragraph: chapter IV's verdict, and a Husserlian criterion of phenomenality that SZ does not accept.

Does SZ ever exhibit being as a phenomenon, "under the demand of givenness in person to intuition"? Does being give itself as a phenomenon satisfying the principle of all principles, and is Dasein defined by its destination to being? (R VI.1 ¶2). The criterion "givenness in person to intuition" is Husserl's. SZ does not hold itself to it: intuition is a "remote derivative of understanding" (SZ 147), and the phenomenon in the distinctive sense, the being of entities, is what "proximally and for the most part does *not* show itself" (SZ 35) and calls for interpretation. Measured by intuitive givenness, SZ fails by definition. Measured by its own criterion, exhibition by interpretation, the question is whether the analytic reaches the sense of being, and SZ's answer is that it does not yet (SZ 437). Marion's question is a contestation that changes the court.

The 1929 lecture repeats the exhibition of being, keeping from the analytic only anxiety; the 1943 Postscript corrects SZ, which held that "being is in each case the being of an entity", by saying that being "holds sway (*west*) fully without entities"; the 1949 edition reverses this and returns to SZ's position ("never … without entities") (R VI.1 ¶3, nn. 6–9 / SZ 9; GA 9, 306). SZ 9 checks ("being is in each case the being of an entity"). The 1943 and 1949 sentences are unchecked. R's own note 9 describes the 1949 reversal as a return "to the position of *Sein und Zeit*". That supports guard 13 from the other side. SZ 152's refusal to set the sense of being against entities is what Heidegger himself restored in 1949, and the "decisive advance" R credits to 1943 is one Heidegger withdrew. R says it will "privilege" the 1943 sentence all the same. That is stated as a choice, and the chapter's later argument depends on it.

### 2 - The Tonalities of "Dasein"

The 1929 lecture seeks direct access to being on the ground of the positive sciences, which relate to "entities themselves — and nothing else"; the "nothing else" introduces a term other than any object (R VI.2 ¶1, n. 10 / GA 9, 105). The lecture is not in the corpus. The SZ counterpart is the thematization of the sciences in §69b, where science objectifies a field of entities already disclosed in an understanding of being (SZ 362–363). In SZ the sciences presuppose the understanding of being. In the lecture, as Marion reports it, they presuppose the nothing. The shift of term is the 1929 move, and R reports it as such.

The objection of objectness: questioning the nothing treats it as an entity; Heidegger anticipates Carnap (1932), for whom "nothing else" is a logical particle; modern metaphysics as a whole needs this reduction (R VI.2 ¶2, nn. 13–14). No SZ locus. SZ's own treatment of the "not" is the sentence Marion will cite at VI.5 ¶2: "the ontological essence of the 'not' in general" remains "obscure", and "ontology and logic have demanded a great deal of the 'not'" (§58, SZ 285–286). In 1927 SZ marks as an open problem what the 1929 lecture undertakes.

Bergson, otherwise opposed to Carnap, reduces the nothing to a "pseudo-idea" and to a misuse of negation (R VI.2 ¶3, nn. 15–18). Outside this pair.

Does the nothing produce negation or does negation presuppose it? "We assert: the nothing is more original than the not and negation"; if the nothing is to be questioned, "it must first be given"; the nothing is defined as "the complete negation of the totality of entities" (R VI.2 ¶4, nn. 20–22 / GA 9, 107–109). The lecture's "it must first be given" is reported by R and unchecked. Its verb is the one SZ avoided (guard 2). If R's quotation is exact, the 1929 lecture uses "given" in the positive sense SZ refused it in §7 (SZ 36: phenomena are at first "not given"). That would make the lecture, not SZ, the Heideggerian text closest to Marion's criterion of givenness, and R's choice of it as SZ's "repetition" is consistent with that.

How does boredom throw us into the midst of entities as a whole? Profound boredom "removes all things, men and oneself along with them, into a remarkable indifference"; GA 29/30 distinguishes three forms (R VI.2 ¶5, nn. 26–28). GA 29/30 is not in the corpus. SZ has no analysis of boredom. Its nearest counterpart is the "pallid lack of mood" (*Ungestimmtheit*) of §29, in which "Dasein becomes weary of itself" and "being has become manifest as a burden" (SZ 134, checked). That is the passage where SZ's "naked 'that it is'" appears (chapter V), and it is the SZ ground for Marion's later use of boredom.

Two remarks: Heidegger does not justify boredom's ontic or ontological reach, and the lecture mentions joy at the presence of a beloved person as another access to entities as a whole, never analysed (R VI.2 ¶6, nn. 29–30). Marion's note 29 lists Heidegger's statements on love, beginning with GA 20. SZ has none: love is absent from SZ's analysis of disposedness, and *Mitsein* (being-with) is treated through solicitude (§26). The omission R points to in 1929 is SZ's too. Marion's own *Prolegomena to Charity* (1986) is the frame, and he says so.

Anxiety, unlike fear, fixes on no entity; its indeterminacy is "the very definition of anxiety"; it is the "fundamental mood" that discloses the nothing (R VI.2 ¶7, n. 31 / SZ 182; GA 9, 111). This is SZ's analysis, and R's account follows it closely, though it is presented through the lecture. "That in the face of which anxiety is anxious is completely indefinite" (§40, SZ 186, checked), and SZ grounds its difference from fear in the same way (§30, §40). The "fundamental disposedness" (*Grundbefindlichkeit*) is SZ's term (SZ 182, 184). On this point the 1929 lecture repeats SZ, and R's reconstruction is accurate to both.

"Anxiety reveals the nothing"; "the nothing itself — as such — was there"; "the question of the nothing remains effectively posed" (R VI.2 ¶8, nn. 32–33 / GA 9, 112–113). SZ has the nothing in anxiety: "the nothing, that is, the world as such" (SZ 187, see II.7). R's question at the close, "what does the nothing, which is there, manifest to being-there?", is answered in SZ: being-in-the-world itself. R takes that answer up in the next section.

### 3 - The Hermeneutic of the Nothing as Being

Anxiety is ambiguous, of a "twofold essence", and the nothing "enigmatically equivocal"; so neither identifies the phenomenon they stage (R VI.3 ¶1, n. 34). The quotations are the lecture's and the Postscript's. SZ's anxiety is not ambiguous in this way. It discloses being-in-the-world and individualizes Dasein (SZ 187–188). The equivocation R finds belongs to the 1929 and 1943 texts.

The nothing is met "at one with entities as a whole"; anxiety's recoil is a "repelling reference" (*abweisende Verweisung*), which expels entities and refers to them as a whole; Marion links it to SZ's *Verweisung*, the reference of equipment (§15) and of appearance (§7) (R VI.3 ¶2, nn. 35–37 / SZ 68, 31, 35). SZ's word is used in both places, as R says: appearance "means a referential relation in the entity itself" (SZ 31, checked), and equipment is constituted by reference (§§15–18). The link is R's, and it is fair as a reading of vocabulary. In SZ, though, *Verweisung* is always a relation among entities or from entity to entity. It is not a relation from the nothing to entities. The 1929 phrase gives an old word a new subject.

To what does the nothing refer? The lecture refers anxiety to the nothing and the nothing to Dasein's transcendence; SZ referred the anxious appearance of the nothing to being-in-the-world: "that in the face of which anxiety is anxious is being-in-the-world itself", not "a total nothing" but "this nothing of the ready-to-hand"; GA 20 likewise (R VI.3 ¶3, nn. 38–41 / SZ 186–188, 343). The SZ quotations check: "this nothing of the ready-to-hand … is not a total nothing" (§40, SZ 187, checked), with "the nothing, that is, the world as such" on the same page. R's note also cites §68b (SZ 343), where the analysis of anxiety is resumed in temporal terms. This is the faithful reading of SZ that chapter II.7 lacked. There Marion contrasted 1927 anxiety, "circumscribed to Dasein alone", with 1929's anxiety before entities as a whole. Here he reports that SZ's nothing is the nothing of the ready-to-hand, grounded in the world. The two accounts are compatible, and this one is SZ's.

The Postscript completes the referral: the nothing, "the wholly other to entities", "holds sway as being"; Dasein "learns to experience being in the nothing"; the passage from anxiety to the nothing is stable from 1925 to 1929, but the referral of the nothing to being competes with its referral to transcendence (1929) and to being-in-the-world (1925, 1927) (R VI.3 ¶4, nn. 44–45). The Postscript is unchecked. R writes "the 1927 lecture" for the lecture of 1929 in this paragraph, an evident slip of the source. R's thesis, that three referrals compete, is well founded on SZ's side: in 1927 the nothing refers to the world. That SZ's referral is the one with the strongest textual basis R does not say, though its next unit shows the 1943 referral was reached "after the fact".

How did Heidegger come to refer the nothing to being, which neither 1929 nor 1927 had done? By three later operations: accentuations, substitutions and an addition (R VI.3 ¶5). The question is the right one, and it holds R to a standard that chapter IV did not apply to SZ's marginalia (next unit).

The accentuations and substitutions are notes of 1949: "nothing as 'being'"; "that is, nothing and being the same"; "*ab-weisen*: the entity for itself; *ver-weisen*: into the being of the entity"; Dasein before "the entity as such" becomes, in a 1949 substitution, "before the being of the entity, before the difference"; and the addition of ontological difference (R VI.3 ¶6, nn. 46–51). These notes are unchecked. Two points of method follow from R's own treatment. First, R tests them as later interpretation, "laborious procedures by which, after the fact, Heidegger believes he can refer the nothing to the phenomenon of being". In chapter IV, R treated the parallel notes in Heidegger's copy of SZ as confirmation ("Heidegger did not have to over-interpret his own texts", IV.3 ¶5) and as the book's own answer (IV.7). The same kind of evidence counts as self-interpretation to be tested in chapter VI and as self-confirmation in chapter IV. Second, R's note 51 reports that in 1949 Heidegger added references to ontological difference to the lecture while conceding that its body did not contain it. That supports this commentary's reading of chapter IV's GA 9 notes (IV.2 ¶3): the "1929" layer of notes to the 1928 essay is of the same kind and date.

"But how will it [the interpretation] be guided, since far from being able to follow the 'phenomenon of being', it is precisely its task to let it appear at last?" (R VI.3 ¶7). The question is a hermeneutic circle, and SZ has an answer to its form. Interpretation is always guided by a fore-structure, and the circle is not vicious but the expression of the existential fore-structure of Dasein (§32, SZ 152–153). Marion poses it as an aporia of the 1943 interpretation. SZ would pose it as the condition of any interpretation.

### 4 - Last Call: the Claim

Anxiety gives access not to the phenomenon of being but only to the nothing, which still requires an interpretation; even after the "radicalization" of 1929, which abandoned "the problematic of the question of being in the narrow sense" and substituted the nothing for being-in-the-world, the most perilous step, from the nothing to being, remains; three questions: why, along what guideline, and with what result? (R VI.4 ¶1, nn. 52–53). The "substitution of the nothing for being-in-the-world" is R's correct description of the difference between SZ and the lecture (previous section). R calls it a radicalization. On SZ's terms it is the loss of the one referral that SZ's analysis grounds. The judgement is R's, and it is stated.

The necessity: in anxiety "all things and we ourselves sink into indifference"; a note of 1949 says "the entity no longer speaks to us (*spricht nicht mehr an*)", which Marion reads as "no longer claims us"; the nothing says nothing, so how can it refer to anything but itself? (R VI.4 ¶2, n. 54). The 1949 note is unchecked. SZ's anxiety has the same "insignificance" of the world: "the world has the character of complete insignificance" (SZ 186). In SZ that insignificance is what discloses the world as world. It does not leave anxiety mute. Anxiety "throws Dasein back upon that for which it is anxious, its authentic potentiality-for-being-in-the-world" (SZ 187). SZ's anxiety refers, and R's "the nothing says nothing" describes the lecture's version, not SZ's.

The Postscript's penultimate paragraph: "the nothing as the other of entities is the veil of being"; taken literally this is untenable, since anxiety takes speech from man and from the entity alike; Heidegger here keeps the gap and does not identify the nothing with being (R VI.4 ¶3, n. 55). The text is the Postscript's and unchecked. R's reading of "veil" as a prudence that "belies other more intrepid identifications" is careful. No SZ locus.

The Postscript's sentence that the abandonment of being is not "a nugatory nothing", "since it belongs to the truth of being that being never holds sway without entities": Marion says the argument presupposes what it must establish (R VI.4 ¶4, nn. 56–59). This is the sentence of 1949 that R VI.1 ¶3 n. 9 called a return to SZ. R's critique of it, that the mutual belonging of being and entities is concealed in anxiety and so cannot be invoked from within it, is a critique of SZ 152 as well. For SZ that belonging is not an argument invoked inside anxiety. It is the structure of the understanding of being that anxiety discloses (SZ 152, 183). R contests the 1949 sentence. It contests SZ by implication, and without its text.

The Postscript introduces a new instance, "the claim of being" (*Anspruch des Seins*), unknown in 1929: "the voice of being … which claims man in his essence, that he learn to experience being in the nothing"; thinking "answers the claim of being"; "being claims man" is glossed in 1949 by "*er-eignet*, *braucht*" (appropriates, uses); so "in the last instance it is not being but the claim it exercises"; "being gives itself only to a response" (R VI.4 ¶5, nn. 60–61). The texts are unchecked. Note 60 says that the lecture of 1929 uses "claim" only once, of science. In SZ the word occurs in its ordinary sense ("in Anspruch nehmen", to lay claim to). The structure R will use, a call that summons and a response, is SZ's call of conscience (§§54–60), which R takes up in the next section. R's "in the last instance … the claim" is R's thesis, and it gives the Postscript a priority of claim over being that the Postscript's own sentence ("being claims") does not state.

### 5 - The Boredom of the Depths

Does the interpretation of the nothing as being, now supported by *Ereignis*, finally stage the "phenomenon of being"? (R VI.5 ¶1). The question is R's and prepares the doubts.

Doubts: (a) being speaks only with a "voice without sound"; how does its silence differ from the silence of the lack of entities? (b) SZ had already analysed a call, that of conscience, which far from calling to and in the name of being "referred to nothing": "What does conscience call to the one summoned? Strictly speaking — nothing"; "Dasein is at once the caller and the summoned"; the call blocks Dasein "in pure indeterminacy", and SZ concedes that "the ontological sense of the notness of this existential nullity remains obscure". (c) The response of gratitude may fail. (d) The corrections of 1949 subordinate thanking (*Danken*) to thinking (*Denken*) (R VI.5 ¶2, nn. 62–66 / SZ 273, 277, 279, 285; GA 9, 306–310; GA 51). The SZ quotations check. "Strictly speaking — nothing. The call asserts nothing, gives no information about events of the world, has nothing to tell" (§56, SZ 273). "Ontologically, however, the answer that Dasein is at once the caller and the summoned is by no means sufficient" (§57, SZ 275), until the analysis fills it: "the sentence: Dasein is at once the caller and the summoned, has now lost its formal emptiness … the caller is Dasein, anxious in its thrownness about its potentiality-for-being. The summoned is this very Dasein, called forth to its ownmost potentiality-for-being" (SZ 277–278). "The ontological sense of the notness of this existential nullity remains obscure" (§58, SZ 285). R's reading of (b) cuts at the point where SZ fills the formula. The call "says nothing" in the sense of giving no information. It does not leave Dasein "in pure indeterminacy". It calls Dasein forth to its ownmost potentiality-for-being, out of its lostness in the "they" (SZ 273, 277–278). The concession of SZ 285 concerns the ontology of the "not", which SZ says has been left obscure by the whole tradition. It is not a concession that the call is empty. This is selection. Marion's note 64 reports that Husserl wrote "Yes!" beside SZ 285 in his copy. Husserl's marginalia are not in the corpus (Bernet frame, §3), so the note is unchecked.

If the claim can be answered only by an exposure that can refuse, the hermeneutic of the nothing *can* fail; this must be seen as "a new existential", or rather "a counter-existential", which suspends Dasein's destination to being (R VI.5 ¶3). SZ has the refusal R needs, and makes it an existential mode. "If the caller and the summoned are in each case one's own Dasein, then in every failure to hear the call, in every mishearing, there lies a *definite mode of being* of Dasein. A free-floating call upon which 'nothing ensues' is, existentially seen, an impossible fiction" (§57, SZ 279, checked). Marion's "counter-existential" is SZ's mode of not hearing, extended to the call of being, which SZ does not have. SZ already counts the refusal of the call as a way of being of the summoned. It denies that a call can float free of any being. The second sentence of SZ 279 is the objection that VI.7's "pure form of the call" must meet.

Boredom is the counter-existential: Heidegger used it only to bring Dasein before entities as a whole, and GA 29/30 frees Dasein for the world as such; but boredom could free us from the call by which being claims us (R VI.5 ¶4, n. 67). GA 29/30 is unchecked. In SZ boredom is not named. Its nearest form is the burden of being in the lack of mood (SZ 134), and there it discloses Dasein's being, the "naked 'that it is'". SZ's form of boredom therefore does not free Dasein from being. It shows it its being as a burden. Marion's boredom is a contestation of SZ §29 by a different mood.

Pascal's boredom: "the condition of man: inconstancy, boredom, unrest"; boredom determines man's worldly condition, his facticity; the entity that knows itself an entity can distance itself from being this entity (R VI.5 ¶5, nn. 68–70). The Pascal texts are outside the corpus. Marion's identification of "condition" with facticity uses SZ's term (§12, SZ 56). For SZ facticity is being "delivered over" to one's being (SZ 134–135), not a distance from it. Pascal's boredom as R reads it is closer to SZ's "burden" than to a release.

This boredom is not nihilism, which still affirms the world in a "great Amen", nor negation, which presupposes predication and passion (R VI.5 ¶6). No SZ locus. The distinction from negation echoes the 1929 thesis (the nothing prior to the "not", VI.2 ¶4) and turns it toward an attitude rather than a phenomenon.

Boredom leaves entities in place, without affecting them; it shirks every call; it "hates what is" (*ennui* from *in odio*) (R VI.5 ¶7, n. 71). The etymology and Cassian are outside this pair. The description has an SZ counterpart Marion does not use: the averageness and levelling of the "they" and its idle talk, curiosity and ambiguity, in which Dasein is absorbed without being touched (§§27, 35–38). SZ treats that indifference as falling. Marion's boredom is a falling that refuses to be measured against authenticity.

A double defeat: the I that is bored abandons itself and becomes impersonal, "I no longer am, *I* is"; and it strikes entities with vanity (R VI.5 ¶8, nn. 71–74). The impersonal "I" has an SZ counterpart: the "they-self", in which "everyone is the other and no one is himself" (SZ 128). R's "I is" is the "they" described from inside. SZ makes the "they" an existential and a mode of Dasein's being (SZ 129), so on SZ's terms boredom would not destitute Dasein of being. It would be one way of being Dasein.

Does boredom disqualify every call, and so the claim of being, and so access to being from the nothing? (R VI.5 ¶9). The question rests on the preceding units. SZ 279's "impossible fiction" is the textual answer SZ gives to a call that nothing follows. Boredom that disqualifies every call is, in SZ's terms, a mode of hearing the call, not its abolition.

### 6 - The Third Reduction

Boredom suspends the claim of being; if Dasein is Dasein by its openness to being and boredom deserts what is, then the I can withdraw from the destiny of Dasein and suspend every claim; two theses must be established: being exposes itself to boredom, and Dasein can endure it ontologically (R VI.6 ¶1, n. 75). Marion quotes the "Letter on Humanism" for Dasein "standing out ecstatically in the truth of being". That is the later Heidegger's definition. SZ's is that Dasein is the entity for which "in its being this being is at issue" (SZ 12). The thesis that the I can withdraw from Dasein's destiny contests SZ 12 directly, and Marion acknowledges that it contradicts "the explicit intention" of Heidegger's enterprise.

Being exposes itself to boredom because it demands wonder: "the wonder of wonders: *that* entities are" (Postscript), Aristotle and Plato on *thaumazein*, Descartes on admiration; what demands wonder can be met with boredom (R VI.6 ¶2, nn. 76–78). The Postscript is unchecked. SZ has no "wonder" of being. Its access is the understanding of being, average and vague (SZ 5–6), and wonder is absent from its disposedness. The argument therefore engages the later Heidegger, not SZ.

Dasein can refuse its being, through two features: existence as possibility, "a possibility of itself to be itself or not itself", so that inauthenticity defines Dasein as essentially as authenticity and may revoke the call of being; and Dasein "has to be (*zu sein hat*) its being as its own", so that it can also not be it; boredom "bears on the joint where Dasein is articulated with being" (R VI.6 ¶3, nn. 80–81 / SZ 12, 42). The SZ texts check (SZ 12, 42). Marion reads SZ with precision here, and the argument is the chapter's strongest engagement with SZ. Two points qualify it. First, SZ's "not itself" is not "not being". Inauthenticity is a way of being Dasein, and "the inauthenticity of Dasein does not signify any 'less' being or any 'lower' degree of being" (SZ 43). Second, SZ's "has to be" (*Zu-sein*) states the burden of being, not an option: Dasein is the entity "delivered over to its being as the being which, existing, it has to be" (SZ 134), whether it takes that being up or not. Marion's inference, "if Dasein has to be its being … it can therefore also not be it", turns a "has to" into a "may", and SZ blocks that turn at §29. This is contestation, and SZ has the resources R does not engage.

Released by boredom from the call of being, the "there" is exposed to "another claim", in the name of another than being; Heidegger admits it in the "Letter on Humanism", opposing to the claim of being "the claim of the Father in Christ" (R VI.6 ¶4, n. 82). The "Letter" is outside the corpus. R's note quotes GA 20, "the inquiring is itself an entity" (GA 20, 199), against identifying the question of being with any entity. For SZ the unit's thesis, that the "there" can stand for another than being, contests SZ 132–133, where the "there" is Dasein's disclosedness and "Dasein is its disclosedness". On SZ's account there is no "there" before or apart from the being of Dasein.

Is this transgression still phenomenological? Yes, for the same reason that allowed the analytic of Dasein to replace the constitution of the transcendental I: "the Husserlian 'breakthrough' as the Heideggerian 'new beginning' proceed according to the reduction, in two figures"; a third reduction now reduces every claim to "the pure form of the call" (R VI.6 ¶5, n. 84). The premise, that Heidegger's new beginning "proceeds according to the reduction", is chapter II's (n. 84 refers back to II.5–7). Chapter V withdrew it: Heidegger in SZ takes up the phenomenological method "without ever doing justice to the reduction" (R V.7 ¶2; guard 21). The third reduction thus counts a second reduction that SZ does not perform and that GA 24 §5 names "in wording, not in substance" (guard 11). Marion's sequence (objects to consciousness, entities to Dasein, entities to being, every claim to the pure form of the call) is R's construction. Its second and third members are not SZ's operations under any name SZ uses. The third reduction may stand on its own. Its claim to repeat a Heideggerian reduction does not.

### 7 - "There" outside Being

Boredom makes the call appear as such; in the pure form of the call a reduction is accomplished: nothing gives itself as a phenomenon unless a response gives itself over to it; "to be amounts to giving, and so demands that one give oneself over"; the claim precedes being; who receives the call, neither constituting I nor Dasein? (R VI.7 ¶1). R's "to be amounts to giving" is the book's thesis, and on SZ's terms it inverts the order of SZ §44, where being "is given" only insofar as truth is, and truth only insofar as Dasein is (SZ 230). SZ's "there is" (*es gibt*) depends on Dasein's disclosedness. R's giving precedes it.

The claim calls *me*: I am named as a "me" before I can say "I"; "Here I am! Speak!"; this is not the critique of the empirical I in favour of a transcendental I (R VI.7 ¶2). The accusative "me" has an SZ counterpart in the call of conscience: the summoned is the they-self, called "to its *own self*" (SZ 273), and the call "comes from me and yet over me" (SZ 275). SZ's summoned is also first addressed, and only then in a position to answer. The difference is that SZ names the caller (Dasein in its uncanniness, SZ 277), and Marion will refuse to name it.

The same analysis applies to Dasein: Dasein must hear the claim of being to stand as the "there" offered to being; so "the 'there' precedes and determines being, *da sein*, and not the reverse" (R VI.7 ¶3). The etymological reading runs against SZ's text. "This entity carries in its ownmost being the character of not being closed. The expression 'there' means this essential disclosedness"; "Dasein *is* its disclosedness" (§28, SZ 132–133). In SZ the "there" is not a place that precedes being and is then occupied. It is Dasein's being, as disclosedness. Marion's reading of *da sein* as "there, then being" is a re-inscription. It needs the "there" to be separable from being in order for a claim other than being's to occupy it, and SZ 132–133 makes it inseparable.

The interlocutor of the claim is named the interlocuted (*der Angesprochene*) (R VI.7 ¶4). The German word is chosen to echo the Postscript's *Anspruch* and SZ's *der Angerufene* (the summoned, SZ 273–279). The figure is SZ's summoned, with its relation to the caller made anonymous.

Its traits: (a) convocation: the interlocuted is reached by the claim, renounces "the autistic autarky of an absolute subjectivity", and this precedes intentionality and being-in-the-world; relation subverts substance; "a subject without subjecti(vi)ty"; (b) surprise (R VI.7 ¶5). The claim that the convocation "precedes … being-in-the-world, even disqualifies it" contests SZ's ordering, in which the call presupposes being-in-the-world: the caller is "the original thrown being-in-the-world" (SZ 277), and the summoned is called out of its lostness in the "world" of the "they". On SZ's account the call is a structure of being-in-the-world. Marion's convocation precedes it, and his ground is a description of being interpellated that SZ would locate inside the world.

(c) Identification: the interlocuted does not dissolve but identifies itself because another provokes it to decide; "mineness … can then be accomplished without self-determination or 'anticipatory resoluteness'"; "the proper name can be proclaimed only at the call"; (d) facticity (R VI.7 ¶6). The phrase "without … 'anticipatory resoluteness'" quotes SZ to set it aside. In SZ, "self-constancy signifies existentially nothing other than anticipatory resoluteness" (SZ 322), and mineness is the character of Dasein's being (SZ 42). Marion keeps mineness and removes its existential ground. The call in SZ already provokes Dasein to decide: understanding the call is "letting oneself be called forth" to one's ownmost possibility (SZ 287). What Marion removes is the identity of caller and summoned. This is contestation of SZ §§57–64, and its stake is stated: identity received from another, not from oneself.

The four traits, convocation, surprise, identification and facticity, institute the interlocuted "without any other presupposition than the pure form of the call", which originates in "the first passion, admiration" (R VI.7 ¶7). The "pure form of the call" is the term SZ 279 calls "an impossible fiction" when it means a call on which nothing ensues. Marion's form is not free-floating in that sense: something ensues, the interlocuted. What it lacks is the identification of the caller, which SZ makes the ontological content of the call. The unit's appeal to admiration (Descartes) closes the circle with VI.6 ¶2.

Objection: who or what claims the interlocuted? God, being, the other, auto-affection would make it a derived instance; but "I recognize myself interlocuted well *before* knowing … what leaves me interlocuted"; the indecision of the claiming instance attests "the pure form of the call as such" (R VI.7 ¶8). SZ meets the same question in §57 and answers it differently. It grants the caller's "peculiar indeterminacy and indeterminability", "a *positive* distinction" (SZ 274–275), and then shows phenomenally that the caller is Dasein itself in its uncanniness (SZ 276–277). Marion stops at SZ 275 and declines the step to SZ 277. His reason is phenomenological: description finds the interpellation before the identity of what interpellates. SZ's reason for the step is also phenomenological: "the call comes *from me and yet over me*. This phenomenal finding is not to be explained away" (SZ 275). The two readings divide on one sentence of SZ, and Marion's reading of the call is SZ's analysis halted before its conclusion.

Across chapter VI, Marion's principal Heidegger is the lecture of 1929 and its later layers, which the corpus does not hold. SZ enters at three points, and the run can measure each. On anxiety, R's account of SZ is accurate: the nothing of SZ is "this nothing of the ready-to-hand", "not a total nothing", referring to being-in-the-world (SZ 187). That corrects chapter II's contrast of 1927 and 1929. On the call of conscience, R's reading is selective. It quotes SZ's "strictly speaking — nothing" (SZ 273) and the formula "caller and summoned at once", and leaves out SZ's filling of the formula (SZ 277–278). It also leaves out SZ 279, which makes failing to hear the call a mode of Dasein's being and calls a free-floating call "an impossible fiction". Both are the texts R's "counter-existential" and "pure form of the call" must meet. On Dasein, R reads SZ 12 and 42 with care, and draws from "has to be" a "may not be" that SZ §29 refuses. Its etymology of the "there" contradicts SZ 132–133. The "third reduction" counts a Heideggerian second reduction that R's own chapter V admits SZ does not perform. The chapter treats the later notes to *What Is Metaphysics?* as interpretation after the fact, which is the right method. That method was not applied to the notes in Heidegger's copy of SZ in chapter IV. What stands is a contestation of SZ's founding decisions (§§4, 28, 57, 64), carried out with SZ's own structure of call, summoned and response, and with the caller left unnamed. The structure is borrowed and the anonymity is Marion's. Where SZ is cited, the borrowing goes unacknowledged.

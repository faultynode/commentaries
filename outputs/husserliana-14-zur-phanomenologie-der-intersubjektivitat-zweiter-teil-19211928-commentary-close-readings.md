# Husserl, *Zur Phänomenologie der Intersubjektivität. Zweiter Teil: 1921–1928*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary-key-passages.md). The [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md) works through Iso Kern's introduction and the texts of Section I as far as Appendix XXVI, block by block. Each reading here follows one understanding that a passage carries: what Husserl means by a term or a claim, how his wording builds it, what the passage does between its neighbours, and where the understanding goes in the rest of the volume and in Husserl's other works.

The text is Husserliana XIV, *Zur Phänomenologie der Intersubjektivität*, second part, 1921–1928, edited by Iso Kern (Nijhoff, 1973). It is quoted from the converted German file in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil.md`). Passages are cited by text number (No.) or appendix and by Hua XIV page, roman for Kern's introduction. Unit references such as "No. 3 §4.5" name the fifth paragraph of the commentary under § 4 of No. 3, as the key-passage list defines them. All English renderings of Husserl and of Kern are my working translations, kept close to the German so that its structure shows.

The volume is a set of research manuscripts, not a book. Most texts carry two layers: the manuscript as written, mostly in 1921 and 1922, and Husserl's later marginal notes and changes, which Kern prints as footnotes. The footnotes are numbered per text and often restart at 1, and Kern's notes (marked "editor's note") and Husserl's own marginalia share one series. Several readings turn on the difference between the layers, and each says which layer it quotes.

The file has limits that bear on more than one reading. Its page numbers come from running heads kept as comments, about one per page, so a page can be fixed but a passage that straddles two pages is cited across both. Italics survive only sparsely, and Husserl's spaced emphasis is not rendered at all, so no reading argues from the absence of emphasis. Margin line numbers of the print have leaked into some hard-wrapped stretches; they are left out of the quotations, and each reading says where. A few lines carry OCR damage (on Hua XIV 6, 45 and 61), and the Greek of No. 3 is a LaTeX conversion. No passage quoted here is repaired. Where the main commentary names an author the source does not (Lipps, Spencer and Schäffle, Fichte and Mead, Tönnies's term *Kürwille*), the name is the commentary's, and the readings do not attribute it to Husserl.

## Introduction, Hua XIV XXII; No. 13, 260: monads with windows

Reads [Intro I.5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#i), fifth paragraph under heading I of the Editor's Introduction.

Husserl's monad is closed and open at once, in two different respects. It is closed in what it really contains (*reell*, as an inherent component of its stream): nothing of another subject's life can become a part of mine. It is open in what it experiences: through empathy the other subject is given to me, as my own past is given to me in recollection. The windows are not a breach in the closure. They are a mode of experience that leaves the closure intact. Kern sets the sentence at the head of the volume's horizon, but he quotes only its first half, and the half he cuts is the one that says what the windows are.

> Every ego is for itself, is for itself a unity, has its stream of lived experience, has its real poles presenting themselves in it, its productions of ideal formations, etc. Every ego is a "monad". But the monads have windows. They have no windows or doors insofar as no other subject can enter really, but through them (the windows are the empathies) it can be experienced just as well as past experiences of one's own can through recollection.

German:

> Jedes Ich ist für sich, ist für sich eine Einheit, hat seinen Erlebnisstrom, hat seine darin sich darstellenden realen Pole, seine Erzeugungen idealer Gebilde etc. Jedes Ich ist eine „Monade“. Aber die Monaden haben Fenster. Sie haben insofern keine Fenster oder Türen, als kein anderes Subjekt reell eintreten kann, aber durch die hindurch es (die Fenster sind die Einfühlungen) so gut erfahren sein kann wie vergangene eigene Erlebnisse durch Wiedererinnerung.

The paragraph is No. 13 (January–February 1922), Hua XIV 260. Kern's introduction (XXII) quotes it from "Every ego" to "can enter really" and closes the quotation with an ellipsis.

The first sentence lists what belongs to an ego "for itself": a stream, its "real poles" (*realen Pole*), its ideal productions. "Real" here names the things of nature, which present themselves (*sich darstellen*) in the stream as poles of its appearances. They are "in" the stream only as what it presents. The fourth sentence then uses the other word: no other subject can enter "really" (*reell*). The two adjectives stand three lines apart and do opposite work. *Real* is said of what the stream intends and constitutes, the thing in nature. *Reell* is said of what the stream is made of, its lived experiences as components. The paragraph depends on the distinction. A thing is a real pole of my stream and of yours, and so the same for both of us; my lived experience can never be a reell part of your stream. The monad's closure is a closure of reell content only.

The term "monad" stands in quotation marks. Husserl takes Leibniz's word and keeps his distance from its metaphysics, as he had done in 1911, when *Philosophy as a Rigorous Science* said that the psychical is distributed, "in a simile and not metaphysically speaking", among "monads that have no windows and stand in *commercium* only through empathy" (*Monaden, die keine Fenster haben und nur durch Einfühlung im* commercium *stehen*; Hua XXV 28). In 1911 the monads have no windows, and empathy is their commerce. In 1922 empathy is itself the window. The adversative "But" (*Aber*) contradicts Leibniz, and Husserl's own earlier sentence with him, in four words. The next sentence takes most of the contradiction back with "insofar" (*insofern*): the monads have no windows "or doors" in one respect, namely reell entry. Husserl reverses Leibniz's formula and then restores it, in a restricted respect. What he rejects is the inference from closure to isolation, not the closure.

The positive clause turns on a comparison. Through the windows the other subject "can be experienced just as well as" (*so gut … wie*) my own past experiences can through recollection. Recollection is the model because it, too, gives something that is not reell present: the remembered experience is no longer a component of my living present, and yet it is given as itself and as mine. Empathy is set on the same level of access. This is a stronger claim than Husserl made in 1910/11, when the synopsis he wrote after the lectures said that "recollection gives the self!" and that "empathizing presentification … does not give the self" (Hua XIII 111). The comparison of 1922 does not cancel the asymmetry, since recollection gives my own past and empathy gives another's present that I can never live. But "just as well" claims that both are experience in the full sense, with a right of their own, and not inferences from what is reell given. The parenthesis "(the windows are the empathies)" supplies the definition in the plural: not one faculty, but each act of empathy is a window.

Kern's ellipsis falls exactly before this clause. His introduction places the quotation between the thesis of No. 1, "empathy creates the first true transcendence" (Hua XIV 8), and a passage from Hua XIII (232 f.) on the "connection" (*verbunden*, in quotation marks) of egos through the nature each constitutes and an "intentional identity". Read in that frame, the cut quotation says only that the monads have windows and that nothing enters; the reader has to supply from the neighbouring quotations what passes through them. The commentary follows the frame and sums up the double thesis as "no real entry of one subject into another, and yet a real connection through a common nature". The wording blurs the point the paragraph turns on. The entry that is excluded is reell, and the connection that is affirmed is intentional: it runs through the real poles that both streams constitute as the same. Neither half is "real" in the sense the commentary's English suggests. (Logged under Corrections.)

In No. 13 the paragraph follows directly on the claim that a nature constituted for a solitary ego can be constituted "as the same" many times over only through the connection of empathy, and that all subjectivities that are to exist together must have constituted in themselves "an identical nature" in which each has its body (Hua XIV 259–60). The windows presuppose that nature. The paragraph after it draws the consequence: for each monad there is a world with animal and human subjects whose bodies are things "in which a monad expresses itself" (Hua XIV 260). Body as expression is the frame of the window. I do not see into the other's stream; I experience it in the body that expresses it, as a pole that is real for both of us.

Within the commentary's range the window acquires its mechanics in No. 10 §5 (Hua XIV 199), where understanding another is a presentification in which "consciousness mirrors itself in consciousness" (Row 36 of the list). Outside it, Husserl kept the restricted windowlessness to the end. An appendix of October 1931 to the *Cartesian Meditations*, whose title opposes the "intentional interpenetration" (*intentionales Ineinander*) of the monads to their "reell being-outside-one-another" (*reelles Aussereinander*), says that "in this sense no monad has windows through which monadic 'matter' could fly in or out" (*In diesem Sinn hat keine Monade Fenster, in die monadische „Materie“ hineinfliegen könnte oder herausfliegen*; Hua XV 376). "In this sense" repeats the "insofar" of 1922. The windowless monad and the monad with windows are one doctrine, divided by the distinction between reell and real, and Kern's cut keeps only the first of them.

## No. 1, Hua XIV 6: the second birth of the foreign body

Reads [No. 1.6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#no-1-the-apperception-of-ones-own-body-as-the-original-bodily-apperception-and-the-mediacy-of-the-apperception-of-the-others-body-the-constitution-of-the-first-true-transcendence-through-empathy-summer-semester-1921), sixth paragraph under No. 1.

The foreign lived body comes into being for me twice. It is first constituted as an external thing among things, and only then is it "translated" into a lived body that carries an inner life and an ego. The metaphor of two births fixes an order of founding, and it fixes it as the mirror image of my own case: my body is first given from within, and its constitution as a physical thing comes later. The other's body begins where mine ends. The passage states the first half of that chiasmus, and No. 3 later states the second.

> So every foreign body, as given in exteriority and thus as given after a first birth as an external thing, must first of all be translated, must undergo a second birth in being apprehended as a lived body, as a lived body constituted in interiority and as carrying with it a whole interiority of consciousness and an ego, as completing itself thereby into an animal and human being. So many bodies, so many separate human beings and human egos, human streams of experience, each for each an exteriority, each related to the same world, etc.

German:

> So muss also jeder fremde Leib als gegeben in der Äusserlichkeit und somit als gegeben nach erster Geburt als äusseres Ding allererst übersetzt werden, eine zweite Geburt erfahren in der Auffassung als Leib, als Leib konstituiert in Innerlichkeit und als mit sich führend eine ganze Bewusstseinsinnerlichkeit und ein Ich, als sich ergänzend damit zu einem animalischen und menschlichen Wesen. Soviel Leiber, soviel gesonderte Menschen und Menschen-Ich, menschliche Erlebnisströme, jeder für jeden Äusserlichkeit, jeder bezogen auf dieselbe Welt etc.

The sentence is built as a consequence, "So … therefore" (*So muss also*). What it follows from is the paragraph before, on my own body. My body is "not a thing like another thing" but a unity distinguished by the modes of appearance it allows "as my body", and the "physical bodily thing" it carries is "not constituted from the outset" with its givenness as a lived body; it belongs to a "special and supervening objectivation" that sets the body on a level with other things in physical causality (Hua XIV 6). The foreign body is the case where the order is the other way round. I cannot take up the attitude toward it from within that makes my body a lived body, and so what is given to me first can only be what is given in exteriority. The paragraph before has lost words at the decisive point: the file reads "als diesen Leib in den *Inneneinstellung* nannten" (as this lived body in the inner attitude [we] called), which no longer makes a sentence. Only the term *Inneneinstellung* (inner attitude) can be taken from it, and this reading takes nothing more.

Three words carry the order. "After a first birth" (*nach erster Geburt*) has no article, as though "first birth" were a fixed term; and "birth" names a coming-into-being for consciousness, an original formation (*Urbildung*), the word the next paragraph uses for the first constitution of an apperception. "First of all" (*allererst*) marks the translation as a step that must come after the first birth and cannot be skipped. And "translated" (*übersetzt*) says what kind of step it is. A translation keeps its text and gives it another sense. The foreign body is not replaced by something else when it is apprehended as a lived body; the same external thing is read again, in the language of interiority. The second birth adds the interiority "carrying with it" (*mit sich führend*) consciousness and an ego, and so the thing "completes itself" (*sich ergänzend*) into an animal or human being. The reflexive gives the completion to the body, as if it were the thing that does it, but the step is wholly on the side of my apprehension (*Auffassung*).

The verb stands out against the next paragraph, which sets the case of things beside it (Hua XIV 7). For mere bodies (*Körper*) "each is equal to every other": any thing could have been apperceived first, and every later one would follow "by analogical transfer" (*in analogischer Übertragung*). For lived bodies "it is otherwise". Transfer moves a sense from one instance to another of the same kind. Translation moves one instance from one kind of sense into another. Husserl does not yet say what the other's body is translated by. The next paragraph answers that the translation is "in principle mediate", since it attaches to the foreign body a co-presence of the same body "in the inner attitude", and so it presupposes that I have already apperceived my own (Row 3 of the list).

The last sentence draws the count. "So many bodies, so many separate human beings": the plurality of subjects follows the plurality of bodies, and the bodies are separate because each is "for each an exteriority" (*jeder für jeden Äusserlichkeit*). Separation comes from the first birth, not from the second. Here the inference is weaker than the wording makes it look. Bodies are separated in space, and that separation is a fact about external things; that the streams of experience they carry are separate in the same way is an addition the "so many … so many" asserts and does not argue. The closing "each related to the same world" names what holds the separate streams together, and the end of No. 1 will make that world prior to the division (Hua XIV 10, Row 5).

The chiasmus is completed in No. 3, which reverses the order for my own body as a physical thing: I first learn to experience it as such "only by the detour through the other" (*erst auf dem Umweg über den Andern*; Hua XIV 63–64, Row 13). The first birth of the foreign body is then the condition of the late, physical birth of mine. Appendix VI calls the attempt of No. 3 "imperfect and probably incorrect" (Hua XIV 75, Row 15), so the second half of the chiasmus is less secure than the first. The translation figure itself survives. A text of February 1927 on empathic appresentation as mediate says that the bodily comportment of the body there "must of course also be translated into a null-comportment" (*zu übersetzen in ein Nullgehaben*), the comportment of "that null-body which that outer body indicates" (Hua XIV 493). By 1927 Husserl names the language into which the foreign body is translated: the null-body of a here, the body as it is lived from within at the zero point of orientation. The second birth of 1921 becomes, six years later, the translation of a there into a here.

## No. 1, Hua XIV 7: the only fully original bodily apperception

Reads [No. 1.8](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#no-1-the-apperception-of-ones-own-body-as-the-original-bodily-apperception-and-the-mediacy-of-the-apperception-of-the-others-body-the-constitution-of-the-first-true-transcendence-through-empathy-summer-semester-1921), eighth paragraph under No. 1.

"Original" in this passage is a word with degrees. My own lived body is given in a way that is "fully original", and it is the only body so given. The other is given "in a certain originality" too: his physical body is perceived, and empathy reaches the only givenness a human being can have in principle. And I am given to myself in the most original way of all, but not as a human being. The passage does not oppose original and derived givenness. It ranks kinds of originality, and it puts the human being, myself included, on the second rank.

> But for the apperception of lived bodies it is otherwise. The original givenness of a lived body can only be the original givenness of *mine* and of no other body. The apperception "my body" is in its primal essence the first, and it is the only fully original one. Only when I have constituted my body can I apperceive every other body as such, and this apperception has in principle a mediate character; in that it assigns to the foreign body a co-presentification of the same body in the inner attitude, it requires for this that I have apperceived my body beforehand. Each, then, has a reference back to my body, as correspondingly every other ego and its inner life has a reference back to my own, as the only one given to me in the original in the most actual and final sense. A foreign one is indeed also given to me in a certain originality, namely insofar as I have the foreign bodily thing given originally and the empathizing co-presentification creates a peculiar apperception, through which precisely something like a human being comes to the only givenness to which it can in principle come. But by essential law I, who am not originally given to myself as a human being but as the I-am that has its body in a wonderful way of its own as the peculiarly functioning centre of its surrounding world, am, as soon as I have posited *others* as human beings, <led> to apprehend myself as a human being too, namely under the aspect that others must have of me. And here still more careful constitutive clarity is to be created.

German:

> Aber für die Leibapperzeption ist es anders. Die ursprüngliche Gegebenheit eines Leibes kann nur ursprüngliche Gegebenheit *meines* und keines andern Leibes sein. Die Apperzeption „mein Leib“ ist urwesentlich die erste und sie ist die einzige völlig originale. Erst wenn ich meinen Leib konstituiert habe, kann ich jeden andern Leib als solchen apperzizieren, und diese Apperzeption hat prinzipiell einen mittelbaren Charakter; indem sie dem fremden Leib zuordnet eine Mitgegenwärtigung desselben Leibes in Inneneinstellung, fordert sie dazu schon, dass ich meinen Leib vorher apperziziert habe. Jeder hat also Rückbeziehung auf meinen Leib, wie entsprechend jedes andere Ich und sein Innenleben Rückbeziehung hat auf mein eigenes als das einzige, das mir im wirklichsten und letzten Sinn im Original gegeben ist. Ein fremdes ist mir zwar auch in einer gewissen Originalität gegeben, nämlich sofern ich den fremden Leibkörper original gegeben habe und die einfühlende Mitgegenwärtigung eine eigentümliche Apperzeption schafft, wodurch eben so etwas wie Mensch zu der einzigen Gegebenheit kommt, zu der es prinzipiell kommen kann. Aber wesensgesetzlich bin ich, der ich mir nicht als Mensch original gegeben bin, sondern als das Ich-bin, das seinen Leib in wunderbarer eigener Weise hat als eigentümlich funktionierendes Zentrum seiner Umwelt, sowie ich *Andere* schon als Menschen gesetzt habe, <dazu geführt>, mich auch als Menschen aufzufassen, nämlich unter dem Aspekt, den Andere von mir haben müssen. Und hier ist noch sorgfältiger konstitutive Klarheit zu schaffen.

*Note on the text.* The verbs "apperzizieren" and "apperziziert" are the file's spelling for *apperzipieren* (apperceive) and are quoted as they stand. The angle brackets around "dazu geführt" (led to it) mark an editorial supply: the main verb of the last long sentence is Kern's, not Husserl's.

The two italic words, *mine* (*meines*) and *others* (*Andere*), mark the poles of the paragraph, and they are not symmetrical. *Meines* closes a sentence about lived bodies: original givenness of a body can only be of mine. *Andere* comes in a sentence about human beings: once others are posited as human beings, I take myself as one. The first sentence makes me the origin; the second makes the others the origin of something about me. The paragraph runs from the one to the other.

The vocabulary of originality is graded with care. "Original givenness" (*ursprüngliche Gegebenheit*) opens the series. The apperception "my body" is then "the only fully original" (*die einzige völlig originale*), and "fully" (*völlig*) already admits that originality can be less than full. My own inner life is given "in the original in the most actual and final sense" (*im wirklichsten und letzten Sinn im Original*), a superlative that needs a comparative below it. That comparative arrives with the concession, "indeed also … in a certain originality" (*zwar auch in einer gewissen Originalität*). The other's originality has two parts: his physical body is "originally" given, as any thing is, and the empathic co-presentification "creates" (*schafft*) an apperception through which a human being comes "to the only givenness to which it can in principle come". The clause turns what looked like a deficit into a norm. Mediacy is not a failure to give the other fully; it is the full givenness of what an other is. Kern notes in his introduction that Husserl came to distinguish "originality" (*Originalität*) from "originarity" (*Originarität*) (Hua XIV XXIII). This passage is an early stage of that distinction: the vocabulary is that of originality, of being given in the original, and the measure is set by what each kind of object allows.

"In its primal essence" (*urwesentlich*) is a coinage that fuses the prefix of origin with the vocabulary of essence. "My body" is the first apperception not as a matter of fact about my development but by essence, and the word blocks a psychological reading of "first". "Only when … can I" (*Erst wenn … kann ich*) states the founding order as a condition of possibility, and the reason given is that the foreign apperception assigns to the foreign body "a co-presentification of the same body in the inner attitude". The other's body is apperceived as a body that has its own inside. I can attach that sense to it only if I have the sense from my own case. The inference is valid only if the sense "lived body from within" has no source other than my own body, which the paragraph assumes and does not show.

The last sentence carries the reversal, and its syntax is unstable. Its subject, "I", is held apart from its verb by two relative clauses: I "who am not originally given to myself as a human being but as the I-am" (*das Ich-bin*), which has its body "in a wonderful way of its own" as a "functioning centre". The hyphenated "I-am" names the self-given ego before any apperception of it as a human being. The verb that would connect it to the result is missing in the manuscript and supplied by the editor. The claim, then, rests on "by essential law" (*wesensgesetzlich*) and "as soon as" (*sowie*), and the step itself is not stated: as soon as others count as human beings, I take myself as one "under the aspect that others must have of me". Husserl's last line admits the gap: "here still more careful constitutive clarity is to be created." My humanity is apperceived through the others' aspect, which is itself apperceived through my body. The paragraph thus builds a loop. It does not yet say whether the loop is a circle.

The text before has just stated the order of the two births (Hua XIV 6, Row 2) and set the case of things beside it, where any thing could be first and the rest follow by transfer. This paragraph explains why bodies are different: a body's sense as lived body is given only from within, and only one body is given to me from within. The text after draws the consequence for the world: empathy creates "the first true transcendence" (Hua XIV 8, Row 4). The paragraph is the bridge. Without the unique originality of my body there would be no mediacy, and without mediacy no transcendence.

The two halves of the paragraph go different ways in the volume. The first half becomes a norm: Appendix XIII calls my body in inner experience the "primal apperception" (*Urapperzeption*) that "gives the necessary norm", of which everything else is "variation" (Hua XIV 126, Row 22). The second half becomes a thesis of its own. A text of 17 January 1927 carries it in its title: "the apperception 'I-human-being' mediated by empathy" (*Die Apperzeption „Ich-Mensch“ durch die Einfühlung vermittelt*; No. 22, Hua XIV 410). And a few weeks later Husserl extends the grading of originality to the other. In ordinary life, when I say "I", I am given to my interlocutor "in the mode *alter*": "I too have the form *alter*, but in a secondary way, and not as a form that belongs to me in an original way", while "my counterpart is an other originally experienced by me — original, insofar as the intentional as such of my original empathizing itself has original character" (*original, sofern das Intentionale als solches meines originalen Einfühlens selbst originalen Charakter hat*; Appendix LXIII, Hua XIV 478). The "certain originality" of 1921 has become, by 1927, original experience of the other in its own right. What stayed fixed is the place of the human being: in both texts I am a human being, and an *alter*, only in the second rank.

## No. 1, Hua XIV 8–9: the first true transcendence

Reads [No. 1.11](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#no-1-the-apperception-of-ones-own-body-as-the-original-bodily-apperception-and-the-mediacy-of-the-apperception-of-the-others-body-the-constitution-of-the-first-true-transcendence-through-empathy-summer-semester-1921), eleventh paragraph under No. 1.

A transcendence is "true" when what it reaches is not a unity of sense formed in my stream, however far that unity outruns my lived experiences, but something my sense-giving only indicates. The things of the solipsistic world transcend my experiences, yet they are my sense-formations, and Husserl calls that world "immanent" in quotation marks. The other's stream is the first thing given to me that my stream does not form. "True" adds to transcendence the difference between forming and indicating, and the parenthesis "transcendence, then, in a peculiar sense" admits that this is a new concept, not a higher degree of the old one.

> Empathy creates the first true transcendence (transcendence, then, in a peculiar sense). For on an immanently instituted world of realities, and among them a body of the type "foreign lived body", there is now founded a reality constituted in a new way, which as its upper stratum <contains> a co-positing of a second lived body (in the specific constitution of a lived body, as I have my body in the inner view, that is, my primal body) with a second oriented world of appearances (world in the inner view) and appearance of the same world that gave itself to me solipsistically, and with second constitutive manifolds, a second ego and ego-contents in action and passion, etc.
>
> Here a second stream of consciousness is co-posited, not as a mere sense-formation of my stream but as only indicated by its sense-formation and conferring of right, and in the second a solipsistically constituted world, in the constitution indicated to me, which is identified, and according to the constituted sense, according to the sense of the indication itself, must be identified, with the world constituted "in me".
>
> Here consciousness for the first time really goes beyond itself, and what is given in going beyond is the foreign ego and ego-consciousness.

German:

> Die Einfühlung schafft die erste wahre Transzendenz (Transzendenz also in einem eigentümlichen Sinn). Denn auf eine immanent gestiftete Welt von Realitäten und darunter einen Körper vom Typus „fremder Leib“ gründet sich nun eine neuartig konstituierte Realität, die als obere Schicht eine Mitsetzung von einem zweiten Leib (in der spezifischen Leibkonstitution, wie ich meinen Leib in der Innenansicht, d.i. meinen Urleib habe) <enthält> mit einer zweiten orientierten Erscheinungswelt (Welt in der Innenansicht) und Erscheinung derselben Welt, die sich mir solipsistisch gab, und mit zweiten konstitutiven Mannigfaltigkeiten, einem zweiten Ich und Ichinhalten in Aktion und Passion usw.
>
> Hier ist ein zweiter Bewusstseinsstrom mitgesetzt, nicht als blosse Sinnbildung meines Stromes, sondern als durch seine Sinnbildung und Rechtgebung nur indiziert, und im zweiten eine solipsistisch konstituierte Welt, in der mir indizierten Konstitution, die identifiziert wird und nach dem konstituierten Sinn, nach dem Sinn der Indizierung selbst, identifiziert werden muss mit der „in mir“ konstituierten Welt.
>
> Hier überschreitet das Bewusstsein zum ersten Mal wirklich sich selbst, und das überschreitend Gegebene ist das fremde Ich und Ichbewusstsein.

*Note on the text.* The angle brackets around "enthält" (contains) mark an editorial supply; the first sentence lacks its verb in the manuscript. The page turns between the first and second paragraphs (Hua XIV 8–9).

The paragraph before prepares the word "true" by withholding it. The earlier "transcendences" (the file has the misspelling *Transzenzen*) yield intentional unities of manifolds that "fall wholly into 'my' stream of consciousness". The unities "were themselves no lived experiences", so they transcend the stream in the familiar sense, but they belonged to immanent manifolds and were "only units of sense with a rule of rational validity". Hence "this solipsistic world was after all only an 'immanent' world. It did not lead beyond me; it was mine as my sense-formation" (Hua XIV 8). The quotation marks around "immanent" mark the strain: a world of real things is called immanent, against the usage that reserves the word for lived experiences, because its whole being is a rule of my stream. The 1910/11 lectures had spoken of "transcendence in immanence" and of "the various concepts of transcendence" (Hua XIII 111). No. 1 adds one more concept and ranks it above the rest.

The new transcendence is defined in the second paragraph by a contrast of verbs. The second stream is co-posited "not as a mere sense-formation (*Sinnbildung*) of my stream but as only indicated (*nur indiziert*) by its sense-formation and conferring of right". The thing is formed by my stream; the other's stream is indicated by it. "Only" (*nur*) belongs to the indicating and limits what my stream contributes: it gives the sign and the right to posit, not the thing posited. The upper stratum is "co-posited" (*Mitsetzung*), and that keeps the act on my side. Husserl does not say the other's stream gives itself. He says my positing reaches something that my positing does not make, and that is the whole content of "true".

The wording then pulls back toward identity. What is indicated includes a world "which is identified, and … must be identified, with the world constituted 'in me'". The other's world is not a second world but a "second … appearance of the same world". The quotation marks around "in me" do the same work as those around "immanent": the solipsistic world is in me only as a sense-formation, and the identification shows that it was never only mine. The first true transcendence therefore transforms the "immanent" world it is founded on. The paragraph after this one draws the consequence: every regular constitution of "substances" in my sphere gives a rule for the constitution of identical substances in the empathized sphere, so that in the "normal" case, "when I put myself into the other, I find 'the same' as he finds when he puts himself into me" (Hua XIV 9). Through the transcendence of the other the things acquire the objectivity "for 'every' subject" that the text has already said is constituted through empathy (Hua XIV 8).

The third sentence repeats the thesis in the vocabulary of act: "for the first time really" (*zum ersten Mal wirklich*) consciousness "goes beyond itself". "Really" answers "true", and "for the first time" answers "first". The emphasis is on an event in the order of constitution, as "creates" (*schafft*) was. Transcendence is not discovered in the other; empathy produces it. The strong verb sits uneasily with "only indicated": what empathy creates is a positing that goes beyond, not the being of what it posits. The inference from "not formed by my stream" to "really beyond my consciousness" holds only if indication cannot itself be a mode of my forming, and that is the problem Husserl's own footnote at the end of No. 1 names (Hua XIV 10, Row 5).

The question returns in the lectures of 1926/27, now as a question. Appendix LIII (January/February 1927) is titled "How is genuine and true transcendence, the intersubjective one, possible?" (*Wie ist die echte und wahre Transzendenz, die intersubjektive, möglich?*). Its text calls it "a second transcendence: transcendence proper" (*die eigentliche Transzendenz*). The world of original experience, presumptively posited, does "not exceed" the own being of subjectivity; it only prescribes "a certain style of its life". Empathy "leads beyond this original sphere", to other egos and their original experiences, and "transcends the own ego and its immanence" (Hua XIV 442). The structure of 1921 is intact: an immanent world of things, a first true transcendence in the other. What has changed is the name of the first stage. In 1921 it is the "immanent" world of the solipsistic ego; in 1927 it is the "original sphere". And the thesis that empathy creates true transcendence has become the question of how it is possible, which is how Husserl's footnote of 1921 had already left it.

## No. 1, Hua XIV 10: one world before two constitutions

Reads [No. 1.17](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#no-1-the-apperception-of-ones-own-body-as-the-original-bodily-apperception-and-the-mediacy-of-the-apperception-of-the-others-body-the-constitution-of-the-first-true-transcendence-through-empathy-summer-semester-1921), seventeenth paragraph under No. 1.

The world's identity for me and for the other is not the result of matching two worlds constituted apart. It comes with empathy itself: because the manifold of appearances that the other's body indicates is "not separated" from mine, my unit of sense is "eo ipso" his. The division of one world into two constitutive achievements comes later, when I reflect on the other as constituting for himself. The order of founding is kept; the order of identification is reversed. Husserl's own footnote, set at the word that carries the claim, says that the reversal is where the problem lies.

> It is therefore not the case that I first constitute my things and my world solipsistically, then by empathy grasp the other ego, which is grasped as constituting solipsistically for itself, as its world, and that only then the one and the other constituted unity would be identified; rather, my unit of sense is, through the fact that the indicated foreign manifold is not separated from mine, *eo ipso* the same as his empathized one.[n. 65] Only when I consider the other as a constituting ego for himself do I find the formation of sense as constituted by him and ascribe it to him and his immanence.
>
> [n. 65:] But here is the problem.

German:

> Es ist also nicht so, dass ich erst solipsistisch meine Dinge und meine Welt konstituiere, dann einfühlend das andere Ich erfasse, das für sich solipsistisch als seine Welt konstituierend erfasst wird, und dass dann erst die eine und andere konstituierte Einheit identifiziert würde, sondern meine Sinneinheit ist dadurch, dass die indizierte fremde Mannigfaltigkeit nicht geschieden ist von meiner, *eo ipso* dieselbe wie seine eingefühlte.[n. 65] Erst wenn ich den Andern als konstituierendes Ich für sich betrachte, finde ich das Sinngebilde als von ihm konstituiert und rechne es ihm und seiner Immanenz zu.
>
> [n. 65:] Hier aber ist das Problem.

*Note on the text.* Note 65 carries no editor's mark, so it is Husserl's own; it is undated. Kern's note on the title of No. 1 records that Husserl wrote the text while reading the 1910/11 lectures in the summer semester of 1921 and that he "read it in 1927", so the note may belong to either reading. The paragraph before has a damaged sentence ("ganz sorgsam herausgestellt ganzen systematische Ordnung"), in which words are lost; only its opening, "This must still be newly considered", is used here.

The sentence denies a sequence of three steps, marked by "first … then … only then" (*erst … dann … dann erst*): solipsistic constitution of my world, empathic grasp of the other as constituting his, identification of the two. The denied picture is not a straw man. It is close to how No. 1 itself began, with "the ideally first constitution" in which my body and my material surroundings are "constituted in solipsistic fashion" (Hua XIV 8), and the "therefore" (*also*) presents the denial as a consequence of what has just been shown. What has just been shown is that I cannot have the other subject "before I have the other body", and that I have that body "only in one act" which grasps the foreign ego as living with this body, seeing so and surrounded so; "one will hardly be able to find stages here" (Hua XIV 10). The denial follows from that single act. It does not withdraw the founding stratum. Empathy is still founded on "an immanently instituted world" (Hua XIV 8). What the sentence withdraws is a separate act of identification. There are not two worlds waiting to be matched, because the second world is never given as separate from the first.

Everything hangs on "not separated" (*nicht geschieden*). The foreign manifold is "indicated" (*indiziert*), the word that in Hua XIV 9 defined the first true transcendence (Row 4). There the indicated stream was "not a mere sense-formation of my stream". Here the indicated manifold is "not separated from mine". The two negations pull in opposite directions. If the other's manifold were simply not separated from mine, it would be a part of my sense-formation, and the transcendence would collapse; if it were wholly separate, the identity would need the act of matching the sentence denies. *Eo ipso* (by that very fact) claims that identity follows from non-separation without further step. It follows only if the non-separation is of a kind that leaves the other's manifold his, and the sentence does not say what kind that is. Husserl's note stands exactly there: "But here is the problem." The adversative "but" (*aber*) sets the note against the confidence of *eo ipso*.

The last sentence assigns the division to reflection. "Only when" (*Erst wenn*) I consider the other "as a constituting ego for himself" do I "ascribe" (*rechne … zu*) the formation of sense to him and "his immanence". The verb is one of bookkeeping. Ascription to his immanence is a reflective act on a sense already shared, as the preceding paragraph said that the ego and its life are grasped only "by reflection, a reflection 'in' this empathizing presentification" (Hua XIV 10). The other's immanence is thus the latest thing in the order of my experience of him, though it is the first in the order of his being. The commentary calls the paragraph a correction "outright" of the construction. It is a correction of the order of identification, made while the founding order of No. 1 stays as it was, and that is why the problem remains.

The problem has a name and a solution six years later. In the preparation for the lecture of 22 February 1927 (No. 35), Husserl describes the perceptual association he calls "pairing" (*Paarung*) as "a coincidence at a distance" (*eine Deckung in Distanz*; Hua XIV 531). The phrase answers the two negations of 1921. Coincidence gives the non-separation: the foreign body and mine overlap in sense. Distance keeps them two: the overlap holds across a gap that it does not close. The same text then restates the difficulty in the words of the note: two external bodies can easily appear paired, but between my null-body and an external body any common orientation is excluded, "hence the problem of how phenomenal pairing is to become possible" (*Daher das Problem, wie phänomenale Paarung möglich werden soll*; Hua XIV 533). The note of 1921 marks the point that the doctrine of pairing was built to fill, and in 1927 it is still called "the problem".

## No. 2, Hua XIV 12: compossibility prescribed by a fact

Reads [No. 2.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#no-2-the-personal-ego-and-individual-character-the-problem-of-genesis-and-of-determinacy-in-genesis-how-can-a-monadic-subject-be-univocally-determined-and-knowable-st-mrgen-1920-or-1921), second paragraph under No. 2.

Compossibility, in No. 2's opening principle, is not a relation among possibilities that holds before anything exists. It is a range that an existing thing sets. That something is "prescribes" what can be there with it and what cannot. Husserl takes Leibniz's term and ties it to a fact, and the consequence runs through the volume: possibility is measured from actuality, so a second world or a second ego that could not be given together with this one is not a further possibility but no possibility at all.

> The fact that something is prescribes in each case a domain of compossibility, an all of what can be there with it or cannot be there with it.

German:

> Die Tatsache, dass etwas ist, schreibt jeweils ein Gebiet der Kompossibilität vor, ein All dessen, was mit dasein oder nicht mit dasein kann.

*Note on the text.* The sentence stands as a paragraph of its own at the head of the text, hard-wrapped over three lines in the file, as is the whole opening of No. 2. The commentary reports that it is "set off by spacing"; the file renders no spaced type, so that emphasis can be neither confirmed nor denied here.

The subject of the sentence is "the fact that something is" (*Die Tatsache, dass etwas ist*). It is not "the possibility of something" or "the essence of something". The verb is one of Husserl's horizon verbs: a fact "prescribes" (*schreibt vor*) a domain, as an experience prescribes (*schreibt vor*, *zeichnet vor*) the course of further experience. That makes compossibility a horizon of the actual. "In each case" (*jeweils*) attaches a domain to each fact, so there are as many domains as facts, and the question that follows is how they fit together.

The appositive defines the domain as "an all" (*ein All*), the totality, of what "can be there with it or cannot be there with it" (*was mit dasein oder nicht mit dasein kann*). The prefix *mit* (with) is split from the verb and repeated, so that the sentence turns on "being-there-with" (*Mit-dasein*) and its negation. Compossibility is not an abstract consistency of descriptions. It is the capacity to exist together. And the domain contains both sides: what can be there with the fact and what cannot. The fact excludes as much as it allows, and so it is a constraint before it is a field.

The next paragraphs show what the principle is for. Every individual being is a concrete individual or a dependent moment of one, and every concrete individual "lasts in time" and is what it is by passing continuously "from presence to presence" (Hua XIV 12). For a subject, "a concrete monad", the unity of being in becoming "means unity of development (genesis in the special sense)", and the content of a later stretch of time "is unthinkable without the earlier ones": an apperception that sets in at one place "is motivated at this one and at no other" and carries in itself "the inseparable stamp of a genesis" (Hua XIV 12). Here the principle is applied to the monad's own history. Each phase of a stream is a fact that prescribes what can follow and precede it. The monad is not a bundle of compossible states that could be recombined; its states are compossible only in the order in which they arose. Physical things are "otherwise": the pure visual thing "does not refer back in its content to an earlier one, to its history" (Hua XIV 12). The question of the text, how a monadic subject can be "univocally determined", is answered first by this principle, which makes the monad's determinacy a matter of genesis.

The principle returns at three levels. Within the monad, Appendix I (June 1921) holds that "not everything that is possible in essence for ego and lived experience of the ego is compossible in the individual unity of a monad" (Hua XIV 39). Between subjects, No. 4 (1 September 1921) concludes that worlds with separate groups of subjects may each be thinkable, "but *a priori* such worlds … are not compossible with one another", and draws the result: "There can be only one world, only one time, only one space with one nature and one multiplicity of animal beings" (Hua XIV 102, Rows 16–17). Appendix XIV (1 October 1921) gives the reason in a parenthesis. "Every other world may be compossible in itself, but it is not compossible with a second world (since the two worlds would have to be knowable as a duality)" (Hua XIV 135). Compossibility between worlds requires that they could be given together, and the fact of this world, which is given, prescribes that nothing outside its domain can be. And for the whole of monads, No. 13 (early 1922) sets as the task "to show that monads are compossible only as a monadic whole governed by laws of development and univocally determined according to them, a whole of communal development in which every phase is prescribed" (Hua XIV 272). The principle of the opening sentence, a fact prescribing its domain, has become the shape of a monadology: one history in which every phase is prescribed by the actual.

The *Cartesian Meditations* restate the principle in the other direction. § 36 speaks of "essential laws of compossibility" that are, "in the fact, rules of being-with-one-another-at-once-or-in-succession and being-able-to-be" (*im Faktum Regeln des Miteinander-zugleich-oder-folgend-zu-sein und -sein-zu-können*), and calls them laws of motivation (Hua I 110). The hyphenated chain spells out the "being-there-with" of 1921, and its "can" keeps the 1921 *kann*. But in 1931 the laws are essential, and the fact is where they appear as rules. In 1921 the fact comes first and prescribes. The difference is the one No. 2 is about: whether the monad's individuality can be derived from essential laws, or whether, as the opening principle implies, each fact is the origin of the range in which anything else can be.

## No. 2, Hua XIV 28–29: the ego becomes there for itself

Reads [No. 2 Elementary Account.5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#an-elementary-account-of-object-pole-and-ego-pole-the-ego-as-functional-centre), fifth paragraph under "An Elementary Account of Object-Pole and Ego-Pole; the Ego as Functional Centre".

The ego-pole's being there for itself is not a standing property of the pole. It is something the ego comes to by functioning: it becomes there for itself as everything else becomes there for it, by affecting and being made a theme. The manuscript said that the pole "is there for itself", and Husserl later changed it to "becomes there for itself too, by entering into function". The revision does not bring in a new doctrine from outside. It aligns the sentence with what the same text says a page later, where the ego is a "functional centre" that "can make itself thematic … and so is there for itself". The pole is the centre of functions before it is present to itself, and its self-presence is one of its functions, a later one.

> This identical ego-pole, the centre of affections and actions (also reactions), is there for itself,[n. 1] and for it, as everything ego-alien, so too every affection and action that has "occurred" is there, as something that can itself in turn affect this same ego.
>
> [n. 1:] "is there for itself" Husserl later changed to: "becomes there for itself too, by entering into function". — Editor's note.

German:

> Dieser identische Ichpol, das Zentrum der Affektionen und Aktionen (auch Reaktionen), ist für sich selbst da,[n. 1] und für ihn ist, wie alles Ichfremde, so auch alle „eingetretene“ Affektion und Aktion da, als etwas, das selbst wieder dieses selbe Ich affizieren kann.
>
> [n. 1:] „ist für sich selbst da“ hat Husserl später abgeändert in: „wird auch für sich selbst da dadurch, dass er in Funktion tritt“. — Anm. d. Hrsg.

*Note on the text.* The revision is undated ("later"). Two lines on, the file has spliced body text into Kern's note 2 on the same page: the note breaks off at "und das" and runs on with "flektives Bewusstsein ist erforderlich, um den Pol zu erfassen" (reflective consciousness is required to grasp the pole), which continues the body's "Denn ein zweites re-" (For a second re-). The end of note 2 is lost there. This reading uses only the note's opening, before the seam, and not the spliced sentence.

The manuscript sentence makes the pole the grammatical subject of a state, "is there" (*ist … da*), and then lists what is there for it: "everything ego-alien" and every affection and action that has "occurred". The two "there"s are symmetrical in form and not in sense. The ego-alien is there for the ego as what can affect it. The ego is there for itself, and the sentence does not say how. The revision supplies the how by changing the verb. "Becomes" (*wird*) makes self-presence an event, "by entering into function" (*dadurch, dass er in Funktion tritt*) gives its condition, and "too" (*auch*) puts the ego's own presence in the series of what is there for it. The ego becomes there for itself as the occurred affections and actions are there for it: as something that "can in turn affect this same ego". Self-presence is a case of affection.

The text a page on states this in its own words, and shows why the revision was needed. The ego living in its life is an affectively or actively "functioning" (*fungierendes*) ego, and as such "it lies before the polarly opposite function, namely to affect itself and to become a theme like anything else that can affect and become thematic, ontic". This second function is "in itself a second thing and necessarily subsequent" (*ein an sich Zweites und notwendig Nachkommendes*), though "subsequent" cannot mean an ordinary succession in time (Hua XIV 29). Husserl then distinguishes the "primally living pole in the primally living act", which, "being, and that means functioning, institutes a present for itself but does not itself signify a present", from the pole "that has become a counterpart". And he concludes: "The ego is *functional centre* (*Funktionszentrum*), which can at any time be functional centre of a function, which can make the same ego, 'itself', thematic as a centre so and so functioning or having functioned, and so is there for itself" (Hua XIV 29). Here "is there for itself" returns, at the end of a chain, as a result: the ego is there for itself because it can make itself thematic. The first sentence on p. 28 had put at the start what p. 29 puts at the end. The revision moves it.

Two equations carry the passage on p. 29: "being, and that means functioning" (*seiend, und das heisst, fungierend*), and the pole that "institutes a present for itself but does not itself signify a present". The first makes functioning the ego's mode of being. The second says that the functioning ego is not itself in the present it institutes, which is why its presence to itself must be the work of a second function. The parenthesis "(also reactions)" in the passage points the same way. The pole is a centre of what happens to it as well as of what it does.

A second revision on p. 29 extends the change to the definition. The sentence "The ego is functional centre, which can at any time be functional centre of a function" became: the ego is functional centre "which at any time, even where it is not active, goes out of itself and toward something, directs itself, can become active functional centre and in particular can be active functional centre of an active function" (Hua XIV 29, n. 2). The later Husserl makes the functioning wider than activity: the ego functions even where it is not active, as a going-out toward something. Kern's note 2 on p. 28 had recorded the same correction in small, changing "the ego directing itself" to "the ego directed" and adding "The ego is affected, and now it directs itself toward what affects it" (Hua XIV 28). Together the revisions turn the "functional centre" of the sub-heading from a centre of acts into a centre of affection and act, whose presence to itself is one more function among them. What No. 2 builds on this is the empty pole of the next paragraph: an identical ego that "is nothing for itself", "completely empty of content", whose only proper and universal feature is "to be functional centre at all" (Hua XIV 29). Without the revision, the empty pole would be a pole that is there for itself, which is a contradiction. With it, the ego is empty until it functions.

## No. 2, Hua XIV 32–33: character as principle of individuation, struck

Reads [No. 2 Elementary Account.16](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#an-elementary-account-of-object-pole-and-ego-pole-the-ego-as-functional-centre), sixteenth paragraph under "An Elementary Account of Object-Pole and Ego-Pole; the Ego as Functional Centre".

The manuscript claims that an ego's closed individual character (*Eigenart*), its univocal and necessary way of deciding under given affections, is what makes a monad unique, and that this is seen in re-imagining myself, not asserted as metaphysics. Husserl's marginal notes reject the inference twice, from two sides. Logically, the coincidence in which I compare my ego with re-imagined ones shows only that two characters cannot be one ego, not that two egos cannot have one character. Phenomenally, intuition does not exclude two egos with one character, since the poets present the double. What survives is a weaker thesis: there is a genus ego with lowest differences, the differences of personality.

> To re-imagine this character means to re-imagine the ego, and that two egos cannot be alike in character, in the univocal necessity of their action, is not a metaphysical assertion but an evidence that is given in the re-imagining of my ego described above: as soon as I re-imagine my character and then, as is also possible, construct a systematically closed character as a possible character, I necessarily have another ego, independently of the hyletic etc. contents. All possible monads and all possible egos I have in my re-imagining in individual coincidence, and there I see the closed character as principle of "individuation", as what makes the monad necessarily unique.[n. 2]
>
> [n. 2:] That is hasty and a fallacy! In individual coincidence my individual ego is incompatible with every other individual kind, as is my hyletic content with a re-imagined one; the two are independent. But what seems to emerge is only that there is a genus ego and lowest differences, differences of personality, and *a priori*. Every poet makes such differences intuitive for us.

German:

> Diese Eigenart sich umdenken, heisst, sich das Ich umdenken, und dass zwei Ich in der Eigenart, in der eindeutigen Notwendigkeit ihrer Aktion nicht gleich sein können, ist nicht eine metaphysische Behauptung, sondern eine Evidenz, die gegeben ist in der oben beschriebenen Umfiktion meines Ich: Sowie ich meine Eigenart umdenke und dann, wie es auch möglich, eine systematisch geschlossene Eigenart als mögliche Eigenart konstruiere, habe ich notwendig ein anderes Ich, unabhängig von den hyletischen etc. Beständen. Alle möglichen Monaden und alle möglichen Ich habe ich in meiner Umfiktion in individueller Deckung, und da erschaue ich die geschlossene Eigenart als Prinzip der „Individuation“, als das, was die Monade zum notwendig Einmaligen macht.[n. 2]
>
> [n. 2:] Das ist eilfertig und ein Trugschluss! In individueller Deckung ist mein individuelles Ich unverträglich mit jeder anderen individuellen Artung, wie auch mein Hyletisches mit einem umfingierten; beides ist unabhängig. Aber was hervorzugehen scheint, ist nur, dass es eine Gattung Ich gibt und niederste Differenzen, Differenzen der Personalität, und *a priori*. Jeder Dichter macht uns solche Differenzen anschaulich.

*Note on the text.* Neither note carries an editor's mark, so both are Husserl's; neither is dated. The note quoted above and the one discussed below (n. 73 in the file's numbering, Hua XIV 33) are notes of No. 2.

The manuscript sentence makes three equations in a row. To re-imagine (*umdenken*) the character "means" (*heisst*) to re-imagine the ego; the impossibility of two alike egos "is not a metaphysical assertion but an evidence"; and in re-imagining "I see" (*erschaue*) the closed character as principle of individuation. The verb of seeing carries the claim of evidence. The sentence before has defined character as "a univocal, necessitating position-taking, a univocal necessary manner … of deciding so and so" under given affections (Hua XIV 32). "Univocal" (*eindeutig*) answers the question of No. 2's title, how a monadic subject can be "univocally determined". If the ego's decisions follow necessarily from its character and its affections, the ego is determined, and its character individuates it.

"Individuation" stands in quotation marks: Husserl takes the scholastic term and marks it as borrowed. And the decisive step sits in the phrase "in individual coincidence" (*in individueller Deckung*). In re-imagining I lay every possible ego over mine, and where the character differs the coincidence breaks: I "necessarily have another ego". That is all the procedure can show. It shows that a change of character yields a different ego, that is, that character belongs to identity. It does not show that a second, actual ego could not have my character, since such an ego is never laid over mine in re-imagining: re-imagining varies me. The manuscript passes from "another character, therefore another ego" to "another ego, therefore another character", and the note names the move "hasty" (*eilfertig*). "Fallacy" (*Trugschluss*) is the logician's word.

The note then says what does follow. In individual coincidence my individual ego "is incompatible with every other individual kind (*Artung*)", as my hyletic content is with a re-imagined one, and the two incompatibilities are "independent". Character does not reduce to hyle, nor hyle to character; this part of the manuscript stands. But it yields only "a genus ego and lowest differences, differences of personality". The vocabulary is that of species, not of individuals. A lowest difference is the last specification of a genus, and nothing in the concept stops two individuals from falling under the same one. The note turns a principle of individuation into a typology. "And *a priori*" keeps the claim of evidence for the typology, and "every poet makes such differences intuitive" names its source. The evidence is real, but it is evidence of kinds of person.

The second note attacks the same step from intuition. The manuscript goes on: one could say that a second monad cannot contain the same ego individually, "because the second monad would then have the same functional character; two monads with the same functional character are however unthinkable". The note answers: "No. If there cannot be two human beings with the same personality, then this must have quite other grounds. It cannot be a necessity drawn from direct intuition, since otherwise the poets would not treat the 'problem' of the double. But the poets make it worse. The double is indeed experienced as the same ego!" (*Der Doppelgänger wird ja erfahren als dasselbe Ich!*; Hua XIV 33). The poets appear in both notes, and they do opposite work. In the first they make intuitive the differences of personality that the typology needs. In the second they make intuitive what the manuscript calls unthinkable, one ego in two. If the double can be presented, the impossibility of two alike egos is no evidence from intuition. "They make it worse" (*machen es schlimmer*): the double is not merely two people alike in character, as the manuscript's own example of siblings "who felt like doubles" suggested (Hua XIV 33), but the same ego experienced twice.

So the passage's thesis is restricted by its own apparatus to what the coincidence shows: character belongs to the identity of an ego, and the space of possible characters is a genus with lowest differences. The uniqueness of the monad, what makes it "necessarily unique", is left without a principle. Appendix I (June 1921) keeps it open in so many words: a theory of the stages of monads still needs "a special consideration of the individuation of a monad, only it is questionable whether that would have to be a comprehensive doctrine. In any case its problem must be kept in view" (Hua XIV 38). The first note of the same page had already marked how little the monads share on the struck view: "Two monads have specific community only in that each has a functional centre, which as such is a common form" (Hua XIV 32, n. 1). Together the notes leave a monad that is individuated by its whole genesis, as No. 2's opening principle implied (Row 6), rather than by a character it could share with another.

## Appendix I, Hua XIV 38: static, typological, individual

Reads [App. I.10](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#appendix-i-phenomenology-of-monadic-individuality-and-phenomenology-of-the-general-possibilities-and-compatibilities-of-experiences-static-and-genetic-phenomenology-june-1921), tenth paragraph under Appendix I.

The division the passage draws is not, at bottom, between static and genetic phenomenology. It is between the general and the individual. Static analysis excludes genesis; a second kind of research treats genesis, but only as a typology of essential possibilities; and only the third, the phenomenology of monadic individuality, treats a genesis "in context", as the becoming of one monad. Genesis therefore appears on both sides of the line that matters. The appendix's title names that line: "phenomenology of monadic individuality and phenomenology of the general possibilities and compatibilities of experiences".

> Static is what I may well call phenomenological investigations that pursue the correlations between constituting consciousness and constituted objectivity and exclude genetic problems altogether. From these I have to distinguish phenomenological investigations that consider the typology of various forms of experiencing and of genesis that present themselves, according to their essential possibilities, compatibilities, etc., but without considering the problems of individuals in their connection. Finally we have the phenomenology of monadic individuality, which includes the phenomenology of a connected genesis in which the unity of the monad grows, in which the monad is by becoming.

German:

> <Als> statisch kann ich wohl phänomenologische Forschungen bezeichnen, die den Korrelationen zwischen konstituierendem Bewusstsein und konstituierter Gegenständlichkeit nachgehen und genetische Probleme überhaupt ausschliessen. Davon habe ich zu unterscheiden phänomenologische Forschungen, die die Typik verschiedener sich darbietender Gestalten des Erlebens und der Genese betrachten nach ihren Wesensmöglichkeiten, Verträglichkeiten usw., ohne aber die Individualprobleme im Zusammenhang zu betrachten. Endlich haben wir die Phänomenologie der monadischen Individualität, darin beschlossen die Phänomenologie einer zusammenhängenden Genese, in der Einheit der Monade erwächst, in der die Monade ist, indem sie wird.

*Note on the text.* "<Als>" (as) is an editorial supply at the head of the first sentence.

The first sentence is a definition offered with reserve. "I may well call" (*kann ich wohl … bezeichnen*) proposes a name. It does not report a settled usage. Static research is defined twice, positively by its object, the correlation of constituting consciousness and constituted objectivity, and negatively by an exclusion: it shuts out "genetic problems altogether" (*überhaupt*). The negative definition does the work. Static analysis is not a different kind of looking but genetic phenomenology with one dimension removed.

The second sentence does not name its research, and that is why the middle term is easy to miss. It considers "the typology (*Typik*) of various forms of experiencing and of genesis", so genesis is already its theme, and it considers them "according to their essential possibilities, compatibilities (*Verträglichkeiten*), etc." The research is eidetic. What it lacks is stated in the qualifying clause: it proceeds "without considering the problems of individuals in their connection" (*ohne aber die Individualprobleme im Zusammenhang zu betrachten*). "In their connection" is the hinge. A typology of geneses can describe how a kind of apperception typically arises, but not how this apperception arose at this place in this stream. The vocabulary of compatibility ties the middle term to No. 2, where the opening principle spoke of compossibility (Hua XIV 12, Row 6), and to the appendix's own sentence that not everything possible in essence is "compossible in the individual unity of a monad" (Hua XIV 39). Essential compatibility and individual compossibility are different questions, and the second needs the third kind of research.

The third sentence uses a formula without a precedent in the paragraph: the monad "is by becoming" (*ist, indem sie wird*). The conjunction *indem* says that becoming is the mode of the monad's being, not a process it undergoes while it is something else. The unity of the monad "grows" (*erwächst*) in a "connected genesis". This is the phenomenology that No. 2 had needed and failed to secure with character (Row 8): individuality is the unity of one history, and only a phenomenology of that history can treat it. The appendix says as much a page earlier: individual geneses and laws of genesis can be described "without systematically attacking the problem of the universal genesis of a monad and the essence of its individuality" (Hua XIV 38).

The next paragraph shows what the division does to Husserl's earlier plan. "A systematic phenomenology as I conceived it" (*wie ich sie mir dachte*, a past tense) follows the stages of possible constitutions from immanent time to nature, animals and thought, and "these are therefore genetic considerations", with descriptions of finished formations "placed into the context of genetic research" (Hua XIV 38). Static description is what is left when the genetic context is bracketed; one "can also describe these correlations for themselves". And three pages later Husserl asks: "I always speak there of static and genetic phenomenology. What was the properly guiding viewpoint?" (*Ich spreche da immer von statischer und genetischer Phänomenologie. Was war da der eigentlich leitende Gesichtspunkt?*; Hua XIV 41). The appendix is not the first use of the pair. It is a reflection on a pair already in use, and the commentary's description of it as "among the first explicit statements of the static–genetic distinction" claims more than the text. What is new here is the three-part form, which shows that the pair was not the guiding viewpoint after all.

Kern notes that Husserl pursued the problem of genesis in 1921 in other texts of the same convolute, one of them printed in Hua XI (336 ff.) (Hua XIV XXII, n. 21). That text, "Static and Genetic Phenomenological Method", makes the same moves in other words. It records that "in the lectures I did not say 'descriptive' but 'static' phenomenology" (Hua XI 341). It says that the universal genesis of a monad contains the histories of the constitution of its objects, and that "in the universal eidetic phenomenology of genesis this same is achieved for all conceivable objects, related to conceivable monads" (*in der universellen eidetischen Phänomenologie der Genesis*; Hua XI 345). And a note lists three phenomenologies: "1) universal phenomenology of the general structures of consciousness, 2) constitutive phenomenology, 3) phenomenology of genesis" (Hua XI 345). The two triads do not match term for term. In Hua XI the third term is genesis; in Appendix I the third term is individuality, and genesis appears already in the second. Appendix I goes further: it holds that genesis is the subject of both an eidetic typology and a phenomenology of the individual, and it puts the boundary of eidetic method between the two.

## Appendix III, Hua XIV 48–50: the self against extended consciousness

Reads [App. III.2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#appendix-iii-the-self-as-a-non-founded-unity-june-1921), second paragraph under Appendix III.

Husserl takes two things from the definition of the person he quotes, and he rejects a third. He takes the word "self" in its reflexive sense, the being that is for itself what it is, and he takes the self's identity across times and places. He rejects the measure the quotation gives that identity: that it "reaches as far as" consciousness extends over past actions. The appendix argues that the self's identity is not built up by the reach of consciousness at all. It comes "before" every content, and it is found even across intuitions that have no connection with one another. Husserl took the definition to be Leibniz's. Kern's note says the words are Locke's, as Philalethes speaks them, and the doctrine the appendix corrects is the one those words state.

> Instead of "I" I should perhaps better always say "self". "The word person means a thinking and intelligent being, capable of reason and reflection, which is able to represent itself as one and the same subject that thinks at different times and places, and which does everything with the consciousness that it itself makes up the ground of its actions. This consciousness always accompanies our present sensations and representations when they are distinct enough, and precisely thereby each is for himself what one calls, in the reflexive sense, a self (*soi-même*). As far as consciousness extends over the actions and thoughts of the past, just so far too reaches the identity of the person, and the self is in this moment the same as then."[n. 83]
>
> [n. 83:] Leibniz, *Nouveaux Essais* II, 27, <§ 9>; translation after Kuno Fischer, <*G. W. Leibniz* …>, p. 469. — <Editor's note: The quotation gives words of Philalethes (Locke).>

German:

> Statt „Ich“ müsste ich vielleicht besser immer sagen „Selbst“. „Das Wort Person bedeutet ein denkendes und intelligentes Wesen, fähig zur Vernunft und Reflexion, das sich selbst als ein und dasselbe Subjekt vorzustellen vermag, welches in verschiedenen Zeiten und Orten denkt und alles mit dem Bewußtsein tut, dass es selbst den Grund seiner Handlungen ausmacht. Dieses Bewußtsein begleitet immer unsere gegenwärtigen Empfindungen und Vorstellungen, wenn sie deutlich genug sind, und eben dadurch ist jeder für sich, was man im reflexiven Sinn ein Selbst nennt (*soi-même*). So weit sich das Bewußtsein über die Handlungen und Gedanken der Vergangenheit erstreckt, ebenso weit reicht auch die Identität der Person, und das Selbst ist in diesem Augenblick dasselbe als damals“.[n. 83]
>
> [n. 83:] Leibniz, *Nouveaux Essais* II, 27, <§ 9>; Übersetzung nach Kuno Fischer, <*G. W. Leibniz* (Geschichte der neuern Philosophie, zweiter Band) 3. Auflage, Heidelberg, Winter, 1889>, S. 469. — <Anm. d. Hrsg.: Das Zitat gibt Worte des Philalethes (Locke) wieder.>

*Note on the text.* The appendix opens on Hua XIV 48, and the file's next running head (49) stands just before this paragraph, so the quotation is cited as 48–49. In note 83 the angle brackets mark Kern's supplies. What stands outside them, "Leibniz, *Nouveaux Essais* II, 27", is by the note's form the manuscript's own reference, so the attribution to Leibniz is Husserl's and the correction to Locke is Kern's. The rest of the appendix has OCR errors ("retionalen" for *retentionalen*, retentional, and "Reiales" for *Reales*, a real thing), which do not affect the sentences used here.

The appendix's first sentence is Husserl's, and it says what he wants from the quotation: a word. "Instead of 'I' I should perhaps better always say 'self'." The quotation then supplies the word's sense, by the reflexive formula "each is for himself what one calls, in the reflexive sense, a self", with the French *soi-même* kept in parentheses by Fischer's German. The ego is a self because it is for itself, and the quotation's self is the subject that "represents itself as one and the same subject" across times and places. All of this Husserl keeps. The appendix's title, "the self as a non-founded unity", names what he does with it.

The last sentence of the quotation states the criterion, and its syntax is a proportion: "As far as (*So weit*) consciousness extends …, just so far (*ebenso weit*) too reaches the identity of the person." Identity has the extent of consciousness's reach into the past. Husserl does not answer the proportion at once. He first tests an analogy of his own, introduced as a mere attempt, "One might try to say": as I find the same thing in the continuous succession of its states, or the same concrete red in a continuous succession of red-moments, so I find the self in reflection as a unity of its states of consciousness in the flowing succession of conscious life (Hua XIV 49). The analogy would fit the quoted criterion. A self that is the unity of a continuous succession reaches as far as the succession is held together, and memory is what holds it together over the past. "However" (*Indessen*), the unity of the stream lies "in" the flowing experiences, and "the unity of the self does not lie in them in the same sense". The self "is not in turn a 'phenomenon'" (Hua XIV 49).

The correction comes a page later, in two steps. First the order of dependence is reversed: in the concrete self the identity of the ego is "unity-creating identity for all contents" and "comes before" them, so that "the unity of the self and even of the person is not a mere identity in change" (Hua XIV 50). "Even of the person" (*und sogar der Person*) reaches back to the word of the quotation. Second, the thing's unity is "one-sidedly founded": it is found only in the continuous passage from difference to difference, and what lies apart in time discontinuously "is not one but several". The ego's unity is not founded: "the one is from the outset one and only then enters into relation with what is different". The proof is a comparison set in quotation marks. Of two unconnected intuitions of red I can assert likeness and similarity, "but never identity. But if I 'compare' myself in childhood and manhood, on the basis of disconnected single intuitions, there I find myself, and absolutely numerically, as the same" (Hua XIV 50).

This last sentence answers the quoted criterion point by point, without naming it. The criterion made identity reach as far as consciousness extends over the past, which requires a connection running from now to then. Husserl's case removes the connection: the intuitions of childhood and of manhood are "disconnected" (*zusammenhangslos*). Identity is found all the same, and it is found as numerical identity, not as likeness. The quotation marks around "compare" mark the irony: what I do with two pictures of myself has the form of comparison, but it yields what no comparison yields. The extension of consciousness is not the measure of identity. If anything, the self's identity is the condition under which disconnected memories can be memories of one self.

So the question of whose doctrine Husserl corrects has a precise answer. He corrects the proportion of the quotation's last sentence, which the *New Essays* give to Philalethes, Locke's spokesman, and he keeps the reflexive self of the sentences before it. He does not correct Leibniz, whom he believed he was quoting, and a note in the same appendix takes Leibniz's side on another point: "Leibniz is right that self-consciousness and delimited lived experience necessarily go together" (Hua XIV 49, n. 2). The misattribution does not change the argument, but it changes its target: the appendix argues against the criterion of the man Leibniz's dialogue answers, under Leibniz's name.

## Appendix IV, Hua XIV 51: the objective as Gegenwurf

Reads [App. IV.5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-14-zur-phanomenologie-der-intersubjektivitat-zweiter-teil-19211928-commentary.md#appendix-iv-the-monad-in-its-general-structure-june-1921), fifth paragraph under Appendix IV.

*Gegenwurf* (literally "counter-throw", what is thrown over against) is Husserl's word in this appendix for what stands opposite the ego and everything subjective. It names objectivity as a direction of opposition, not as a kind of thing: the objective is what is thrown over against the subject and yet is conscious within the subject's consciousness. A marginal note then narrows the word. It "fits better" the external "substances" in whose constitution empathy has played a part. On the note's reading, the full sense of opposition belongs to what is opposite to more than one subject, and the word has moved from the general correlate of consciousness to the intersubjective thing.

> Everything we call consciousness is, as the life-medium of the ego, egoic, subjective in a special sense; in another sense, of course, the doing and suffering (being-affected). The objective is *Gegenwurf*[n. 86] of the ego and of everything subjective, and yet in the subjective of consciousness something of which there is consciousness, and in this manner of the intentional a "correlate" and in conscious "relation" with the ego.
>
> [n. 86:] "*Gegenwurf*", however, fits better external "substances", in which empathy has played <a> constitutive role.

German:

> Alles, was wir Bewusstsein nennen, ist als Lebensmedium des Ich in besonderem Sinn ichlich, subjektiv, in anderem Sinn natürlich das Tun und Erleiden (Affiziert-sein). Das Objektive ist Gegenwurf[n. 86] des Ich und alles Subjektiven, und doch im Subjektiven des Bewusstseins Bewusstes und in dieser Weise des Intentionalen „Korrelat“ und mit dem Ich bewusstseinsmässig in „Beziehung“.
>
> [n. 86:] „Gegenwurf“ passt aber besser auf äussere „Substanzen“, in denen Einfühlung <eine> konstitutive Rolle gespielt hat.

*Note on the text.* Note 86 has no editor's mark and is Husserl's; it is undated. "<eine>" is an editorial supply.

The paragraph ranks the subjective before it names the objective. Consciousness is subjective "in a special sense" as the ego's "life-medium" (*Lebensmedium*). The ego's "doing and suffering" is subjective "in another sense". The objective is then defined against "the ego and everything subjective", so against all these grades at once. *Gegenwurf* carries the definition in its morphology. The prefix "counter" (*Gegen-*) gives the opposition, and the noun from "throw" (*werfen*) gives it as the result of a movement. It is a German rendering of the Latin *objectum*, built on the same image as the Latin (*ob-iacere*, to throw against), where the ordinary German *Gegenstand* (object, literally "what stands against") has lost the movement and kept only the position. The appendix uses *Gegenstand* freely elsewhere, for the "objects" of the ego's acts and affections (Hua XIV 51). Here it chooses the rarer word for the objective as such.

The sentence then takes back what the word seems to give. "And yet" (*und doch*) the objective is "in the subjective of consciousness something of which there is consciousness" (*Bewusstes*). It is thrown over against consciousness, and it is found within consciousness. The two scare-quoted words, "correlate" and "relation", mark the strain: the objective is a correlate only "in this manner of the intentional", and its relation to the ego is a relation "in conscious fashion" (*bewusstseinsmässig*). The *Gegenwurf* is not outside the subject. It is the subject's own counter-pole. The paragraph before has just said that the subject-pole "has over against it manifold object-poles" and that each object "is conscious but not consciousness, conscious straightforwardly and not by 'reflection'" (Hua XIV 51). The counter-throw is the direction of that straightforward consciousness, away from the ego.

The note asks what the word fits best, and it answers with a restriction and a reason. It fits external "substances", in quotation marks, and it fits them because empathy "has played a constitutive role" in them. The restriction excludes the immanent objects that the paragraph's general definition included. In Appendix II, another of Husserl's notes had applied the word to both: "we have to distinguish immanent objectivity and transcendent. Object = *Gegenwurf* = not intentional lived experience" (Hua XIV 44). The equation of Appendix II defines the word by what the object is not, an intentional lived experience, and so covers sensation-objects as well as things. The note in Appendix IV keeps the word for the transcendent thing whose objectivity is constituted with others. The reason is the thesis of No. 1. The solipsistic world was "only an 'immanent' world", and the objective "for 'every' subject" is constituted through empathy (Hua XIV 8, Row 4). What is thrown over against me in the full sense is what is also thrown over against the other, the same thing as pole for both of us. The note does not say this, but its "because" (empathy's constitutive role) makes no sense without it.

The word had another referent in Husserl's earlier use. In *Philosophy as a Rigorous Science* (1911) he asked: "If the immanent psychical is in itself not nature but the *Gegenwurf* of nature, what do we investigate in it as its 'being'?" (*Ist nun das immanente Psychische in sich selbst nicht Natur, sondern der Gegenwurf von Natur*; Hua XXV 31). There the counter-throw is the psychical, set against nature; its opposition is to the physical thing. In 1921 the counter-throw is the objective, set against the ego; and in the note it is, of all objects, the external thing. The word has turned around. In 1911 it marked the psychical as what nature is not. In 1921 it marks the thing as what the subject is not, and the note adds that the thing is most fully what the subject is not when other subjects have helped constitute it. The movement of the word follows the movement of the volume, which finds the transcendence of things only on the far side of the transcendence of the other.

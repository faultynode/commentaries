# Henry, *The Essence of Manifestation*, on Heidegger, *Being and Time*: a comparative commentary

## Introduction. The problem of the being of the ego and the fundamental presuppositions of ontology

Henry (R) cites *Sein und Zeit* (SZ) in the Niemeyer printing of 1941, whose pagination the later printings keep; R is cited by division, paragraph and note, since the edition has no page numbers. Four traps govern the reading of this pair. Henry takes SZ as a finished doctrine, though SZ announces itself as provisional and unfinished (SZ 17, 436–437). Henry's "essence", "horizon" and "milieu of visibility" are his own terms for being as manifestation and must not be written into SZ, for which the horizon of the understanding of being is time (SZ 1, 17) and the opening belongs to Dasein (SZ 133, 220–221). Henry's Husserlian vocabulary of evidence and intuition is not SZ's, which derives both from understanding (SZ 147). Much of what Commentary R reports as SZ in this division is Heidegger's 1929 writing (*Vom Wesen des Grundes*) or a doctrine of Henry's own.

### Prelude

The epigraph quotes SZ 24 word for word: Descartes leaves undetermined "die Seinsart der res cogitans, genauer den Seinssinn des »sum«" (the mode of being of the res cogitans, more exactly the meaning of the being of the "sum"; R Prelude, epigraph, n. 1 / SZ 24). The eleventh-edition text reads "der Seinssinn"; Henry's accusative is a trivial variant that changes nothing. The departure lies in the frame. In SZ the sentence is the second station of the destruction, entered through Kant, who "takes over Descartes's ontological position" and with it the omission of an ontology of Dasein; the sentences that follow promise to show why Descartes thought the "absolute Gewißsein" (certainty) of the cogito released him from the question. Henry keeps the diagnosis and cuts it loose from the history of presence in which SZ sets it (SZ 25–26), so that it reads as a verdict on the cogito's starting point. For SZ the omission belongs to a tradition to be loosened historically. For Henry it becomes the thesis that the being of the ego is not homogeneous with being in general (R §7 ¶1). The epigraph states that the book starts from SZ and already holds the point at which it will leave it.

Henry glosses the epigraph: the theme is the meaning of the being of the ego, what we mean by "je, moi". The appeal to what "se donne le plus souvent comme allant de soi" (gives itself most often as self-evident) and what "tout le monde sait ou comprend" (everyone knows or understands) reconstructs SZ 4, where being is the "selbstverständliche Begriff" (self-evident concept), everyone understands "ich bin froh" (I am glad), and this average intelligibility "demonstriert nur die Unverständlichkeit" (demonstrates only unintelligibility). The re-inscription lies in the object. In SZ the self-evident is being, and the ego enters as one being whose being goes unasked (SZ 46). Henry makes the ego the self-evident par excellence ("l'ego n'appartient-il pas d'une façon éminente ?", does the ego not belong to it in an eminent way), which SZ would class as an existentiell familiarity (SZ 12–13), not a self-evidence of being. The treatment of psychology follows SZ 45–46 (§10) closely: its results are not denied but rest on an unclarified ground, and titles such as "Subjekt", "Seele" and "Bewußtsein" carry the *subjectum* in ontologically even when one resists "Verdinglichung" (reification) ontically (SZ 46). Henry's formulation of what psychology never asked, "la condition de possibilité de tout objet comme tel" (the condition of possibility of any object as such), is Kantian and Husserlian. SZ's question is the mode of being of the being under study, object or not.

Henry demands that every question first become "transparente à elle-même" (transparent to itself) and say whether it is "originaire et fondamentale" or dependent on a prior inquiry; only an "absolute" problematic, drawing knowledge from itself and having already clarified what makes knowledge possible, is independent. The word is SZ 5's *Durchsichtigkeit* (transparency) of the explicit question; the criterion is Husserl's ideal of a presuppositionless first philosophy, which Henry says philosophy "a comprise depuis longtemps" (has long understood). This is a re-inscription with a contestation latent in it. In SZ transparency means clearing the constitutive moments of the question (what is asked about, what is interrogated, what is to be found out) and the being of the questioner (SZ 5, 7), and SZ says that the "presupposing" of being in posing the question is no premise but "vorgängige Hinblicknahme" (prior looking-toward) that grows out of average understanding (SZ 8). A problematic that "ne tire ses connaissances que d'elle-même" (draws its knowledge only from itself) is what SZ declines to promise: its question is bound back to the questioner by a "Rück- oder Vorbezogenheit" (relatedness back or forward, SZ 8). Henry's ideal of independence is not SZ's, and it prepares §6, where the dependence of ontology on Dasein is counted against the project.

Henry asks whether the problem of the ego's being belongs to first philosophy, and answers that every question about the ego presupposes an answer, or at least a search, for the meaning of being in general, since "je suis content" or "je suis" is possible only because "déjà l'être luit" (being already shines). The Cartesian beginning is therefore not "radical". This reconstructs SZ and re-inscribes it. SZ 13 says what Henry says: the carrying out of the analytic of Dasein "hängt an der vorgängigen Ausarbeitung der Frage nach dem Sinn von Sein überhaupt" (hangs on the prior working-out of the question of the meaning of being in general). The example ("ich bin froh") and the point that "was ist Sein?" already moves in an understanding of the "ist" come from SZ 4–5, and the scare quotes on "radical" are SZ 24's. The re-inscription is "luit". What SZ finds in the "ist" is a *Verständnis* (understanding), a vague average understanding that is "ein Faktum" (a fact, SZ 5) and that Dasein is. The language of light is SZ's *Lichtung* (clearing), which SZ assigns to Dasein: it is "erleuchtet" (illuminated) not by another being but because it "selbst die Lichtung ist" (itself is the clearing, SZ 133; likewise SZ 147). Henry places the light in being, ahead of any understanding. Section I depends on that relocation, and it is not exegesis.

Henry defines ontology as the science of "l'être en tant qu'être" (being as being), "nécessairement universelle" (necessarily universal), and writes: "La philosophie première est l'ontologie universelle" (first philosophy is universal ontology). The take-up is selective reconstruction. SZ 38 says "Philosophie ist universale phänomenologische Ontologie, ausgehend von der Hermeneutik des Daseins" (philosophy is universal phenomenological ontology, proceeding from the hermeneutic of Dasein), and that being is no genus: "Seine »Universalität« ist höher zu suchen" (its "universality" is to be sought higher, SZ 38; cf. SZ 3–4 on the prejudice that being is the most general concept). Henry's "Son objet n'est point telle chose ni tel genre" (its object is neither this thing nor this kind) agrees. What he leaves out is SZ's qualifying clause, that the universal ontology starts from and returns to the hermeneutic of Dasein, "dort festgemacht, woraus es entspringt und wohin es zurückschlägt" (made fast where it springs from and strikes back to, SZ 38). Here the subordination of the ego to universal ontology and the dependence of ontology on Dasein look like two theses; in SZ they are one circle (SZ 7–8, 38).

### § 1. The idea of an apodictic evidence as privileged pathway of access to the being of the ego

Henry explains why Descartes thought he could dispense with the ontological context: it seemed a source of confusion, to be shed with all other prejudices as the condition of a free beginning, a return "aux choses mêmes" (to the things themselves), which Henry calls "un enseignement cartésien" (a Cartesian teaching). SZ 24 promises exactly this explanation, and it gives a different one: Descartes was led to believe that the "absolute Gewißsein" of the cogito freed him from the question. The phrase "to the things themselves" is SZ's statement of the phenomenological maxim (SZ 27–28), where SZ calls it a "Selbstverständlichkeit" (a self-evident matter) in need of clarification. Henry therefore reads Descartes through Husserl's Cartesianism and replaces certainty with a method of beginning. What SZ's irony needs is silent: the Descartes who throws off every tradition keeps the one ontological presupposition he never doubts, the *ens creatum* (SZ 24–25). Henry reaches it only in §7 ¶2.

Henry says that the central place of the ego cogito carries presuppositions, since philosophy obeys the idea of a truth to be reached, and that "L'évidence est le τέλος de toute vie intentionnelle" (evidence is the telos of all intentional life), the frame in which the ego arises as a theme. This is Husserl (the Cartesian Meditations, *Ideen*), and it makes no claim about SZ; its relation to SZ is that it sets up what SZ puts in question. SZ will not grant the telos of evidence its authority: the "Evidenz" of attunement is not to be lowered by measuring it against "apodiktische Gewißheit eines theoretischen Erkennens von purem Vorhandenen" (apodictic certainty of a theoretical knowing of the merely present-at-hand, SZ 136), and "die Evidenz einer unmittelbaren Gegebenheit der Erlebnisse, des Ich und des Bewußtseins" (the evidence of an immediate givenness of lived experiences, of the I and of consciousness) "muß notwendig hinter der Gewißheit zurückbleiben" (must necessarily fall short of) the certainty contained in anticipation (SZ 265). Henry opens by accepting the apodicticity of the ego that SZ 265 disputes. He will not dispute it either; he will relocate it. Whether that relocation answers SZ 265 is a question the later divisions must decide.

Henry states that the problematic of evidence belongs to the phenomenology of reason and radicalizes a problematic of intuition, which is "le fondement de toute assertion rationnelle" (the foundation of every rational assertion). The paragraph is Husserl's and independent of SZ. It prepares the Introduction's central opposition, since SZ 147 says that "»Anschauung« und »Denken« sind beide schon entfernte Derivate des Verstehens" (both "intuition" and "thinking" are already remote derivatives of understanding) and that phenomenological *Wesenschau* (essential seeing) is grounded in existential understanding. Henry's overcoming of "intuitionism" in §3 never cites SZ 147, though it reaches a nearby result.

Henry sets out the phenomenology of intuition: types of intuition, regions of being, the privileged manner of givenness of each region, degrees of evidence, and the claim that each region's ontological structure prescribes the type of evidence possible in it. The paragraph is Husserl's *Ideen* apparatus and contains no SZ claim. It matches in structure, not in kind, SZ's demand that the question of being secure "die rechte Zugangsart zum Seienden" (the right mode of access to the being) it interrogates (SZ 7). In SZ the mode of access is a mode of being of the questioner. In Henry's paragraph it is an eidetic prescription of the region. The same sentence thus serves two doctrines, a case of agreement in wording without agreement in function.

Henry ranks the types of evidence by a teleology immanent to intentional life: the immediate and original evidence is highest, and only apodictic evidence guarantees a "véritable commencement" (genuine beginning). This is Husserl's account (the Cartesian Meditations). Its P-relevance is again negative: the "commencement" is what SZ's epigraph calls the "radical" beginning in scare quotes, and SZ 265 denies that the evidence of the ego's immediate givenness reaches the being it claims to give. The paragraph is the premise of the argument Henry will turn, not the one SZ turned.

Henry argues that the ego cogito becomes the theme of reflection necessarily, not originally ("il ne l'était pas à l'origine"), because it is the only being to give adequate fulfilment to the telos of apodicticity. This is the nearest Henry comes in §1 to SZ 24's promised explanation of why Descartes thought he was released from the question, and it reconstructs it in Husserl's terms: where SZ says certainty, Henry says apodictic evidence that no other region can supply. SZ 46 agrees that Descartes examined the "cogitare des ego – in gewissen Grenzen" (the cogitare of the ego, within certain limits) and left the "sum" unexamined, which Henry's account does not contradict. It adds Henry's claim that the ego was no original theme of Cartesian consciousness; SZ makes no such claim, and the extension depends on the Husserlian reading of the Cartesian project.

Henry names a paradox: the consciousness whose sense is the universal is tied to the singular ego grasped in apodictic evidence, and "la philosophie classique n'a jamais élevé un tel lien à l'état de problème" (classical philosophy never raised this link to a problem), the link "qui unit dans l'origine le problème de la vérité à celui de l'ego" (that unites at the origin the problem of truth and that of the ego). The proposal is the seed of the book's thesis (R §7 ¶5, "L'être de l'ego est la vérité"). SZ poses a link between truth and the one who understands being, and poses it in 1927: truth in the primary sense is "die Erschlossenheit des Daseins" (the disclosedness of Dasein), and "Wahrheit »gibt es« nur, sofern und solange Dasein ist" (truth "is there" only as long as Dasein is, SZ 220–221, 226). Henry's "classical philosophy" can stand for Descartes and Husserl and not for SZ §44. The link he calls unproblematized is posed there, though SZ ties it to Dasein's disclosedness and understanding of being and not to an ego's immanent self-revelation. The silence is not an error of fact, since Henry names classical philosophy, but it removes from view the nearest prior treatment of the paradox.

Henry says that the cogito, once posited, opens two ways: a "rational phenomenology of the ego" as a particular research with exceptional rational interest because of its apodicticity, or the architectonic whole of phenomenological research with the telos of the "élucidation systématique de l'être" (systematic elucidation of being), within which the ego loses its exclusive privilege. This is Husserl's architecture. Its relation to SZ lies in what it states from within Husserl and SZ states from outside: the phenomenological interpretation of the person in Husserl and Scheler "stellen die Frage nach dem »Personsein« selbst nicht mehr" (no longer pose the question of "being a person" itself, SZ 47). Henry shows the same relativization of the ego by locating it in the region-architecture, and he will use it in §4–5 to ask what the ego's being could be if not a region's.

### § 2. The necessity of the prior edification of a universal phenomenological ontology

Henry argues that the research beginning with the ego cogito remains conditioned by that theme: the Cartesian type of evidence cannot be extended to all regions, and "c'est le caractère auquel obéit la problématique de l'ego cogito qui assigne d'indiscutables limites aux démarches ultérieures de la recherche phénoménologique" (it is the character to which the problematic of the ego cogito submits that assigns indisputable limits to the later steps of phenomenological research). This is an extension of SZ's claim about Kant to Husserl. SZ 24 says that Kant, taking over Descartes's position, inherits the omission of an ontology of Dasein, and SZ 46 says that the posit of an initially given I and subject "den phänomenalen Bestand des Daseins von Grund aus verfehlt" (misses the phenomenal stock of Dasein from the ground up). Henry applies the same shape of argument, an initial position conditioning what follows, to the phenomenology of reason. The mechanism changes: in SZ the inheritance is an unexamined ontology (*ens creatum*, SZ 24–25), in Henry a teleology of evidence.

Henry says that the project of the regional ontologies is an infinite regulative Idea, that the cogito is the prototype of rational truth in a double sense (a true being in an exemplary way; the model of every rational apprehension), and that "le cogito n'est que l'une des vérités rationnelles" (the cogito is only one of the rational truths). The paragraph is Husserl's and independent of SZ except for the word: SZ 25 calls the res cogitans the "als fundamentum inconcussum angesetzte Seiende" (the being posited as the unshakable foundation). The double prototype is Henry's Husserlian Descartes; SZ says nothing of a prototype of evidence.

Henry argues that "La raison n'est pas une faculté de l'universel" (reason is not a faculty of the universal): it is bound to particular truths, and the more scrupulously it determines them, "plus décisif aussi et plus fatal est l'oubli où se meut la philosophie" (the more decisive and fatal is the forgetting in which philosophy moves), a forgetting of "l'universel lui-même" (the universal itself). Henry re-inscribes SZ's forgetting. SZ begins with the question of being as "in Vergessenheit gekommen" (fallen into oblivion, SZ 2) and traces the forgetting to a tradition that makes the question seem superfluous (SZ 2–4, 21–22), with the sciences proceeding "naiv und roh" (naively and crudely) in their domains (SZ 9). Henry names a different cause, the rational pursuit of particular truths, and a different content, the universal, which will turn out to be the essence. SZ's forgetting is Dasein's own falling to tradition, not a defect of reason's telos.

Henry says a "crise des fondements" (crisis of foundations) can recall the sciences to the ontological ground they use implicitly, but the phenomenology of reason stays limited because its sense of being remains subordinated to regions, and he asks: "Comment l'ontologie régionale pourrait-elle s'égaler à la tâche *fondamentale* de l'ontologie, elle qui demeure aveugle à l'égard de son propre fondement ?" (How could regional ontology equal the fundamental task of ontology, when it remains blind to its own foundation?). This reconstructs SZ 9–11 closely: the "Grundlagenkrise" (crisis of foundations) of mathematics and the "Krisis ihrer Grundbegriffe" (crisis of their basic concepts) in the sciences (SZ 9–10), and the italicized sentence that all ontology "bleibt im Grunde blind" (remains basically blind) if it has not first clarified the meaning of being (SZ 11). The wording "blind" and "fundamental" is SZ's. Henry's extension is the target: SZ names no one at SZ 11, and Henry applies the sentence to Husserl's regional ontologies, which SZ 38 elsewhere credits as the ground on which its own investigations became possible. The sentence is true of Husserl only if Husserl's regional ontologies leave the meaning of being unasked, which is a claim about Husserl that SZ's sentence does not itself make.

Henry distinguishes universal phenomenological ontology from formal ontology, which, he says, dominates the regional ontologies only formally and in fact draws its origin from the material essences, since the pure essence of a region in general is relative to some concrete region. The distinction and its argument are Husserl-internal (*Ideen I*). SZ has no counterpart, since it discusses no formal ontology; its nearest claim is that being is no genus and no universal by subsumption (SZ 3–4, 38), which Henry's paragraph presupposes without saying so.

Henry opposes to the dependence of formal ontology the autonomy of universal ontology, whose universal "est un terme concret que présuppose chaque région de l'être" (is a concrete term that every region of being presupposes): "Il est la présence même, il réalise l'essence de la présence" (it is presence itself, it realizes the essence of presence). He asks what the "est" is apart from this thing or that person. The question about the "est" repeats SZ 4–5. The identification of being with presence reverses SZ's diagnosis: SZ 25–26 holds that Greek ontology grasped being as "Anwesenheit" (presence), "mit Rücksicht auf einen bestimmten Zeitmodus, die »Gegenwart«" (with regard to a determinate mode of time, the "present"), with no knowledge of the guiding thread at work. Henry takes as his own concept of being what SZ names the tradition's unthought temporal determination. This is a contestation in advance, and Henry will not say so; it is the point to watch in Section I's "présence et aliénation" (R §10).

Henry says that the essence announces itself in the dissolution of all effective presence; that it is not the simple negation of the existent but "l'être n'est être que sur le fondement du Néant en lui" (being is being only on the ground of the Nothing in it); that "le néant n'est pas rien, il est l'opération effective par laquelle l'être se réalise"; that being appears first as a "néant relatif" (relative nothing) and rests on a "néant réel" (real nothing); and that "dans l'indigence où s'angoisse la pensée de l'être", what rises is "l'identité de l'être et du néant". SZ has no such thesis. Its Nothing is the "Nichts und Nirgends" (nothing and nowhere) before which anxiety stands, and it discloses Dasein's being-in-the-world, not being's essence (the analysis of anxiety, §40, SZ 184–191); its *Nichtigkeit* (nullity) is a determination of Dasein as ground of itself (§58, SZ 283–285). The identification of being and nothing and the "néantir" (nihilating) belong to the 1929 *Was ist Metaphysik?*, which Henry takes up through a translation of 1938 and which falls outside P. The comparison at this unit is therefore a Mediation: the "angoisse" is SZ's word, the "néant réel" is not. Henry's later use of SZ's *Nichtigkeit* (R §42) will have to be set against this unit.

Henry says that universal phenomenological ontology must face the objection that it is "un jeu de mots et de concepts", but that once the objection has clarified itself it becomes progress toward the essence, that the essence is positive as the condition of everything, and that this ontology "est bien l'ontologie fondamentale" (is indeed fundamental ontology). SZ uses *Fundamentalontologie* of one thing: "Daher muß die *Fundamentalontologie*, aus der alle andern erst entspringen können, in der *existenzialen Analytik des Daseins* gesucht werden" (hence fundamental ontology, from which all others can first arise, must be sought in the existential analytic of Dasein, SZ 13). Henry gives the term to a universal ontology of the essence, with no analytic of Dasein, and returns to SZ's usage in §6 ¶1 ("L'ontologie fondamentale, déclare *Sein und Zeit*, doit être cherchée dans une analytique du *Dasein*"). The Introduction uses the same title for two different things and calls the second a difficulty, though it is a divergence Henry himself introduced.

### § 3. The overcoming of intuitionism and the liberation of the universal phenomenological horizon

Henry says that universal ontology requires a radical overcoming of intuitionism: "en général" does not mean a survey of the regions and their senses of being, and the generality aimed at "dépasse délibérément tout genre" (deliberately exceeds every genus), quoting Husserl for what it is not ("l'explicitation du sens de *tout type d'être* que moi, l'ego, je peux imaginer", MC 72, nn. 2–3) and for its "sens authentique et universel de l'être en général" (MC 74). The concern agrees with SZ's: being is no genus and its universality lies "höher" (higher, SZ 38). Henry makes the point through Husserl, not through SZ, and the agreement is real but not a citation: the Cartesian Meditations passage is about the ego's constitution of every sense of being, and Henry takes from it only the contrast between "all types" and "being in general". This is mediation by an intermediary whose own sense (constitutive) differs from the one SZ gives the same distinction.

Henry argues that intuition always aims at a particular being or a determinate structure and is therefore "par principe, une pensée finie" (in principle a finite thought), so "la pensée de l'être ne pourra s'accomplir sur le mode d'une réalisation intuitive, mais seulement sur celui du mystère" (the thought of being cannot be accomplished in the mode of an intuitive realization, but only in that of mystery). SZ 147 demotes intuition as well, but by derivation, not finitude: "Anschauen" and "Denken" are "entfernte Derivate des Verstehens" and *Wesenschau* is grounded in existential understanding. The two demotions have different grounds and different consequences. For Henry, the failure of intuition leaves a thought that is not conceptual elucidation; for SZ, being is "in Dunkel gehüllt" (wrapped in darkness) and this "beweist die grundsätzliche Notwendigkeit" (proves the fundamental necessity) of repeating the question (SZ 4), a conceptual and explicit work (SZ 5–6). "Mystery" has no counterpart in SZ, where the unclear understanding of being is a "Rätsel" (riddle, SZ 4) the investigation must make transparent. Henry's word turns SZ's task into its renunciation.

Henry says that the regional project stays under the telos of intuitive realization, so the phenomenology of reason cannot be called "ontological" without restriction; the cogito is a particular determinate being; the Cartesian consciousness "a manqué l'essence et, par là même, la vraie *Stimmung*" (missed the essence and so the true *Stimmung*), which is given to the thought of being "comme la venue en nous de notre accord avec une essence qu'on ne saurait réaliser sur le mode de la présence intuitive qu'en la détruisant" (as the coming in us of our accord with an essence that one could realize in the mode of intuitive presence only by destroying it). That the cogito is a particular being agrees with SZ 25 (the res cogitans determined as *ens*). *Stimmung* is the contested word. In SZ attunement (*Befindlichkeit*, *Stimmung*) discloses thrownness and the "Daß" of the Da, "in unerbittlicher Rätselhaftigkeit" (in relentless enigma, SZ 136), and anxiety is its fundamental case (SZ 184–191). It is a mode of Dasein's disclosedness, not an accord with an essence. The phrase "accord avec l'essence" belongs to Heidegger's 1929/30 course on the fundamental concepts of metaphysics, which Henry knew through Wahl (n. 5) and which is outside P and the corpus.

Henry distinguishes three senses of intuition: vision in general; intuitive consciousness proper, whose signifying intentions are at least partly fulfilled; and the fulfilment itself, the given. He says that these are tied by the teleology of consciousness and that intuitive and rational life therefore stays oriented to "la détermination de l'être-là" (the determination of being-there). The distinction is Husserl's (*Logical Investigations* VI, *Ideen*). SZ has no counterpart and treats intuition as one undifferentiated title (SZ 147). The paragraph builds the vocabulary Henry needs to say what intuition cannot reach, and it adds nothing to the comparison with SZ.

Henry argues that understanding the conditions of intuitive givenness sends us from the intuited given to the intuitive consciousness to consciousness in general, and that every intuitive consciousness is surrounded by a horizon of unfulfilled intentions; a fully intuitive consciousness is only the ideal of reason, "un idéal dangereux" (a dangerous ideal) in which "s'engendre l'oubli originel, l'oubli de l'origine et du fondement" (the original forgetting, the forgetting of the origin and the ground is engendered). The horizon here is Husserl's (*Ideen I*, *Erfahrung und Urteil*): the margin of unfulfilled intentions around a given. SZ's "horizon" is another thing, the horizon of the understanding of being, time (SZ 1, 17), and the two senses are not yet distinguished in Henry. The forgetting is a second re-inscription of SZ's *Vergessenheit*: now the cause is the regulative ideal of fulfilment. The paragraph shows the Husserlian meaning of "horizon" in force; the shift to the other meaning happens at ¶12.

Henry says that the phenomenology of reason can thematize the horizon but forgets or falsifies its meaning, for three reasons. The paragraph only announces them. It is independent of SZ and its function is to organize the three arguments that follow.

The first reason: the interest in the horizon is subordinated to the determinate content of the transcendent effectivity, so that "le contenu de l'horizon s'est substitué comme thème de la réflexion à la forme même de cet horizon, c'est-à-dire à son essence" (the content of the horizon has been substituted, as the theme of reflection, for the very form of the horizon, that is, for its essence). The figure is SZ's difference between being and beings, extended to Husserl: the first step is "nicht μῦθόν τινα διηγεῖσθαι, ›keine Geschichte erzählen‹", not to determine a being by tracing it back to another being as if being had the character of a possible being (SZ 6), and being is what remains concealed and falls back into covering over (SZ 35). Henry does not use the vocabulary of ontological difference here (it appears only in ¶12), so this is an Extension of the difference to the analysis of horizon, made without the name.

The second reason: each region has its own eidetic horizon-structure and the phenomenology of reason studies only determinate structures, so it "manque l'essence de l'horizon en tant que tel" (misses the essence of the horizon as such). The argument is internal to Husserl. Its SZ counterpart is the contrast between the basic concepts of regional ontologies and the meaning of being in general (SZ 11), and this unit adds no SZ reference.

The third reason: intuitionism seeks to give itself the horizon's essence in eidetic intuition, but then faces an eidetic singularity that appears only within an opening horizon; the horizon "est précisément ce qui échappe à la pensée au moment même où elle veut en intuitionner l'essence" (is precisely what escapes thought at the very moment it wants to intuit its essence), and thought "s'en trouve le plus éloignée" (is farthest from it) when it takes it as theme (n. 4: mathematical essences have a mathematical horizon). The figure, that thematizing covers, is SZ 35–36: what must become phenomenon is "zunächst und zumeist" not shown, can be covered over, buried or disguised, and "jeder ursprünglich geschöpfte phänomenologische Begriff und Satz" is exposed to degeneration "als mitgeteilte Aussage" (every originally drawn phenomenological concept and statement is, as a communicated assertion, exposed to degeneration, SZ 36–37). The cause differs: SZ's concealment comes from the factical falling of Dasein into tradition and communication; Henry's from the structure of the horizon, which transcends all determination. Same figure, different diagnosis.

Henry says that universal phenomenological ontology does not escape this difficulty but needs only to be aware of the principled "obscurité" (obscurity) of the essence, "non point pour la surmonter [...] mais pour la vivre comme telle dans le mystère" (not to overcome it, but to live it as such in mystery), while the phenomenology of reason cannot think the contradiction "comme un caractère positif de l'essence" (as a positive character of the essence). The word "positive" is SZ's: the indeterminacy of the understanding of being "ist selbst ein positives Phänomen, das der Aufklärung bedarf" (is itself a positive phenomenon that needs clarification, SZ 6). The function is opposite. For SZ a positive phenomenon is one that calls for elucidation; for Henry it is one to be lived without elucidation. This is the clearest case in the Introduction of agreement in wording with disagreement in function.

Henry says that the analysis of confused consciousness is a surer guide than the survey of types of evidence, since the horizon is not masked from non-intuitive consciousness, and the horizon must be grasped as "la condition transcendantale d'un objet en général, comme la forme pure de l'objectivité" (the transcendental condition of an object in general, the pure form of objectivity) and not as a psychological horizon confused with its contents. The paragraph draws on Husserl (the analysis of "confused" consciousness, *Erfahrung und Urteil*) and on Kantian vocabulary. SZ is independent of it: where "transzendental" occurs in SZ it attaches to being as *transcendens* (SZ 38) and to time as horizon (SZ 1). The shift to "forme pure de l'objectivité" is Husserl's and prepares ¶12.

Henry writes that what allows every being to manifest itself, to become "phenomenon", is "le milieu de visibilité où il peut surgir à titre de présence effective" (the milieu of visibility in which it can arise as effective presence); that the unfolding of such a milieu, as "horizon transcendantal de tout être en général", is "l'œuvre de l'être lui-même"; that every predicative truth presupposes an ontic truth, which in turn presupposes the ontological truth, since manifestation occurs "à l'intérieur d'un milieu déjà ouvert" (within an already open milieu); that "l'ouverture de ce milieu, c'est l'apérité de l'être" (the opening of this milieu is the openness of being); and "C'est seulement parce que l'être est dévoilé que l'étant peut se manifester" (only because being is unveiled can beings manifest themselves). Commentary R finds SZ §44 here "almost verbatim"; by the text it is not. The scheme of predicative truth, ontic truth and ontological truth is Heidegger's 1929 *Vom Wesen des Grundes*: propositional truth is rooted in a "vorprädikative Offenbarkeit von Seiendem" (prepredicative manifestness of beings), called ontic truth, which is itself possible only through "Enthülltheit des Seins" (unconcealedness of being), called ontological truth (GA 9 130–131), whose first step cites SZ §44, SZ 212–230, and for the propositional (GA 9 n. 11, referring to SZ §33, SZ 154). The Mediation is therefore GA 9, probably in Corbin's translation of 1938, and the language of "dévoilé" is its. The departures are three. First, what opens: SZ assigns the opening to Dasein ("Wahrheit im ursprünglichsten Sinne ist die Erschlossenheit des Daseins", truth in the most original sense is the disclosedness of Dasein, SZ 220–221; truth and being "gibt es" only as long as Dasein is, SZ 226, 230), and GA 9 grounds the ontological difference in "die Transzendenz des Daseins" (GA 9 134–135). Henry's version removes even that ground and gives the work to being. Second, the horizon: in SZ it is time (SZ 1, 17); here it is a milieu of visibility. Third, "présence effective": the milieu is the place of presence, which is the determination SZ 25–26 criticizes in the Greek interpretation. The paragraph is the Introduction's thesis about manifestation, and it is a doctrine of Henry's, drawn from Heidegger's 1929 writing and not from SZ; Section I's "monisme ontologique" builds on it.

### § 4. The insertion of the *ego cogito* and of its problematic inside the horizon liberated by universal phenomenological ontology

Henry says that ontology struggles to keep its project pure: philosophy "succombe à la tentation de l'étant" (yields to the temptation of the being), substituting ontic considerations for the analysis of being, and has been, "comme le dit Heidegger" (as Heidegger says, n. 5), a mixture of first philosophy and discussions of God or the immortality of the soul. Aquinas continues the Greek error by bringing the idea of God into the study of the transcendentals, and Descartes's *Meditations* give the divine a dominant place. Henry cites no SZ here; his source is the unpublished course on the fundamental concepts of metaphysics that he knew through Wahl, which is outside P and the corpus. SZ holds a related but different thesis. It finds the ancient ontology of the tradition tacitly determined by *Geschaffenheit* (createdness), "im weitesten Sinne der Hergestelltheit von etwas" (in the widest sense of being produced), so that the res cogitans is determined as ens creatum and God as ens infinitum is the ens increatum (SZ 24–25). That is a determination of the sense of being by a theology, not a temptation to turn to beings. Henry's Thomas, moreover, is not SZ's: SZ 14 cites Aquinas for the *anima* as the being that agrees with every being, a priority "ontologisch nicht geklärt" (ontologically unclarified) but with "nichts gemein mit einer schlechten Subjektivierung" (nothing in common with a bad subjectivization), and not for God among the transcendentals. The unit is a Mediation, with SZ 24–25 the nearest P-locus.

Henry describes an oscillation in Descartes, already visible in Aquinas, between God and the ego cogito as principle; the subordination of ontology to egology is no better justified than the old primacy of theology, because the cogito, "sous le titre de « sujet » ou d'« esprit », de « personne » ou de « raison »" (under the title of "subject" or "spirit", of "person" or "reason"), remains an existent that cannot be confused with an ontological ground. He adds that Hegel falsifies Greek ontology by reducing it to a moment in the evolution of consciousness, and that for idealism the ontological project stays attached to the prior reality of a singular existence. The list of titles is SZ 22's almost item for item: "das ego cogito Descartes', Subjekt, Ich, Vernunft, Geist, Person" remain, "entsprechend dem durchgängigen Versäumnis der Seinsfrage, unbefragt auf Sein und Struktur ihres Seins" (in keeping with the thoroughgoing neglect of the question of being, unquestioned as to the being and structure of their being), and the traditional categories are transferred to them (SZ 22; cf. SZ 46). The reconstruction is faithful and uncited. The Hegel claim is another matter. SZ says that Greek ontology, uprooted, becomes for Hegel "bloß neu zu bearbeitendes Material" (mere material to be reworked, SZ 21–22), not a moment of consciousness; Henry's version belongs to his reading of Hegel's *Phenomenology* (Section II) and is not SZ's.

Henry argues that consciousness has a double aspect, as the power that unfolds the horizon and as the singular existent in which that transcendence is rooted, so that transcendence becomes a character of the existent consciousness; "l'entente ontologique ou pré-ontologique de l'être est dénaturée quand elle devient l'attribut d'une détermination ontique" (the ontological or pre-ontological understanding of being is denatured when it becomes the attribute of an ontic determination). Even to say that transcendence is the "substance" and "la subjectivité du sujet" (the subjectivity of the subject), not a predicate synthetically added to a prior subjectivity, does not escape the paradox that "fait reposer la condition sur le conditionné" (makes the condition rest on the conditioned) so long as the being of that subject has not been elucidated. The phrase "subjectivity of the subject" is SZ 24's ("einer vorgängigen ontologischen Analytik der Subjektivität des Subjekts", a prior ontological analytic of the subjectivity of the subject), but the thesis that transcendence constitutes the subject and is no added predicate is Heidegger's 1929 doctrine: "Das Subjekt existiert nie zuvor als »Subjekt«, um dann, *falls* gar Objekte vorhanden sind, *auch* zu transzendieren" (the subject never exists beforehand as a "subject" in order then, if objects are present at all, also to transcend; GA 9 138). The target is not named. What Henry contests is therefore not an idealism alone: his formulation reconstructs a Heideggerian position (GA 9 138) and says it leaves the being of the subject unelucidated. SZ 12 does make the understanding of being a determination of Dasein's being ("Seinsverständnis ist selbst eine Seinsbestimmtheit des Daseins"), but of its being as existence, not as an attribute of an existent. Whether the existential analytic elucidates "d'où le sujet peut tenir sa substantialité" (where the subject can get its substantiality) is the question Henry presses in §6.

Henry argues that transcendence cannot be inserted in a singular existence: it is "le néant, l'œuvre interne de l'être" (the nothing, the inner work of being), the "trouée" (opening) made by the Nothing in which things emerge; being lends the existent, "sous la forme d'un horizon de présence" (in the form of a horizon of presence), aid and protection, for which the "subject" must humbly ask. "L'être est un événement impersonnel. L'existant humain ne peut le revendiquer comme sien" (being is an impersonal event; the human existent cannot claim it as its own), and the spirits wait for the "travail infini du négatif" (the infinite work of the negative). Nothing of this is SZ's. SZ says that Dasein is the being to which its own being is at issue (SZ 12, 42) and ties the "giving" of being to Dasein: "Sein – nicht Seiendes – »gibt es« nur, sofern Wahrheit ist. Und sie *ist* nur, sofern und solange Dasein ist" (being, not beings, "is given" only insofar as truth is, and truth is only insofar and as long as Dasein is, SZ 230; cf. SZ 212). The vocabulary of a sheltering and giving being that the human must ask for, and of being as event, is closer to the Heidegger of the *Letter on Humanism* (1947), which Henry could have read, than to SZ; this paragraph shows no sign that he does, and the "travail du négatif" is Hegel's phrase. The paragraph states a doctrine of being that SZ's text contradicts on the point of who gives; as a reading of SZ it is a misattribution, and as Henry's thesis it is an argument against SZ that he does not mark as such.

Henry says that one sinks into contradiction if, while claiming to safeguard the essence, one inserts it into human subjectivity; the transposition of the themes of the ontology of being into a philosophy of the cogito deforms it, in two ways: the nothing loses its nature and becomes "une simple opération subjective", and transcendence, as the property of a determinate being, cannot avoid confusion with a psychological act. Nor can the human subject be assimilated to the essence: "c'est à l'intérieur d'un tel milieu que nous, et toutes les choses, pouvons nous manifester, à titre de « phénomènes », dans la lumière du monde" (it is within such a milieu that we and all things can manifest ourselves, as "phenomena", in the light of the world); the transcendence that makes the world "nous dépasse radicalement, nous, les hommes, au même titre que les choses" (surpasses us radically, us humans, in the same way as things). This contests SZ's ascription of transcendence to Dasein. SZ 38 says that "Sein ist das transcendens schlechthin" (being is the transcendens pure and simple) and that "die Transzendenz des Seins des Daseins" (the transcendence of the being of Dasein) is a distinguished one, because in it lie the possibility and necessity of the most radical individuation. Henry's phrase "au même titre que les choses" cancels that distinction and with it SZ 13's premise that Dasein's understanding of being is also an understanding of "Welt" and of the beings within it. The objection to psychologism is one SZ shares in its own way (SZ 46); the redistribution of transcendence from Dasein to a milieu in which humans appear like things is Henry's.

Henry says that subjectivity is not the absolute condition and is falsified when made to play that role; it is "une vie particulière et, à ce titre, profondément réelle" (a particular life and, as such, profoundly real), which idealism, to give it an ontological function, empties into a "pure forme", "la forme « vide » d'une pensée en général" (the "empty" form of thought in general); "les penseurs subjectifs" (the subjective thinkers) have denounced the dissolution of inner life in the "sujet constituant". The critique of the subject-position matches SZ 46 in aim and differs in ground. SZ 46 holds that every idea of "subject" "macht noch ... den Ansatz des *subjectum* (ὑποκείμενον) *ontologisch* mit" (still takes part in the ontological positing of the subjectum), even where one resists the "Seelensubstanz" or the "Verdinglichung des Bewußtseins" ontically, because thinghood itself needs a demonstration of its ontological origin. Henry's diagnosis is the opposite: the subject emptied of reality, not weighed down by thinghood. He also asserts here that subjectivity is a real, particular life, a thesis of his own that the book will later transform (the absolute subjectivity of Section IV); the unit places it in a paragraph that otherwise follows SZ's critique of the subject-title.

Henry states a dilemma: one cannot identify the ontological ground "l'être" with a singular existent. Either philosophy leaves the question of being and loses itself in ontic determinations, or, remaining ontological, it withdraws "un existant indûment privilégié" (an unduly privileged existent) from the condition of ground; but then the contradiction only shifts, since that existent will either lose its condition as existent or fail in its role. The way out attempted is to strip the existent of its nature as existent, up to "l'identité de la subjectivité et du néant" (the identity of subjectivity and nothingness), an "affirmation absurde" (absurd affirmation). The explicit target is "l'idéalisme du XIXᵉ ou du XXᵉ siècle" and the thesis of the identity of subjectivity and nothingness, which is Sartre's (EN is in Henry's list of abbreviations); SZ is not named. The first horn is SZ 22's charge against the tradition. The second horn describes, without naming it, what SZ does with Dasein: the "ausgezeichnet" being (SZ 12–13) is set apart from the field of beings as the one that understands being. The dilemma is thus prepared for application to SZ in §6, where Henry asks whether the "privilege" of Dasein can survive.

### § 5. The problem of the insertion of the *ego cogito* inside the universal phenomenological horizon: the "being" of the absolute ego

Henry raises an objection to the insertion of the ego cogito in the universal horizon: consciousness may not be a region at all but "l'*Urregion*", "la protocatégorie de l'être en général", so that the analysis of the cogito would itself be universal ontology; for the ego cogito's "sens de l'être, c'est justement de conférer un sens à l'être, c'est, plus profondément, d'être la source de ce sens, l'origine absolue d'où celui-ci jaillit chaque fois comme une libre création" (meaning of being is precisely to confer a meaning on being, more deeply, to be the source of that meaning, the absolute origin from which it springs each time as a free creation). This is Husserl (*Ideen I*, the Cartesian Meditations), and SZ stands against it at the point of "free creation". SZ describes the projection of the understanding of being as "geworfener Entwurf" (thrown projection) and warns that the project is "dem Faktum der Geworfenheit in das Da überantwortet" (delivered over to the fact of thrownness into the Da, SZ 148). Dasein gives sense by projecting, but it projects as already thrown; and the sense is what is accessible to understanding, never a ground behind it: "Der Sinn von Sein kann nie in Gegensatz gebracht werden zum Seienden oder zum Sein als tragenden »Grund« des Seienden, weil »Grund« nur als Sinn zugänglich wird" (the meaning of being can never be set against beings or against being as a supporting "ground" of beings, because "ground" becomes accessible only as meaning, SZ 152). Henry's paragraph does not cite SZ, and its premise is one that SZ 148 and 152 withhold.

Henry says that the reality of consciousness can be thought correctly only in a transcendental frame; above a noetic or functional phenomenology stands "la phénoménologie transcendantale de la conscience absolue" (the transcendental phenomenology of absolute consciousness), the absolute ego being the "*Urstruktur* de toutes les structures possibles et de tous les sens possibles de l'être" (primal structure of all possible structures and meanings of being) in its fully concrete sense. The paragraph is Husserl's account. SZ's judgement on this kind of position is that it "kommt nicht in die Dimension der Frage nach dem Sein des Daseins" (does not enter the dimension of the question of the being of Dasein): Husserl and Scheler "stellen die Frage nach dem »Personsein« selbst nicht mehr" (no longer pose the question of "being a person" itself, SZ 47). Henry's report of Husserl needs no more than that statement to be read against SZ; he does not draw it.

Henry says that the orientation of the problematic toward the ego cogito cannot mean forgetting the question of being if the transcendental self-explication of the ego is the very mode of pursuing the ontological design; constitutional research is one with the phenomenological explication of the ego, and its "signification ontologique ne peut plus être mise en question" (ontological significance can no longer be questioned). This is Husserl's claim stated as Henry's premise. SZ would not grant it: what Husserl's constitutional analyses leave unasked is the being of the subject that constitutes (SZ 47), and ontological significance is assigned in SZ by the question of the meaning of being, not by the infinite task of constitution. The paragraph depends on a premise that SZ contests and that Henry himself questions in the next paragraph but one.

Henry credits Husserl's phenomenology with the overcoming of intuitionism through constitutional problems: the ego is no longer an intuitive given offering an occasion for rational positing but "cette vie transcendantale elle-même", "l'ego absolu, le naturant originaire" (the absolute ego, the original naturing), which belongs to no region. The paragraph is Henry's report of Husserl, and "naturant" is his own word for the constituting ego (it echoes *natura naturans*). SZ is independent of it; the unit prepares the problem, shared in form with SZ 24, of the being of that ego.

Henry asks whether the problem of the being of the absolute ego is resolved: philosophy has not asked "ce qui fait la subjectivité du sujet" (what makes the subjectivity of the subject), and the question of how "l'origine peut [...] être portée à la condition de « phénomène »" (the origin can be brought to the condition of "phenomenon") arises with more urgency, since it can become visible "seulement dans la lumière de la transcendance" (only in the light of transcendence), under the jurisdiction of the transcendental horizon of being. "Le problème de l'être de la subjectivité nous renvoie, inévitablement, au problème du sens de l'être en général" (the problem of the being of subjectivity inevitably refers us to the problem of the meaning of being in general). This extends SZ 24 from Kant to Husserl: SZ says Kant lacked "eine vorgängige ontologische Analytik der Subjektivität des Subjekts", and Henry says the same of the Husserlian absolute ego. The closing sentence reconstructs SZ 13, quoted above in the Prelude. The question "how can the origin become a phenomenon" is Henry's own and exceeds SZ; SZ's counterpart is its concept of the phenomenon as "das Sich-an-ihm-selbst-Zeigende" (that which shows itself in itself, SZ 28–31) and the claim that being, though "zunächst und zumeist" (proximally and for the most part) not shown, can be made to show itself (SZ 35). The "light of transcendence" is the answer Henry will put in question in the next division.

Henry says that the problem of the ego's being is the same as how the ego can accede to the rank of "phenomenon"; since it concerns the absolute ego, the question is how the transcendental field as origin of being "peut-il surgir devant nous, dans la lumière" (can arise before us, in the light), and that the research turns to "le dévoilement préalable du champ qu'elle veut explorer" (the prior unveiling of the field it wants to explore). The move takes SZ's problem of the *Phänomenwerden* of being (SZ 35: what "fordert, Phänomen zu werden", demands to become phenomenon, is not this or that being but being) and transfers it to the ego. The transfer is the thesis of the book in outline. It treats the ego as a candidate for the place SZ gives to being, and it adopts SZ's description of the task, a prior making-visible, without SZ's reason for it, which is that being is covered over by what shows itself first (SZ 35–36).

Henry says that the phenomenological reduction precedes the exploration of the transcendental field, that the epoché is the "radical" method, that the grasp of transcendental life requires "si je me place au-dessus de cette vie tout entière" (if I place myself above this whole life; MC 18) in intuition and evidence, and that the transcendental experience, which Husserl wants to confine to the pure data of reflection (MC 30), thus remains subordinate to "l'horizon transcendantal de l'être en général". The paragraph is Husserl and Henry's reading of Husserl. SZ does not name the reduction. Heidegger did in the lecture course of 1927 (GA 24 §5, published 1975, retrospective), where he keeps the word "dem Wortlaut, nicht aber der Sache nach" (in wording, not in matter) and defines the reduction as leading the gaze from the being grasped in any way to the understanding of the being of that being, not to constituting experiences (GA 24 29). The retrospective text shows that Heidegger's own use of the reduction was not Husserl's, and that Henry's subordination of the Husserlian reduction to the horizon of being agrees with it in direction; Henry could not have known it.

Henry says that the overcoming of intuitionism by a transcendental philosophy is only apparent, since the "rétro-référence à soi-même de la phénoménologie ne pourrait avoir une signification ontologique que si l'intuition était à elle-même son propre fondement" (the self-reference of phenomenology could have an ontological significance only if intuition were its own ground); elucidation is a mode of realizing evidence and works within a frame of evidence which plays the role of "un fondement ultime, quoique implicite" (an ultimate though implicit ground). The figure of a phenomenology which must be made critical against itself is SZ 36: the difficulty of the research is "sie gegen sich selbst in einem positiven Sinne kritisch zu machen" (to make it critical against itself in a positive sense), since its concepts degenerate when communicated. The grounds differ. SZ's phenomenology is endangered by its own transmission; Henry's by the concealed ground of evidence that every elucidation uses. The reconstruction of the figure is faithful, and the question of ground is Henry's.

Henry argues that the subordination of phenomenological description to the essence does not come from the reflective attitude of the phenomenologist: evidence already characterizes the way the ego is present to itself in unreflective life, and the unfolding of the horizon is the presupposition of reflection, not its result; "L'explicitation ne s'opère pas à partir de rien, elle porte sur quelque chose qui doit toujours, d'une certaine façon, être déjà là" (explication does not proceed from nothing, it bears on something that must always in some way already be there); even the pure ego, reduced and eidetic, requires "un horizon de présence" (a horizon of presence) as condition of its manifestation. The structure of the first claim is the fore-structure of interpretation in SZ: interpretation "gründet jeweils in einer *Vorhabe*" (is grounded in each case in a fore-having) and moves in what is "schon verstanden" (already understood, SZ 150), which is why SZ declares the circle of the question of being no vicious one (SZ 7–8). The extension is Henry's: he carries the "already" from understanding to a milieu of presence, and from Dasein's projective understanding to an evidence of the unreflective ego. The first move transposes SZ faithfully; the second belongs to the thesis of the book and to Husserl's analyses of unreflected life.

Henry says that the transcendental ego brings with it a world of objects as intentional correlate, a "univers" in which cogitata can manifest themselves; to this universe the pure ego is ground and origin, but the ego and the cogitatum immanent to it can themselves become phenomena and arise in being only "sur le fond de celui-ci en eux" (on the ground of it in them). The paragraph is Husserl; its last clause is the thesis Henry has been building, that being precedes the ego as the ground of its appearing. SZ has the dependence the other way round in one respect: being is given only insofar as there is truth, and truth only as long as there is Dasein (SZ 226, 230). The two orders of priority are not contradictory in SZ, which also holds that Dasein understands being only in a thrown projection, but the order Henry assumes (being first, the ego on its ground) is not one SZ asserts. §6 is where he tests it against SZ's own words.

### § 6. The difficulties relative to the edification of universal phenomenological ontology

Henry says that the thought of being, which must overcome every determinate existent, is thrown back infallibly onto a singular existent; ontology gives itself "un fondement d'ordre ontique" (a foundation of ontic order) and its freedom is put in question by "l'interpellation d'un existant singulier qui revendique avec force le caractère d'être un « commencement »" (the summons of a singular existent that forcefully claims to be a "beginning"). "L'ontologie fondamentale, déclare *Sein und Zeit*, doit être cherchée dans une analytique du *Dasein*" (fundamental ontology, *Being and Time* declares, must be sought in an analytic of Dasein). The sentence is a faithful reconstruction of SZ 13, and Henry's framing as a difficulty is his own. SZ states the same point as a result: the existential analytic is "letztlich *existenziell*, d. h. *ontisch* verwurzelt" (ultimately existentiell, that is, ontically rooted, SZ 13), and it treats the ontic rooting as part of the "ontic priority" of the question (SZ 13–14). Nor does SZ leave the choice of the existent to chance: the exemplary being must be chosen by the structure of the question (SZ 7). The aporia is therefore the shape in which Henry reads SZ's own thesis. Whether the thesis is an aporia, or the necessary consequence of the question being a mode of being of the questioner, is what §6 argues.

Henry sets out the formal structure of the question of being as three terms: "l'être lui-même [...] ce qui est recherché *(Gefragtes)*" (being itself, what is sought), "un étant déterminé" (a determinate being) *(Befragtes)*, and "la question elle-même" (the question itself), which is a comportment of a questioning reality and gives that reality the being of a "réalité-qui-questionne"; the being that makes this possible "s'historialise" (comes to pass) in it "comme une possibilité propre, d'ordre éidétique" (as an own possibility, of eidetic order) and defines it as "réalité humaine". SZ 5 has three terms of the question and Henry's third is not one of them. SZ's are *Gefragtes* (what is asked about: being), *Befragtes* (what is interrogated: beings) and *Erfragtes* (what is to be found out, "das eigentlich Intendierte", the actually intended, "das, wobei das Fragen ins Ziel kommt", where the questioning reaches its goal: the meaning of being, SZ 5–6). The questioning as comportment of the questioner is a separate analysis in SZ (SZ 5, 7), not a term of the triad. Henry therefore does three things: he glosses *Gefragtes* as "ce qui est recherché", which is SZ's gloss of *Erfragtes*; he drops *Erfragtes*, so that the meaning of being drops out of the formal structure; and he puts the questioner in the third place. All three suit his argument, which turns on the questioner's being and not on the meaning sought. The vocabulary of the rest differs from SZ's as well. "D'ordre éidétique" is Husserlian: SZ says the essence of Dasein "nicht durch Angabe eines sachhaltigen Was" (not by specifying a what-content) but in that it has "je sein Sein als seiniges zu sein" (to be its being in each case as its own, SZ 12). "Réalité humaine" is Sartre's and Corbin's rendering of *Dasein*; SZ avoids titles such as "Mensch" and "Leben" for this being (SZ 46). The unit is a Selection and silence with a Re-inscription: the question is made to point at "human reality" in an eidetic sense that SZ's wording excludes.

Henry says that being is always the being of a being, and that the question is therefore addressed to a being in order to "déchiffrer en lui le sens de l'être" (decipher in it the meaning of being); the being chosen is not indifferent, it is the questioner, the *Befragtes*, and "l'*être de la réalité humaine* doit d'abord faire le thème de la problématique" (the being of human reality must first be the theme of the problematic). This reconstructs SZ 9 ("Sein ist jeweils das Sein eines Seienden", being is in each case the being of a being) and SZ 7, where the question of the "exemplary being" is posed and answered by Dasein, the being "das wir, die Fragenden, je selbst sind" (that we, the questioners, in each case ourselves are). "Déchiffrer" renders SZ 7's "abgelesen werden" (be read off) adequately. The only qualification SZ makes and Henry omits is SZ 8's caution that so far "weder der Vorrang des Daseins erwiesen, noch über seine mögliche oder gar notwendige Funktion als primär zu befragendes Seiendes entschieden" (neither the priority of Dasein has been proved, nor its possible or even necessary function as the being primarily to be interrogated decided); the priority is announced, and the proof comes in §4 (SZ 11–14).

Henry argues that being itself dominates every being, but that to human reality it belongs to be related to being as the one who "le comprend" (understands it); the understanding is the basic fact determining human reality, whether it remains implicit or is altered, and the question of being is "la radicalisation de cette compréhension ontologique ou, plutôt, pré-ontologique de l'être qui est immanente à la réalité humaine comme son pouvoir le plus propre et comme son essence même" (the radicalization of this ontological or rather pre-ontological understanding of being that is immanent to human reality as its most proper power and as its very essence). The clause is SZ 15 almost word for word: "Die Seinsfrage ist dann aber nichts anderes als die Radikalisierung einer zum Dasein selbst gehörigen wesenhaften Seinstendenz, des vorontologischen Seinsverständnisses" (the question of being is then nothing other than the radicalization of an essential tendency of being belonging to Dasein itself, the pre-ontological understanding of being). Henry also reconstructs correctly SZ's point that alterations of the understanding presuppose it: the priority of Dasein is the ground of its constitution remaining covered (SZ 15–16). The slips are small and run in one direction. "Pouvoir le plus propre" turns a *Seinstendenz* (tendency of being) into a capacity; "essence même" imports an essence where SZ puts existence (SZ 12, 42); and "immanente à la réalité humaine" says in Henry's word what SZ says of Dasein's constitution, but "immanent" will carry a very different weight in the book.

Henry says that human reality holds a determining place in the question: a precedence at once ontic (the question of being is a determinate mode of "cette existence que nous sommes nous-mêmes", this existence that we ourselves are) and ontological, since an understanding of being belongs to it "en un double sens", its own being and "l'être de l'étant qu'elle n'est pas" (the being of the beings it is not). This is SZ 12–13 compressed. SZ has three priorities (ontic, ontological, and the third, as the "ontisch-ontologische Bedingung der Möglichkeit aller Ontologien", ontic-ontological condition of the possibility of all ontologies, SZ 13), and the double sense is the "gleichursprünglich" (equiprimordial) understanding of "Welt" and of the being of beings within the world (SZ 13). Henry takes over what he needs for the next argument, that the relation to being is double, and leaves out the third priority, which makes Dasein the condition of other ontologies and which he will need to read as methodological.

Henry asks why ontology cannot found itself ontologically and must place an existent at its center, to the point of being confused with its existential analysis, and answers that the condition can be imposed only through "le rapport qui unit, dans l'essence, l'être et l'étant", whereas the structure of the question gives only "une analyse tout extérieure d'un tel rapport" (an entirely external analysis of such a relation); "le lien qui unit l'être et l'étant demeure foncièrement obscur, sa structure n'est pas homogène, sa signification est polyvalente" (the link that unites being and beings remains basically obscure, its structure is not homogeneous, its meaning is polyvalent). Henry reports a lacuna that SZ acknowledges. SZ does not claim to have clarified how being relates to beings: "Was es bedeutet: Sein »ist«, wo es doch von allem Seienden unterschieden sein soll, kann erst konkret gefragt werden, wenn der Sinn von Sein und die Tragweite von Seinsverständnis überhaupt aufgeklärt sind" (what it means that being "is", where it is supposed to be different from all beings, can be asked concretely only when the meaning of being and the scope of the understanding of being have been clarified, SZ 230). SZ's final pages repeat the question (SZ 436–437). Henry's verdict that the relation is obscure is therefore supported by SZ; the reasons he goes on to give, that the structure is "pas homogène" and the meaning "polyvalente", are his, and SZ would rather say that the relation is not yet asked in the right way.

Henry says that what characterizes the relation of human reality to being is that it is given to it to live it, and that this privilege is tied to that of language, "toute parole n'est prononcée que sur le fond d'un entretien plus primitif, qui est celui de l'homme et de l'être" (every word is spoken only on the ground of a more primitive conversation, that of man and being), a privilege which a non-Dasein being cannot claim, not through ontic deficiency but through its "réalité ontologique propre" (own ontological reality); when the relation of human reality to the essence is defined from this understanding "nous l'appelons un rapport transcendantal" (we call it a transcendental relation), which implies no primacy of "subjectivity" because "son fondement ne réside pas dans la réalité humaine elle-même, mais bien plutôt dans l'être qui donne à celle-ci, en l'ordonnant à lui, la possibilité de le penser" (its ground lies not in human reality itself but in the being that gives it, by ordering it to itself, the possibility of thinking it). The language thesis is not SZ's. SZ treats language as *Rede* (discourse), an existential equiprimordial with attunement and understanding, grounded in Dasein's disclosedness (§34), and it uses the *zôon logon echon* as the tradition's guiding thread for Greek ontology (SZ 25–26). The "conversation of man and being" belongs to the later Heidegger (the Hölderlin essays, the *Letter on Humanism*). The sentence on the ground is Henry's placement: SZ makes the understanding of being "eine Seinsbestimmtheit des Daseins" (a determination of the being of Dasein, SZ 12), so that its ground is Dasein's existence; Henry places the ground in being and makes human reality dependent on it. The phrase "rapport transcendantal" is his own as well. The paragraph presents a reading that holds for Heidegger after SZ and for Henry's thesis, and it appears here as an account of SZ's priority of Dasein.

Henry says that human reality is subject to being in an entirely different sense when being is for it nothing more than for any being, so that "l'expression « être de la réalité humaine » est donc foncièrement ambiguë" (the expression "being of human reality" is therefore basically ambiguous): it designates both the essence of human reality as transcendentally related to being, and the ground in it that makes it be "au même titre toutefois que n'importe quel étant" (in the same way as any being). The ambiguity is Henry's analysis and not a finding in SZ. SZ has a name for the second sense, but as a determination within the one structure: thrownness, the "Daß" of the Da that attunement brings Dasein before "in unerbittlicher Rätselhaftigkeit" (in relentless enigma, SZ 136; cf. SZ 134–136). Thrown, Dasein is not independent of what it did not make, but thrownness is a character of existence, projecting thrown (SZ 148), not a second relation to being that would be the same as that of a stone. Henry's splitting separates what SZ binds, and the later chapters (R §13, "The ambiguity of 'Dasein'") develop the split as an objection. At this stage the unit states the objection's premise. The premise is open to dispute, since the text it is supposed to describe has no second relation of Dasein to being.

Henry turns to the objection of the circle: fundamental ontology rests on the analytic of Dasein, but the idea of being is already implied in the constitution of human reality; this ceases to be an objection if research is turned toward the presupposition to clarify it, and "l'ontologie fondamentale ne repousse pas l'idée d'un progrès circulaire de son analyse, elle l'accueille" (fundamental ontology does not reject the idea of a circular progress of its analysis, it welcomes it). An ambiguity remains about the manner in which the ground founds: either the analytic relies on Dasein as bearing the power to relate originally to being (the transcendental relation), or it proceeds under the implicit presupposition of the essence, in which case the essence's relation to the being of human reality is the same as to the being of any being. The sense of the doctrine is "le recouvrement et, finalement, l'identification du rapport transcendantal avec le simple rapport d'immanence qui confère chaque fois l'être à l'étant" (the overlap and finally the identification of the transcendental relation with the simple relation of immanence that in each case confers being on beings), and its paradoxical consequence would be "la pure et simple suppression du privilège du *Dasein*" (the plain suppression of Dasein's privilege). Henry reports SZ's reply to the circle accurately: no "Zirkel im Beweis" (circle in proof) but "Rück- oder Vorbezogenheit" (relatedness back or forward) of what is asked on the questioning (SZ 7–8). The ambiguity is not in SZ, however. SZ says the "presupposing" of being in the analytic is "die vorgängige Hinblicknahme auf Sein" (the prior looking-toward being) that "entwächst dem durchschnittlichen Seinsverständnis, in dem wir uns immer schon bewegen, *und das am Ende zur Wesensfassung des Daseins selbst gehört*" (grows out of the average understanding of being in which we always already move, and which in the end belongs to the essential constitution of Dasein itself, SZ 8). The presupposition is Dasein's own understanding, the first of Henry's two senses; the second, being's relation to Dasein as to any being, has no support in SZ 8. Henry's alternative therefore describes a possibility that SZ's text excludes, and its use is to introduce the thesis that the privilege of Dasein dissolves.

Henry says that the need for ontology to take an ontic foundation is no difficulty in itself: "La référence de l'ontologie à la réalité d'un existant singulier n'est que la transposition méthodologique du lien qui unit, dans l'origine, la transcendance et la finitude" (the reference of ontology to the reality of a singular existent is only the methodological transposition of the link that unites, at the origin, transcendence and finitude); being has "point par lui-même le pouvoir de se manifester" (no power by itself to manifest itself) and manifests itself in the being, whose manifestation is at the same time what hides it, "conformément à une situation dialectique qui n'est point provisoire mais insurmontable" (according to a dialectical situation that is not provisional but insurmountable). The link of transcendence and finitude is the thesis of the Kantbuch (the subject of R's Section II) and not of SZ, which finds Dasein's finitude in originary temporality ("Die ursprüngliche Zeit ist endlich", SZ 331). The concealment is a re-inscription of SZ's *Verdeckung*. SZ's covering belongs to phenomena and has stages (undiscovered, buried, disguised), and it admits "notwendige" (necessary) coverings, those that "in der Bestandart des Entdeckten gründen" (are grounded in the constitution of what has been discovered, SZ 36); but its examples are the degeneration of concepts in communication and the dogmatic systems (SZ 36), and its cause is the factical falling of Dasein, not the nature of being. Henry's "dialectical" and "insurmountable" extrapolate from SZ 36 a structure of being that SZ does not assert. The verdict is a defensible extrapolation on SZ 36's "notwendige Verdeckungen", an unacknowledged departure on the claim that being lacks "le pouvoir de se manifester".

Henry says that the ultimate reason for the reference to an ontic foundation does not explain the privilege of Dasein, and asks whether it is decisive or only methodological; "la subordination de l'analytique existentiale à l'ontologie fondamentale est constamment affirmée dans *Sein und Zeit*" (the subordination of the existential analytic to fundamental ontology is constantly affirmed in *Being and Time*, n. 8: "L'évolution ultérieure de la doctrine ne fera que confirmer ce point de vue"); the analytic remains incomplete and provisional, to be repeated "lorsqu'aura été mise en lumière la signification temporelle de l'être" (when the temporal meaning of being has been brought to light); "Celle-ci, pourtant, ne va-t-elle pas nous renvoyer à l'être du « Dasein » comme à son fondement ?" (will this not refer us back to the being of Dasein as its ground?); and, if the being of Dasein is constituted by temporality, which is the origin of time, and time is the horizon of being, "la subordination de l'être du *Dasein* au sens de l'être en général n'est-elle pas, plus que jamais, ambiguë ?" (is the subordination of the being of Dasein to the meaning of being in general not more than ever ambiguous?). This is the best-supported reading in the Introduction. SZ 17 says that the analytic "ist aber nicht nur unvollständig, sondern zunächst auch *vorläufig*" (is not only incomplete but at first provisional) and demands its "Wiederholung auf der höheren und eigentlichen ontologischen Basis" (repetition on the higher and genuine ontological basis); it shows temporality as the meaning of Dasein's being and time as the "Horizont alles Seinsverständnisses" (horizon of all understanding of being, SZ 17). Henry's question is SZ's own at the close: "Kann die Frage ihre Antwort im Rückgang auf die *ursprüngliche Seinsverfassung* des Sein-verstehenden Daseins gewinnen?" (can the question find its answer by going back to the originary constitution of being of Dasein, which understands being?), and "Offenbart sich die Zeit selbst als Horizont des Seins?" (does time itself reveal itself as the horizon of being?; SZ 436–437). Henry reads SZ's circle as an ambiguity in SZ's thesis of subordination, where SZ states it as the open question of the unfinished work. The footnote claims that "the later development of the doctrine" confirms the subordination; that is a claim about writings I have not examined here (the *Letter on Humanism* of 1947 and the lectures of the 1930s); it is noted as an open thread. For this paragraph the verdict is reconstruction, accurate and acknowledged by SZ itself.

Henry says that "le merveilleux choc en retour du questionné sur le questionnant qui se fait jour dans la question de l'être" (the marvelous recoil of the questioned on the questioner that appears in the question of being) does not lift the basic difficulty immanent to it, the problem of "l'*homogénéité de l'être*", and that only a decisive answer to this problem could define the place and role of human reality in the question. The recoil is SZ's "Rück- oder Vorbezogenheit des Gefragten (Sein) auf das Fragen als Seinsmodus eines Seienden" (relatedness back or forward of what is asked about, being, on the questioning as a mode of being of a being, SZ 8); "merveilleux" is Henry's. The "homogeneity of being" is an extension of SZ's own problem of the unity of the meaning of being across its modes: SZ asks for a "Genealogie der verschiedenen möglichen Weisen von Sein" (genealogy of the different possible modes of being, SZ 11) and separates the categories of non-Dasein beings from the existentials of Dasein (SZ 44–46). Henry takes the separation as a question about whether being is said in the same sense of the ego and of beings. In SZ the answer is the difference of modes of being under one meaning to be found; in Henry it becomes the claim, at §7 ¶1, of an ultimate inhomogeneity.

### § 7. The problematic concerning the being of the ego interpreted as an originary and fundamental problematic

Henry says that the necessary insertion of the phenomenology of the ego in universal ontology can be questioned only if ontology gets its fundamental dimension from an elucidation of the central phenomenon of the ego, and that the precedence of the problematic of the ego does not mean that "un certain étant (par exemple celui qui dit « je »)" must be questioned first about its being; what is in question is "la solidarité du sens de l'être de la réalité humaine avec celui de l'être en général" (the solidarity of the meaning of being of human reality with that of being in general). It is "originaire et fondamentale" because the being of the ego "n'est pas homogène à « l'être en général »", not in the restricted sense of a distinct region but "en un sens ultime, quoiqu'encore incompréhensible pour nous" (in an ultimate sense, though still incomprehensible to us); the question is whether the primacy of the analytic of human reality is methodological or has "une signification ontologique ultime". SZ's priority is more than methodological and less than ultimate: the priority of Dasein is that of the being "primär zu Befragende" and "ontisch-ontologische Bedingung der Möglichkeit aller Ontologien" (SZ 13–14), and SZ declares that the exemplary being is chosen for the question's sake while the proof of its function awaits the analysis (SZ 8, 14). Henry's alternative is his own, as is the "inhomogeneity". What SZ contains in that neighborhood is the separation of existentials from categories (SZ 44–46), which Henry's claim would radicalize, from a distinction of modes of being under the question of one meaning into a break between the ego's being and being in general. The unit is a Radicalization of a SZ distinction whose function SZ gives differently.

Henry asks whether philosophy has ever understood why the problematic of the ego cogito arises in Descartes "en l'absence de tout contexte"; he denies that the meaning of the being of the ego remains undetermined in Cartesianism: "Très rapidement, au contraire, c'est comme *ens creatum* que l'être de l'ego est interprété, au même titre que celui de toute nature simple, [...] à la lumière des conceptions philosophiques et théologiques de la pensée médiévale, elle-même issue de l'ontologie grecque" (very quickly, on the contrary, it is as *ens creatum* that the being of the ego is interpreted, in the same way as that of every simple nature, in the light of the philosophical and theological conceptions of medieval thought, itself issuing from Greek ontology; n. 9, "Cf. *SZ*, 24"). Is this determination improper because a wrong conception of being in general is imposed, or "n'est-ce pas plutôt l'idée même d'une telle subordination qui est irrecevable ?" (is it not rather the very idea of such a subordination that is inadmissible?). The correction of the epigraph is SZ's own. SZ 24 says Descartes leaves the sum's meaning of being undetermined in the "radical" beginning, and SZ 24–25 goes on: "Für *Descartes* bleibt es jedoch nicht allein bei diesem Versäumnis und damit bei einer völligen ontologischen Unbestimmtheit der res cogitans" (for Descartes, however, it does not stay with this omission and so with a complete ontological indeterminacy of the res cogitans), he transfers medieval ontology to it, the res cogitans being determined as ens whose sense of being is fixed as ens creatum, and "Geschaffenheit" is "ein wesentliches Strukturmoment des antiken Seinsbegriffes" (an essential structural moment of the ancient concept of being). Henry's "n'est pas exact" does not correct SZ; it distinguishes the two sentences SZ already distinguishes. "Nature simple" is Descartes's (the *Regulae*) and is Henry's addition. What goes beyond SZ is the last question. SZ's remedy for the omission is precisely to determine the meaning of the being of the *sum* by the question of the meaning of being in general (SZ 13, 24, 46). Henry calls that subordination inadmissible. The epigraph, quoted as SZ's diagnosis, thus ends as the ground of an objection to SZ's remedy. The objection is not an exegesis, and it is the thesis of the book.

Henry asks how the ego can become a "phenomenon", whether it must submit to a horizon of visibility; whether transcendence is the condition of the being of the ego. The investigations show the need to answer in the negative: "le *mode* selon lequel l'ego *devient un phénomène* est quelque chose de si *fondamental* qu'il ne peut être soumis à aucune condition" (the mode in which the ego becomes a phenomenon is something so fundamental that it cannot be subjected to any condition). "Le problème de la philosophie est le problème de la vérité"; the problem of truth is more original than that of reason; "L'être de l'ego est la vérité", not the truth that "n'est possible que par la transcendance" (is possible only through transcendence), but "une vérité plus haute en origine, plus ancienne, et sans laquelle la transcendance elle-même ne serait pas" (a truth higher in origin, older, and without which transcendence itself would not be), the "vérité originaire". The formal thesis is SZ's: truth "im ursprünglichsten Sinne verstanden, gehört zur Grundverfassung des Daseins. Der Titel bedeutet ein Existenzial" (understood in the most original sense, belongs to the basic constitution of Dasein; the title means an existential, SZ 226), identified with "die Erschlossenheit des Daseins" (SZ 220–221), and "veritas transcendentalis" is the disclosure of being (SZ 38). Henry's "l'être de l'ego est la vérité" has the same form, the being of the being that is open is the truth. The content is the reverse. SZ's disclosedness is made of attunement, understanding and discourse, in the world, and rests, in the second division, on ecstatic temporality; it is the truth of transcendence. Henry's truth precedes transcendence and is its condition. This is a Radicalization of SZ's identification of truth with a way of being, turned against the content SZ gave it, and the unit shows the structure of the book's argument: it keeps SZ's identity of truth and being-of-the-questioner and removes the transcendence from which SZ derives it.

Henry asks whether it is not losing the proper sense of the essence to identify it with a real, determinate being. The essence is the ultimate condition of possibility of all existence, a ground in the ontological sense, not a particular existence even a privileged one; the first truth is not the truth of a singular content; "la condition de possibilité de l'expérience" in Kant is a "pure possibilité" (pure possibility) that is "vide", "strictement rien", since the problem of its being is never posed, a thing that "flotte librement" (floats freely) as "une hypothèse", and "subjectivité transcendantale" as its title is a "mystérieuse" affirmation "sans aucun fondement dans la doctrine" (without any ground in the doctrine). The criticism of Kant's silence on the being of the condition is SZ 24's. SZ says that Kant, "einer vorgängigen ontologischen Analytik der Subjektivität des Subjekts" lacking, fails in the question of time and the "I think", and the connection between them "in völliges Dunkel gehüllt" (wrapped in complete darkness). Henry extends the diagnosis to the concept of a "condition of possibility". SZ's reply to the worry about mere possibility is a different one: Dasein's possibility is its being (understanding is "Seinkönnen", potentiality-for-being, SZ 147–148), and "Höher als die Wirklichkeit steht die *Möglichkeit*" (higher than actuality stands possibility, SZ 38). Henry has no use for this possibility and asks for a real one.

Henry says that the reality of the essence is safeguarded by thinking the essence as being; "si l'être est identique au néant, ce n'est qu'au regard de l'étant que ce néant n'est « rien »", in itself it is a "néant réel"; "La possibilité ontologique est la réalité absolue"; but the grounding must prove its reality, and being "doit pouvoir *se montrer*. La méthode de l'ontologie est phénoménologique": what claims "le titre de « phénomène »" if not "ce qui ne se montre pas tout d'abord mais demeure le plus souvent caché, à savoir l'être lui-même" (what does not show itself at first but remains for the most part hidden, namely being itself); how can the condition of all manifestation become manifest? "« L'ontologie, dit Heidegger, n'est possible que comme phénoménologie »" (n. 10, SZ 35). The quotation and the argument that follows are SZ: "*Ontologie ist nur als Phänomenologie möglich*" (SZ 35–36), and the phenomenon in the eminent sense is what "sich zunächst und zumeist gerade *nicht* zeigt" (proximally and for the most part does not show itself) and is "das *Sein* des Seienden" (SZ 35). The reconstruction is faithful. The identity of being and nothing, the "néant réel" and "la possibilité ontologique est la réalité absolue" are Henry's own and, for the nothing, Heidegger's 1929 (see the discussion of §2 ¶7 above). "La possibilité ontologique est la réalité absolue" runs against SZ 38's ordering of possibility over actuality: SZ says that phenomenology's essential is to be grasped as possibility, which Henry's sentence contradicts by its words, though not by an argument against them. The rendering "tout d'abord" and "le plus souvent" splits SZ's one phrase "zunächst und zumeist", which names a structural mode of everyday proximity (SZ 16–17), into an order in time ("at first") followed by a statistical remainder; this prepares the reading, in ¶11, of the ego's revelation as "ni obtenue ni perdue" in a history. It is a rendering that already carries Henry's thesis.

Henry says that the thematic of phenomenological ontology is not the determinate content of a manifestation but "le « comment » de cette manifestation et de toute manifestation possible en général" (the "how" of this manifestation and of every possible manifestation in general): what makes a phenomenon "quelque chose qui est susceptible d'apparaître" (something capable of appearing). Being, the condition of all manifestation, cannot become a "phenomenon" in the sense of the singular content of a determinate manifestation, so what is the project of a phenomenological ontology? The "comment" is SZ's *Wie*: "Der Ausdruck »Phänomenologie« bedeutet primär einen *Methodenbegriff*. Er charakterisiert nicht das sachhaltige Was der Gegenstände der philosophischen Forschung, sondern das *Wie* dieser" (the expression "phenomenology" means primarily a concept of method; it characterizes not the factual what of the objects of philosophical research but their how, SZ 27; cf. SZ 28, 35). The re-inscription is that SZ's *Wie* is the mode of the investigation (the way of showing and demonstrating what is treated), while Henry's "comment" is the mode of the manifestation itself. The shift lets Henry ask what a "how" is when it is not the how of a content and later claim, in ¶8, that in one case the how is its own content. SZ keeps what and how apart (SZ 27), and the unit has already moved the how from method to being.

Henry asks whether the question of the reality of the ground is tied to the possibility of being becoming a phenomenon, or whether one should recognize a thought which conceives "l'œuvre originelle du dévoilement comme une dissimulation de ce à partir de quoi cette œuvre peut chaque fois s'accomplir" (the original work of unveiling as a dissimulation of that from which this work can each time be accomplished) and which, without being dialectical, "repose [...] sur une expérience effective dont le sens est de révéler la structure antinomique du fondement" (rests on an effective experience whose sense is to reveal the antinomic structure of the ground); the essence "est réelle, en tant qu'elle fonde la vérité", yet "n'est point elle-même la vérité, mais plutôt une non-vérité plus originelle" (is not itself truth but rather a more original non-truth) to which the phenomenon always refers. Ontology remains possible on a phenomenological basis; what is put in question, "d'une façon essentielle", is "la possibilité d'une connaissance absolue". SZ has a non-truth equiprimordial with truth, and it is Dasein's: "Das Dasein ist gleichursprünglich in der Wahrheit und Unwahrheit" (Dasein is equiprimordially in truth and untruth, SZ 223; cf. SZ 222), Unwahrheit being its coveredness through thrown falling. Henry moves that structure from Dasein to the ground and calls it "plus originelle", a non-truth prior to truth, which Heidegger's 1943 essay on the essence of truth does with concealment (outside P and, in this form, after SZ). The mediation is by the later Heidegger, if Henry's formulation derives from him, which this paragraph does not show. The unit is an Extension of SZ's untruth from Dasein to the ground, with a change of function: in SZ the untruth is part of Dasein's disclosedness, in Henry it is the reason the ground cannot appear.

Henry says that the ground can be understood as thoroughly "phénomène" and as truth only once a radical elucidation of the concept of phenomenon has led the problematic to the idea of "une révélation qui ne doit rien à l'œuvre de la transcendance" (a revelation that owes nothing to the work of transcendence); the elucidation will show that the determination of the phenomenon as what shows itself "dans l'horizon de lumière" (in the horizon of light) in which everything can become visible "reste en fait unilatérale", an insufficiency that has "commandé presque toute l'histoire de la pensée humaine" (governed almost the whole history of human thought). A new foundation arises when philosophy circumscribes an original phenomenon whose mode of revelation is irreducible to the "comment" of transcendent phenomena, a mode that "a aussi, et cela d'une façon paradoxale, une signification matérielle. *La révélation originaire est à elle-même son propre contenu. Le « comment » de cette révélation est un être réel*" (also, paradoxically, has a material meaning; the original revelation is its own content; the "how" of this revelation is a real being): this "manière d'être un phénomène" is called "ego". The charge that the concept of phenomenon is unilateral aims at SZ among others, and it has a basis in SZ. SZ's formal concept ("das Sich-an-ihm-selbst-Zeigende", SZ 28, 31) leaves open what shows itself and how, but its working-out is bound to seeing: the *logos* is "Sehenlassen" (letting-see, SZ 32–33), phenomenology is "von ihm selbst her sehen lassen" (letting be seen from itself, SZ 34), and the existential meaning of sight is the clearing of the Da (SZ 147). In this sense the objection is supported by the text: SZ's phenomenon is a showing within a seeing. Against SZ 27 the paragraph also abolishes the difference of what and how for one case, the ego, and thus uses the SZ distinction (the how) in order to exempt a being from it. That being has no counterpart in SZ, whose Dasein is the being that is open in a world, and the paragraph is Henry's thesis.

Henry says that the ground is what allows something "d'*être en se manifestant*", that it concerns the mode of manifestation, which refers, when manifestation means the work of transcendence, to a more original mode of revelation: "La transcendance repose sur l'immanence. La vérité originaire est le vrai fondement." The ground is itself a phenomenon in an irreducible sense, as an immanent revelation, which gives it its reality by giving it "le moment de la présence" (the moment of presence), a presence not subject to any horizon of presence and which is not the horizon's own never-present being; this is "celle de l'ego lui-même", and "La réalité de la possibilité ontologique est l'être de l'ego." The thesis is Henry's, and SZ has no counterpart except as a thing to be refused. "Ground" in SZ is accessible only as meaning: "»Grund« nur als Sinn zugänglich wird, und sei er selbst der Abgrund der Sinnlosigkeit" (ground becomes accessible only as meaning, even if it be itself the abyss of meaninglessness, SZ 152), and the meaning of being is never to be set against beings or against being as "tragenden Grund" (SZ 152). Henry offers a ground accessible as revelation without meaning, in immanence. The "moment de la présence" lies in the vocabulary that SZ 25–26 attributes to the Greek understanding of being as presence. The paragraph states the thesis whose first form is the epigraph's question, and SZ's text stands against it at SZ 152 as a text that refuses any ground not mediated by understanding.

Henry says that if the ground is itself a phenomenon, the way of access to it is the ground itself; what is maintained in this identity is "la vie elle-même", the transcendental life of the absolute ego; the ground is neither light nor the shining thing but "une révélation *immanente* qui est une présence à soi-même, quoiqu'une telle présence demeure « invisible »", an inner experience with "une forme monadique", in which "s'enracine l'ipséité de l'ego"; such an experience "existe par elle-même, sans aucun contexte, sans le support d'aucun être extérieur et « réel »", it is "l'existence même, celle qu'il convient de penser sous le titre de « réalité humaine »"; it owes nothing to transcendence, precedes and makes it possible: "Plus originaire que la vérité de l'être est la vérité de l'homme." Under the title of "existence" and "human reality" the paragraph contradicts SZ at its first definition. "Zum Dasein gehört aber wesenhaft: Sein in einer Welt" (it belongs essentially to Dasein: being in a world, SZ 13); Dasein's understanding of being concerns "gleichursprünglich" the world and the being of the beings within it (SZ 13); and *Existenz* in SZ is a relation to possibilities of the self (SZ 12–13), not an inner self-presence. The last sentence separates what SZ unites: "the truth of man" and "the truth of being" are in SZ one, since the veritas transcendentalis is the disclosure of being and is the disclosedness of Dasein (SZ 38, 220–221). Henry's claim that the first is "plus originaire" is a Radicalization that no longer builds on SZ's text but replaces it, and the replacement keeps SZ's titles (existence, truth, Dasein's priority) for a structure SZ excludes.

Henry says that the interpretation of the ground as immanent revelation leads to rethink the link between ontology and phenomenology; the phenomenological treatment is indeterminate as long as the concept of phenomenon has not been fixed, and dangerous if the problematic advances under a unilateral conception. Ontology understands its task as "élucidation", "rendre manifeste", "porter dans la lumière"; what needs elucidation is determined as "ce qui ne se montre pas tout d'abord mais demeure le plus souvent « caché »" (n. 11, SZ 35). "En fait, *le travail méthodologique de la phénoménologie est d'ores et déjà interprété à la lumière d'une philosophie de la transcendance*." The ultimate meaning of phenomenology is "la découverte d'un « phénomène » qui est le fondement lui-même"; this discovery is not a "mise à jour" of something "primitivement « caché »", since the ground is present to itself in an inner experience that can be neither "obtenue" nor "perdue"; "l'ego n'a point à se manifester dans le milieu de l'être transcendant, un jour ou l'autre, tôt ou tard, au cours d'une histoire, individuelle ou universelle, ou au sein du progrès de la philosophie". The quotation of SZ 35 repeats the split rendering of ¶5 and bears the argument of this paragraph: a hidden-first, shown-later reading of "zunächst und zumeist" gives Henry the "history" and the "progress" he then denies to the ego. SZ's own sense is the everyday mode, which allows no developmental reading; the denial is directed at something SZ does not hold. The claim that phenomenology is already interpreted in the light of a philosophy of transcendence has support in SZ: phenomenology is the "Zugangsart" to what is to be ontology's theme and the counter-concept of phenomenon is *Verdecktheit* (SZ 35–36), so that a phenomenology of the ground shows what was covered. The dispute is whether that is a limitation of method or only one mode of manifestation among others. The text of SZ gives Henry the premise and not the conclusion.

Henry asks whether phenomenology can take on this ultimate ontological meaning only by losing all methodological interest: why should the ground undergo a method of clarifying if it reveals itself by principle, and where is the progress of a research on what has always been revealed? The aim of the work is to show "qu'il existe une *connaissance absolue* et que celle-ci n'est pas solidaire d'un progrès quelconque", "le milieu même de l'existence, l'essence de la vie"; "La phénoménologie est plutôt une critique de toute révélation". The paragraph is independent of SZ. It poses the worry SZ poses of its own maxim: why put into the title of research a "Selbstverständlichkeit" (self-evidence)? (SZ 28). SZ's answer is that the self-evident is what is covered and needs demonstration (SZ 4, 35–36); Henry's is that it is revealed in life without demonstration, so that philosophy is a critique of revelations and not a bringing to light. Both answers use the vocabulary of self-evidence for opposite ends.

Henry says that the task of phenomenology is complex: the question of the phenomenological treatment of the ground "n'est autre que celui des rapports de la philosophie et de la vie", leading to the possibility of "une « science absolument subjective »", "dont Husserl se crut le possesseur", and to the idea that "si la philosophie est seconde par rapport à la vie, il doit cependant exister un mode de philosopher qui ne porte point préjudice à l'essence". SZ is not cited and yet occupies the paragraph's central word. SZ 46 observes of the philosophies of "Leben" that "»Leben« selbst nicht als eine Seinsart ontologisch zum Problem wird" (life itself does not become an ontological problem as a mode of being), calls this their "grundsätzlicher Mangel" (basic defect), counts Dilthey and Bergson among them, and declines to use "Leben" and "Mensch" as titles for Dasein. Henry's key term for the ground is the one SZ names as unquestioned in the tradition nearest to him, and the book will answer by an ontology of life (Sections III–IV). Whether that answer meets SZ 46 or restates the "Mangel" with an ontology attached is a question the later divisions can decide.

Henry says that the mode of philosophizing that does not harm the essence is reached only by an effective overcoming of intuitionism, which is not achieved by a reflection on the transcendental conditions of presence in intuition (liberation of the universal phenomenological horizon) nor by integrating a philosophy of intuition into a transcendental philosophy of constitution; both remain "exclusivement conditionnée[s] par les préoccupations d'une problématique de l'objet" (exclusively conditioned by the concerns of a problematic of the object), unable to cross the limits of the Kant who "donna cette signification ontologique que nous admirons tant". The "critique du paralogisme de la psychologie rationnelle" has been chosen as the object of "une destruction ontologique" showing the absence of an ontology of subjectivity; the cogito of Descartes and the philosophy of existence of Kierkegaard "ne jouent pas [...] le rôle d'un commencement", they are moments when "se « manifeste » paradoxalement" a current usually subterranean (n. 12 announces that the analyses could not find place in the book). Henry's method here is SZ's: "Destruktion" of a transmitted ontology down to its sources (SZ 22–23), proceeding at "grundsätzlich entscheidenden Stationen" (fundamentally decisive stations, SZ 23), and with Kant as the first station for the problem of time and Descartes as the second (SZ 23–24). SZ itself treats the paralogisms, as a proof that the problematic of the self is without ontological ground "von Descartes' *res cogitans* bis zu Hegels Begriff des Geistes" (from Descartes's res cogitans to Hegel's concept of spirit, SZ 321; cf. SZ 318). Henry's announced destruction would therefore be a repetition at a point where SZ has made its own, and the footnote shows he did not carry it through. The Kierkegaard remark can be read against SZ 235 n. 59, which grants Kierkegaard the existentiell grasp of the problem of existence and denies him the existential problematic; whether Henry follows that distinction is not stated here.

Henry says that these reasons become intelligible to a thought that "met en cause le primat ontologique de la transcendance": "La lumière universelle n'est pas le séjour de tous les phénomènes. L'« invisible » est le mode d'une révélation positive et, à vrai dire, fondamentale", the ambiguity of a philosophy of Night dissolves before the reflection that distinguishes, from the obscurity that is "le *partage* de la transcendance", the "premier frémissement intérieur du savoir" (first inner trembling of knowing) where knowing "se *révèle* d'abord à lui-même"; "Toute manifestation est par principe inadéquate. Mais là où il n'y a pas de transcendance, il n'y a pas non plus de finitude." The thesis ties finitude to transcendence, as in the Kantbuch, and denies SZ's account of finitude as that of originary temporality, which does not depend on a transcendence of the object: "Die ursprüngliche Zeit ist endlich" (SZ 331), grounded in the future of being-toward-death. Henry's sentence is therefore a thesis against SZ about what finitude is, stated without reference to it. "Philosophy of Night" is a nod to Romantic and Heideggerian night-language (SZ's night is not an issue; anxiety's "Nichts und Nirgends", SZ 186–187, is its nearest relative), and its dissolution rests on the "invisible", a term the book will carry.

Henry says that the sphere of absolute knowledge is rigorously defined and that the ontological work which leads to it is what allows reflection to pose the problem of self-knowledge on a correct base; as long as philosophy is prisoner of the idea of a transcendent horizon, "le rapport de l'ego à lui-même" can be understood only as "un cas particulier du rapport transcendantal de l'être-au-monde"; once "les interprétations fallacieuses de l'être qui commandent, le plus souvent, la compréhension existentielle de soi-même" are set aside, the problem of the being of the ego is on the ontological plane legitimately subordinated to that of the meaning of being; "La relation à soi ne peut se produire que dans l'errance." The refutation of the transcendence of the ego plays the role of "une démonstration par l'absurde" for the ontological theses; "le concept d'aliénation perd toute signification ontologique" once the immanence of the ego is shown, and understanding the existential meaning of human alienation requires "une distinction rigoureuse entre le plan de l'existence et celui de l'ontologie", which prepares the essence of freedom (n. 12). The distinction between the plane of existence and that of ontology is SZ's, existentiell and existential (SZ 12–13), and the claim that self-relation takes place in errancy and that the existential understanding of oneself is commanded by fallacious interpretations of being reconstruct SZ's *Verfallen*, which "entfremdet" (estranges) Dasein from its ownmost potentiality ("Entfremdung, die dem Dasein seine Eigentlichkeit [...] verschließt", estrangement that closes off its authenticity, SZ 178). Henry's refusal that alienation has an ontological sense also agrees with SZ, for which falling is a mode of being of Dasein, not a loss of an ontological ground. "Errance" is not SZ's word; it is Heidegger's of 1943. In this unit Henry grants that on the ontological plane the problem of the ego's being is legitimately subordinated to that of the meaning of being, which is SZ's thesis. The book's own thesis (¶¶1–15) is that the subordination is inadmissible, and he does not say how the two statements are to be held together. The best reading is that he means by "le plan ontologique" the plane of transcendence, which his thesis then leaves behind.

Henry says that, by tearing existence from the absolute milieu of exteriority, the investigations draw attention to the "subjective" character of this existence and invite us to ask whether it is not suitable "aujourd'hui, de redonner un sens au concept de « vie intérieure »" (today, to give back a meaning to the concept of "inner life"). The closing sentence names the book's adversary in Heidegger's terms and its program in terms SZ avoids. "Milieu absolu de l'extériorité" is Henry's name for the horizon, the world of SZ's *In-der-Welt-sein* read as exteriority (SZ's analysis denies the inside-outside scheme for Dasein, the thesis that the book will have to meet in Section I). "Vie intérieure" is a title SZ declines in the list of "Leben", "Seele", "Subjekt", "Bewußtsein", "Geist" and "Person" (SZ 22, 46). Henry's "existence" is, at the end of the Introduction, a self-presence in which a world does not take part; SZ's *Existenz* is the relation to possibilities of the self (SZ 12–13). The conclusion shows where the work of the Introduction ends: with SZ's vocabulary in service of a program that SZ's first sections exclude.

Henry's Introduction takes from SZ five load-bearing arguments. It reconstructs the diagnosis of Descartes at SZ 24–25 and the strictures on the titles of the subject at SZ 22 and 46 with fidelity, and it extends the argument from Kant and Descartes to Husserl. It reconstructs SZ 13 on the priority of Dasein and SZ 15 on the question of being as a radicalization of the pre-ontological understanding; it reconstructs the definition of ontology as phenomenology at SZ 35, and its self-critical figure at SZ 36. Where it follows, it follows well and mostly without citation, and its reading of SZ's circle and of the provisional character of the analytic (§6 ¶11) is the best-supported reading in the division, since SZ's last pages ask Henry's question. The reading fails in four places. It drops *Erfragtes*, so that the meaning of being leaves the formal structure of the question, and it puts "human reality", an eidetically defined title SZ avoids, in the place of Dasein. It relocates the opening of the horizon from Dasein's disclosedness and ecstatic time to being, with a vocabulary of truth, unveiling and the nothing that comes from Heidegger's 1929 writings (*Vom Wesen des Grundes*, *Was ist Metaphysik?*) and not from SZ, though Commentary R and the book present it as SZ. It adopts being as presence, the determination SZ 25–26 traces to the Greeks as a temporal interpretation never grounded, and it identifies being with the Nothing, which SZ does not. And it keeps SZ's titles (existence, truth, priority of the questioner) for a structure SZ's first pages exclude, an existence "sans aucun contexte" against SZ 13's being-in-a-world, and a truth "plus originaire" than transcendence against SZ 226's identification of truth with disclosedness. These are not mistakes in reading, since Henry's wording makes plain that the book is a counter-position; but they are not exegesis either, and the Introduction says too little about where the line falls. The verdict is local to this division. The epigraph says the book begins from SZ; the remainder says that it begins from SZ's diagnosis and from Heidegger's 1929 doctrine of being, and turns both against the analytic of Dasein.

## Section I. Elucidation of the concept of phenomenon: ontological monism

### § 8. The elucidation of the essence of the phenomenon, central task of phenomenology

Henry defines phenomenology as "la science des phénomènes" (the science of phenomena), a description "antérieure à toute théorie et indépendante de toute présupposition" (prior to every theory and independent of every presupposition) of whatever offers itself as existent, which brackets hypotheses and unifying principles, takes scientific theories as data, and thereby protects science from becoming "une nouvelle métaphysique" by inserting its edifices "dans le contexte de l'expérience humaine" (into the context of human experience). The definition is Husserl's. SZ's nearest statement shares its prohibitive core: "Deskription" means "Fernhaltung alles nichtausweisenden Bestimmens" (keeping away every determination that does not demonstrate itself, SZ 35), the maxim being "entgegen allen freischwebenden Konstruktionen" (against all free-floating constructions, SZ 28). Two differences matter for what follows. SZ does not claim to be independent of every presupposition; its fundamental question presupposes, in a sense it explains, an understanding of being (SZ 7–8). And SZ has no mission to defend the sciences; it finds them naive in their approach to their domains (SZ 9). Henry's opening definition is therefore a Husserlian premise that SZ's text qualifies on its first point, and Henry reverses the point himself at ¶10.

Henry says that phenomenology is "positivist" in Husserl's sense, recognizing as a source of right for knowledge "non pas uniquement la vision empirique, mais la vision en général, en tant que conscience donatrice originaire sous toutes ses formes" (not only empirical vision but vision in general, as originary giving consciousness in all its forms; n. 1, *Ideen I* 66), so that a phenomenological statement can be an "absolute beginning", "absolue est en effet l'apparence à laquelle il renvoie, en tant justement qu'elle est une apparence" (the appearance to which it refers is absolute, precisely as appearance); Scheler adds that absolute being is knowable "d'une façon évidente et adéquate" (evidently and adequately). The paragraph is Husserl and Scheler, and its relation to SZ is that SZ also generalizes seeing. "Die Tradition der Philosophie ist aber von Anfang an primär am »Sehen« als Zugangsart zu Seiendem *und zu Sein* orientiert. Um den Zusammenhang mit ihr zu wahren, kann man Sicht und Sehen so weit formalisieren, daß damit ein universaler Terminus gewonnen wird, der jeden Zugang zu Seiendem und zu Sein als Zugang überhaupt charakterisiert" (the tradition of philosophy has from the start been oriented primarily toward "seeing" as the mode of access to beings and to being; to keep the connection with it, sight and seeing can be formalized so far that a universal term is gained, characterizing every access to beings and to being, SZ 147). SZ's "vision in general" is thus a formalization of the tradition's, which it then derives from understanding. Henry takes the Husserlian principle at face value, and the comparison with SZ 147 will become the ground of his later charge that the concept of phenomenon is unilateral (¶16).

Henry says that a philosophy resting on the given does not evade the problem of non-truth but replaces error with illusion: the appearance whose "contenu" is always "vrai" and whose illusion results from the transport of a *Sachverhalt* (state of affairs) into another layer of being than its own; error is a case of illusion, an inadequate relation between a *Sachverhalt* thought in judgment and the corresponding one present in intuition (n. 4, Scheler); it suffices "de laisser apparaître l'apparence telle qu'elle apparaît" (to let the appearance appear as it appears). The account is Scheler's and Husserl's. SZ has a convergent point reached by another route: for the Greeks perceiving "ist immer wahr" (is always true) with respect to its proper objects ("Sehen entdeckt immer Farben", seeing always discovers colors, SZ 33), and falsity arises only where something is addressed *as* something, a covering-over (SZ 33). Henry's "the content of the appearance is always true" and SZ's "perceiving is always true" meet in the claim that error belongs to a higher-level relation. The grounds differ (a layering of being for Henry, the structure of letting-see for SZ), and Henry does not use SZ's account.

Henry concludes that the absolute significance of phenomenology rests on "la présence de la chose, c'est-à-dire sur son apparence" (the presence of the thing, that is, on its appearance); read in a philosophy of consciousness it becomes a "dogmatisme de l'intentionnalité" giving the ontological argument a real ground, yet the absolute is soon relative, since every appearance is surrounded by "une zone d'ombre" (a zone of shadow), the given "renvoie à quelque chose qui n'est pas là" (refers to something not there); phenomenology as method elucidates what is "impliqué" (implied, n. 5, MC 41), but the given must sacrifice its presence to the process, so "il n'y a pas de totalité intuitive"; and the finitude by which an appearance always demands elucidation may refer to "une finitude plus essentielle" by which elucidation is never possible. Presence, appearance and phenomenon are set equal in the first sentence, and SZ spends three pages separating the last two. For SZ presence (*Anwesenheit*) is the Greek temporal determination of being (SZ 25–26), and "Erscheinung" is a "Sich-nicht-zeigen", a reporting of something that does not show itself by something that does (SZ 29). The identification of presence with appearance is Henry's; the question with which the paragraph ends, whether elucidation is possible at all, is where his own thesis will enter.

Henry says that the law prescribing the relation of the appearance to a process of explicitation is that of the essence, which positivism leaves unthought: "Qu'est-ce donc [...] qui rend possibles des phénomènes au sens du positivisme, qu'est-ce qui fonde la présence pour nous de ce qui apparaît, sinon l'acte même d'apparaître, l'essence du phénomène et de la présence en tant que telle ?" (what makes phenomena in the sense of positivism possible, what grounds the presence for us of what appears, if not the very act of appearing, the essence of the phenomenon and of presence as such?). The natural consciousness laments the temporal fate of being while the thought that cares for the essence understands the law which commands it; the content that appears does not contain the "raison" that posits it in appearance or snatches it from existence. The question reconstructs, and re-inscribes, SZ 35–36. SZ asks what "fordert, Phänomen zu werden" (demands to become phenomenon) and answers: not this or that being but its being, which proximally and for the most part does not show itself. Henry's answer is the "acte d'apparaître". Henry does not keep SZ's central distinction: "Phänomene sind demnach *nie* Erscheinungen" (phenomena are never appearances, SZ 30), and "Das Sichzeigen ist kein beliebiges noch gar so etwas wie Erscheinen" (the showing-itself is neither arbitrary nor anything like appearing, SZ 36). If "apparaître" is *Erscheinen*, SZ says it is founded on a *Sichzeigen* and cannot ground it. If it is *Sichzeigen*, it is SZ's phenomenon and not its ground. "Apparence" and "apparaître" cover for Henry what SZ takes pains to keep apart (*Erscheinung*, *Erscheinen*, *Schein*), and SZ calls a definition of phenomenon through an unclear appearance a turning of everything "auf den Kopf" (upside down, SZ 30). The act of appearing is Henry's term, and the gap is not neutral.

Henry says that the fundamental dependence of what appears on the act of appearing makes the latter the theme of the problematic: "la présence de ce qui est présent", the essence on the ground of which all that is given can be there for us, "la simple manifestation" in Hegel's phrase about the religions of light. The thought reaches it by going beyond the singular existent to "l'être de cet existant, c'est-à-dire à l'acte d'apparaître comme tel", phenomenology being "la science des phénomènes *dans leur réalité*", with the essence of the phenomenon as its object; the phenomenological reduction "est une avec la réduction éidétique" and frees the essence which cannot be reduced. The "vérité transcendantale" is "la vérité même, identifiée avec l'être"; "L'être et la vérité sont contemporains, dit Heidegger" (n. 6, "Cf. *SZ*, 230"), which means that appearing confers being on everything and that truth is the act of appearing considered in and for itself; this truth is "la Forme". SZ 230 reads "Sein und Wahrheit »sind« gleichursprünglich" (being and truth "are" equiprimordial), after "Sein – nicht Seiendes – »gibt es« nur, sofern Wahrheit ist. Und sie *ist* nur, sofern und solange Dasein ist" (being, not beings, "is given" only insofar as there is truth, and truth is only insofar and as long as Dasein is). The quotation is accurate in its reference and shifted in three ways. "Contemporains" renders *gleichursprünglich* (equiprimordial, of equal origin) as simultaneity, and the French gives the sentence a temporal reading that the German does not carry. The gloss turns an equiprimordiality of two items into an identity of being, truth and appearing, which SZ does not assert. And it omits the clause that makes the statement a thesis about Dasein: truth in SZ is the disclosedness of Dasein (SZ 220–221) and is "nur, sofern und solange Dasein ist". "The Form" and the "universal and omnipresent essence of every phenomenon" are Henry's. The "dépassement de l'existant vers l'être" is the transcendence of SZ 38, to which SZ attaches the return to Dasein ("zurückschlägt"), and Henry leaves that return out here.

Henry says that error, illusion and truth are co-determined by a common ground: "C'est la vérité absolue qui permet à l'illusion de se manifester et la fonde ainsi dans son *être*", the essence being "l'acte d'apparaître dont a besoin même l'apparence pour être une simple apparence" (n. 7, H 129). Hegel's question whether true knowledge can prove itself against untrue knowledge is answered by the "immanence du savoir absolu au sein du savoir non vrai": untrue knowledge appears, and its being a knowing is what makes it true in an absolute sense; "le savoir vrai n'est vrai qu'en un sens second, car, comme le soleil luit sur les justes et sur les injustes, la vérité [...] ne fait pas de distinction". SZ makes the structural claim: "Erscheinung und Schein sind selbst in verschiedener Weise im Phänomen fundiert" (appearance and semblance are themselves founded in different ways in the phenomenon, SZ 31), and "Wieviel Schein jedoch, soviel »Sein«" (as much semblance, so much "being", SZ 36). Henry's claim that illusion is founded in the showing that it distorts reconstructs it. The step beyond is the equalization. In SZ semblance is a privative modification of the phenomenon (SZ 29–30), untruth is a covering that Dasein falls into (SZ 222–223, "gleichursprünglich in der Wahrheit und Unwahrheit"), and neither is as true as truth in any sense of "absolute". Henry's equalization of true and untrue knowledge "au regard du savoir absolu" follows the Hegelian text that Heidegger's *Holzwege* essay reads (the subject of the companion run on GA 5), and SZ's text does not support it.

Henry says that phenomenology is "la science de l'essence du phénomène" and that "C'est parce qu'il comprend l'être comme l'essence du phénomène que Heidegger peut dire que la phénoménologie est la science de l'être et, comme telle, l'ontologie"; a "phénoménologie première" (first phenomenology) with the ultimate problems is distinguished from a "phénoménologie seconde" (second) clarifying the meaning of being in the regions, and the question is posed whether the phenomenology of the ego and of time belongs to the first, in which case ego and time "appartiendraient [...] à sa structure interne et entreraient ainsi, à titre d'éléments constitutifs, dans la définition immanente de la vérité absolue" (belong to its internal structure and enter as constitutive elements into the immanent definition of absolute truth). The reason attributed to Heidegger inverts SZ's. SZ says that the phenomenon in the phenomenological sense "meint als das Sichzeigende das Sein des Seienden" (means, as what shows itself, the being of beings, SZ 35–36): the phenomenon is being, as what shows itself. Henry says that being is the essence of the phenomenon, as what makes showing possible. SZ also does not call phenomenology "science of being"; it says the title "nennt weder den Gegenstand ihrer Forschungen, noch charakterisiert [...] deren Sachhaltigkeit" (names neither the object of its research nor characterizes its content, SZ 35) and calls it the "Zugangsart" (mode of access) to what ontology is to treat, so that "Ontologie ist nur als Phänomenologie möglich" (SZ 35–36). The closing hypothesis is a faithful extension of SZ's architecture. SZ's two poles, Dasein and time, are the questioned being and the horizon of the question; Henry asks whether they belong to the inner structure of the essence. The extension is made in Henry's language, and what SZ puts in a relation of question and horizon, he turns into one of membership in an essence.

Henry says that the object of first phenomenology "ne saurait lui être extérieur", that it grounds itself on it and finds in it its principle, so that phenomenology is the "comment" and what it treats is the "comment"; "« Ontologie et phénoménologie ne sont pas, dit Heidegger, deux disciplines [...] Les deux titres caractérisent la philosophie même d'après l'objet et le mode de traitement » (n. 9, SZ 38). Mais, dans le cas qui nous occupe, l'objet est le mode de traitement lui-même." Phenomenology is the means of bringing near the essence of presence, "l'absolu en tant qu'il est la Parousie", but the means is the absolute itself. The quotation is accurate (SZ 38: "Die beiden Titel charakterisieren die Philosophie selbst nach Gegenstand und Behandlungsart"). What follows is a departure from SZ's sentence: SZ keeps two titles for object (being) and manner of treatment (phenomenology), though they are one philosophy, and says they are not two disciplines. Henry's "dans le cas qui nous occupe" collapses object into manner of treatment, which is the thesis of the book. "Mode de traitement" translates *Behandlungsart*, a mode of investigating (the *Wie* of SZ 27), and Henry then reads it as the manner of the reality's manifestation, as in the Introduction §7 ¶6. "Parousie" restates in the vocabulary of the *Holzwege* essay what SZ 25 reports of Greek ontology, that being is understood as *parousia*, which means "Anwesenheit" (presence); the term is used here as the name of the absolute, not as SZ's object of critique.

Henry says that phenomenology applied to the essence of the phenomenon "se meut dans un cercle", "le signe de son caractère absolu", which does not mean it is without presupposition: "Elle admet au contraire une présupposition fondamentale en tant que cette présupposition est le fondement lui-même, l'absolu. [...] La phénoménologie est son propre objet." SZ treats the circle at SZ 7–8 and 152–153, and Henry's use of it goes against the later passage's sense. In SZ the circle is no proof of an absolute but "der Ausdruck der existenzialen *Vor-struktur* des Daseins selbst" (the expression of the existential fore-structure of Dasein itself, SZ 153): interpretation moves in a "Vorhabe", "Vorsicht" and "Vorgriff" that Dasein already has (SZ 150), and "Das Entscheidende ist nicht, aus dem Zirkel heraus-, sondern in ihn nach der rechten Weise hineinzukommen" (what is decisive is not to get out of the circle but to get into it in the right way, SZ 153). SZ warns against characterizing Dasein with the circle, since "Zirkel" ontologically belongs to a mode of being of presence-at-hand (SZ 153). Henry's circle is a self-grounding of the absolute and its presupposition is the ground itself; SZ's is a fore-structure of understanding that grounds nothing. The paragraph also reverses the Prelude's demand that a fundamental problematic "ne tire ses connaissances que d'elle-même": the fundamental presupposition is now the ground, and independence is gained by making the ground the presupposition. This is a Re-inscription of the circle that SZ's own text warns against, and the unit is where Henry's use of SZ's term parts from its sense.

Henry says that the ultimate problems of phenomenology concern its reflection on itself and its ground, and that the sense of phenomenology depends on the nature of the ground. The "comment" of the approach is subordinated to the "Comment" of the reality approached, "réalité qui est le « Comment » lui-même"; the manner in which this reality comes toward us must command the way we welcome it, and "L'œil par lequel l'absolu nous regarde est le même que celui par lequel nous regardons l'absolu" (the eye by which the absolute looks at us is the same as that by which we look at the absolute). The saying is Eckhart's, which the book takes up in Section III (§39), and it has no SZ counterpart. SZ's sight is the clearing of the Da, the way Dasein is open (SZ 147), and the question "Quelle est la nature de la vision ?" is where Henry will quarrel with SZ's own conception of sight at SZ 147: sight in SZ is Dasein's openness, not the absolute's, and the reciprocity of eyes belongs to a mystical tradition that SZ does not use.

Henry says that the understanding of the link between ontology and phenomenology stays indeterminate until thought reaches the inside of the structure of the essence; the project of elucidation "demeure incertain à l'égard de lui-même, de son sens et de son fondement"; the objection of the circle does not stop the work, "à moins que ce ne soit une volonté de l'essence de refuser cette transparence finale et de se maintenir définitivement dans le mystère"; the essence "n'est certes pas étrangère à la conscience naturelle", to which, "en tant que celle-ci est une conscience, un être-là, le savoir absolu lui est immanent"; but would the thematic grasp, "le savoir du savoir absolu", consist in representing the essence? That depends on whether the essence consists in representation. The structure is SZ's pre-ontological understanding in Hegelian dress: Dasein "hat" understanding of being without an ontological concept (SZ 12–13), so that the question of being is the radicalization of what Dasein already is. Henry's natural consciousness stands for Dasein, "le savoir absolu lui est immanent" stands for pre-ontological understanding, and the terminology comes from Hegel through Heidegger's *Holzwege* essay (H 136, 146, treated in the GA 5 run). The re-inscription changes the sense of *immanent*: in SZ pre-ontological understanding is a projection that Dasein is in a world (SZ 148), in Henry it will become the immanence of a self-revelation. The question whether the grasp of the essence is representing is Henry's, set against the *Holzwege* essay's account of representing.

Henry quotes Husserl: "Dans la sphère du psychique, il n'y a pas de différence entre être et apparence" (n. 10, *Logos* 1913, via Scheler), and says that this has been taken to ground the absolute character of phenomenology of immanence but stays vague until "le fait d'apparaître" is defined; the fact of not appearing is as indeterminate as pure manifestation and may have only a negative sense relative to a "conception unilatérale" of the essence; pursuing the determination of the essence may bring out "une Forme, un Comment plus fondamental dont la loi confère une présence, quoique d'un autre ordre, à ce qui était primitivement pensé comme « n'apparaissant pas »". SZ's relevant sentence is the one that says that nothing stands behind the phenomena of phenomenology: "»Hinter« den Phänomenen der Phänomenologie steht wesenhaft nichts anderes, wohl aber kann das, was Phänomen werden soll, verborgen sein" (behind the phenomena of phenomenology there essentially stands nothing else, but what is to become phenomenon can be concealed, SZ 36). Henry's suggestion that not-appearing may be only relative to a one-sided concept agrees with SZ in direction: SZ's concealment is a counter-concept of the phenomenon, a *Verdecktheit* with causes (SZ 36). The step to a "Forme plus fondamentale" that gives a presence "d'un autre ordre" to the not-appearing is the invisible of Section III, and SZ has no counterpart. The paragraph is a preparation, and it runs against SZ at the one place where SZ says there is nothing behind.

Henry says that the determination of the essence must provide the ontological frame for discussing the relation of the essence to the existent that finds its ground in it, and decide whether "le dépassement du positivisme est, au point de vue ontologique, définitif et sans appel" (the overcoming of positivism is, ontologically, final and without appeal), whether "la transgression de l'existant s'opère sans retour", and whether the essence, so acquired, can close on itself, "s'abstraire de la détermination ontique" and keep its absoluteness in its autonomy. This is the question SZ answers with a return: universal phenomenological ontology starts from the hermeneutic of Dasein and the analytic of existence "dort festgemacht, woraus es *entspringt* und wohin es *zurückschlägt*" (made fast at the point where it springs from and to which it strikes back, SZ 38), and the ontological analytic is ontically rooted (SZ 13). Henry's "sans retour" and "s'abstraire de la détermination ontique" name the alternative that SZ excludes; he does not say so here, and he treats the question as open, which for SZ it is not. The paragraph is a Contestation in the form of a question.

Henry says that only the determination of the internal structure of the essence can delimit the field of the ultimate problems and tell whether the phenomenology of the ego belongs to it. The paragraph is a transition and independent of SZ.

Henry concludes that the determination of the essence of the phenomenon is the central task of phenomenology, urgent because philosophy has always posed and solved its problems "sur le fondement d'une conception inexplicitée du phénomène", and that the elucidation will show that when the essence was "enfin" made the theme of an explicit problematic, it only ratified, "en les portant à l'absolu, les présuppositions ontologiques qui ont [...] guidé mais aussi, et bien plutôt, égaré la recherche et la pensée philosophiques". The claim of an unexplicated concept of the phenomenon does not hold for SZ, which gives its explication in §7: the concept of phenomenon with its distinctions of appearance and semblance (SZ 28–31), the concept of logos (SZ 32–34), and the preliminary concept of phenomenology (SZ 34–39). Henry's "enfin" and "portant à l'absolu" refers, on the evidence of the sentence, to the moment where the essence became explicit, which in the book is the Hegelian and Heideggerian one (Section II, §§17–21). It follows that the charge here includes SZ's explication among the ratifications, and that it will be argued, not read from SZ. SZ's own etymology of *phainomenon* from *phôs* (light), "das, worin etwas offenbar, an ihm selbst sichtbar werden kann" (that in which something can become manifest, visible in itself, SZ 28–29), gives the charge a ground in the text.

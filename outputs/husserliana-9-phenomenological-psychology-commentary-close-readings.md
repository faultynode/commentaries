# Husserl, *Phänomenologische Psychologie*: close readings of key passages

These readings take up the passages approved in the [key-passage list](../runs/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary-key-passages.md) and read them for their wording. Each heading links to the block of the [main commentary](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md) that it extends. The German is quoted from the Husserliana edition (Hua IX, ed. Walter Biemel) in `faultynode/sources` (`sources/husserl/husserl-hua-9-phaanomenologische-psychologie-vorlesungen-sommersemester-1925.md`). That file holds the whole volume: the lectures of summer 1925, the drafts of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* article, the Amsterdam Lectures and thirty-two Beilagen (appendices). The commentary cites § numbers and headings only. Headings here add the Hua IX page, taken from the page markers in the file. The markers are not one per page, so a page can be off by one.

All English renderings of Husserl, and of the Latin and Greek he quotes, are working renderings. They stay close to the German so that its structure shows, and they are not taken from Scanlon's translation, which the corpus holds for the lectures only and which is used here for cross-checks.

The file is on the corpus's list of texts to check before quoting, and five cautions affect more than one reading. First, italics survive and can be argued from, but Husserl's spaced type is almost entirely lost, so the absence of emphasis is no evidence. Second, in four stretches of the Systematic Part the print's margin line numbers came through as page markers (`<!-- 5 -->` to `<!-- 35 -->`). They are not pages, and quotations here omit them. Third, footnote labels repeat: in the Introduction several notes on one page all carry the same label, and in the Encyclopaedia Britannica drafts two notes are defined under each of two labels. A note is identified here by its line, and its attachment is inferred from the order of markers and definitions. Fourth, at the opening of §37 the body text runs on inside a footnote definition; no words seem lost. Fifth, at §37 the file reads "philosophically-transcendent" (*philosophisch-transzendente*) where the English translation has "transcendental"; no reading argues from that word. Each reading says where any of this touches its passage, and nothing is filled in from memory.

## §2, Hua IX 14–15: the lived connection

Reads [§2, Paragraph 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#the-reasons-for-the-limited-effect-of-dilthey-on-his-contemporaries-inadequacy-of-their-understanding-and-the-limits-of-his-approach).

The passage turns on one word, "connection" (*Zusammenhang*), and on who owns it. Dilthey's explanatory psychology builds a "causal connection" (*Kausalzusammenhang*) out of assumed elements, a connection no one experiences. His descriptive psychology finds the connection already there, lived, as the one stream of psychic life. Husserl reports this "almost verbatim", and the report is not neutral. He takes over the lived connection as a thesis of his own, holds back the "typical human" to whom Dilthey tied it, and in a note recasts the word "explanatory" in a sense Dilthey did not give it. By the end of the course, the constructed connection has become Husserl's own target under another name, naturalism, and Dilthey is counted among those it misled.

> Against the "explanatory" psychology guided by the model of natural science he demands a "describing-dissecting" one. The former seeks, in a hypothetical-constructive procedure, to construct out of assumed psychic elements, say sensation-data and feeling-data, a causal connection that is itself unexperienceable, and, like physical natural science (and in psychophysics intertwining with it), to achieve "explanation" through causal laws. Toward this psychology, the modern naturalistic experimental psychology, Dilthey is in a certain respect sceptical. It is incapable of serving the human sciences as a foundation. What is needed rather is a describing, analysing psychology that proceeds purely intuitively (and not hypothetically-constructively). This is (I quote almost verbatim) the presentation of the components and connections that occur uniformly in every developed human psychic life. Naturalistic psychology overlooks that psychic connections are lived, and that they are all bound together in a single connection, namely the total connection of the psychic life streaming along. Connection is therefore not something hypothetically added in thought, something inferred, but, as lived in purely inner experience, accessible to a direct articulating analysis and description. Psychology is nothing other than description and analysis of this one connection, given inwardly as life itself; and indeed, as Dilthey holds, it is everywhere a matter of life-connections of the developed "typical human".

German:

> Gegenüber der von dem naturwissenschaftlichen Vorbilde geleiteten ,,erklärenden" Psychologie fordert er eine ,,beschreibend-zergliedernde". Die erstere sucht in einem hypothetisch-konstruktiven Verfahren aus angenommenen seelischen Elementen, etwa Empfindungsdaten und Gefühlsdaten, einen selbst unerfahrbaren Kausalzusammenhang zu konstruieren und wie die physische Naturwissenschaft (und in der Psychophysik sich mit ihr verflechtend) ,,Erklärung" durch Kausalgesetze zu leisten. Gegen diese Psychologie, die moderne naturalistisch experimentelle Psychologie, verhält sich Dilthey in gewisser Hinsicht skeptisch. Sie ist unfähig, den Geisteswissenschaften als Fundament zu dienen. Es bedarf vielmehr einer rein intuitiv (und nicht hypothetisch-konstruktiv) verfahrenden beschreibenden analysierenden Psychologie. Diese ist (ich zitiere fast wörtlich) die Darstellung der in jedem entwickelten menschlichen Seelenleben gleichförmig auftretenden Bestandteile und Zusammenhänge. Die naturalistische Psychologie übersieht, daß seelische Zusammenhänge erlebt sind und daß sie alle in einem einzigen Zusammenhang verbunden sind, nämlich dem Gesamtzusammenhang des dahinströmenden Seelenlebens. Zusammenhang ist also nicht etwas hypothetisch Hinzugedachtes, etwas Erschlossenes, sondern als erlebt in rein innerer Erfahrung einer direkten artikulierenden Analyse und Deskription zugänglich. Psychologie ist nichts anderes als Beschreibung und Analyse dieses einen als das Leben selbst innerlich gegebenen Zusammenhangs, und zwar, wie Dilthey meint, überall handelt es sich um Lebenszusammenhänge des entwickelten ,,typischen Menschen".

*Note on the text.* The file breaks the first sentence across a page marker (Hua IX 14/15), omitted here, and gives a note marker after "beschreibend-zergliedernde". Four notes on this stretch all carry the same label. Read in order, the third belongs to this marker: Husserl's remark that in the lectures he used the Aristotelian word "apodictic" for "explanatory" knowledge. The quotation drops the marker. No word in the passage is italicized in the file, and the commentary's italics on "lived", "connection" and "hypothetically added in thought" are its own.

The report runs on two compounds of the one noun. The "causal connection" is "itself unexperienceable" (*selbst unerfahrbar*), and the verb that governs it is "construct" (*konstruieren*), repeated from "hypothetical-constructive". The lived connection is governed by no verb of making. It "is" lived, it is "accessible" (*zugänglich*), it is "given inwardly as life itself". Between the two stands the decisive sentence, built on a negation of three terms: connection is "not something hypothetically added in thought, something inferred" (*nicht etwas hypothetisch Hinzugedachtes, etwas Erschlossenes*). The participles name what the mind does to data, adding in thought and inferring, and the sentence denies that the connection is a product of either. The "therefore" (*also*) claims more than the sentences before it show. That connections are lived does not by itself exclude that further connections must be inferred. Dilthey's argument needs the stronger claim that every psychic connection is one lived connection, and the passage gets it from the superlative "a single connection … the total connection" (*einem einzigen Zusammenhang … dem Gesamtzusammenhang*), not from an argument.

The parenthesis "(I quote almost verbatim)" sets one sentence apart, the definition of psychology as presentation of "components and connections" that occur "uniformly". Everything after it is in the indicative, with no reported speech, so the report slides into assertion. Only one clause is marked again as Dilthey's: "as Dilthey holds" (*wie Dilthey meint*), attached to the "developed typical human", which the file sets in quotation marks. The marking is selective. Husserl signs the lived connection and leaves the typical human to Dilthey, and two pages on he names it as the defect: Dilthey's psychology threatens to be nothing more than "a descriptive natural history of human psychic life, of the 'developed typical human' — as Dilthey expressly says" (Hua IX 17, src L392). A science of types gives uniformity, never necessity. The lived connection is what Husserl keeps; the typology is what he will replace with an eidetic a priori.

The note on "describing-dissecting" makes the same move on the other word of the contrast. Husserl records that in the lectures he called "explanatory" knowledge "apodictic", and divides it: explanation from laws is "a) pure a priori, b) related to it, the apodictic form of the empirical lawfulness of mathematical physics" (src L420). Dilthey's "explanatory" meant causal hypothesis; Husserl's means necessity, and its first form is the pure a priori. The pair *erklären*/*verstehen* (explain/understand), which the passage's next sentence states in Dilthey's form ("Nature we explain, psychic and spiritual life we understand"), is thus already displaced. The lectures will not set understanding against explanation. They will ask for an explanation of the psychic, from essential laws, that Dilthey's understanding lacked.

The passage stands between the opening of a new lecture, where Husserl says he will "not only repeat" but add thoughts that make Dilthey's intentions clearer (src L374), and the critique that follows (Hua IX 16–17). It does not report anything new. §1 had already given Dilthey's case against experimental psychology, which "designs hypothetical substructions of causal connections not experienced" (*hypothetische Substruktionen nicht erfahrener kausaler Zusammenhänge*, Hua IX 7–8, src L266–268). What §2 adds is the positive term, the one lived connection, and it adds it in the form of a report Husserl has half adopted. That is what the critique needs. Husserl can fault Dilthey for lacking an a priori only after granting that the field Dilthey pointed to, the streaming connection of inner life, is the right one.

The rest of the lectures carries the constructed connection forward and drops Dilthey's name from it. §24 describes the naturalistic picture of the soul as "a realm of its own of elements, which under an exclusively inductive regulation of their changes form a psychic-real connection" (*einen psychisch-realen Zusammenhang*, Hua IX 143, src L2710), bound to the body by "merely inductive natural causality". The verdict is now Husserl's, in a paragraph of one sentence: "The naturalistic prejudice must fall" (*Das naturalistische Vorurteil muß fallen*, src L2716). Beilage II, written for §2, then turns the argument round on Dilthey himself. A note there says what is "not the essential thing": that the psychology of the nineteenth century wanted to be "exact", explanatory on the model of the natural sciences, and "operates with unexperienceable hypothetical elements". "The basic defect is naturalism, which Dilthey too did not see through" (*Der Grundmangel ist der Naturalismus, den auch Dilthey nicht durchschaut hat*, Hua IX ca. 357, src L6026). The point that the passage reports almost verbatim, the unexperienceable construction, is declared secondary. Husserl keeps Dilthey's lived connection and his rejection of construction, but he locates the error deeper, in an attitude that Dilthey's own descriptive psychology still shared. The "almost" in "almost verbatim" marks that distance in advance.

## §3d, Hua IX 33: the slight turn

Reads [§3d, Paragraph 5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#d-brentano-as-pioneer-for-the-investigation-in-inner-experience--discovery-of-intentionality-as-fundamental-character-of-the-psychic).

Brentano's originality, on Husserl's account, lies in a change of what intentionality is used to distinguish. The Scholastics used it to sort objects: truly actual objects on one side, objects that exist only as meant on the other. Brentano used it to sort experiences, as the descriptive mark that sets the psychic apart from the physical. The content barely changes and its function changes entirely. That is the "seemingly slight turn" (*scheinbar geringe Wendung*), and Husserl makes the history of psychology hang on it: once intentionality is the essential character of the psychic, psychology must begin as the descriptive science of intentionality.

> Already in Scholasticism intentionality had been seen; a distinction had been drawn between truly actual objects, as being in themselves and for themselves, and merely-intentional objects, as meant within the meaning, experienced within the experiencing, judged within the judging. In this lay that psychic life as consciousness has in itself the peculiarity of relating itself to objectivity. But it is fundamentally wrong to say that Brentano only rediscovered an old scholastic doctrine. His great discovery and his true originality consist in this, that — guided by the idea of a descriptive psychology, which he, still naturalistically minded, thought of as the underlay of an explanatory psychology of the natural-scientific kind — he sought a descriptive principle for separating the physical and the psychic, and in doing so was the first to establish that intentionality is the essential character, to be grasped descriptively, of the specifically psychic. Seemingly this is a slight turn of the scholastic doctrine of intentionality. But such seemingly slight turns make history and decide the fate of science. It was the consequential and now self-evident consequence of this descriptive basic finding that psychology must at first begin as a purely descriptive science of psychic life, and that, so delimited, it was nothing other than the descriptive science of intentionality, of the manifold configurations of consciousness as consciousness of something, with all the contents distinguishable in it in inner intuition.

German:

> Schon in der Scholastik war die Intentionalität gesehen worden; es war zwischen wahrhaft wirklichen Gegenständen, als an sich und für sich seienden, und bloßintentionalen Gegenständen, als innerhalb des Vermeinen vermeinten, innerhalb des Erfahrens erfahrenen, innerhalb des Urteilens geurteilten, unterschieden worden. Darin lag, daß das psychische Leben als Bewußtsein in sich die Eigenheit hat, sich auf Gegenständlichkeit zu beziehen. Es ist aber grundverkehrt zu sagen, daß Brentano eine alte scholastische Lehre nur wiederentdeckt habe; seine große Entdeckung und seine wahre Originalität bestehen darin, daß er — geleitet von der Idee einer deskriptiven Psychologie, die er noch, naturalistisch eingestellt, als Untergrund einer naturwissenschaftlicher Art erklärenden Psychologie dachte — nach einem deskriptiven Prinzip der Scheidung zwischen Physischem und Psychischem suchte und dabei als erster feststellte, daß die Intentionalität der deskriptiv zu erfassende Wesenscharakter des spezifisch Psychischen sei. Scheinbar ist das eine geringe Wendung der scholastischen Lehre von der Intentionalität. Aber solche scheinbar geringen Wendungen machen Geschichte und entscheiden das Schicksal der Wissenschaft. Es war die folgenreiche und nun selbstverständliche Konsequenz dieser deskriptiven Grundfeststellung, daß die Psychologie zunächst anheben müsse als eine rein deskriptive Wissenschaft vom psychischen Leben und daß sie so begrenzt nichts anderes sei als die deskriptive Wissenschaft von der Intentionalität, von den mannigfaltigen Gestaltungen des Bewußtseins als Bewußtsein von etwas, mit allen an ihm in innerer Anschauung unterscheidbaren Gehalten.

*Note on the text.* The file runs "bloß intentionalen" together and reads "innerhalb des Vermeinen vermeinten", where the parallel genitives ("des Erfahrens", "des Urteilens") lead one to expect "Vermeinens". The rendering follows the parallel; nothing in the reading depends on the form. The last sentence is hard-wrapped with line-end hyphens in the file ("Inten-tionalität", "Bewußt-seins"), joined here.

The scholastic sentence is built on objects. Its two members are kinds of "objects" (*Gegenständen*), and its three "within" phrases (*innerhalb des … vermeinten, … erfahrenen, … geurteilten*) place the merely intentional object inside an act by making the participle of the act's verb into its adjective. What the Scholastics distinguished was a mode of being of objects. The next sentence draws a consequence they did not draw: "In this lay" (*Darin lag*) that psychic life relates itself to objectivity. The verb grants a content implicit in the doctrine and denies that the doctrine stated it. Brentano's half of the paragraph has a different grammatical subject throughout. He sought "a descriptive principle for separating the physical and the psychic", and intentionality became "the essential character of the specifically psychic". The object has dropped out as the thing sorted. The same insight, relation to an object, now classifies the experiences that have it.

The phrase "essential character" (*Wesenscharakter*) is Husserl's word, not Brentano's, and it carries the next step of the lectures. Brentano's sentence in the *Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkt* says that every psychic phenomenon "is characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (also mental) inexistence of an object" (*ist durch das charakterisiert, was die Scholastiker des Mittelalters die intentionale (auch wohl mentale) Inexistenz eines Gegenstandes genannt haben*, Brentano, *Psychologie* I 124, src L8439–8442 of the corpus file, a scan-grade OCR whose wording is intact here). Brentano himself cites the scholastic name, and that is what the opinion Husserl calls "fundamentally wrong" (*grundverkehrt*) rests on. Husserl does not use the name. He mentions it only in Beilage I, as an aside: "Following Scholasticism, Brentano also speaks of intentional or immanent inexistence" (Hua IX 354, src L5919). By replacing "characterized" with "essential character", Husserl reads Brentano's descriptive mark as an essential trait. That is the reading which §3e and the *Logical Investigations* will need, when the descriptive psychology becomes an eidetic one.

The dash-clause limits the praise in the same sentence that gives it. Brentano was guided by the idea of a descriptive psychology, "which he, still naturalistically minded (*noch, naturalistisch eingestellt*), thought of as the underlay of an explanatory psychology". The "still" puts the discovery inside the frame that §2 had faulted in Dilthey's opponents, and that Beilage II faults in Brentano by name (Hua IX ca. 357, src L6026). The discovery is credited as a find, not as a method. The paragraph's own claim is double-edged in the same way. "Seemingly" (*scheinbar*) is said twice, first of this turn and then of all such turns, so the slightness is conceded and denied in one breath. "Make history" is asserted, not shown, and the next sentence carries the weight. Psychology "must at first begin" (*zunächst anheben müsse*) as descriptive, and "so delimited" (*so begrenzt*) is "nothing other than" the descriptive science of intentionality. The "at first" and the "so delimited" mark a stage, not a definition. The essential character of the psychic does not yet tell the science how to analyse it.

The paragraph before prepares the scholastic point without naming it. In the perception of a house, "descriptively the object-relation belongs to the experiencing, whether the object actually exists or not" (*Also deskriptiv gehört zum Erleben die Objekt-Beziehung, ob nun das Objekt wirklich existiert oder nicht*, Hua IX 32, src L675). That is the scholastic merely-intentional object, restated as a descriptive finding about the experience. The paragraph after names the price of the turn. The doctrine was beset with "the greatest difficulties and obscurities": how intentional "analysis" was to be carried out, and "which kind of generality is possible and to be striven for in this sphere" (src L686–691). The descriptive mark leaves open the question of generality, which §3e answers with the eidetic method. Brentano's slight turn gives the field. It does not give the a priori that §2 found missing in Dilthey.

The work keeps both halves of the judgment. In §34 Husserl defends the scholastic name against his own terminology. The Scholastics noticed, "though without deeper insight of principle", that the object is conscious in the experience itself, and called it "not without reason" the "immanent object". The name has "the same right" as the name "subjective" (Hua IX 177, src L3118). What §3d denied to the Scholastics, an insight of principle, §34 denies again, and what it granted them, having seen something, it grants again. The Encyclopaedia Britannica drafts harden the verb. The First Draft calls Brentano's achievement a "revaluation (*Umwertung*) of the scholastic concept of intentionality into an essential mark of the 'psychic phenomena'", and adds what the naturalism blocked: "still hampered by naturalistic prejudices", Brentano "does not see the problems of synthesis and of intentional constitution" (Hua IX 247, src L4286). The Second Draft keeps the word (Hua IX 267–268, src L4614). "Revaluation" says openly what "seemingly slight turn" understated. The turn decides the fate of psychology because it changes the value of an old distinction without changing its content, and the lectures are the attempt to cash that value in a method Brentano did not have.

## §3d, Hua IX 34–35: Dilthey's right

Reads [§3d, Paragraph 10](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#d-brentano-as-pioneer-for-the-investigation-in-inner-experience--discovery-of-intentionality-as-fundamental-character-of-the-psychic).

Husserl grants that Dilthey was right, and the grant has a precise object and a precise limit. The object is a judgment: that phenomenology and Dilthey's describing-dissecting psychology form an "inner unity". The limit is in the prefix that Husserl attaches to everything he concedes. Dilthey's writings are a "pre-view and pre-stage" (*Vorschau und Vorstufe*) of phenomenology, and the psychology he aimed at "hovered before" him (*vorschwebte*) without his giving it the form of strict science. Dilthey was right about where phenomenology stands, not about how to get there. The verdict reverses Husserl's earlier indifference and keeps the earlier criticism in place.

> The further I myself have advanced in the development of the phenomenological method and in the phenomenological analysis of spiritual life, the more I had to recognize that Dilthey was in fact right <with the> judgment, so very strange to me, about the inner unity of phenomenology and describing-dissecting psychology. His writings contain a brilliant pre-view and pre-stage of phenomenology. They are by no means antiquated; even today they are over-rich in most valuable concrete stimuli for work in the methodically advanced phenomenology, which is shaped differently <in> its whole problematic. I would therefore most warmly recommend to you the study of all the writings of this [extra]ordinary spirit. They bring true treasures of intuitive exhibitions and descriptions into the historical connection, and wherever really pure description reigns, in most faithful adaptation to the givenness of actual intuition, there the phenomenologist has a prepared and fruitful working ground. But a training in that purely inward-turned psychology which Dilthey actually had in view, and which he himself nevertheless did not yet bring into the shape of strict science, with conceptually strict method and decisively carried-out findings, one cannot gain from them. Gratifying as Dilthey's late great influence is, it can also work unfavourably when his manner is imitated externally, as can very well be observed.

German:

> Je weiter ich selbst in der Ausbildung der phänomenologischen Methode und in der phänomenologischen Analyse des Geisteslebens fortgeschritten bin, umsomehr mußte ich erkennen, daß *Dilthey* in der Tat <mit dem > mich so sehr befremdenden Urteil über die innere Einheit von Phänomenologie und beschreibend-zergliedernder Psychologie Recht hatte. Seine Schriften enthalten eine geniale Vorschau und Vorstufe der Phänomenologie. Sie sind keineswegs antiquiert, auch heute noch überreich an wertvollsten konkreten Anregungen für die Arbeit in der methodisch fortgeschrittenen und <in > ihrer ganzen Problematik anders gestalteten Phänomenologie. Das Studium aller Schriften dieses ordentlichen Geistes möchte ich Ihnen also aufs wärmste empfehlen. Sie bringen wahre Schätze intuitiver Aufweisungen und Beschreibungen in den geschichtlichen Zusammenhang, und überall, wo wirklich reine Beschreibung in treuester Anpassung an die Gegebenheit wirklicher Anschauung waltet, da hat der Phänomenologe einen vorbereiteten und fruchtbaren Arbeitsboden. Aber eine Ausbildung in jener rein innengewendeten Psychologie, die *Dilthey* eigentlich vorschwebte und die er selbst doch noch nicht in die Gestalt strenger Wissenschaft, mit begrifflich strenger Methode und entscheidend durchgeführten Feststellungen gebracht hat, kann man aus ihnen nicht gewinnen. So erfreulich die späte große Wirkung *Diltheys* auch ist, sie kann bei äußerlicher Imitation seiner Art auch ungünstig wirken, wie sehr wohl zu beobachten ist.

*Note on the text.* The passage crosses a page marker (Hua IX 34/35), omitted, and is hard-wrapped with line-end hyphens, joined here. The angle brackets are the editor's supplements. The file reads "dieses ordentlichen Geistes" (of this orderly spirit), where the line break seems to have swallowed a prefix; Scanlon has "this extraordinary mind", and the rendering marks the supplement. Only the name *Dilthey* is italic in the file; the commentary's emphasis on "in fact right", "pre-view" and "pre-stage" is its own.

The sentence of concession is built so that Husserl's own progress does the conceding. "The further I myself have advanced … the more I had to recognize" (*Je weiter … umsomehr mußte ich erkennen*): Dilthey's judgment is confirmed by the development of the method, not by rereading Dilthey. The judgment was "so very strange" (*befremdend*) to Husserl. The paragraph before tells why: under the influence of Ebbinghaus's "brilliant anti-critique" he had not read Dilthey's *Ideen*, and the "strong leaning towards positivism" of the *Einleitung in die Geisteswissenschaften* had repelled him (Hua IX 34, src L725–730). It was Dilthey who "established this relation" between the *Logical Investigations* and his own psychology. The "in fact" (*in der Tat*) concedes after resistance, and what it concedes is Dilthey's reading of Husserl. Dilthey had judged the *Logical Investigations* to be "a first basic piece of an actual execution of the psychology that hovered before him as ideal" (*der ihm als ideal vorschwebenden Psychologie*, src L734–735). Husserl now agrees with Dilthey's estimate of Husserl.

The prefix *vor-* carries the limit. *Vorschau* and *Vorstufe* make Dilthey an anticipation: he saw ahead, and he stands on the step below. *Vorschweben*, used of Dilthey twice on these two pages, says that the psychology was before his eyes as an aim and not in his hands as a science. The same verb closed the critique of §2: one cannot recommend "the imitation of the method that hovered before him" (*die ihm vorschwebte*, Hua IX 16, src L388). The praise in the middle of the passage is all of intuition: "treasures of intuitive exhibitions", "really pure description", a "prepared and fruitful working ground". The phenomenologist inherits a soil, and the method is to be brought to it. The "but" (*Aber*) then withholds the one thing a lecture course offers, "training" (*Ausbildung*), the same noun Husserl used of his own advance ("in the development of the phenomenological method", *in der Ausbildung*). Husserl was formed by the method; Dilthey's readers cannot be.

The passage resolves the course's relation to Dilthey, which §§1–2 had left in suspense. §§1–2 reported Dilthey's critique of explanatory psychology and faulted his lack of an a priori. §3a–d traced Husserl's own route through Brentano and the *Logical Investigations*. Here the two lines meet, and the next paragraph draws the consequence: "So the psychology into which I want to introduce you … will not be simply of Diltheyan stamp, but phenomenological psychology" (*nicht ohne weiteres Diltheyschen Gepräges*, src L766–768). The qualifier "not simply" (*nicht ohne weiteres*) repeats the concession and the limit once more.

The later course does not withdraw the verdict, but it moves the defect. §3e still pairs them: "already Brentano and also Dilthey had seen" that empiricism and psychologism hold "a core of inviolable right" (Hua IX 41–42, src L848–850). Beilage II, written to §2, takes the defect from method to attitude. The "basic defect is naturalism, which Dilthey too did not see through" (Hua IX ca. 357, src L6026). An unripe method can be ripened, and a naturalistic attitude has to be overturned. That is harder on Dilthey than §3d, and it explains why his "external imitation" can "work unfavourably". Copying Dilthey's manner without the reduction copies his naturalism too.

An earlier text in the corpus shows the verdict already forming fourteen years before. In "Philosophie als strenge Wissenschaft" (1911), Husserl rejects Dilthey's historicism and does not see how Dilthey's analysis of the structure and typology of world-views could yield arguments against scepticism. He adds: "The matter becomes different, and this seems to move his thinking inwardly, when the empirical attitude, which aims at empirical understanding, is exchanged for the phenomenological attitude of essence" (*wenn die empirische Einstellung, die auf empirisches Verstehen geht, mit der phänomenologischen Wesenseinstellung vertauscht wird*, Hua XXV 45 n., `Hua 25 - Aufsätze und Vorträge (1911-1921).md`:600). In 1911 that is a conditional, and Dilthey's right lies in a change he has not made. In 1925 it is a concession, and what Dilthey saw is granted as a pre-view of what the change would show. In both, Dilthey is right only on the far side of the eidetic attitude that he did not take.

## §3f, Hua IX 43: two parallel sciences

Reads [§3f, Paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#f-the-consistent-extension-and-deepening-of-the-problem-setting-of-the-logical-investigations-demonstration-of-the-necessity-of-an-epistemological-grounding-of-the-a-priori-sciences-by-transcendental-phenomenology--the-science-of-transcendental-subjectivity).

The thesis of parallelism says that one body of eidetic findings about consciousness belongs to two sciences, and that what separates them is an attitude, not a content. An a priori psychology in the natural sense studies the psychic as part of the world. Transcendental phenomenology studies the same structures as the subjectivity on which all validity of the world rests. Each proposition of the one can be converted into a proposition of the other. The passage introduces the thesis in the form of a history: the idea of a science of reason "forced" the new psychology to split. Its wording, "only" by attitude, makes the difference sound slight, while the paragraphs that follow make it the most radical difference there is.

> Yet in further consequence the consideration of the sense of a radical and ultimate-principled science of reason, such as the transcendental philosophy developed in modern times actually wanted to be and, as philosophy, had to be, compelled an essential differentiation of the new idea of an a priori and descriptive psychology into two parallel sciences, distinguished only [by] principled attitude. The one was the exact analogue of pure geometry and, more concretely, of a priori natural science, an a priori psychology in the natural sense of the word.

German:

> Doch in weiterer Folge nötigte die Erwägung des Sinnes einer radikalen und letztprinzipiellen Vernunftwissenschaft, wie es die in der Neuzeit entwickelte Transzendentalphilosophie eigentlich sein wollte und als Philosophie sein mußte, zu einer wesentlichen Differenzierung der neuen Idee apriorischer und deskriptiver Psychologie in zwei parallele, nur prinzipielle Einstellung unterschiedene Wissenschaften. Die eine war das genaue Analogon der reinen Geometrie und konkreter der apriorischen Naturwissenschaft, eine apriorische Psychologie im natürlichen Wortsinn.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "nur prinzipielle Einstellung unterschiedene", which lacks a preposition (one expects "nur durch prinzipielle Einstellung"). Scanlon renders "distinguished only by their fundamental attitude". The rendering supplies "by" in brackets; the sense is not in doubt. The commentary's italics on "forced", "differentiation", "two parallel sciences" and "principled attitude" are its own: the file has none in this passage.

The subject of the main clause is not Husserl and not the psychology. It is "the consideration of the sense" of a science of reason, and its verb is "compelled" (*nötigte*). The split was not found in the psychology's material. It was imposed by what transcendental philosophy "actually wanted to be and, as philosophy, had to be". The modals carry the argument. Philosophy's own sense required a science that could ground all sciences, the a priori psychology included, and no science that stands among the others can do that. The paragraph after the passage makes this explicit: none of the a priori sciences, "not even a priori psychology, is so self-sufficient that it can at the same time pose and solve the epistemological problems for itself" (Hua IX 44, src L901–903). The parallelism therefore begins as a demand of philosophy on psychology, and only later is it shown in the material.

"Parallel" is said twice in one paragraph, of two different relations. The a priori psychology is "the exact analogue" of pure geometry and a priori natural science, and "in parallel" (*In Parallele*, src L892) it would establish what is necessary to the psychic as such, as geometry establishes what is necessary to nature. That parallel joins two regions by a common form. The other parallel joins two sciences of one region by a common content. "The one" (*Die eine*) announces the second member, and it does not arrive for a page. When it does, it comes with a reservation about its name: a "specifically transcendental psychology (for which, however, the historical word psychology no longer fits well)" (src L904–906). The parallelism needs both members to be sciences of the same subjectivity, so the other one has to be called psychology, and the name is withdrawn as soon as it is used.

The word "only" (*nur*) is contradicted within the page. The transcendental phenomenology "stays entirely outside" the association of objective sciences, and "by its whole researching attitude, thus from the most radical ground" (*also aus radikalstem Grunde*), it separates itself from all the sciences that have developed since Aristotle (src L911–916). The difference that is "only" one of attitude is the most radical difference in European science. The two statements are consistent once "only" is read as a claim about content. Nothing is added or taken away in the material; everything changes in what the material counts as. That is the sense the next page gives the thesis: "transcendental phenomenology has the peculiarity that each of its propositions can be converted into an a priori-psychological one in the natural sense" (*daß jeder ihrer Sätze sich in einen apriorisch-psychologischen im natürlichen Sinne umwandeln läßt*, Hua IX 45, src L932). The findings gained "from transcendental interest and transcendental attitude" must be taken over into psychology "in a corresponding change of attitude" (*in entsprechender Einstellungsänderung*, Hua IX 46, src L937). The inference is weaker than the wording makes it look. That each proposition can be converted shows a correspondence of propositions. It does not show that the field so reached is the same field seen twice, which is what the image of two parallel lines of one content suggests.

§3f places the passage at the hinge of the Introduction. §3e had shown the *Logical Investigations* turning descriptive psychology into an a priori psychology of intentionality. §3f now splits that a priori psychology in two, and §4 defines the half the lectures will pursue by "remaining in the natural dogmatic attitude". The lectures thus begin with the thesis that justifies their own restriction. They can teach phenomenological psychology without transcendental questions because whatever it finds will later convert.

The thesis returns in every treatise of the volume, and its sharpest return answers the doubt the "only" raises. In the First Draft of the Encyclopaedia Britannica article the two phenomenologies coincide "sentence for sentence" and differ "with a different sign" (row 17). The Amsterdam Lectures open on the "twofold sense" of phenomenology (row 21). The Fourth Version names the relation and limits it at once. The parallelism of the transcendental and psychological spheres has become intelligible "as a kind of identity of the interpenetration of the sense of being, from mere change of attitude" (*als eine Art Identität des Ineinander des Seinssinnes aus bloßer Einstellungsänderung*), and then: "Obviously the parallelism signifies nothing less than theoretical equivalence" (*Offenbar besagt der Parallelismus nichts weniger als theoretische Gleichwertigkeit*, Hua IX 294, src L5019–5030). Transcendental intersubjectivity is the "absolute ground of being" from which everything worldly draws its sense. In the lectures the "only" of attitude hid the asymmetry. In the Fourth Version the parallel is a kind of identity, and the two members are no longer of equal rank. The word itself keeps an uneasy neighbour in the lectures: §23 rejects the "parallelistic doctrine of Spinoza" (*parallelistische Lehre Spinozas*, src L2650), two exactly parallel worlds of body and soul, as absurd (row 10). Husserl's parallel is of the opposite kind. It does not run two realities side by side. It sets one content in two attitudes, and the Fourth Version's "kind of identity" is the formula that keeps it from being mistaken for the other.

## §4, Hua IX 46: remaining in the natural attitude

Reads [§4, Paragraph 3](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#summarizing-characterization-of-the-new-psychology).

The new psychology is defined by five catchwords, and the last is of a different kind from the others. Apriority, eidetics, intuition and intentionality say what the psychology does. "Remaining in the natural dogmatic attitude" says what it refrains from doing. The natural attitude is the standing presupposition of the world as experienced actuality, and the psychology stays in it although its findings are a priori. The fifth mark is therefore not a deficiency of method. It is a decision about where the lectures begin, and Husserl phrases it so that it already points beyond itself: one "remains" only where one could leave.

> If we survey the basic characters of the new psychology, they were, in catchwords: apriority, eidetics, intuition or pure description, intentionality, and, to name this too: remaining in the natural dogmatic attitude instead of the specifically philosophical, indeed transcendental attitude.

German:

> Überblicken wir die Grundcharaktere der neuen Psychologie, so war es in Schlagworten: Apriorität, Eidetik, Intuition bzw. reine Deskription, Intentionalität und, um auch das zu nennen: das Verbleiben in der natürlichen dogmatischen Einstellung anstatt der spezifisch philosophischen, ja transzendentalen Einstellung.

*Note on the text.* None of the catchwords is italicized in the file; the commentary's italics are its own. The sentence is the third paragraph of §4 in the file, after a sentence in parentheses ("Only a little is to be added…", src L948) that the commentary counts as a block of its own.

The list is built of four nouns and one nominalized verb. "Apriority", "eidetics", "intuition", "intentionality" name properties of a science or of its object. "The remaining" (*das Verbleiben*) names a conduct, and it is the only item that carries its own contrast: "instead of" (*anstatt*) the philosophical attitude. The phrase "to name this too" (*um auch das zu nennen*) sets it off as an afterthought, and the colon after it gives it a sentence of its own. The adjectives pair "natural" with "dogmatic", and "philosophical" is intensified by "indeed transcendental" (*ja transzendentalen*). The pairing comes from §3f, which had called all sciences other than transcendental phenomenology "dogmatic (positive) or natural-naive" (Hua IX 44, src L918). Dogmatic here means positing, not doctrinaire: a science that takes its object as given in the world.

The numbered gloss that follows does not keep the count. Item 1 explains apriority and absorbs eidetics into it ("essential generalities and necessities", src L951). Item 2 is intuition, as "the source of this a priori". Item 3 is intentionality. Item 4 is not the natural attitude at all. It says what "all such investigations" become "in the transcendental attitude": philosophical investigations of fundamentals, leading to a universal philosophy (src L962). The fifth mark is explained by its opposite. What the psychology refrains from is named in full, and what it remains in is defined only two paragraphs later, after the decision has been made.

The decision is a "will", hedged at once. "But we as psychologists do not want to be philosophers, that is, at least at first we do not want to be" (*d. h. mindestens zunächst wollen wir es nicht sein*, src L964). The reason is a claim of essence: "As a philosopher no one can be born, for essential reasons. Everyone can only begin as a natural, unphilosophical human being." A priori, a systematic grounding of philosophy demands "a subjective and likewise a historical ascent from the natural standpoint to the philosophical". The psychology that remains in the natural attitude is "perhaps" an a priori possible "natural starting point" for that ascent, and so "an educative, motivating pre-stage of philosophy (though by no means its fundamental discipline)" (*eine erziehliche, motivierende Vorstufe der Philosophie*, Hua IX 47–48, src L966–969). The word *Vorstufe* is the one §3d gave Dilthey (row 3). Dilthey's psychology was a pre-stage of phenomenology; phenomenological psychology is a pre-stage of philosophy. The parenthesis guards against the inference the lectures could invite. A pre-stage is not a foundation, and the parallelism of §3f (row 4) does not make the psychology the ground of transcendental phenomenology.

Only then does §4 say what the natural attitude is. The world, "posited in the self-evidence of its existence and constantly presupposed", which it draws "from the unanimity of our constant experience", is the universal theme of the sciences of this attitude. "Precisely this presupposition of experienced world-actuality … characterizes the natural attitude" (*Eben diese Voraussetzung erfahrener Weltwirklichkeit … charakterisiert die natürliche Einstellung*, Hua IX 48, src L973). The next sentence removes the confusion that the list invites. The a priori sciences, geometry among them, "do not actually lose this natural attitude", although their themes contain and presuppose nothing of a world-fact (src L975). Apriority and eidetics do not take a science out of the world. The first two catchwords and the fifth are therefore compatible, and this is what the list needs in order to be one list. An eidetic psychology of ideally possible psychic forms is still a science of the world, because the possibilities it varies are possibilities of the worldly psychic. That an "other attitude, with the exclusion of this presupposition, is possible" is merely "indicated"; "we need not go into it".

The rest of the work tests how long the psychology can remain. §37 introduces a phenomenological-psychological reduction and insists that it is not the transcendental one (row 14), so the psychology brackets and still remains in the natural attitude. The later treatises harden the pre-stage. The Encyclopaedia Britannica Fourth Version gives a section the title "Pure psychology as propaedeutic to transcendental phenomenology" (*Die reine Psychologie als Propädeutik zur transzendentalen Phänomenologie*, Hua IX 295, src L5036). It speaks of the "independence" of transcendental phenomenology from pure psychology and of the "propaedeutic usefulness" of sketching the psychology first (src L5038). The "perhaps" of §4 has become a method of exposition, and the parenthesis that denied the psychology the rank of fundamental discipline has become a thesis about independence. The fifth catchword ends where the first sentence of §4 placed it. The lectures say what the new psychology "wants to be and must be", and one of the things it must be is a science that has not yet taken the step for whose sake it is taught.

## §9b, Hua IX 75–76: variation and alteration

Reads [§9b, Paragraph 1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#b-variation-and-alteration).

Alteration and variation are both ways of being other, and they differ in what stays the same. An alteration keeps an individual: a colour fades, and the same colour, this individual, is otherwise in every phase of its duration. A variation keeps no individual. It keeps only what all the variants share, and that is the eidos. Husserl draws the distinction to protect the method of §9 from a confusion. If the variants of essential intuition were phases of a change, the eidos would be a thing that endures, and the a priori would be won by watching the world. The distinction has a second edge, which the commentary's block does not record. Every alteration can also be looked at as a series of variants, and then it shows that no alteration can leave its highest genus. The eidos does not arise from change, but it governs it.

> One thing more needs clarifying. We speak of variation and variants, not of alterations and phases of alteration. In fact the two kinds of concept, although some connection is not lacking, are essentially distinct. An alteration is alteration of something real, taken quite generally as something temporally existing, something enduring, lasting through a duration. Everything real is alterable and is only in alteration or non-alteration. At the same time, closely regarded, non-alteration is only a limiting case of alteration. Alteration means continuous being-other or becoming-other, and yet being the same, individually the same, in the continuous becoming-other; for example, the alteration of a colour. Non-alteration, again of a colour say, at bottom also means being the same in continuous becoming-other, but in doing so continuously staying alike in every phase of the duration. In alteration, the becoming-other means in each new phase being-other, but at the same time not-staying-alike.
>
> In the direction of regard on the phases of the duration of the real and what fills these phases, we have a manifold, always something other, thus many, and from phase to phase, as the case may be, alike or unlike. But in another direction of regard, a correlative one, we experience the unity, the identical, which alters and at times does not alter, which lasts, continues, through the flow of the manifolds.
>
> This unity is not, say, the universal of the individual temporal phases, just as these are not variants. It is precisely the one individual. On the other hand it belongs to alteration that the phases can also (though in an altered attitude) be treated as variants, and then it emerges that no alteration is possible in which all the phases of alteration do not belong together generically. A colour can alter only into a colour again, and not, say, into a tone. Every possible alteration takes place, as can then be seen, within a highest genus, which it can never overstep.

German:

> Noch eines bedarf der Klärung. Wir sprechen von Variation und Varianten, nicht von Veränderungen und Veränderungsphasen. In der Tat sind beiderlei Begriffe, trotzdem es nicht an einigem Zusammenhang fehlt, wesentlich unterschieden. Eine Veränderung ist Veränderung eines Realen, ganz allgemein gefaßt als eines zeitlich Seienden, eines Dauernden, durch eine Dauer hindurch Währenden. Jedes Reale ist veränderlich und ist nur in der Veränderung oder Unveränderung. Dabei ist, genau besehen, Unveränderung nur ein Grenzfall der Veränderung. Veränderung besagt stetig Anderssein bzw. Anderswerden und doch Dasselbe-, individuell Dasselbe-sein im stetigen Anderswerden; z.B. Veränderung einer Farbe. Unveränderung etwa wieder einer Farbe besagt im Grunde auch Dasselbe-sein im stetigen Anderswerden, aber dabei sich stetig Gleich-bleiben in jeder Phase der Dauer. Bei der Veränderung besagt das Anderswerden in jeder neuen Phase Anderssein, aber zugleich Sich-nicht-gleich-bleiben.
>
> In der Blickstellung auf die Phasen der Dauer des Realen und was diese Phasen besetzt, haben wir eine Mannigfaltigkeit, immer Anderes, also Vieles, und je nachdem von Phase zu Phase Gleiches oder Ungleiches. Aber in anderer Blickstellung, einer korrelativen, erfahren wir die Einheit, das Identische, das sich verändert und bald nicht verändert, das durch den Fluß der Mannigfaltigkeiten hindurchwährt, fortdauert.
>
> Diese Einheit ist nicht etwa das Allgemeine der einzelnen Zeitphasen, wie diese nicht Varianten sind. Sie ist ja gerade das eine Individuum. Andererseits gehört es zur Veränderung, daß die Phasen auch (obschon in geänderter Einstellung) als Varianten behandelt werden können, und dann tritt hervor, daß keine Veränderung möglich ist, in der nicht alle Veränderungsphasen gattungsmäßig zusammengehören. Eine Farbe kann sich nur wieder in eine Farbe ändern und nicht etwa in einen Ton. Jede mögliche Veränderung vollzieht sich, wie dann zu sehen ist, innerhalb einer obersten Gattung, die sie nie überschreiten kann.

*Note on the text.* The file has no emphasis in these paragraphs; the commentary's italics are its own, and so is its example of a leaf. Husserl's example is a colour, and the colour is what lets the last paragraph pass from alteration to genus.

The two concepts are drawn on paired stems: "variation and variants" against "alterations and phases of alteration". The pairing marks what each concept counts. Variation yields variants, each a possible object in its own right. Alteration yields phases, which are not objects but stretches of one object's duration. The definition of alteration is then built from compounds of *anders* (other) and *dasselbe* (the same): "continuous being-other or becoming-other, and yet being the same, individually the same, in the continuous becoming-other" (*stetig Anderssein bzw. Anderswerden und doch Dasselbe-, individuell Dasselbe-sein im stetigen Anderswerden*). The hyphenated infinitives turn states into moments of one process, and the "and yet" (*und doch*) holds otherness and sameness together without resolving them. "Individually" (*individuell*) is the word that does the work. The sameness of an alteration is the sameness of this individual, not of a kind.

The surprising step is the one that makes rest a kind of change. "Closely regarded, non-alteration is only a limiting case of alteration", and non-alteration "at bottom also means being the same in continuous becoming-other", only with a "continuously staying alike" (*sich stetig Gleich-bleiben*) in each phase. Husserl separates two words that ordinary German runs together: to stay "the same" (*dasselbe*) is identity, to stay "alike" (*gleich*) is likeness of content. Alteration and non-alteration share identity and differ in likeness. Even an unchanging colour "becomes other" because each phase of its duration is a new phase. Otherness here is temporal before it is qualitative. That is why every real thing is "only in alteration or non-alteration": to be real is to endure, and enduring is already continuous becoming-other.

The second paragraph gives the two directions of regard (*Blickstellung*), one on the phases, which yields a manifold, and a "correlative" one on the unity, which yields the identical. The third paragraph turns both against the method. The unity of an altering thing "is not, say, the universal of the individual temporal phases, just as these are not variants. It is precisely the one individual." An eidos is a universal over variants; an enduring thing is an individual over phases. The structures look alike, one over many in each case, and the passage denies that they coincide. Then it reverses direction. The phases "can also (though in an altered attitude) be treated as variants", and seen so, they belong to one genus. "A colour can alter only into a colour again, and not, say, into a tone." The genus is not abstracted from the alteration. It is a law the alteration cannot break, and it can be "seen" only when "we do not ask about the real", but lift all actuality "into pure possibility" (Hua IX 75–76, src L1750–1753). The parenthesis "though in an altered attitude" is the hinge. The same phases, looked at for what they are rather than as phases of this thing, become examples, and a real process yields an eidetic finding.

The section stands between the method's first statement and its moments. §9a defined the eidos as the invariant of a free variation. §9b ends on the sentence that follows the passage: free arbitrariness "also has its peculiar boundness, in that every variation carries its eidos in itself as a law of necessity" (src L1753). The highest genus that bounds every alteration is the same boundness, met from the side of the real. §9c can then set out the moments of ideation without the confusion that variants are snapshots of a change. The sentence just before §9b prepares the example: colours and tones yield "a different eidos", they are "other-in-kind" (*andersartig*) in their pure species (src L1738–1740). §9b borrows that result to show why a colour cannot alter into a tone.

The distinction returns where the lectures define reality. In §13 real unity is the unity of what not only "preserves unity in its non-alteration and alteration" but also keeps a fixed causality through the change of its properties (Hua IX 101, src L2161–2168). Alteration is the mark of the real, and causality the mark of its specifically real unity. §16 then uses the concept to set cultural sense apart. The historical change in the interpretation of a past work is "not an alteration of it in the reality-sense in which we speak of the alteration of a physical thing or of a human being", but a manifold of conflicting interpretations "of the same" (Hua IX 117, src L2340). The cultural object has a sameness without alteration, which is one reason it is irreal (row 8).

*Erfahrung und Urteil*, which Landgrebe compiled from Husserl's manuscripts, reproduces §9b almost word for word as §87f, "Variation und Veränderung", and changes it at two points (`husserl-erfahrung-und-urteil.md`:16453–16505, EU ca. 419–420). It drops "closely regarded" and the claim that non-alteration "also" means continuous becoming-other; non-alteration there "means: being the same in the duration, but in doing so staying continuously alike". The provocation of Hua IX, rest as becoming-other, is smoothed away. And it adds the sentence that Hua IX leaves to the reader: "Variation, by contrast, rests precisely on our dropping the identity of the individual and re-imagining it into another possible individual" (*Hingegen beruht die Variation eben darauf, daß wir die Identität des Individuellen fallen lassen und es umfingieren in anderes mögliches Individuelles*). The later text names what the 1925 lecture only implies by contrast. Alteration preserves the individual's identity; variation gives it up, and what survives the loss is the eidos.

## §11, Hua IX 95: the world as object of experience

Reads [§11, Paragraph 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#characterization-of-the-science-of-the-natural-world-concept-demarcation-of-the-concept-of-experience-from-the-kantian-concept-of-experience-space-and-time-as-the-most-general-structures-of-the-world).

Experience, for Husserl, reaches exactly as far as concordant synthesis reaches. Many perceptions and memories of one thing join into "one experience" of it; my experiences and another's join into experience "of the same". Nothing in this concept stops at the single thing, and so the world, as the open unity of all such syntheses, is experienced too. Kant's proposal that the world is no object of possible experience is refused on that ground. The refusal does not answer Kant's argument. It replaces his concept of experience with one defined by its synthetic reach, and the science of the natural world-concept, which §§10–16 sketch, needs exactly that replacement: it can only describe the world's structure if the world is something given.

> Now Kant teaches emphatically: the world is no object of possible experience — whereas we constantly, in all seriousness, speak of the world precisely as the universal object of a universally stretched and to-be-stretched experience.
>
> I cannot acknowledge the Kantian proposition, however the concept of experience is formed, if it is to remain useful. For us, real particulars are experienced, but the world <is> experienced too, and the two are even inseparable. We experience a thing, say; we perceive it, come back to it in recollections; we have in the chaining of these recollections a synthesis of many single experiences into the unity of one experience, as experience of the same. But also: what I experience, another can just as well experience, and not only do my experiences chain into the unity of one experience, but also my and others' experiences again into experience of the same.
>
> As far as concordant synthesis into the unity of an experience then concordant is possible, so far reaches our concept of experience. As experiential actuality, as existing real which we afterwards designate as member of the real and actually existing world, we count something experienced in so far as it counts for us as concordantly experienceable through ever new experience, our own and others', actual or to be made possible, as continually confirming itself for us, or, as the case may be, as what could have confirmed itself consistently. In this I recall our earlier basic determination of the concept of pure experience.

German:

> Nun lehrt Kant eindringlich, die Welt ist kein Gegenstand möglicher Erfahrung — während wir doch beständig allen Ernstes von der Welt eben als dem universalen Gegenstand einer universal gespannten und zu spannenden Erfahrung sprechen.
>
> Ich kann den kantischen Satz nicht anerkennen, wie immer der Begriff der Erfahrung, wenn er nützlich bleiben soll, geformt wird. Für uns sind reale Einzelheiten erfahren, aber auch die Welt \<ist\> erfahren, und beides ist sogar untrennbar. Wir erfahren etwa ein Ding, wir nehmen es wahr, kommen darauf in Wiedererinnerungen zurück; wir haben in der Verkettung dieser Erinnerungen eine Synthesis vieler einzelner Erfahrungen zur Einheit einer Erfahrung als Erfahrung von dem Selben. Aber auch: was ich erfahre, kann ebensogut ein Anderer erfahren, und es verketten sich nicht nur meine Erfahrungen zur Einheit einer Erfahrung, sondern auch meine und Anderer Erfahrungen wieder zur Erfahrung von demselben.
>
> Soweit einstimmige Synthese zur Einheit einer dann eben einstimmigen Erfahrung möglich ist, so weit reicht unser Begriff von Erfahrung. Als Erfahrungswirklichkeit, als seiendes Reales, das wir nachher als Glied der realen und wirklich seienden Welt bezeichnen, gilt uns ein Erfahrenes, sofern es uns gilt als durch immer neue, eigene und fremde, wirkliche oder zu ermöglichende Erfahrung einstimmig Erfahrbares, als sich fortgesetzt für uns Bestätigendes, oder eventuell, was sich konsequent hätte bestätigen können. Ich erinnere dabei an unsere frühere Grundbestimmung des Begriffs purer Erfahrung.

*Note on the text.* The file keeps the editor's supplement "\<ist\>" with escaped brackets and gives a note marker after "purer Erfahrung", omitted here. Nothing in the passage is italicized in the file. The commentary's emphasis on "emphatically teaches", on Kant's proposition and on "the world is experienced" is its own, and the commentary sets Kant's proposition in italics as if it were a quotation. The file gives it without quotation marks, as indirect report.

Kant's thesis is reported, not quoted, and its verb is "teaches emphatically" (*lehrt eindringlich*). The answer is set against it in one long clause, "whereas we constantly, in all seriousness, speak" (*während wir doch beständig allen Ernstes … sprechen*). The opposition is between a doctrine and a way of speaking that the lectures have kept up "in all seriousness". Husserl's world is the "universal object of a universally stretched and to-be-stretched experience" (*einer universal gespannten und zu spannenden Erfahrung*). The participle pair carries the point before any argument does. The experience of the world is never finished: it is stretched over what has been experienced and is still to be stretched over what has not. Kant's objection, that the world is never wholly given, is granted in the gerundive "to be stretched" and denied in the noun "object".

"However the concept of experience is formed, if it is to remain useful" claims more than the paragraph then does. The claim is universal: no useful concept of experience could exclude the world. What follows is not a survey of concepts but a single one, introduced as "our concept" (*unser Begriff*). Its definition is a correlation of extent: "As far as (*Soweit*) concordant synthesis … is possible, so far (*so weit*) reaches our concept of experience." The same words, run together and then set apart, bind the reach of experience to the reach of synthesis. The definition is stipulative, and the universal claim rests on the judgement that a concept cut off before the world would no longer be "useful", for a science of the world's structure among other things. The step that the concept needs comes in the example. Synthesis runs from perception to recollection, which gives "one experience as experience of the same", and then from my experiences to another's: "not only my experiences … but also my and others' experiences again into experience of the same". The intersubjective synthesis turns a personal unity into the unity of a common world. Nothing in it marks a limit at which a synthesis of experiences of the world would stop being experience.

The third paragraph shows the price. What counts as "experiential actuality" counts so "in so far as it counts for us" (*sofern es uns gilt*) as concordantly experienceable "through ever new experience, our own and others', actual or to be made possible", as "continually confirming itself", or even as "what could have confirmed itself consistently". Experience now includes the possible and the counterfactual. The world is experienced in a concept of experience whose objects are those of possible concordant confirmation. That is close to Kant's own "possible experience", and Husserl's quarrel is with the word "object". Kant had argued that the world as a whole is never given, only the regress through its appearances. Husserl answers that a whole given as a horizon of further concordant experience is given in the only sense in which a thing is given either: the thing, too, is the unity of an open synthesis.

The passage stands at a turn of §11. Just before it Husserl declines to go into the "close relation of our problems to those of Kant", because "no philosophical interests in the sense of a transcendental philosophy move us", and grants an "essential kinship" with the Transcendental Aesthetic, Analytic "and even Dialectic" (Hua IX 95, src L2018). The Dialectic is where Kant's thesis belongs. The passage then hands the definition to the rest of §11. "Purely experienced" is only what is passively pregiven and appropriated in mere receptivity; "a predicative formation 'gold is yellow' is not experienced, but the gold may be, and so may the yellow" (src L2026). The world that is experienced is the world before predication, and the science of the natural world-concept describes the structure of that world, with space and time as its most general forms.

The lectures keep the thesis and later qualify its status. §19 takes up the certainty that "an objective, finally true world" exists, although every experienced reality leaves correction open, and calls it "a presumption reaching beyond every actual experience" (*eine über jede aktuelle Erfahrung hinausreichende Präsumtion*, Hua IX 125, src L2463). The section heading names it "a necessary presumption". Already §6 had distinguished the world-belief of the sciences from experience itself: "only the constant horizon-presumption belongs to it", that experience can always be set going anew and that everything will finally come together in concordance (Hua IX 63, src L1412–1418). The world that §11 calls experienced is thus experienced as the object of a presumption that belongs to experience itself. §11's refusal of Kant stands, but its ground has been named. The world is the universal object of experience because concordance is presumed to continue, and that presumption is what experience consists in.

The Kant file in the corpus confirms what the passage leaves implicit. In the seventh section of the Antinomy, the world "is only in the empirical regress of the series of appearances and not to be met with for itself at all. Hence, if this series is always conditioned, it is never wholly given, and the world is therefore no unconditioned whole" (*Sie ist nur im empirischen Regressus der Reihe der Erscheinungen und für sich selbst gar nicht anzutreffen. Daher, wenn diese jederzeit bedingt ist, so ist sie niemals ganz gegeben, und die Welt ist also kein unbedingtes Ganzes*, `kant-kritik-der-reinen-vernunft-1787.md`:1961; the file gives no B pagination). Kant denies that the world is an unconditioned whole. Husserl, in Kant's name, denies that it is an object. The two denials differ, and Husserl's answer meets the second while agreeing with the first. His world is never wholly given and never a whole in itself, and it is still the object of experience, because for him an object never needed to be wholly given.

## §16, Hua IX 117: the irreal layer

Reads [§16, Paragraph 20](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#the-configurations-in-which-the-spiritual-appears-in-the-experiential-world-the-peculiarity-of-the-cultural-object-which-is-determined-in-its-being-by-subject-relatedness).

The sense that makes a body into a cultural object is irreal: it does not alter, it has no states spread through a duration, and the same sense can belong to bodies that are really separate. Yet it is in the world. It is bound to its body, lasts as long as the body carries it, and acts on the people who understand it. Husserl gives it each of these worldly traits with a qualifier, "a kind of", "something like". The cultural sense is irreal in what it is and quasi-real in how it is placed, and the qualifiers are the whole doctrine. They let the experiential world include culture without making culture a part of nature, and they prepare the abstraction of §17, which removes these senses to reach pure reality.

> The sense is thus a peculiar irreal layer on a bodily reality; through this it receives a position in the real world, a kind of temporal duration, spatial binding, indeed something like spatial extension; and again something like effect in the world, in so far as, just as it sprang from subjective accomplishment, it can also act back into the [subsequently understanding] subjectivity, motivating it to new, personal accomplishments.

German:

> Der Sinn ist also eine eigentümliche irreale Schicht an einer körperlichen Realität; durch diese erhält er Stellung in der realen Welt, eine Art zeitlicher Dauer, räumlicher Bindung, ja so etwas wie Raumerstreckung; und wieder so etwas wie Wirkung in der Welt, sofern er, wie aus subjektivem Leisten entsprungen, so in die nachstehende Subjektivität auch wieder hineinwirken, sie zu neuen, personalen Leistungen motivieren kann.

*Note on the text.* The file reads "in die nachstehende Subjektivität" (into the subjectivity standing after, or below). Scanlon has "the subjectivity which subsequently understands it" and the commentary "the post-understanding subjectivity", which suggest *nachverstehende*; the file uses *Nachverstehen* (understanding after) on the next page (src L2346). The rendering marks the reading in brackets, and nothing here depends on the word. The file has no italics in the passage; the commentary's on "peculiar irreal layer", "place" and "effect" are its own.

The sentence has two halves joined by a semicolon, and the semicolon divides being from placing. The first half says what the sense is: "a peculiar irreal layer on a bodily reality". The second says what it gets "through this" (*durch diese*), through the bodily reality it is layered on. Every worldly trait of the sense is borrowed from the body. Its "position in the real world" is the body's position, its duration the body's duration. The qualifiers grade the borrowing. "A kind of" (*eine Art*) governs temporal duration and spatial binding, the traits a sense has simply by belonging to a lasting body. "Indeed something like" (*ja so etwas wie*) governs spatial extension, which a sense has least of all and which the "indeed" offers as a concession that goes surprisingly far. "And again something like effect" (*und wieder so etwas wie Wirkung*) governs efficacy, the trait that would make it a cause. The qualifier is the same, but the ground is not borrowed from the body. A sense acts by being understood: it "acts back into" subjectivity and "motivates" new accomplishments. Its efficacy is motivation, not causality.

"Layer" (*Schicht*) has to be read against the page before. A cultural sense, "in so far as it belongs to the bodily lived body, so to speak, of the work", belongs to it "not in the manner of a reality-layer" (*nicht in der Art einer Realitätsschichte*, Hua IX 116, src L2332). The passage keeps the noun and changes its adjective. Soul and body are two reality-layers of an animal, and §15 analyses that double layering. The cultural sense is a layer that is not real. "Body", too, is marked as metaphor: the work's "bodily lived body, so to speak" (*dem körperlichen Leib sozusagen*). The vocabulary of animation is lent to the work and withdrawn in the same phrase, which is how Husserl keeps culture from becoming a second kind of soul.

The "thus" (*also*) gathers three arguments made just before, and they are of unequal force. A real thing is "a unity of alteration", with states extended through a duration under causal rule (src L2332). The sense of a work has no such states and no "possible altering or not altering" (src L2334), so a darkened painting is not an altered artwork but "another picture" now experienced, and falsely (src L2336). Historical reinterpretation is not alteration "in the reality-sense" but a manifold of conflicting interpretations "of the same" (Hua IX 117, src L2340). These show that the sense is not real in §9b's sense of an altering individual (row 6). The third argument is the strongest: "different things can never have identically the same states", but different cultural formations "can very well have identically the same sense with really separated individual corporeality". A mathematical proposition embodied in German can come "with completely identical mathematical sense" to embodiment in other languages (src L2342). Real properties are individuated by their bearers; a sense is not. That is the ground for "irreal", and the example is chosen from the region where the irreal was first established.

The example points back to §3a. There the Pythagorean theorem is "a single one in the realm of propositions, whenever I or anyone thinks and proves it" (Hua IX 22, src L468), and Husserl concludes that "there are without doubt *irreal* objectivities and associated *irreal* truths" (Hua IX 24, src L492–493). The irreal of §3a is the ideal object of logic, with no place in the world at all. The irreal of §16 is bound: it has a position, a duration, an effect. The mathematical proposition joins the two. As a proposition it is the irreal of §3a; as embodied in a German text it is "a German cultural formation" (src L2342) and acquires the quasi-place of §16. The qualifiers of the passage measure that binding.

What comes after uses the passage at once. The next paragraph insists that these objects be taken "as they are experienced" and not pushed aside "because they are not thing-realities", nor counted as psychic objects because of their "subjectivity". They are "nothing psychically real, but intersubjectively identifiable actualities" (Hua IX 118, src L2346). §17 then performs the abstraction the passage makes possible: "nothing stands in the way" of abstracting from "these subject-related irreal characters" of the experiential world and looking only at "the universe of pure thingly realities" (src L2358). The irreal layer can be removed because it was never a reality-layer. Pure nature is reached by subtraction, and the subtraction is possible because the cultural sense was given only borrowed traits. The lectures then carry the word further inward. In §34 the perceived object itself, as intended, is an "irreal pole" over against the reell flow of experience (row 13). The irreal begins in logic, is bound to bodies in culture, and ends in every object of consciousness, and §16's qualifiers are the first place where Husserl measures how an irreality can still be in the world.

## §21, Hua IX 130–132: two concepts of animation

Reads [§21, Paragraph 1](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#stratified-structure-of-the-psychic).

§21 answers a question about place: how does the psychic, and especially the personal spirit, come to be somewhere in the spatial world? Husserl's answer is "animation" (*Beseelung*), and he splits the word. The lower animation makes a physical body into a lived body (*Leib*). Sensation is localized in it, and the somatological psychic is experienced as one with the physical. The higher animation is that of the lived body by the personal spirit, which "holds sway" in it and shows itself in expression without being localized in the proper sense. Through the two together spirit gains "locality, so to speak its spatialization", and with its bodily underlay, reality. The stratification of §21 is thus a stratification of how the psychic is in space, not, as the commentary's block has it, a ladder of hyletic data, objectivating acts and valuing acts (see the list's Corrections).

> One can obviously also put the matter thus: there are two concepts of animation belonging together in layers.
>
> 1) The animation which makes the lived body a lived body, a system of subjective organs fused with inwardness; or everything together into a two-sided being in which other psychic matter is embodied point by point.
>
> 2) The higher animation, as which this two-sided lived body is itself in turn animated in a higher sense. This higher animating is above all personal subjectivity, the spirit, which in its I-acts holds sway in the lived body and through it in its surrounding world, and which in bodily expression makes itself known as co-existing with the lived body. In the first case, then, what is called animated is the physical [lived-body body], and in this animation it is called lived body. In the second case what is called animated is the lived body itself, by the holding-sway I-subject.
>
> Animation designates the way in which spirituality gains locality in the spatial world, so to speak its spatialization, and, at one with its bodily underlay, reality.
>
> Reality, namely, in so far as the spiritual, in its being co-given with the physically natural, is not merely co-existent with it at all but really-causally one with it.

German:

> Man kann die Sache offenbar auch so fassen: es gibt zwei schichtenweise zusammengehörige Begriffe von Beseelung.
>
> 1) Die Beseelung, die den Leib zum Leib, zu einem System subjektiver mit Innerlichkeit verschmolzener Organe macht; oder alles zusammen zu einem doppelseitigen Wesen, in dem anderes Seelisches Zug um Zug verkörpert ist.
>
> 2) Die höhere Beseelung, als welche diesen doppelseitigen Leib selbst ihrerseits in einem höheren Sinn beseelt. Dies höher Beseelende ist vor allem die personale Subjektivität, der Geist, der in seinen Ichakten im Leib und durch ihn in seiner Umwelt waltet und im leiblichen Ausdruck sich als mit dem Leib mitdaseiend bekundet. Im ersten Fall heißt also beseelt die physische Leibkörpererfassung und in dieser Beseelung heißt er Leib. Im zweiten Fall heißt beseelt der Leib selbst durch das waltende Ich-Subjekt.
>
> Die Beseelung bezeichnet die Weise, wie Geistigkeit in der Raumwelt Lokalität, gewissermaßen ihre Verräumlichung gewinnt, und in eins mit ihrer körperlichen Unterlage Realität.
>
> Realität nämlich, sofern das Geistige in seiner Mitgegebenheit mit dem physisch Naturalen nicht bloß überhaupt Mitseiendes, sondern mit ihm real-kausal Einiges ist.

*Note on the text.* The §21 heading survives in the file only inside an HTML comment (src L2535). Item 1 reads "den Leib zum Leib" (the lived body into a lived body), where the sense requires the physical body to become a lived body; and "die physische Leibkörpererfassung" (the physical lived-body-body-apprehension) is followed by "heißt er Leib", whose masculine pronoun points to *Leibkörper*, not to the feminine *-erfassung*. Both look like slips of transmission. The rendering follows the file in the first case and brackets the likely noun in the second; the reading rests on neither. Elsewhere on these pages the file has "Herbei" for *Hierbei* and, two paragraphs on, an English "the" for *die* ("the Physis des Tieres", src L2575).

The section opens by drawing its line. Husserl will make precise "an essential difference of levels and layers … between the psychic of the lower levels, psychic passivity, and the specifically spiritual, namely life running its course in I-centred acts" (Hua IX 130, src L2543). The examples on the spiritual side are acts of grasping, comparing, theorizing, acting, and also the affections directed at the I. The lower level is "everywhere the presupposition of personality", because every "I take notice, I grasp" presupposes something already lying ungrasped in the field and exerting an affection (Hua IX 131, src L2549). Up to here the section speaks of levels of the psychic. Then the subject changes to the body. "To the lower level belongs, evidently, the most immediate and most original animation of physical corporeality" (src L2551). The lower psychic is the "somatological psychic", "the directly incorporated, directly animating" (src L2555). The levels of the psychic turn out to be levels of its relation to the body, and that is why the passage can restate them as two concepts of one word.

The first concept turns a thing into an organ. Animation makes a "system of subjective organs fused with inwardness", a "two-sided being" (*doppelseitigen Wesen*), and the foot is the example on the page before: as material body it is moved, as "my foot" it carries the field of touch, and its movement is at once "physical movement in space and … subjective moving of the foot" (src L2553). The second concept takes the two-sided lived body as a whole and animates it again "in a higher sense". Its agent is "the spirit, which in its I-acts holds sway" (*waltet*), and its relation to the body is not localization but expression: it "makes itself known (*bekundet*) as co-existing with the lived body". The paragraph before the passage says this outright. Higher acts are built on a somatic "sensory" underlay, "but they are not themselves localized in the proper sense". Anger, an energetic character, a noble mind are "'seen in'" (*„angesehen“*) the body "and yet not seen as actually embodied" (Hua IX 132, src L2561). The quotation marks around "seen in", like those around the preposition "on" (*„an“*) just before, distance the spatial language from what it describes. The spirit is on the body as a meaning is on a gesture.

"Layers belonging together" (*schichtenweise zusammengehörige*) keeps the two concepts from becoming two souls. The higher animation does not have a body of its own; it animates the body that the lower one has already made a lived body. Then comes the sentence that gives the section its point. Animation "designates the way in which spirituality gains locality in the spatial world, so to speak its spatialization, and, at one with its bodily underlay, reality". "So to speak" (*gewissermaßen*) qualifies spatialization exactly as §16's "something like" qualified the spatial extension of a cultural sense (row 8). The spirit is placed by its body and is not extended. But the second gain is unqualified: reality. The last sentence defines it. Reality means that the spiritual is "not merely co-existent" with the physical but "really-causally one" (*real-kausal Einiges*) with it. Here the parallel with §16 breaks. The cultural sense gained a quasi-place and a quasi-effect, by motivation only, and remained irreal. The spirit gains a place in the same borrowed way, through expression, but it enters causality, and that makes it real. The next paragraph draws the consequence: causality links "the physis of the animal with its psyche and conversely", so the objective world "is no mere unity of physical causalities, but of psychophysical ones" (Hua IX 133, src L2575–2577).

That consequence is what the following sections examine and restrict. §22 defines physical reality as enduring substance of causal determinations; §23 (row 10) shows that this causality, between body and soul, is inductive, and refuses to infer from it a parallel structure of the soul. The two concepts of animation tell §23 where the inductive causality runs. It runs through the somatological layer, where the psychic is localized, and reaches the spirit only as far as the spirit is animated through that layer. The specifically spiritual, which is only "seen in" the body, has a unity of its own that inductive causality does not exhaust. §24's "naturalistic prejudice must fall" (src L2716) is prepared here, in the split between what is localized and what is expressed.

*Ideas II* treats the same question with a different emphasis, so far as its converted text allows. There the localization of the soul is wholesale: "The soul is in the lived body and there where the lived body now is. There too are the such-and-such groups of states of consciousness, the such-and-such presentations, stirrings of thought, judgements" (*Die Seele ist im Leibe und dort, wo der Leib jetzt gerade ist*, Hua IV 178, `husserl-hua-4-ideen-ii-phanomenologische-untersuchungen-zur-konstitution.md`:3215). The corpus file breaks off a sentence later, before a new heading, so the passage cannot be read to its end, and Hua IV is on the list of files with lost text. As far as it goes, *Ideas II* places judgements where the body is. Hua IX distinguishes: the somatological psychic is localized, the acts of the spirit are only indicated by expression. In 1925 the spirit has a locality only "so to speak", and its reality is causal, not spatial.

## §23, Hua IX 139: the parallelism refused

Reads [§23, Paragraphs 5–6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#physical-causality-as-inductive-the-peculiarity-of-psychic-interweavedness).

Husserl accepts one parallelism and refuses another. The animal is a real unity because its bodily and psychic states are coordinated, "parallel-ordered", and linked by inductive causality, so the soul must have an inductive typicality of its own. What he refuses is the step from coordination to identity of structure: that because the soul is inductively coupled to a body that is nothing but an inductive unity, the soul too is nothing but an inductive unity. Spinoza's doctrine of two exactly parallel worlds is that step made into metaphysics. The reductio that dismisses it turns on expression and spatialization, the two terms of §21, and the passage refuses the parallel because the relation of soul and body is not symmetrical.

> But here one must guard against an enormous error, which has played a fateful role long enough. The fact that each of the two reality-layers inductively linked into the unity of a spatial-objective reality, the physical-bodily and the psychic, considered for itself, has the style of an inductive unity, and even of a certain structural form, which alone makes possible an overall unity of parallelistic body-soul construction at all, — that by no means says that what has come together, under the titles bodily lived body and soul, into the real unity of an animal being is actually parallel throughout, namely actually of a throughout parallel structure of being. More precisely: the physical thing in which, as lived-body body, a soul is embodied is, as physical thing, nothing other at all than a unity out of inductive belonging-together, for itself and in its connection in universal physical nature merely a real unity out of inductive causality. But by no means does the parallelism of the associated psychic say that the soul too is a merely real unity out of inductive causality. One can think such a thing only if, instead of immersing oneself in what is proper to the essence of psychic being itself, one lets oneself be guided, as psychology already did in its first beginnings with Descartes and Hobbes, by the prejudice that the new natural science is the prototype for every genuine objective science, and that therefore every objective science must be of the same style and, correlatively, every objective being, and that meant every objectively spatial reality, must have exactly the peculiarities of physical reality. This conception determined the fateful parallelistic doctrine of Spinoza. According to it, physical world and psychic world would be two exactly parallel worlds of absolutely identical structural form, form of being as well as form of law, built only out of fundamentally different material.
>
> That, however, closely regarded, is downright absurd. And not only because, which would already suffice, the souls do not have a space proper to the soul which would have its psychic geometry in it, <but also> because on this conception it would just as well have to be said that the bodies spatialize themselves in the parallel psychic space, <would have to> present themselves in the souls through expression, etc., just as the souls embody themselves in physical space and corporealize and express themselves physically. All that is absurd, just as the whole metaphysical ontology of Spinoza which still lies behind it is to be rejected.

German:

> Aber hier muß man sich vor einem gewaltigen Irrtum hüten, der lang genug eine verhängnisvolle Rolle gespielt hat. Die Tatsache, daß jede der beiden zur Einheit einer räumlich-objektiven Realität induktiv verknüpften Realitätsschichten, die physisch-leibliche und die seelische, für sich betrachtet den Stil einer induktiven Einheit hat und sogar von einer gewissen Strukturform, die ja eine Gesamteinheit von parallelistischem Leib-Seelen-Aufbau überhaupt erst möglich macht, das besagt keineswegs, daß, was da unter den Titeln körperlicher Leib und Seele zur realen Einheit eines animalischen Seins gekommen ist, wirklich durchgängig parallel ist, nämlich wirklich von durchgängig paralleler Seinsstruktur. Genauer gesprochen: das physische Ding, in dem sich als Leibkörper eine Seele verleiblicht, ist als physisches Ding überhaupt nichts anderes als eine Einheit aus induktiver Zusammengehörigkeit, für sich und in seinem Zusammenhang in der universalen physischen Natur bloß reale Einheit aus induktiver Kausalität. Keineswegs aber besagt der Parallelismus des zugehörigen Seelischen, daß auch die Seele eine bloß reale Einheit aus induktiver Kausalität sei. So etwas kann man nur meinen, wenn man, statt sich in das Eigenwesentliche des seelischen Seins selbst zu vertiefen, sich, wie die Psychologie schon in ihren ersten Anfängen bei Descartes und Hobbes, von dem Vorurteil leiten läßt, daß die neue Naturwissenschaft der Prototyp für jede echte objektive Wissenschaft sei und daß darum jede objektive Wissenschaft von gleichem Stil und korrelativ jedes objektive Sein, und das sagte, jede objektiv räumliche Realität, genau die Eigenheiten der physischen Realität haben müsse. Diese Auffassung bestimmte die verhängnisvolle parallelistische Lehre *Spinozas*. Nach ihr wären physische Welt und psychische Welt zwei genau parallele Welten von absolut identischer Strukturform, Seinsform wie Gesetzesform, nur aus grundverschiedenem Material gebaut.
>
> Das ist aber, genau besehen, geradezu absurd. Und nicht bloß darum, was schon genügte, weil die Seelen nicht einen eigenseelischen Raum haben, der darin seine seelische Geometrie hätte, <sondern auch > weil dieser Auffassung ebensogut zu sagen wäre, daß sich die Körper im parallelen seelischen Raum verräumlichen, sich in den Seelen durch Ausdruck darstellen < müßten > etc., so wie sich die Seelen im physischen Raum verleiblichen und sich physisch verkörpern und ausdrücken. Das alles ist absurd, ebensowohl, wie die ganze metaphysische Ontologie des *Spinoza*, die noch dahinter steckt, abzulehnen ist.

*Note on the text.* From "Einheit aus induktiver Kausalität" on, the file is hard-wrapped with line-end hyphens, joined here; the angle brackets are the editor's. Only the name *Spinoza* is italic in the file. The commentary's italics on "parallelistic doctrine of Spinoza", "absolutely identical structural form", "downright absurd", "soulful geometry" and "spatialize themselves" are its own.

The paragraph before the passage states the parallelism Husserl needs. The embodiment of psychic being in the spatial world "presupposes that parallel-ordered complexes and courses of states" of body and soul "are inductively-causally linked with one another", so that "the souls, considered purely for themselves, must carry in themselves an inductively effective typicality" (Hua IX 138, src L2633). The soul has to be regular enough to be coordinated with a regular body. The passage's first sentence grants all of this and turns on the words "by no means" (*keineswegs*), said twice. The structural form of each layer "alone makes possible an overall unity of parallelistic body-soul construction", and that "by no means says" that body and soul are "actually parallel throughout" (*wirklich durchgängig parallel*), "of a throughout parallel structure of being". "Throughout" is the word the refused doctrine adds. A parallel of coordinated states is compatible with very different structures behind each series. "More precisely" (*Genauer gesprochen*) names the difference. The body, as physical thing, is "nothing other at all than" (*überhaupt nichts anderes als*) an inductive unity. The soul is coordinated with such a unity; "by no means" does that make it "a merely real unity out of inductive causality". The adverb "merely" (*bloß*) carries the claim. The soul is an inductive unity too, as §23's preceding paragraph required, but not only that. What else it is, the passage does not say, and the lectures supply it in §24 and in the reflective analyses of §§28 ff.

The error is traced to a "prejudice" (*Vorurteil*), and its content is a chain of "therefore" and "correlatively": natural science is the prototype of all objective science, "therefore" every objective science has the same style, and "correlatively" every objective being has "exactly the peculiarities of physical reality". The parenthetical "and that meant" (*und das sagte*) narrows objective being to "objectively spatial reality". Once that is granted, anything with a place in space must be nature, and the soul, which §21 gave a place by animation, follows. The prejudice is attributed to the beginnings of psychology "with Descartes and Hobbes", and Spinoza's doctrine is its consequence: "two exactly parallel worlds of absolutely identical structural form … built only out of fundamentally different material". The phrase reverses what Husserl has just granted. He allowed one parallel ordering of states and denied identity of structure. Spinoza asserts identity of structure and difference only of material.

The reductio has two parts. The first, "which would already suffice", is that souls have no space of their own with "its psychic geometry". The second carries more of the lectures' argument. If the two worlds were exactly parallel, bodies would have to "spatialize themselves" (*sich verräumlichen*) in a psychic space and "present themselves in the souls through expression", as souls embody themselves and express themselves physically. The words come from §21. There animation was "the way in which spirituality gains locality in the spatial world, so to speak its spatialization" (Hua IX 132, src L2569), and the spirit "makes itself known" in bodily expression (row 9). Expression runs one way. The soul is spatialized in the body and expresses itself through it; nothing physical expresses itself in a soul. A strict parallelism would have to make the relation run both ways, and §21's analysis shows that it does not. The absurdity is not a contradiction in Spinoza's system. It is a conflict with what animal experience gives as the sense of embodiment. The next paragraph says so: "we do not construct metaphysically" but unfold "the sense enclosed in natural world-experience" of its objects, among them "the souls appearing in it as animation" (src L2667–2671).

The final sentence distinguishes two verdicts, which the commentary's block merges. "All that is absurd", the strict parallelism and its consequence, "just as (*ebensowohl, wie*) the whole metaphysical ontology of Spinoza, which still lies behind it, is to be rejected" (*abzulehnen ist*). The doctrine is called absurd; the ontology is to be rejected. The block renders the sentence "All of this is absurd, just as is the whole metaphysical ontology of Spinoza", which calls the ontology absurd too (logged under Corrections). The difference fits the lectures' method. An inference that contradicts the sense of experience can be shown absurd from experience. A metaphysics is set aside, not refuted, by a science that does not construct.

The refusal is what §24 builds on. There the naturalistic picture of the soul, a realm of elements in "a psychic-real connection" under "exclusively inductive regulation", is named a prejudice that "must fall" (Hua IX 143, src L2710–2716). The sentence that the somatological soul has "a closed nature-side" (src L2714) keeps what §23 granted, the inductive coupling of sensation and body, and §24's "but the concrete and full world does not have the mere style of a nature" repeats what it refused. Husserl's own parallelism of §3f (row 4) uses the same word for something of the opposite kind, one content in two attitudes, not two realities side by side. The *Krisis* later returns to Spinoza from the history of science. Spinoza's *Ethics* is "the first universal ontology", through which, he thought, natural science and "the psychology to be built similarly as parallel to it" would gain their real systematic sense (*die als Parallele zu ihr ähnlich aufzubauende Psychologie*, Hua VI 66, §11, `husserl-hua-6-die-krisis-der-europaischen-wissenschaften-und-die-transzendentale-phanomenologie.md`:1188). The *Krisis* section is titled for dualism as "the foundation of naturalistic psychology". The parallel that §23 refuses as an inference from experience, the *Krisis* explains as the programme of a rationalism that took natural science as its model.

## §28, Hua IX 159–160: extension without space

Reads [§28, Paragraph 12](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#carrying-out-the-reflective-turn-of-the-gaze-onto-the-subjective-the-perception-of-physical-things-in-the-reflective-attitude).

A perspective has extent but no place. When a cube's square face is seen from an angle, the square presents itself in a shading-shape (*Abschattungsgestalt*) with unequal angles, and that shape spreads out, part corresponding to part. But it is not a second, smaller square somewhere in space, nor a picture of the square in a space of images. Husserl calls presenting (*darstellen*) a relation between the subjective and the objective that is not a relation between two things in space, and he gives it the adverb "un-spatially" (*unräumlich*). Everything the lectures later say about hyletic data, the sensation field and the object as an irreal pole presupposes that the presentation of a thing in its perspectives is not depiction.

> Thus we have to establish here, and to keep in view for ever, that the objective shape, the objective extension, presents itself perspectivally in shading-shapes, in a correlative co-extensive of the shading kind: but in such a way that these shading-shapes are not themselves shapes in space. They too have a spread, their kind of extension, but the self-extending of the perspectives of something spatial is no self-extending in space. If we call what presents itself the objective of the perception (though in other contexts it may rightly be called subjective again), then in relation to it its subjective shadings are not in the same space, but also not in a second, let alone a depictive, space. The perspectives that belong to perception itself are not silhouettes of the object on a board. A board is itself objective in space and is perceivable only through subjective perspectives of it. So its drawn-in silhouettes, too, are perceivable only through perspectives that are not themselves silhouettes. Silhouettes are objective images of something objective. Perspectives of something objectively spatial, by contrast, present it subjectively, un-spatially.

German:

> So haben wir hier festzustellen und für immer im Auge zu behalten, daß die objektive Gestalt, die objektive Extension sich perspektivisch darstellt in Abschattungsgestalten, in einem korrelativen abschattungsmäßig Koextensiven: derart aber, daß diese Abschattungsgestalten nicht selbst Gestalten im Raum sind. Auch sie haben eine Ausbreitung, ihre Art der Extension, aber das Sich-ausdehnen der Perspektiven von Raumlichem ist kein Sich-ausdehnen im Raum. Nennen wir das, was sich darstellt, das Objektive der Wahrnehmung (mag es auch in anderen Zusammenhängen mit Recht wieder Subjektives heißen), so sind in Relation dazu seine subjektiven Abschattungen nicht in demselben, aber auch nicht in einem zweiten, etwa gar abbildlichen Raum. Die zur Wahrnehmung selbst gehörigen Perspektiven sind nicht Schattenrisse des Objekts auf einer Tafel. Eine Tafel ist selbst objektiv im Raum und ist nur wahrnehmbar durch subjektive Perspektiven von ihr. Also auch ihre eingezeichneten Schattenrisse sind nur wahrnehmbar durch Perspektiven, die nicht selbst Schattenrisse sind. Schattenrisse sind objektive Abbilder von Objektivem. Hingegen Perspektiven eines objektiv Räumlichen stellen dieses subjektiv, unräumlich dar.

*Note on the text.* The passage lies in one of the stretches where the print's margin line numbers came through as page markers: `<!-- 5 -->` stands before "Recht" and `<!-- 10 -->` before "Hingegen". They are omitted here. The text-critical apparatus places "nicht in demselben" [not in the same] at Hua IX 160, line 2, so the passage begins at the foot of p. 159, a paragraph after the file's marker for p. 160 (src L11652). The apparatus also records the four sentences on silhouettes, from "Die zur Wahrnehmung" to "von Objektivem" (160,3–9), as an addition to the manuscript (*Ergänzung*), without dating it. Its entry for 160,2 is garbled in the file and is not used. The file has "Raumlichem" without its umlaut; it is quoted as the file has it and read as *Räumlichem* (something spatial). Nothing in the passage is italic in the file. The commentary's italics on "shading-shapes", "not themselves shapes in space" and "un-spatially" are its own, and the loss of spaced type means the file cannot show that Husserl emphasized nothing here.

The passage has no vocabulary of its own for what it describes. It takes the words of space, shape (*Gestalt*), extension (*Extension*), the co-extensive (*Koextensives*), and marks them with "shading": shading-shapes, a co-extensive "of the shading kind" (*abschattungsmäßig*). The compounds keep the correspondence and refuse the identity. "Correlative" says that the shading-shape is extended as the shape is, part for part, and the next paragraph spells out that "every division in the perspective itself, in its own spread, is itself again presenting for a corresponding part of the objective shape" (Hua IX 160, src L2907). "Not themselves shapes in space" then withholds the one thing the shared word suggests. The concession that follows is exact. The perspectives "too have a spread, their kind of extension", and even their "self-extending" (*Sich-ausdehnen*) is granted; only "in space" is denied. Husserl sets this distinction into fixed terms a page later, and he does it by revising. The objective colour "extends itself" (*sich … ausdehnt*) in objective extension, while each subjective colour "spreads" (*sich … ausbreitet*) in the subjective shape, "that is, in the shading of the objective" (Hua IX 161, src L2915). In the first writing of that sentence, the perspectival shape still offered "shaping and extension" (*Gestaltung und Ausdehnung*) for the perspectival colour, which spread over "this 'subjective' spread", the adjective in quotation marks (161,2–8, src L11652). The revision takes extension (*Ausdehnung*) away from the perspective and drops the quotation marks. Spread (*Ausbreitung*) becomes the word for extent without place.

The middle sentence names its two terms provisionally. "If we call what presents itself the objective of the perception" fixes a relative title, and the parenthesis warns that the same perceived object "may rightly be called subjective again" in other contexts. The whole field is the subjective, the theme the reflective turn has just opened, and within it "objective" names only the presented side of one relation. The words "rightly" (*mit Recht*) return in §34, where Husserl defends the Scholastics' name "immanent object" for the object of consciousness: "the right of this designation is of course the same as the right of the designation 'subjective'" (Hua IX 177, src L3118). The negation that follows has two steps. The shadings are not in the object's space, and "not in a second, let alone a depictive (*abbildlichen*), space". The second step is aimed at the theory of perception by inner images, and "let alone" (*etwa gar*) marks it as the worse error.

The argument against the image theory is a regress, and it is an addition to the manuscript. A silhouette on a board is itself an objective thing in space and can be seen only through perspectives of the board and of what is drawn on it. If perspectives were silhouettes, seeing a silhouette would need further silhouettes without end. The distinction is between two verbs: silhouettes are "objective images" (*Abbilder*) of something objective, perspectives "present" (*stellen … dar*). Before the addition, the paragraph passed from the denial of a depictive space straight to its last sentence. The regress shows only that perspectives cannot be images; it does not show that they have no space of any kind. The final "by contrast" (*Hingegen*) asserts the positive claim, "subjectively, un-spatially", and the paragraph gives no argument for the second adverb. Thirty lines earlier the quotation marks on "distorts" (*„verzerrt“*), said of the right angle that appears ever more obtuse as the cube turns (Hua IX 159, src L2899), already held the image vocabulary at a distance. Distortion is a relation between a spatial original and a spatial copy, and the passage now says why that cannot be the relation here.

The paragraphs before the passage set it up as a rule of description. The phenomenologist must "at no point go beyond the perceived as such", and must also "overlook nothing that is constitutive for it" (Hua IX 159, src L2903). "Thus" (*So*) draws the passage from that rule. To read the perspective as a small shape in space would add to the perceived what is not given in it. The phrase "establish and keep in view for ever" marks a standing caution rather than a result.

The lectures then make good the missing argument, and change the passage's terms as they do. In §29 the perspectives of single things join into a total perspective, and Husserl finds in it "a fixed form … as a space-analogue (*Raumanalogon*), in which the constant objective form, perceptual space itself, shades itself" (Hua IX 162, src L2929). That form is the visual sensation field. Husserl's pencil note beside these pages reads: "The whole order of the investigation would have to be changed" (162,19ff., src L11652). Unlike perceived space, it "can never be extended to infinity", and "here we never get beyond a two-dimensional field, and a finitely closed one" (Hua IX 162–163, src L2933). The shading-shapes' "kind of extension" now has a description: two-dimensional, finite and closed. §30 supplies the reason the passage withheld. "It would be wrong to say that objective space presents itself in a two-dimensional space, say even in something like a plane or other surface. A space is a form of real, thing-like objects, and the possibility of motion belongs to it", while "in the sensation field the concept of a motion makes no sense" (Hua IX 164, src L2961). The field is not a space because nothing can move in it. That is the argument for "un-spatially", and it is drawn from the essence of space, not from the image regress. The commentary's §30 block does not record it (see Corrections).

The same pages split the word "perspective". At the end of §29 the hyletic data enter as "pure sensation data" that "have their own being, a non-objective being. They themselves are not perspectives, but they become such through what we also call apprehension" (Hua IX 163, src L2938). The margin there reads "cf. next lecture instead" (163,13ff.), and §30 restates the point (src L2979–2981). In §28 "perspective" named the shading-shape as it functions in presenting. Now the datum and its function come apart. The datum has the spread, and the apprehension gives it the character of "appearance *of*". §31 then makes the shading function "a special case" of the hyletic datum's role as matter for consciousness (Hua IX 167, src L2997). §34 draws the line the passage first drew in spatial terms between the reell and the irreell. "Only the appearing is reell immanent, that is, only the hyletic data and the characters of consciousness-of that belong to them and are adapted to one another" (Hua IX 173, src L3094), while the object is the irreal pole of their synthesis. Presentation was "un-spatial" in §28; by §34 the divide between presenting and presented is a divide between two kinds of being.

*Ideas I* had drawn the same line by genus. In §41 the shading, "though named the same, is in principle not of the same genus as the shaded. Shading is lived experience. But lived experience is possible only as lived experience and not as something spatial", and to take the shape-shading of a triangle "for something spatial and possible in space" is a "countersense" (*Widersinn*) (Hua III/1 86, `husserl-hua-3-1-ideen-i-allgemeine-einfuhrung-in-die-reine-phanomenologie-recheck.md`:1931–1939; the file is unreviewed, and Kersten's English at `husserl-cw2-…`:1163 agrees). The 1913 argument is categorical. A lived experience cannot be spatial, so its components cannot be either. The 1925 lectures concede more and argue differently. They grant the shading-shape "its kind of extension", they meet the image theory with a regress, and they find the difference from space by describing the field, which is two-dimensional, finite and without motion. *Ideas I* noted that shading and shaded share a name. The lectures build the shared name into the compound "shading-shape", and then show by description that its extension is not extension in space.

## §31, Hua IX 166–167: hyle as a role

Reads [§31, Paragraph 6](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#hyle--hyletic-data-as-matter-for-intentional-functions).

In these lectures *hyle* names a role, not a kind of content. A datum is hyletic because it can be the core of a function of consciousness, the matter through which something other than itself is made conscious. Shading, the function the lectures have described since §28, is one such function among others. The term is introduced to widen the concept of the sensation datum to every datum that can play this role, and to free it from the word "sensation". The definition is unsettled in one respect. The data are named for what they can do in consciousness, and they are also said to contain nothing of consciousness in themselves. The manuscript shows the second claim added to the first.

> Hyletic data are colour data, tone data, smell data, pain data and so on, considered purely subjectively, that is, here without thinking of the bodily organs and of the psychophysical.
>
> The expression hyle indicates this being-core (being matter for functions of consciousness). A special case is then the mode of functioning in which the subjectively perceptual datum is shading, and so becomes the perceptual appearance of something objective.
>
> The general concept hyle then offers the utmost widening of the concept of the sensation datum, a concept to be drawn from the purely subjective sphere, and it removes all the confusions that force themselves on us with the blurred, ambiguous word "sensation".

German:

> Hyletische Daten sind Farbendaten, Tondaten, Geruchsdaten, Schmerzdaten usw. rein subjektiv betrachtet, also hier ohne an die Leibesorgane und an Psychophysisches zu denken.
>
> Der Ausdruck Hyle deutet dieses Kern-sein (Materie für Bewußtseinsfunktionen sein) an. Ein besonderer Fall ist dann diejenige Funktionsweise, in der das subjektiv wahrnehmungs-mäßige Datum abschattendes ist und so zur Wahrnehmungserscheinung von einem Objektiven wird.
>
> Der allgemeine Begriff Hyle bietet dann die äußerste Erweiterung für den aus der rein subjektiven Sphäre zu schöpfenden Begriff des Empfindungsdatums und beseitigt alle mit dem verschwommenen vieldeutigen Wort „Empfindung“ sich aufdrängenden Vermengungen.

*Note on the text.* "wahrnehmungs-mäßige" is a line-break hyphen carried into the file, read as *wahrnehmungsmäßige* (perceptual). Nothing in the passage is italic in the file. The text-critical apparatus (src L11652) changes how the passage reads in three places. The first sentence, the list of hyletic data with its exclusion of the bodily organs, is an addition to the manuscript (*Ergänzung*, 166,37–167,2), as are both clauses in the preceding paragraph that call the data free of all characters of consciousness "in their own essence" (166,24–26 and 166,35–36). The parenthesis in the second sentence is the editor's. The manuscript reads "deutet dieses Kern-, Materie- für Bewußtseinsfunktionen Sein an" [indicates this core-being, matter-for-functions-of-consciousness-being], two compounds on one "being". And that sentence originally went on after "something objective": "to something given in person precisely in the manner of shading" (167,7–8). The apparatus does not date the additions.

The expression *hyle* "indicates" (*deutet … an*) this being-core. Husserl does not say what *hyle* means. He gives no etymology and does not translate the Greek; the word points to a role he has already described. In the manuscript the role is a way of being: "core-being, matter-for-functions-of-consciousness-being", two hyphenated compounds sharing a final "being" (*Sein*). The editor recast the second compound as a parenthesis, and the printed sentence now looks like a word followed by its gloss. The key-passage list's reason, that the term "enters by a gloss on the word", rests on that typography. Husserl's own wording does not gloss the Greek. It coins two names for one function, and "matter" (*Materie*) is the first of them translated into German. The paragraph before uses the same vocabulary. The data are "core-contents" (*Kerngehalte*) and "matters for modes of consciousness that lead beyond them", and the functional characters make other objects conscious "by means of these core-contents, and as it were spiritualizing them" (*sie gleichsam vergeistigend*, Hua IX 166, src L2991). "As it were" holds the image of a soul entering matter at a distance.

The added first sentence fixes the extent of the class, and its list shows that the role is wider than perception. Pain data stand beside colour and tone data. A pain presents no thing in shadings, so the class of hyletic data cannot be defined by the shading function. The second sentence states this as a consequence: shading "is then a special case". "Then" (*dann*) reverses the order in which the lectures found the matter. Shading came first, and §30 gained "the concept of the sensation datum" by "taking together all data-genera suited to the shading function" (Hua IX 166, src L2983). It then announced "a step further". The shading character is "only a special case of a general wondrous occurrence", a datum that makes "something different from it" conscious, as when a datum "reminds of another or points to another" without presenting it (src L2985). *Hyle* is the name of that further step. The "utmost widening" (*äußerste Erweiterung*) of the third sentence widens the concept from data that can shade to data that can bear any function of consciousness. It keeps the concept "to be drawn from the purely subjective sphere".

The confusions of the word "sensation" are not spelled out, but the added first sentence names one: "without thinking of the bodily organs and of the psychophysical". "Sensation" carries the sense organ and the stimulus with it, so the word belongs to the psychophysics that the reflective turn of §28 set aside. A second is the one §28 guarded against: the word can name the colour that shades and the colour shaded alike. §28 kept the colour of the thing apart from the colour perspective, and the hyletic datum is the one that is "not itself objectively perceived" (src L2991).

The definition rests on two claims that do not support each other. The data are named for their role, "being matter for functions of consciousness". And they are "in their own essence free of all characters of consciousness", which the manuscript adds twice. §30 gave the evidence for the second claim. "We cannot even say that such data must necessarily stand in such a shading function." The same or similar data change their function in doubt and illusion, "and then present something different objectively" (Hua IX 165, src L2979). That shows the function to be variable. It does not show a datum with no function at all. Husserl does not claim it does: the possibility of data without shading function is "suggested" (*nahegelegt*). The additions turn a suggestion into a statement of essence.

The lectures go on to treat the hyletic datum as an object in its own right, which the definition required. In §32 "we could single out the hyletic data from the perspectives and make them into subjective objects for themselves, disregarding what makes them shadings" (Hua IX 169, src L3023). In §34 "only the hyletic data and the characters of consciousness-of that belong to them" are reell immanent (Hua IX 173, src L3094). In §36 a hyletic datum, "taken not as the shading of something objective but purely in its own being", is "an object of perception in every reflection that singles it out" (Hua IX 185, src L3204). Matter for consciousness becomes an immanent object of consciousness, and the role-name stays attached to it. A pencil note in the margin a few lines after the passage reads: "But much in what follows is mistaken. Rethink. Only the next lectures give something better" (167,26ff., src L11652). It stands beside the turn to immanence and transcendence, not beside the definition.

*Ideas I* already called *hyle* a "functional concept". In §85 Husserl needs "a new term that expresses the whole group through the unity of function and the contrast to the forming characters", and chooses "hyletic or material data" (*hyletische oder stoffliche Data*, Hua III/1 194, `husserl-hua-3-1-ideen-i-allgemeine-einfuhrung-in-die-reine-phanomenologie-recheck.md`:3859–3864; Kersten's English at `husserl-cw2-…`:2275 agrees). Two things differ in 1925. *Ideas I* pairs the term with "intentional *morphe*", matter with form, and calls the intentional experiences unities "through sense-giving" (*Sinngebung*, Hua III/1 193, :3855). Hua IX §31 has no form. Its counterpart to *hyle* is "intentional characters", functions that a datum bears. The commentary's next block, which calls the two moments "the core-content (sensory matter) and the meaning-giving form", brings back the vocabulary of 1913 (logged under Corrections). The target differs too. In 1913 the new term replaced "primary content" and "sensuous experience"; in 1925 it replaces the word "sensation" and the sense organs that word carries. The lectures keep the functional concept of *Ideas I*, drop the pairing with form, and set the term against the psychophysics of sensation, which a psychology starting from the reflective turn had to leave behind.

## §34, Hua IX 179–180: the object as ideal, not as a piece

Reads [§34, Paragraphs 4–5](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#distinction-of-immanent-and-transcendent-reell-and-irreell-in-perception-the-object-as-irreal-pole).

The perceived object belongs to the stream of perceptions without being a part of it. Husserl calls it "irreal" (*irreell*) against the "reell" parts of experience, the hyletic data and their characters, and "ideal" (*ideell*) because it is numerically one and the same through phases of experience that have nothing reell in common. The passage guards the term against the reading its own wording invites. An irreal object is not an irreal *piece*, a rigid common element running through the experiences and differing from their reell parts only in not being reell. It is a pole that "builds itself up" in the flow, with a structure that grows as perception goes on. Husserl later judged the terminology of the whole section unsuitable, and the passage is the point where that judgment starts.

> Let us tie on again to the ideal containedness of the object in the reell. The matter is by no means this: that this ideal reaches through all the intentional experiences in which it ideally dwells, and in all of whose phases it already dwells, like a rigid piece common to them, only one that is not common to them as a reell piece. Certainly, in the flow of shading perspectives this same and even objectively rigid, unaltered table appears; but while it appears as identical, it appears with ever new marks, in ever new sides. What appears constantly in the unity of each momentary phase of perception is what we call the specially appearing side of the table. But looked at more closely, what appears in each phase is not this mere side of the table; it is rather the perceived table as specially seen from this side.
>
> Likewise, in the whole continuity of shading appearances it is not the sequence of specially perceived sides of the table; rather, in the whole continuity the table <itself>, which presents itself "specially" now from this side, now from that, is the ideal that dwells in the reell continuity of the shadings. And that means here: the perceptual object builds itself up, as we notice, in their flow of experience, and yet not in the manner of a piecing-together, as the image of building up suggests. And let us add at once: in accordance with this building itself up, it has its ideal build, an ideal structure, which widens progressively in the building.

German:

> Knüpfen wir wieder an das ideelle Enthaltensein des Objekts im Reellen an. Die Sache ist keineswegs die, daß dieses Ideelle durch alle intentionalen Erlebnisse, denen es ideell einwohnt und denen es in allen ihren Phasen schon einwohnt, hindurch reicht als wie ein ihnen gemeinsames starres Stück, nur eben als ein solches, das ihnen nicht als reelles Stück gemein ist. Gewiß, im Fluß der abschattenden Perspektiven erscheint dieser und selbe und sogar objektiv starre, unveränderte Tisch; aber während er als identischer erscheint, erscheint er doch mit immer neuen Merkmalen, in immer neuen Seiten. Was konstant in der Einheit jeder momentanen Wahrnehmungsphase erscheint, ist das, was wir speziell erscheinende Tischseite nennen. Aber näher besehen ist ja in jeder Phase nicht diese bloße Tischseite das in ihr Erscheinende, das ist vielmehr der wahrgenommene Tisch als von dieser Seite speziell gesehen.
>
> Ebenso in der ganzen Kontinuität abschattender Erscheinungen ist nicht die Folge der speziell wahrgenommenen Tischseiten, vielmehr in der ganzen Kontinuität ist der Tisch <selbst>, welcher, einmal von dieser, dann von jener Seite sich „speziell“ darstellt, das Ideelle, das der reellen Kontinuität der Abschattungen einwohnt, und das heißt hier: das Wahrnehmungsobjekt baut sich, wie wir merken, in ihrem Erlebnisfluß auf und doch nicht in der Weise eines sich Zusammen-Stückens, wie es das Bild vom Aufbau nahelegt. Und fügen wir gleich bei: diesem Sich-Aufbauen gemäß hat es seinen ideellen Aufbau, eine ideelle Struktur, die sich im Bauen fortschreitend erweitert.

*Note on the text.* "dieser und selbe" is quoted as the file has it and rendered "this same"; a word may be missing, and the reading does not depend on it. The angle brackets are the editor's. Nothing in the passage is italic in the file, and the commentary's italics are its own. The section has no § heading between §34 (src L3042) and §35 (src L3150), so the passage, which falls after the new lecture of Hua IX 178, still belongs to §34. The text-critical apparatus (src L11659) records several changes here and nearby, cited below by Hua page and line.

The passage refuses a picture its own vocabulary has built. §34 has called the object "something irreal" that each phase of the synthesis has "in" itself, the preposition in quotation marks (Hua IX 174, src L3100). A blue-pencil note in the margin of the same page sums it up as "the object irreally enclosed as pole" (174,32ff.). Read that way, "irreal" is an adjective on a part: the object would be a constituent of the experiences, merely of another kind than the data. The first sentence states exactly this view in order to reject it: a "rigid piece common to them, only one that is not common to them as a reell piece". "Only" (*nur eben*) shows how little the view concedes. It changes the piece's status and keeps the piece. The commentary's block takes the clause "it is not common to them as a reell piece" as Husserl's own assertion. In the source it belongs to the view refused (logged under Corrections).

Husserl sets a word family against the picture. The object is "ideally contained" (*ideelles Enthaltensein*) in the reell, and "dwells" (*einwohnt*) in the experiences, twice in one clause. It is not "in" them as a part is in a whole. "Piece" (*Stück*) returns three times: the rigid piece, the reell piece, and "piecing-together" (*Zusammen-Stückens*), the hyphen setting the root apart. Husserl rejects the piece in every form, including the one in which it is built. "Rigid" (*starr*) returns as well, with a turn. The table may be "objectively rigid, unaltered", but its rigidity as a thing is no rigidity of its presence: "while it appears as identical, it appears with ever new marks, in ever new sides". The identity that perception gives is the identity of what keeps being determined.

The two sentences on the sides of the table carry the argument. One would expect the side to be what appears in a phase, and the sequence of sides to make up the table. Husserl denies both. In each phase what appears is "the perceived table as specially seen from this side", and in the whole continuity what appears is the table, which presents itself "specially", in quotation marks, from one side and then another. A side is a mode of the table's appearing, not a component of it, so there is nothing for the phases to piece together. The second paragraph then keeps the image of building it has just disowned: the object "builds itself up" (*baut sich … auf*), "not in the manner of a piecing-together, as the image of building up suggests", and it "has its ideal build, an ideal structure, which widens progressively in the building". Husserl names the image as an image (*das Bild vom Aufbau*) and keeps it for what it shows: the structure grows. The first writing of that sentence promised that "this building itself up" would be discussed "more closely" (180,7–8). §35 does it, in the same words: "If we attend not to the manner of the inner building itself up, but to the build, the object itself", it is "an ideal pole that has the character of the substrate" for its successive marks, and each mark is "itself an ideal pole" (Hua IX 180, src L3152).

The pair of terms comes from the paragraphs before the passage. "Ideal" is justified by identity. The object "could in this respect also be called ideal, because it is evidently identically the same, while the separate phases of experience can contain nothing identical reell" (Hua IX 174, src L3100). The paragraph just before the passage settles the name: whatever appears in the change of experiences belongs to them "inseparably as something irreal or, as can also be said for good reasons, something ideal" (Hua IX 179, src L3138). The first writing had "irreal, if one will, ideal" (179,7–8). Husserl upgraded "ideal" from a permitted alternative to a name with reasons. He does not separate *ideal* from *ideell*: the pole is "ideally identical" (*ideal identischer*, src L3104) and "ideal" (*ideeller*, src L3152) in the same discussion, and the file cannot show a distinction between them. The heading's "irreal pole" and the body's "inseparable ideal pole" (*unabtrennbaren idealen Pol*, Hua IX 177, src L3118) name the same thing from two sides: what it is not (a reell part) and what it is (one identical over many).

The sentence with "ideal pole" is itself late. The defence of the scholastic name, that the Scholastics "not without reason" called the object of consciousness an "immanent object", and that "the right of this designation is of course the same as the right of the designation 'subjective'", ends with that phrase, and from "the right" to "pole" is an addition (176,37–177,2). The first writing claimed the immanence directly: "in the adequately given stream of experience … the intentional object lies enclosed, purely as such, 'immanently'" (176,30–32). The revision moves the claim to the Scholastics and grants it only the relative right of a name. The Scholastics enter the lectures for the second time here. In §3d they had seen intentionality, and Brentano's "seemingly slight turn" made it the mark of the psychic (row 2).

Husserl's own note, attached by order to the sentence that opens the distinction at the end of §33 (Hua IX 171, src L3040; defined at src L3182), withdraws the frame. "That is an unsuitable terminology, as is the grounding of immanence and transcendence on it. Improved in the next lecture." The unsuitability is "that the irreal intentional object as such, the perceived as such, is taken as transcendent", instead of the perceived simply, "whose transcendence consists in its being the believed simply, which can be given fully determined, closed, in no perceptual synthesis however far it is carried". The passage stands at the turn. It keeps "irreal" and "ideal" for the object as perceived, and its growing structure already points to the second transcendence, the open horizon of what is not yet given. §35 confirms "what we predicted last time": the object "is not simply a something that, though as an irreal piece, is stuck in the process of subjective experiences as identical throughout" (Hua IX 183, src L3176). §36, the promised next lecture, gives the improvement. The object is meant "not in the manner of a finished, even if ideally indwelling, datum of perception", but "with a transcendent sense, with an open horizon of sense" (Hua IX 183, src L3186). "Finished" (*fertig*) is the rigid piece once more, now refused for the ideal as well.

*Ideas I* draws the line with the same word. In §97 the reell components of an experience are what is contained in it "as its parts are in a whole, its pieces and its moments that cannot be detached as pieces" (*seine Stücke und unabstückbaren Momente*), and the noema, "with its 'tree' in quotation marks", is "just as little reell contained in the perception as the tree of actuality" (Hua III/1 226, `husserl-hua-3-1-ideen-i-allgemeine-einfuhrung-in-die-reine-phanomenologie-recheck.md`:4418–4425). In 1913 "piece" defines the reell, and the noema is set against it as a correlate with a name of its own. The 1925 lectures have no noema at this point. The word first appears in the heading of §41 (Hua IX 206, src L3399). Without it, §34 has to say what the object is in the vocabulary of parts, "irreal" and "ideal", and then deny that it is a part. The passage does that work. The new lecture of §36 then replaces the vocabulary of containment with that of sense and horizon.

## §37, Hua IX 188: a change of interest, not of belief

Reads [§37, Paragraph 2](../commentaries/husserl/husserliana-9-phenomenological-psychology-commentary.md#the-phenomenological-reduction-as-method-of-unveiling-the-immanent).

The phenomenological reduction changes what we are interested in, not what we believe. To inhibit the positing of a perceived object leaves its certainty as it was. What changes is the theme: the object stops being what experience is out to know, and the perceiving of it becomes the theme instead. Nor is validity abolished. It moves, and the phenomenologist puts into validity only what reflective perception offers. Because the method is defined in this way, it is one method for two aims. §37 does not set a psychological reduction against a transcendental one. It says that "the understanding of the whole of phenomenology" depends on this single method, whichever phenomenology it serves. The commentary's block draws the contrast between two reductions at this point. The source does not (see the list's Corrections).

> On the understanding of this method depends the understanding of the whole of phenomenology; only through it do we gain phenomena in the sense of phenomenology; and this whether we aim (as I recently distinguish) at a philosophically-transcendent phenomenology or, as here, at a psychological phenomenology, which is to deliver to us the purely self-enclosed realm of psychic phenomena in their concrete total unity. The inhibiting, indeed the putting-out-of-validity, of the spatial-thingly perceptual object, of anything transcendent at all, does not mean positing it as invalid, or doubting it, or altering its certainty in any other way. That does not stand at all at our will. Where motives of questionableness, of doubtfulness and so on are not at work, where we lack "grounds" for doubt, there we cannot do otherwise than be conscious of the perceived as certainly-existing; at most we can at will think ourselves into a doubting and the like, and in doing so we also think along, phantasy along, motives for it; we can then also posit being-doubtful, not-being and the like hypothetically. But none of this is to be meant.
>
> It is a matter of something else, likewise at the disposal of our will: simply perceiving, noticing, looking more closely, we are interested in the perceived object, which thus appears as certainly existing and being so; interested in its being-there and being-so, thus in getting to know it, the existent, in gaining by determination the properties that belong to it in its being, in appropriating them habitually as an abiding possession. So the world is, in the natural attitude of perception, our theme of experience.

German:

> Von dem Verständnis dieser Methode hängt das Verständnis der gesamten Phänomenologie ab, nur durch sie gewinnen wir Phänomene im Sinn der Phänomenologie; und das, ob wir es (wie ich neuerdings scheide) auf eine philosophisch-transzendente Phänomenologie abgesehen haben oder wie hier auf eine psychologische Phänomenologie, die uns das rein in sich geschlossene Reich der psychischen Phänomene in deren konkreter Gesamteinheit liefern soll. Das Inhibieren, ja das Außer-Geltung-setzen des raumdinglichen Wahrnehmungsobjektes, eines Transzendenten überhaupt, besagt nicht, es als ungültig setzen oder es bezweifeln oder sonstwie seine Gewißheit verändern. Das steht durchaus nicht in unserer Willkür. Wo nicht Motive der Fraglichkeit, der Zweifelhaftigkeit usw. wirksam sind, wo es uns an Zweifels-„Gründen“ fehlt, da können wir gar nicht anders als das Wahrgenommene als Gewiß-Daseiendes bewußt zu haben, höchstens können wir uns willkürlich in ein Zweifeln und dergleichen hineindenken, womit wir eben auch Motive dafür mitdenken, mitphantasieren; wir können dann auch das Zweifelhaft-sein, Nicht-sein und dergleichen hypothetisch ansetzen. Aber all das soll nicht gemeint sein.
>
> Es handelt sich um anderes, ebenfalls unserer Willkür zu Gebote Stehendes: schlechthin wahrnehmend, gewahrend, näher betrachtend sind wir am wahrgenommenen, also als gewiß-daseiend und soseiend erscheinenden Objekt interessiert; interessiert an seinem Da-sein und So-sein, also es, das Seiende kennenzulernen, die ihm seinsmäßig zukommenden Eigenschaften bestimmend zu gewinnen, sie uns habituell zuzueignen als bleibende Habe. So ist die Welt in der natürlichen Einstellung der Wahrnehmung unser Erfahrungsthema.

*Note on the text.* The file reads *philosophisch-transzendente* (philosophically-transcendent), and Scanlon has "philosophically transcendental". The text-critical apparatus records no change on Hua IX 188: its entries run from 187,31 to 189,1 (src L11771). It therefore offers neither a correction nor a later hand, and the print is not in the corpus. The rendering follows the file, and nothing below argues from the suffix. The marker for p. 189 stands before the second paragraph, but the apparatus puts "die Welt in der natürlichen Einstellung der Wahrnehmung" at 189,1–2, so the paragraph begins on p. 188. Those words are an addition. The first writing of that sentence read "So ist sie" (so it is our theme of experience), where "it" is the object. Nothing in the passage is italic in the file, and the commentary's italics are its own. The passage follows the seam at the opening of §37, where the body text continues inside a footnote (src L3222, L3227), and it is not affected by it.

Two operations on validity are set side by side and kept apart. "Putting-out-of-validity" (*Außer-Geltung-setzen*) is a hyphenated coinage built against "positing as invalid" (*als ungültig setzen*). The second is a new positing with a negative content, a modalization of belief. The first withdraws a positing from play and puts nothing in its place. The "indeed" (*ja*) raises the stakes from "inhibiting", a mere checking, to a phrase that sounds like cancellation, and Husserl at once forbids that reading three times: not invalid, not doubted, not "otherwise altered" in its certainty. The sentence before the passage shows where the validity goes. The phenomenologists "inhibit every perceptual positing of the object", yet "only the perception of this outer perception … is the medium of the validity exercised by us; or only that do we put into validity which offers itself in this medium" (src L3227). The reduction posits too. It confines validity to reflective perception.

The argument turns on "will" (*Willkür*), which recurs three times. Certainty "does not stand at all at our will", because belief answers to motives. Without "grounds" for doubt, a word the quotation marks hold at a distance, as motives rather than reasons, we cannot help taking the perceived as certainly there. What is at our will is to "think ourselves into" doubting, to "phantasy along" its motives, and to posit not-being hypothetically. All of this is dismissed: "none of this is to be meant". The interest that the reduction changes is "likewise" (*ebenfalls*) at our will. The adverb sets interest beside imagined doubt, not beside belief. Belief follows motives, interest follows the will, and the reduction works on the second. The inference is weaker than it looks. The next paragraph grants that the change of interest is "an essential change of the way in which … the perception is in execution, and in particular how its belief is in execution, how its object counts for us" (*wie ihr Objekt uns gilt*, Hua IX 189, src L3239). Certainty is left untouched, but its mode of execution is not. The whole method rests on that difference, and §37 does not analyse it.

"Interest" has a precise sense here. To be interested in the object is to be out "to get to know it", to determine its properties and to appropriate them "habitually as an abiding possession". This is the "possession" (*Habe*, in quotation marks) of the paragraph that opens §37's account: to have the perceived "simply" is to have it as existing (src L3220). The reduction stops a striving toward knowledge, not an assent. It is needed for a reason given just before. Natural reflection "transcends the perception itself", because our certainty of being also bears on "the object that is irreal over against the perception" and on infinities that only endless new perceiving would make real (Hua IX 187, src L3220; the first writing had only "the open infinity of possible new perceptions", 187,26–29). That is the transcendent object as "idea" of §36 (Hua IX 186, src L3210), and of row 13's ideal pole. The reduction is the method of staying inside a perception whose own meaning reaches beyond it. §38 then says that all the pure descriptions so far were already made "in phenomenological reduction, only that we had not made this scientifically clear" (Hua IX 192, src L3265). §37 names a practice the course had already been following.

The apparatus divides the section into layers. The passage is the 1925 lecture text. Much of the vocabulary that now surrounds it is later. The sentence "The radicalism of the phenomenological reduction consists in this, that every interest in objective being is consistently suppressed" is an addition (189,29–32). So are "In phenomenology we say: the object in brackets or quotation marks, not the object simply" (190,11–13, src L3241, the sentence row 14's reason cites) and "Speaking in a simile: every experiencing and its object of being receives the bracket" (190,22–28). So is "Just this striving directedness is forbidden in phenomenology" (190,38–39). The whole exposition of the two attitudes replaces a passage struck through in blue pencil (190,1–191,19). Its first version had the phenomenologist "posit the object purely as a transcendence-meaning in outer perceiving and hold it in validity" (*setzen und in Geltung halten*, src L11771). The world, too, comes in later. In the lecture the passage ends with the object as the theme of experience. The revision makes it "the world in the natural attitude of perception" and adds a paragraph on being interested in "the objective world … presupposed as idea" (189,3–13). As first delivered, the reduction is practised "on every outer, transcendent perception" (src L3218), one object at a time. The later layers bring in the brackets of *Ideas I* and extend the reduction to the world.

The parenthesis "as I recently distinguish" places the two aims outside the method. §3 had made the same point from the other side. "An essentially similar phenomenological method" can found a natural a priori psychology "if one only omits the radical change of attitude in which the transcendental first becomes theme" (Hua IX 45, src L932). The lectures name the further step once, in §44: "One step further in the universal consideration of the world, by carrying out the transcendental-philosophical reduction, and we make the world as such the theme, together with every natural consideration of the world" (Hua IX 222, src L3734–3738). The difference between the two phenomenologies is the scope of the reduction, whether it reaches the world as a whole, not a second kind of method. §45 then marks the limit that §37's definition by interest needs. Each of the natural and the human sciences leaves the other's domain out of its theme, yet the humanist "performs no phenomenological reduction with regard to nature, that is, thematic exclusion does not mean the putting-out-of-validity of experienced nature in the sense of the phenomenological epoché" (Hua IX 232, src L4011–4014). Nature "is not in brackets" but "constantly presupposed" (src L3987–3989). A change of interest alone, then, does not make a reduction. The later sentence on "radicalism", "every interest in objective being", supplies the missing condition: the exclusion has to be universal and consistent. Beilage XXI, which the edition assigns to §37, sets out the two reductions as contrasting attitudes (row 23).

*Ideas I* reached the same operation by another route. Its §31 starts from Descartes' attempt at universal doubt. That attempt "belongs to the realm of our perfect freedom" (*gehört in das Reich unserer vollkommenen Freiheit*, Hua III/1 62), and the epoché is lifted out of it as a "certain abstention from judgment" that we practise "in full freedom" and that is compatible with "unshaken" conviction. In the epoché "the thesis is 'put out of action', bracketed" (Hua III/1 64, `husserl-hua-3-1-ideen-i-allgemeine-einfuhrung-in-die-reine-phanomenologie-recheck.md`:1545, :1554). In 1913 freedom lies in the attempt to doubt, and the bracket modifies the thesis. In 1925 doubting is not at our will, imagined doubt is set aside, and freedom lies in interest. The reduction becomes a narrowing of the theme. Both texts keep conviction intact. What changes is where Husserl locates the act, and with it the image. In the lecture the image is thematic: the object is "released from the thematic domain, put out of reckoning" (src L3239). The brackets of 1913 return to §37 only in Husserl's later additions, and there they arrive with the words "simile" and "forbidden". That leaves a tension the lectures do not settle. A reduction defined by interest serves either aim. But as §45 shows, the definition is too wide until it is made universal, and once it is made universal it comes close to the step that §44 reserves for transcendental philosophy.

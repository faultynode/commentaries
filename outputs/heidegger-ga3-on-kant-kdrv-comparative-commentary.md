# Heidegger, *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* (GA 3), read against Kant, *Kritik der reinen Vernunft*

Heidegger's Kantbook is cited by the page numbers of GA 3 (Klostermann, 1991), read from the running heads of the German text; the prefaces carry the volume's roman pagination. Each unit is one of Heidegger's paragraphs, numbered within its preface, the Introduction or its section and followed by its GA 3 page ("¶3, 6–7"). Where several short paragraphs make one step they are treated together and the unit says so. Kant is cited by the A and B pagination of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, as Heidegger cites him. Heidegger used Raymund Schmidt's Meiner edition of 1926, which prints the two editions side by side, and he cites "A" and "B" together throughout (GA 3, 1 n. 1). Kant is quoted in the Cambridge translation of Paul Guyer and Allen Wood, checked against the German of 1781 and 1787. Heidegger is quoted in Richard Taft's translation (fifth edition, 1997), whose margins carry the GA 3 pages. The copy of Taft in the corpus is a damaged conversion, so every quotation from it has been checked against the German; where the copy is corrupt, the English is this commentary's own and is marked as a working rendering. German appears only where a term carries the argument or where the English hides a shift. The two translations mark the two voices with different words: Guyer and Wood render Kant's *Erkenntnis* as "cognition", Taft renders Heidegger's as "knowledge". The German word is the same. This commentary keeps "cognition" when it reports Kant and "knowledge" when it reports Heidegger. The commentary on the *Critique* that this run reads alongside the text covers the prefaces, both introductions, the Transcendental Aesthetic and the Transcendental Logic as far as §24 of the B-Deduction; for the A-Deduction, the Schematism, the Principles, the Dialectic and the Doctrine of Method, this commentary works from Kant's text alone. The other writings of Kant that Heidegger cites (the *Prolegomena*, the essay on the progress of metaphysics, the metaphysics lectures, the *Logic*, the *Anthropology*, the reply to Eberhard, the *Nachlass*) are not in the corpus, and quotations from them are reported, not checked. The *Critique of Practical Reason* is in the corpus. The range is the four prefaces and the main text, Introduction to §45; the six appendices are not treated.

## Prefaces

The pair has its own traps, and this commentary guards against them throughout. GA 3 speaks in four voices: Kant quoted, Kant paraphrased under Heidegger's interpretation, Heidegger's own fundamental ontology, and the later Heidegger of the marginal notes in his copy and of the 1973 preface, who corrects the book from outside it. Each claim has to be assigned before its relation to Kant can be judged. Heidegger cites "A, B" together even where a passage exists in one edition only. His central thesis, that Kant shrank back from the imagination between 1781 and 1787, depends on reading A against B: the A-Deduction (A 95–130) has no B text, and B 67–69, B 72 and §§15–27 of the B-Deduction have no A text. Heidegger's key words are not Kant's. Finitude, ontological and ontic knowledge, the understanding of being, transcendence, letting-stand-against, the look and taking-in-stride each re-inscribe a Kantian term. The commonest slide runs from Kant's "transcendental", which concerns our a priori mode of cognition (B 25), to Heidegger's "transcendence", the stepping-over of finite reason to beings. Kant reserves the overstepping of experience for the "transcendent" and insists that "transcendental and transcendent are not the same" (A 296/B 352). Kant leaves the common root of sensibility and understanding unknown (A 15/B 29); Heidegger names it, and each step toward naming it has to be tested against where Kant himself puts the imagination (A 78/B 103, A 94, A 124, B 151–152). Kant's self-affection (B 67–68, B 152–157) is the affection of inner sense by the understanding, introduced to explain why we know ourselves only as we appear. Heidegger's time as pure self-affection (§34) radicalizes it, and his mapping of the threefold synthesis (A 98–110) onto the three dimensions of time (§33) is his. Kant calls the *Critique* a propaedeutic whose speculative use is "only negative" (A 11/B 25) and gives up "the proud name of an ontology" (A 247/B 303); Heidegger reads it as the laying of the ground of ontology. Two errors of method threaten from the other side as well: reading Kant through Heidegger's categories until the *Critique* seems to confirm the reading, and taking every departure for a misreading, when Heidegger announces his departures himself (GA 3, XVII; §35, 202).

### Preface to the Fourth Edition (1973)

**¶1, XIII.** Before the book speaks, a note from Heidegger's copy, dated by its hand to the mid-1930s, states his relation to Kant in shorthand: a "refuge", "not new discoveries in Kant Philology", and under the heading "Schematism" an equation, "[Being] Beingness—Objectness and 'time'", followed by the admission that "the particular way is obstructed and is made susceptible to misinterpretation". The equation is the whole take-up in four words. Kant's schemata are "nothing but *a priori* time-determinations in accordance with rules" (A 145/B 184), and his categories are "concepts of an object in general" (B 128); what they determine is the objectness of objects of experience. Kant does not say that this objectness is being; his one thesis about being calls it "merely the positing of a thing" (A 598/B 626). The note concedes, from a decade later, that the Kantbook read Kant's objectness as beingness and that the identification blocked Heidegger's own path. It belongs to the years of the lecture course on Kant's principles (*Die Frage nach dem Ding*, winter 1935/36), and it is retrospective: it does not describe what the 1929 text thought it was doing. The last line, which the editor expands as "concepts of reflection", points to the Amphiboly (A 260/B 316 ff.), a chapter the Kantbook does not treat.

**¶2, XIV.** Heidegger names the motive for publication: the misunderstanding of the question of being raised in *Being and Time*. While preparing the lecture course of winter 1927/28 he noticed the Schematism chapter and "glimpsed therein a connection between the problem of Categories, that is, the problem of Being in traditional Metaphysics and the phenomenon of time". The "that is" carries the departure. The connection of category and time is Kant's own explicit doctrine: a "transcendental time-determination is homogeneous with the **category** ... insofar as it is **universal** and rests on a rule *a priori*", and with the appearance insofar as time is contained in every empirical representation (A 138–139/B 177–178). What Heidegger glimpsed, Kant had stated. The new step is to take the category as a concept of being, which Kant's own pedigree for it half-licenses ("our aim is basically identical with" Aristotle's, A 79/B 105), and to read its time-determination as the temporal sense of being. *Being and Time* had already said that Kant "as it were shrinks back" before the schematism (SZ 23); "refuge" and "advocate" are two sides of that verdict. An advocate who must first be shown what he shrank from is enlisted, not reported, and the preface says so.

**¶3, XIV.** The refuge, Heidegger goes on, led him to interpret the *Critique* within the horizon of *Being and Time*; "in truth, however, a manner of questioning foreign to Kant's question, though one that conditions it, was laid beneath it" (working rendering: the corpus copy of Taft garbles the sentence). The verb is *unterlegen*, to lay beneath, and it answers the title's *Grundlegung*: the Kantbook laid a ground under Kant. The concession is precise and limited. "Foreign" grants the philologists their point. "Though one that conditions it" keeps the 1929 thesis that the question of being is the unasked condition of Kant's question of the possibility of synthetic judgments a priori. Heidegger retracts the attribution, not the priority.

**¶4, XIV.** In later writings he "attempted to retract the overinterpretation [*Überdeutung*]" without rewriting the book. The word is overinterpretation, not misinterpretation: too much read into Kant, not something false read out of him. The writings meant are named in the 1965 note (below): the lecture course on the principles and the essay on Kant's thesis about being. Both turn to Kant passages the Kantbook passes over, the Analytic of Principles and the thesis that "being is obviously not a real predicate" (A 598/B 626). The retraction works by moving to other Kant texts, not by re-reading the ones the Kantbook used.

**¶5–¶9, XIV–XV.** These paragraphs name Hansgeorg Hoppe's account of the change in Heidegger's reading, Hermann Mörchen's Marburg dissertation on the imagination in Kant (1928), the writing of the book "immediately after" the Davos course of spring 1929, and the appendices that print the Davos lectures and the report of the disputation with Cassirer. They cite no Kant. One of them matters for the comparison. Heidegger says Mörchen's dissertation "supplements" the Kantbook's discussion of the imagination: a parallel reading from his own Marburg circle, contemporary with the lecture course, carries part of the weight of the thesis Kant's text is made to bear. The Davos setting matters too. Cassirer was the editor of the Kant edition Heidegger cites for Kant's other works, and the Kantbook's polemic against reading the *Critique* as epistemology is aimed at his school.

**¶10–¶12, XV.** The Kantbook "remains an introduction, attempted by means of a questionable digression [*Umweg*], to the further questionability which persists concerning the Question of Being". The sentence after it, on a growing "anxiety in the face of thinking" that no longer admits the forgetfulness of being "which determines the age", and the thanks to the publisher and proofreaders, close the preface. Kant's book has become a detour, and the preface's last diagnosis is not Kant's at all. Forgetting of being as an epoch is the later Heidegger's frame; the 1929 text knows forgetting as something that belongs "necessarily and constantly" to Dasein's own understanding of being and is answered by a "re-collection" (§42, 233, working rendering). Read against Kant, the 1973 preface leaves the *Critique* where the 1929 book placed it, at the service of another question, and changes only the verdict on the service: in 1929 a retrieval, by 1973 a detour.

### Preface to the First Edition (1929)

**¶1, XVI.** The interpretation was first given in the four-hour lecture course of winter 1927/28 and then in lectures at Riga (1928) and Davos (1929). The lecture course is GA 25, published only in 1977. It reads the *Critique* in Kant's order through the Aesthetic and the Analytic of Concepts, ends with the A-Deduction, and gives the Schematism only a closing subsection on its "significance". The Kantbook, by contrast, runs Kant's text through five "stages" of its own devising, with the Schematism as the fourth. The course is the fuller philological witness, and the preface does not say so.

**¶2, XVI.** The interpretation "arose in connection with a first working-out of Part Two of *Being and Time*". *Being and Time* had planned that part as a destruction of the history of ontology, with Kant's doctrine of schematism and time as its first stage (SZ 40). The Kantbook is therefore the one published piece of the destruction, and its Kant is chosen in advance for one function: the first, abandoned step toward temporality as the horizon of being. Every Kant passage the book quotes has been selected for that function.

**¶3, XVI.** In Part Two of *Being and Time* "the theme of the following investigation" is to be treated (the German is future; the corpus copy of Taft puts it in the past) on a broader basis, whereas "a progressive interpretation" of the *Critique* is dispensed with there; the present book supplies it as a preparatory supplement. The promise was not kept: Part Two never appeared. What the preface calls a supplement became the only execution, and readers have had to take as Heidegger's reading of Kant what he here offers as the philological side of an argument conducted elsewhere.

**¶4, XVI.** At the same time the book serves as a "'historical' introduction" to the problematic of the first half of *Being and Time*. The quotation marks keep the word away from *Historie*, the record of what Kant said, and toward *Geschichte*, the happening of a tradition that is retrieved. The marks announce the book's licence. Kant is to be read for what his text makes possible for the question of being, not for what he meant to settle about the possibility of metaphysics as science (B 22).

**¶5–¶6, XVI.** The essay *Vom Wesen des Grundes* (1929) is named as a further clarification of the guiding question, and the book is dedicated to Max Scheler, whose last conversation with Heidegger was about its content. The essay traces the concept of world through Kant's "cosmological" sense and finds a second, "existential" sense in Kant's anthropology, a Kant the Kantbook does not use. The dedication sets the book's final target. Scheler's philosophical anthropology is the position §§36–38 reject, when they take up Kant's question "What is the human being?" and argue that it cannot be posed anthropologically. The book is dedicated to the thinker whose way of posing Kant's fourth question it will refuse.

### Preface to the Second Edition (1950) and Preliminary Note to the Third (1965)

**¶1, XVII.** The book reappears unaltered, "in the form in which it has been both successful and unsuccessful in various ways". No Kant locus. The refusal to revise is itself a decision about the relation to Kant: no correction from the scholarship of twenty years is let into the text.

**¶2, XVII.** Readers "have taken constant offense at the violence of my interpretations", and the charge "can indeed be supported by this text". Historical research is even right "each time" it raises the charge against attempts at "a thoughtful dialogue between thinkers", which stands "under other laws" than philology, laws more easily violated. The concession has to be read against the book's own defence of violence. In 1929 Heidegger wrote that "every interpretation must necessarily use violence" to wrest from Kant's words what they want to say, and he rested the claim on Kant: in the reply to Eberhard, Kant charged the historian of philosophy who sees only what earlier philosophers "said" and misses what they "wanted to say" (§35, 201–202). The *Critique* has its own version of the maxim, that one may understand an author "even better than he understood himself" (A 314/B 370), which Kant applies to Plato. In 1950 the philologist Kant's maxim had dismissed is granted a right of his own. The 1929 violence was presented as fidelity to what Kant wanted to say; the 1950 preface presents it as the risk of a different genre.

**¶3–¶4, XVII.** The shortcomings of the attempt have become so clear that Heidegger refuses to patch the book with supplements, and "thinkers learn from their shortcomings to be more persevering" (Taft; the German, *Denkende lernen aus dem Fehlenden nachhaltiger*, says more literally that those who think learn more lastingly from what is lacking). The preface does not say what was lacking in the reading of Kant, though the 1973 preface does: the question of being, and the objectness the book had taken for being. Nothing in the 1950 text names a Kant passage misread.

**¶5–¶6, XVII–XVIII.** The 1965 note explains the title's double genitive: what is the problem *for* metaphysics, "the question concerning beings as such in their totality", lets metaphysics *as* metaphysics become a problem. It then names two supplements, *Kants These über das Sein* and *Die Frage nach dem Ding*. Kant's own "problem of metaphysics" has a single sense: "How is metaphysics possible as science?" (B 22), a question that also admits the answer that it is not possible. Heidegger's doubled title replaces Kant's question of possibility with a question about what metaphysics asks. The two supplements again turn to passages the Kantbook passes over, the thesis on being (A 598/B 626) and the system of principles (A 148–235/B 187–294).

The prefaces, read together, are Heidegger's own commentary on the relation this run examines, and they move in one direction. The 1929 preface presents the Kant interpretation as one stage of the destruction promised in *Being and Time*. In 1950 Heidegger concedes the violence and assigns it to dialogue rather than to philology, against the 1929 claim that the violence served what Kant "wanted to say". In 1965 he points away to other Kant texts, and in 1973 he names the core identification of Kant's objectness with being and retracts it as overinterpretation, while keeping the claim that his question conditions Kant's. None of the later prefaces names a Kant passage misread. The retraction concerns attribution, not any locus, and the units that follow have to supply what the prefaces do not.

## Introduction: The Theme of the Investigation and Its Articulation

**¶1, 1.** The task: to interpret the *Critique* "as a laying of the ground for metaphysics and thus of placing the problem of metaphysics before us as a fundamental ontology". The departure lies in the first phrase. Kant never calls the *Critique* a laying of the ground of metaphysics. He calls it a "propaedeutic to the system of pure reason", a "critique" whose use in regard to speculation is "only negative" (A 11/B 25), and "a treatise on the method, not a system of the science itself" (B xxii). He did use the word *Grundlegung* in 1785, for the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, a book that prepares a metaphysics without being one. Heidegger's title transfers that grounding role to the first *Critique* and makes it positive: what the *Critique* founds is ontology. Kant's own question, whether metaphysics is possible as science at all (B 22), drops out of the formula.

**¶2, 1.** Fundamental ontology is "that ontological analytic of the finite essence of human beings which is to prepare the foundation for the metaphysics which 'belongs to human nature'", the metaphysics of Dasein, distinct from all anthropology, and it poses the concrete question "What is the human being?". The quoted phrase about human nature has not been located in the *Critique*; Kant's nearest is "metaphysics as a natural predisposition" (B 21–22). The question "What is the human being?" is not in the *Critique* either. Kant's canon names three questions (A 805/B 833), and the fourth comes from the *Logic*, outside the corpus. Two of the paragraph's three anchors in Kant are therefore drawn from outside the book it interprets. The genitive "metaphysics of Dasein" copies the form of Kant's architectonic, which divides metaphysics into a metaphysics of nature and a metaphysics of morals (A 841/B 869), and fills it with an object Kant's architectonic does not contain.

**¶3, 1–2.** Laying the ground is not sliding a foundation under a finished building but "the projecting of the building plan itself", the "architectonic circumscription and delineation of the inner possibility of metaphysics". Heidegger rejects the image of a foundation; Kant's text holds both images. The Doctrine of Method opens with the plan Heidegger wants: the Doctrine of Elements has "made an estimate of the building materials", and what remains is the plan of an edifice (A 707/B 735), while the architectonic speaks of outlining "a whole architectonically" (A 835/B 863). But the Dialectic describes the *Critique*'s labour in the image Heidegger excludes: "making the terrain for these majestic moral edifices level and firm enough to be built upon" (A 319/B 375–376). Heidegger takes Kant's architectonic vocabulary, which in Kant belongs to the Doctrine of Method and to the system to come, and makes it describe the *Critique* as a whole. By Kant's own account the Doctrine of Elements estimates materials and makes the terrain firm; it does not draw the plan. The footnote that closes the paragraph records Heidegger's edition: Schmidt prints A and B side by side "in exemplary fashion", and "in what follows" both are always cited. This is the convention that later allows him to set the two editions against each other (§31).

**¶4, 2.** Laying the ground means "letting the supporting power of the already-laid ground become operative", and whether and how this happens is "the criterion of the originality and scope of a ground-laying". No Kant locus. The sentence sets the standard by which Kant will be judged: a ground not allowed to bear has not been laid. Kant's *Critique* has a criterion of its own, "completeness" and "certainty" (A xiii–xv). Heidegger's criterion of originality is not one Kant set himself, and the book's eventual verdict, that Kant reached the ground and shrank back from it, follows from it.

**¶5, 2.** If the interpretation brings the originality of the origin to light, that originality is understood only when the laying of the ground is "retrieved" (*wiederholt*). Kant's text gives retrieval a partial warrant. The *Critique* says that the "systems ... all had their schema, as the original seed, in the mere self-development of reason" and that a philosopher may be understood better than he understood himself (A 834–835/B 862–863; A 314/B 370). Kant uses the second maxim on Plato. The Architectonic adds a third that fits Heidegger's purpose still better: sciences "must not be explained and determined in accordance with the description given by their founder, but rather in accordance with the idea, grounded in reason itself", since the founder may "fumble around with an idea that they have not even made distinct to themselves" (A 834/B 862). The 1927/28 lecture course quotes this passage as its hermeneutic rule; the Kantbook does not, and it reaches for the reply to Eberhard instead (§35, 202). Heidegger turns Kant's maxims on Kant, and retrieval becomes the warrant for reading out of Kant's text a ground Kant did not let bear.

**¶6, 2–3.** Since metaphysics belongs to human nature and "factically exists with" the human being (Taft's copy misreads "tactically"), it has always taken some shape; an explicit laying of the ground never happens "out of nothing" but within the strength and weakness of a tradition, and every laying of the ground is "a transformation of the same task". Hence the four Sections: starting point, carrying-out, originality, retrieval. The departure is from Kant's self-understanding. Kant agrees that "there has always been some metaphysics or other to be met with in the world, and there will always continue to be one" (B xxxi; B 21). But he presents the *Critique* as a break: one "can and must regard as undone all attempts made until now" (B 23–24), and the critique undertakes "an entire revolution" (B xxii). Heidegger's "transformation of the same task" turns Kant's revolution back into a moment of continuity. The next Section calls it the "first and deepest shock wave" to strike the tradition (§2, 12), but a shock within it, not a new beginning. The title over the four Sections, "The Unfolding of the Idea of a Fundamental Ontology through the Interpretation of the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a Laying of the Ground for Metaphysics", fixes the order of ends and means once more.

The Introduction makes three decisions about Kant before it quotes him more than once. It names the *Critique* by a title Kant reserved for a different book, sets a criterion, the bearing power of the ground, that Kant did not set himself, and places Kant's revolution inside a tradition he claimed to have suspended. Each decision is announced, not hidden, and none is yet tested against Kant's text.

## First Section: The Laying of the Ground of Metaphysics in Its Starting Point

**¶1, 5.** Exhibiting Kant's starting point means answering one question: "Why for Kant does laying the ground for metaphysics become the *Critique of Pure Reason*?" Three part-questions follow: which concept of metaphysics Kant found, the starting point of its grounding, and why that grounding is a critique of pure reason. Kant puts the question the other way round. For him a critique of pure reason comes first, as "a science of the mere estimation of pure reason, of its sources and boundaries" (A 11/B 25), and it is meant to decide whether metaphysics is possible as a science at all (B 22) or whether "a satisfactory proof that the possibility that it demands to have explained does not in fact exist at all" is the outcome (B 19). Heidegger's question presupposes that a laying of the ground of metaphysics takes place and asks only why it takes the form of a critique. The negative outcome Kant allowed for has been set aside before the Section begins.

### §1. The Traditional Concept of Metaphysics

**¶1, 5–6.** Heidegger takes Baumgarten's definition, "the science which comprises the first principles of human knowledge", as the horizon within which Kant saw metaphysics, and finds in it "a peculiar and at first a necessary ambiguity". Kant had judged the same definition, and Heidegger does not say so. In the Architectonic Kant writes that when "it was said that metaphysics is the science of the first principles of human cognition, an entirely special kind of cognition was not thereby marked off, but only a rank in regard to generality". Only "the complete heterogeneity and difference of origin", a priori against a posteriori, can bound the science (A 843–844/B 871–872). The two diagnoses part at the definition's weak point. Kant finds it defective because it measures by degree of generality instead of source; Heidegger finds it ambiguous between principles of what is known and principles of the knowing, the ambiguity he will soon trace back to Aristotle's double first philosophy. Kant's objection, the difference of origin, will reappear in §3 as "pure reason", but without Kant's own statement that the school definition fails on exactly this point. In the 1927/28 lecture course, published in 1977 but delivered before the Kantbook was written, Heidegger had settled the ambiguity outright: Baumgarten's metaphysics "is a science of the principles of beings, not the principles of knowledge" (GA 25, English translation). The Kantbook keeps the ambiguity open because §§2–3 need both readings, principles of beings and principles of knowing, joined in "ontological knowledge". A marginal note from Heidegger's copy shifts the definition toward "what human knowing represents"; it is retrospective and moves the definition further from Kant's criterion of origin. The footnote against Pichler, Heimsoeth, Wundt and Kroner faults them for keeping the *Critique* as "epistemology" and adding the "metaphysical motives" beside it. Heimsoeth and Wundt are the metaphysical Kant-readers of the 1920s, and Heidegger sets himself apart from his nearest allies.

**¶2, 6–7.** The bibliographical title *meta ta physika* turned into a doctrine about what the treatises contain, and whether the Aristotelian "Metaphysics" is metaphysics "must be doubted". Kant is quoted from Heinze's edition of the metaphysics lectures: the science is called metaphysics "from μετά, *trans*, and *physica*", "outside of the field of physics". The quotation cannot be checked in the corpus. The *Critique* has no etymology of the name, but its definitions agree with the lecture: metaphysics is "a wholly isolated speculative cognition of reason that elevates itself entirely above all instruction from experience" (B xiv), and its proper end lies "beyond the boundaries of experience" (B 7). The departure is plain. Kant takes the name as an insight; Heidegger takes it as the record of a decision that the tradition then forgot it had made.

**¶3, 7.** The title *meta ta physika* names "a fundamental philosophical difficulty" (*Verlegenheit*): the later schools had no discipline into which to fit what Aristotle sought as first philosophy. No Kant locus; the paragraph builds the frame in which Kant will be placed. Kant has a history of metaphysics of his own, the last chapter of the *Critique*, which sorts the past by object, origin and method of cognition (A 852–855/B 880–883). In Kant's history the Greeks are parties in a dispute that criticism ends. In Heidegger's, the Greek beginning is an open question that the tradition closed and that Kant reopens. Heidegger's frame determines in advance that the *Critique* will be read as the reopening.

**¶4, 7.** In Aristotle's determination of first philosophy a "doubling" appears: knowledge of beings as beings and knowledge of the most eminent region of beings, from which beings as a whole are determined. No Kant locus. The doubling is the ancestor of the school division into metaphysica generalis and metaphysica specialis that the next paragraphs describe, and Kant's own architectonic still has its form: "transcendental philosophy" or *Ontologia* on one side, a "rational physiology" whose transcendent part is the "transcendental cognition of the world" and of God on the other (A 845–846/B 873–874). Kant inherits the doubling in a reorganized shape and does not mark it as a problem; Heidegger's point is that it is one.

**¶5, 7–8.** The double characterization must be neither split, nor reduced to one side, nor hastily reconciled; the grounds of the apparent disunity have to be brought to light, and the doubling has governed the problem of being since the beginnings of ancient philosophy. No Kant locus. Kant has a name for one form of the unity Heidegger refuses to accept hastily, and Heidegger does not use it: "ontotheology", the transcendental theology that "believes that it can cognize that existence [of an original being] through mere concepts" (A 632/B 660). Kant's word names the doctrine that the being of beings can be read off the concept of the highest being. Kant coined it to reject that doctrine, and the later Heidegger made it the name for the essence of metaphysics. The Kantbook has the problem without Kant's word for it.

**¶6, 8.** Metaphysics, "by way of anticipation", is "the fundamental knowledge of beings as such and as a whole", a definition that announces three questions: what the knowledge of the being of beings is, how it unfolds into knowledge of beings as a whole, and why it points to "a knowledge of the knowledge of Being". The third question re-describes in advance Kant's definition of the transcendental: cognition "occupied not so much with objects but rather with our mode of cognition of objects insofar as this is to be possible *a priori*" (B 25). It also echoes the A preface, where reason is summoned to "the most difficult of all its tasks, namely, that of self-knowledge" (A xi). Kant's self-knowledge of reason is a court that rules on reason's claims (A xi–xii). Heidegger's "knowledge of the knowledge of Being" is an ontology of the understanding of being. The two coincide in form, knowledge reflecting on knowledge, and the rest of the book depends on letting the second stand for the first.

**¶7, 8.** Metaphysics after Aristotle owes its form not to an Aristotelian system but to the failure to understand "the questionable and open nature" in which Plato and Aristotle left the central problems; two themes shaped the school concept and blocked a return to the original problematic. No Kant locus. Kant's own Plato is the Plato whose ideas "even go far beyond the concepts of the understanding", and his Aristotle the founder of the categories and "the head of the **empiricists**" (A 313/B 370, A 854/B 882). Neither is Heidegger's Plato or Aristotle of an open question. Heidegger is preparing to say that Kant brings himself into dialogue with Aristotle and Plato (§2, 12), and the Plato and Aristotle he has in mind are his own.

**¶8, 8–9.** The first theme, the content-division, stems from Christianity's interpretation of the world: God, nature and the human being, hence theology, cosmology and psychology as metaphysica specialis, with ontology as metaphysica generalis. Here Heidegger's genealogy contests Kant's without naming it. Kant derives the three special metaphysics from reason itself, from the three relations of representation that the forms of syllogism express. The transcendental ideas fall into "three classes": the unity of the thinking subject, of the series of conditions of appearance, and of the condition of all objects of thought, so that pure reason provides the ideas for "a transcendental doctrine of the soul", "a transcendental science of the world" and "a transcendental cognition of God" (A 334–335/B 391–392). Kant adds that "such a project is exclusively a pure and genuine product or problem of pure reason". Kant holds that the tripartition is natural to reason; Heidegger treats it as a historical, theological inheritance. On Kant's derivation the human being's privileged place in metaphysica specialis is a structure of reason, not a doctrinal residue, and Heidegger's genealogy prepares the Fourth Section by removing Kant's reason for it. The disagreement is real, and only Heidegger's side of it is on the page.

**¶9, 9.** The second theme, the manner of knowing: a science of the highest dignity, the "queen of the sciences", must have the strictest knowledge, and so assimilates itself to the mathematical ideal and becomes a "science established on the basis of mere reason". The "queen" is Kant's (A viii), though Kant reports the title as past and the queen as "despised on all sides"; "in which 'everyone takes an interest' (Kant)" has not been located in the *Critique*. The silence here is large. Kant devotes the first chapter of the Doctrine of Method to showing that philosophy cannot imitate mathematics, since philosophy can give no definitions, axioms or demonstrations in the mathematical sense (A 712–738/B 740–766). The critique of the mathematical ideal that Heidegger sets out as his own diagnosis of school metaphysics is a doctrine Kant worked out in the *Critique* itself.

**¶10, 9.** Kant keeps the aim of this metaphysics and shifts it further toward metaphysica specialis, "authentic metaphysics", "metaphysics in its final end" (from the essay on the progress of metaphysics, not in the corpus). In view of its constant "miscarriage", every attempt to extend pure rational knowledge must be held back until the question of the inner possibility of the science is settled. This is a reconstruction, and the *Critique* supports it independently of the uncheckable source: "These unavoidable problems of pure reason itself are **God, freedom** and **immortality**. But the science whose final aim in all its preparations is directed properly only to the solution of these problems is called **metaphysics**" (B 7, a B addition). The suspension of dogmatic metaphysics is Kant's too (B 23–24; A xi–xii). Heidegger's one departure is the name of the outcome. Kant's suspension ends in a verdict on what reason may claim; Heidegger calls the task a laying of the ground "in the sense of an essential determination of metaphysics". A verdict on claims has become a determination of essence.

### §2. The Point of Departure for the Laying of the Ground for Traditional Metaphysics

**¶1, 10.** In metaphysics an "overstepping" (*Überschritt*) takes place beyond what experience can offer, toward supersensible being; its procedure has been "a mere groping, and what is the worst, a groping among mere concepts" (B xv, quoted exactly). The word "overstepping" is the first step of the book's central re-inscription. Kant's verb for metaphysics going beyond experience is *überschreiten*, and a principle "which indeed bids us to overstep" the limits of experience "is called **transcendent**" (A 296/B 353). Heidegger begins from Kant's sense, overstepping as metaphysics' claim to the supersensible. By §3 the same word, as "transcendence", will name the prior relation of finite reason to beings, which Kant calls transcendental. Kant's warning that "transcendental and transcendent are not the same" (A 296/B 352) marks the distinction Heidegger's word will cross.

**¶2, 10.** A laying of the ground must aim at the final end of metaphysics, metaphysica specialis, but the question of its possibility is "thrown back upon the more general question of the inner possibility of a general making-manifest of beings as such"; laying the ground becomes the elucidation of "a comporting toward beings in which this essence shows itself in itself". The order follows Kant: the B preface's experiment secures the first part of metaphysics, which concerns a priori concepts whose objects can be given in experience, before deciding about the second, which concerns the unconditioned (B xviii–xxi), and the Analytic precedes the Dialectic. The vocabulary does not follow Kant. "Making-manifest", "comportment toward beings" and beings that "show themselves in themselves" come from *Being and Time* §7, not from the *Critique*. Kant's question, how a priori cognition of objects is possible, is restated as how beings can show themselves. A question about the validity of cognition has become a question about the manifestness of beings.

**¶3, 10–11.** The "indication" of what makes such comportment possible is the procedure of the natural scientists: "They realized that reason has insight only into what it produces itself according to its own design [*Entwurf*] ... and that it must constrain Nature to answer its own questions" (B xiii, quoted exactly). Heidegger then speaks of "the 'previously projected plan' of one Nature in general", which "determines in advance the constitution of the Being of beings". Two things happen. The quotation in scare-quotes comes from the clause after the one Heidegger quoted. Kant wrote that "accidental observations, made according to no previously designed plan, can never connect up into a necessary law", and in his text the plan is the experimenter's design for an experiment (B xiii). Heidegger lifts the phrase out of its negation and makes it the plan of nature as such. And Kant's word *Entwurf*, a design or sketch, is the word *Being and Time* made into "projection", the existential structure of understanding; quoted here, it carries Heidegger's concept into Kant's sentence without a change of letter. Kant's examples are Galileo, Torricelli and Stahl, and he says he considers natural science here "only insofar as it is grounded on **empirical** principles" (B xii), adding that reason seeks in nature what it must learn from it, "though not merely ascribe to it" (B xiii–xiv). Heidegger turns an account of experimental method into the thesis that "what makes the comporting toward beings (ontic knowledge) possible is the preliminary understanding of the constitution of Being, ontological knowledge". The distinction of ontic and ontological, which carries the whole book, enters here on the strength of one word of Kant's.

**¶4, 11.** Mathematical natural science gives only an "indication" of the conditioning relation between ontic experience and ontological knowledge; whether metaphysica specialis may be projected after the model of positive knowledge has still to be decided. This is a reconstruction. Kant also limits the analogy: the sciences are to be imitated "at least as an experiment" and only "insofar as their analogy with metaphysics, as rational cognition, might permit" (B xvi). The limit is aimed at the Marburg reading, for which the *Critique* was the theory of mathematical natural science. On this point Heidegger stands closer to Kant's preface than his opponents do.

**¶5, 11.** The projection of the possibility of metaphysica specialis is led back to the possibility of what makes ontic knowledge possible, the preliminary understanding of being, and so to metaphysica generalis. This is a reconstruction of Kant's order, and the Architectonic supports it: metaphysics in the narrower sense consists of "**transcendental philosophy**" (*Ontologia*) and the physiology of pure reason (A 845/B 873). It would be misleading to call this a priority of general over special metaphysics in Kant. The *Critique* reaches the special disciplines in the Dialectic only to show that they cannot be theoretical sciences, and it does not ground them in the Analytic.

**¶6, 12.** With this starting point Kant "is brought immediately into the dialogue with Aristotle and Plato. Ontology now becomes a problem for the first time." Whether Kant achieved full clarity is "a subordinate question"; ontology is not primarily related to grounding the positive sciences but rests on "a 'higher interest' which human reason finds in itself", and since metaphysica generalis provides the "preparation" for metaphysica specialis, the essence of the latter must be transformed in grounding the former. The claim that ontology becomes a problem for the first time has its strongest support in the passage Heidegger cites in §3, where Kant says that "the proud name of an ontology ... must give way to the modest one of a mere analytic of pure understanding" (A 247/B 303). Kant's form of making ontology a problem is to demote it. "Higher interest" has not been located in the *Critique*, and "preparation" (*Zurüstung*) comes from the essay on the progress of metaphysics. The separation of ontology from the grounding of the sciences selects one of Kant's purposes and leaves out another. The B Introduction asks "How is pure natural science possible?" in the same breath as "How is metaphysics possible as science?" (B 20–22), and the *Prolegomena* are built on the first question. "A subordinate question" is the decisive licence: Heidegger reserves the right to press the problem beyond Kant's clarity, and the Third Section exercises it.

**¶7, 12–13.** Laying the ground of metaphysics as a whole means "unveiling the inner possibility of ontology", the true, "metaphysical" sense of the "Copernican Revolution". Kant is quoted from B xvi: objects must conform to our cognition, "which would agree better with the requested possibility of an *a priori* cognition of them, which is to establish something about objects before they are given to us". The quotation is exact and stops where Kant goes on. Kant's next sentences apply the hypothesis to intuition and to concepts (B xvii) and then state its result: our a priori cognition "reaches appearances only, leaving the thing in itself as something actual for itself but uncognized by us", which the antinomies confirm (B xix–xxi). Kant's Copernican turn is the doctrine of appearances and things in themselves, and the experiment is its proof. Heidegger's "metaphysical sense" keeps the reversal and drops the idealism. The selection is not neutral: it lets "objects" mean beings, where Kant's sentence means objects as appearances.

**¶8, 13.** "Kant wants to say" that not all knowledge is ontic and that ontic knowledge is possible only through ontological knowledge; the turn does not shake the old concept of truth as *adaequatio* but "actually presupposes it, indeed even grounds it for the first time"; "Apparentness of beings (ontic truth) revolves around the unveiledness of the constitution of the Being of beings (ontological truth)". The claim that Kant presupposes the correspondence concept has a Kantian text Heidegger does not cite: "The nominal definition of truth, namely that it is the agreement of cognition with its object, is here granted and presupposed" (A 58/B 82). On that point Heidegger reconstructs Kant. The second step is a re-inscription. The pair manifestness/unveiledness is *Being and Time* §44's uncoveredness and disclosedness in new words, and the clause Taft's copy drops ("To this last knowledge the objects, that is, their ontic determinability, must conform", working rendering) makes objects conform to ontological knowledge, where Kant has them conform "as appearances" to "our way of representing" (B xx). "Kant wants to say" is the formula of the paraphrasing voice, and what follows it is Heidegger's thesis on truth.

**¶9, 13.** The laying of the ground begins with the question of the inner possibility of ontology; why does it become a critique of pure reason? A transition. By now "metaphysics" in Kant's question has become "ontology" in Heidegger's, a substitution §3 will make in Kant's own sentences.

### §3. The Laying of the Ground for Metaphysics as "Critique of Pure Reason"

**¶1, 13–14.** "Kant reduces the problem of the possibility of ontology to the question: 'How are a priori synthetic judgments possible?'" Kant knows judging as the tradition does, and knowledge that "brings forth the quiddity [*Wasgehalt*] of the being, i.e., knowledge which unveils the being itself, Kant calls 'synthetic'". Two re-inscriptions open the section. Kant calls the question "the real problem of pure reason", on which "metaphysics now stands or falls" (B 19), not the problem of ontology. And Kant defines the synthetic judgment logically: its predicate "lies entirely outside the concept" of the subject, so that synthetic judgments are "judgments of amplification" that "add to the concept of the subject a predicate that was not thought in it at all" (A 6–7/B 10–11). Heidegger's definition, knowledge that brings the what-content of the being and unveils the being itself, turns a relation between concepts into a relation to beings. The remark that Kant takes knowing as judging "in keeping with the tradition" reserves a later correction: §4 will argue that knowing is primarily intuiting.

**¶2, 14.** The ground of legitimacy of these judgments about the being of beings cannot lie in experience, "for experience of beings is itself always already guided by ontological understanding". Kant reaches the same conclusion by another route. For him experience cannot ground such judgments because it gives no necessity or strict universality, the "secure indications" of a priori cognition (B 3–4), and because in synthetic a priori judgments "this means of help", experience, "is entirely lacking" (A 8/B 12). Heidegger's reason is the circle of understanding: experience cannot warrant what already guides it. Kant's reason concerns the modal character of the judgment; Heidegger's concerns the priority of an understanding of being. The two arguments reach one conclusion and do not rest on the same premise.

**¶3, 14–15.** Kant calls our faculty of knowing from a priori principles "pure reason" (from the *Critique of Judgment*), and pure reason is "that which contains the principles for cognizing something absolutely *a priori*" (A 11/B 24, quoted exactly). Delimiting the essence of pure reason is at once "the differentiating determination of its nonessence" and so its "limitation and restriction (critique)". The reconstruction holds as far as limits go: Kant's critique is "a science of the mere estimation of pure reason, of its sources and boundaries" (B 25). The re-inscription is "essence". Kant's critique estimates claims and keeps reason "free of errors"; it "would not be a **doctrine**" and serves "not for the amplification but only for the purification of our reason" (A 11/B 25). In Heidegger, critique determines what pure reason is, and the "nonessence" it marks off is a matter of essence. Kant's negative judicial function has become a positive determination of essence, and the word "purification" does not appear.

**¶4, 15.** Ontological knowledge, the a priori synthesis, is that "for the sole sake of which the whole critique is undertaken" (A 14/B 28). Kant uses "synthesis" in many senses, and they interweave in the problem-formula: every judgment is already an "I connect", so even analytic judgments are synthetic as judgments, while synthetic judgments are synthetic twice over, as judgments and because the connection is warranted by the being judged about. The citation is accurate. Kant's sentence says the *Critique* refrains from complete analysis because analysis lacks "the difficulty encountered in the synthesis on account of which the whole critique is actually undertaken". Heidegger's first claim, that analysis presupposes synthesis, has the B-Deduction behind it: "the dissolution (**analysis**) that seems to be its opposite, in fact always presupposes it; for where the understanding has not previously combined anything, neither can it dissolve anything" (B 130). His second, that the synthetic judgment's connection is "brought forth" from the being itself, restates Kant's synthetic a posteriori judgment, in which experience is the ground of the connection (A 8/B 12), in the vocabulary of beings. Both are faithful in substance and set up the third synthesis.

**¶5, 15–16.** In synthetic a priori judgments a further synthesis is at work, which brings something about the being that is not drawn from experience: a "preliminary self-relating to the being" that "forms first and foremost the that-upon-which [*das Worauf*] and the horizon within which the being in itself becomes experienceable". Kant names an investigation of it transcendental: "I entitle all knowledge transcendental that is occupied in general not so much with objects as with the kind of knowledge we have of objects, insofar as this is possible a priori." Heidegger concludes that transcendental knowledge investigates "the possibility of the preliminary understanding of Being", and that it concerns "the stepping-over (transcendence) of pure reason to the being". Here the re-inscription becomes explicit. Heidegger quotes the B definition (B 25) and lists A 11 beside it; the A text differs, speaking of cognition occupied "with our *a priori* concepts of objects in general" (A 11–12). The B wording, with its "mode of cognition", suits his reading better, and the joint citation conceals that he chose it. The decisive move is the parenthesis "(transcendence)". Kant's transcendental is reflection on our mode of cognition; Kant's overstepping is the mark of the transcendent (A 296/B 352–353). Heidegger fuses the two into one word, whose sense is neither Kant's reflection nor Kant's illegitimate overstepping: a stepping-over toward beings that first makes experience of them possible. "Horizon" is Heidegger's word too, introduced here before Kant can be cited for it.

**¶6, 16.** To make the possibility of ontology a problem is to ask about the essence of this transcendence, "to philosophize transcendentally"; hence Kant calls metaphysica generalis (*Ontologia*) "Transcendental Philosophy" and speaks of "the transcendental philosophy of the ancients". Of the three loci cited from the *Critique* (the essay on the progress of metaphysics is cited as well, and is not in the corpus), one supports the claim. In the Architectonic transcendental philosophy "considers only the **understanding** and reason itself in a system of all concepts and principles that are related to objects in general ... (*Ontologia*)" (A 845/B 873). The second, A 247/B 303, is the passage in which "the proud name of an ontology" must give way to "a mere analytic of pure understanding": it shows Kant renaming ontology downward, not calling it transcendental philosophy. The third, B 113, introduces the scholastic transcendentals (*quodlibet ens est unum, verum, bonum*) only to argue that "these supposedly transcendental predicates of **things** are nothing other than logical requisites and criteria of all **cognition of things** in general", "carelessly made into properties of things in themselves" (B 113–114). Kant uses "transcendental philosophy of the ancients" for a doctrine he corrects by turning predicates of things into conditions of cognition. Heidegger cites the phrase as evidence that Kant took his own enterprise for the heir of ancient ontology. The text runs the other way. This is selection and silence: two of the three passages carry Kant's critique of ontology, and Heidegger cites them for Kant's continuity with it.

**¶7, 16.** The *Critique* gives no "system" of transcendental philosophy but is "a treatise on method" (B xxii), which means the working out of "the 'whole contour' and the 'whole internal, articular structure' of ontology", in which "the complete sketch of a system of metaphysics is drawn" (B xxiii). Two small changes in the quotation carry Heidegger's thesis. Kant's sentence reads that the critique "catalogs the entire outline of the science of metaphysics, both in respect of its boundaries and in respect of its entire internal structure" (B xxii); in the German, *derselben* refers back to "the science", which is metaphysics. Heidegger makes the outline and structure those of "ontology", and he leaves out "in respect of its boundaries". The limiting clause disappears at the point where metaphysics becomes ontology, and the treatise on method becomes an articulation of ontology.

**¶8, 16–17.** The intention of the *Critique* is "fundamentally misunderstood" if it is taken as a "theory of experience" or of the positive sciences; it "has nothing to do with a 'theory of knowledge'"; at most it would be a theory of ontological, not ontic, knowledge, and even this misses that ontology is here "grounded" and "seen for what it is". Kant has no word for epistemology, and the target is the Marburg school and its *Kant's Theory of Experience*. Measured against Kant, the polemic holds and overreaches. It holds, because Kant states that the *Critique* concerns metaphysics: it is "a treatise on the method" of metaphysics (B xxii) and decides "the possibility or impossibility of a metaphysics in general" (A xii). It overreaches, because Kant defines the same work as the estimation of reason's "sources and boundaries" (B 25) and its first use as teaching us "never to venture with speculative reason beyond the boundaries of experience" (B xxiv). That is a theory of the limits of cognition in any ordinary sense. Heidegger's "nothing to do with" denies to Kant a purpose Kant states in the same pages as the one Heidegger keeps.

**¶9, 17–18.** If truth belongs to knowledge, the transcendental problem is the question of "the essence of the truth of ontological transcendence", of "'transcendental truth,' which precedes all empirical truth and makes it possible" (A 146/B 185), for "no knowledge can contradict it without losing all content at the same time, i.e., all relation to any object and consequently, all truth" (A 62–63/B 87). Ontic truth conforms to ontological truth; nothing may be presupposed, "least of all the *factum* of the truth of the positive sciences". The two quotations are exact, but they come from different contexts, and Heidegger joins them. Kant's sentence on transcendental truth ends by saying where it lies: it "consists in the general relation to" the "entirety of all possible experience" (A 146/B 185), in the Schematism's conclusion that the categories have "none but a possible empirical use". Transcendental truth is the relation of cognition to possible experience, not a truth of the understanding of being prior to experience. The second quotation is said of the transcendental analytic as "a logic of truth", whose rules no cognition can violate without losing its object (A 62–63/B 87). The refusal to presuppose the "fact" of the sciences turns Kant against the Marburg starting point, and here Kant's text is divided. The *Prolegomena* (§4), which Heidegger quotes next, distinguish the *Critique*'s synthetic procedure, which relies on no fact, from their own analytic one. But the B Introduction imports the analytic procedure into the *Critique*: of mathematics and pure natural science, "since they are actually given, it can appropriately be asked **how** they are possible; for that they must be possible is proved through their actuality" (B 20–21). Heidegger's claim fits the first edition and the method the *Prolegomena* ascribe to the *Critique*. It does not fit the B Introduction, which he cites throughout.

**¶10, 18.** Kant says of the *Critique* that it "demands a reader resolved to think himself gradually into a system in which nothing yet lies at its ground as given except for reason itself, and [who] thus seeks to develop knowledge from its original seeds without seeking the support of any fact" (*Prolegomena* §4, not in the corpus). Heidegger takes "seeds" (*Keime*) as the programme for his regress to a ground. The *Critique* says where the seeds lie. The Analytic of Concepts will pursue the pure concepts "into their first seeds and predispositions in the human understanding" (A 66/B 91). In Kant the seeds of a priori cognition are in the understanding. The Third Section will trace the a priori synthesis to the imagination and from there to time, a ground Kant's own sentence about seeds does not name.

**¶11, 18.** The task arises of showing how this development of the possibility of ontology from its seeds is carried out. A transition, and the substitution is now complete: Kant's "cognition" developed from reason's seeds has become "the possibility of ontology".

The First Section has made three Kantian texts load-bearing: the Copernican hypothesis (B xvi), the definition of the transcendental (B 25), and the question of synthetic judgments a priori (B 19). Where it reconstructs Kant's order it holds against the text. The special metaphysics are thrown back onto the general, the *Critique*'s first concern is the possibility of a priori cognition, and Kant presupposes the correspondence concept of truth (A 58/B 82). Where it re-describes, the shifts are small and consistent, and they all run one way. "Metaphysics" becomes "ontology" inside Kant's sentences (B 19, B xxii). Kant's experimental design (*Entwurf*, B xiii) becomes projection. The transcendental absorbs the overstepping Kant called transcendent (A 296/B 352). The quotations stop before Kant's limits: the doctrine of appearances after B xvi, "in respect of its boundaries" in B xxii, the relation to possible experience in A 146/B 185. Kant's own critiques of the school (of Baumgarten's definition, of the mathematical method, of the scholastic transcendentals, his derivation of the special metaphysics from reason) go unmentioned, and two of them would have qualified Heidegger's genealogy. Several departures are announced. Heidegger calls Kant's clarity "a subordinate question" (§2, 12), and the prefaces later concede the overinterpretation. The quotations themselves are exact. The reading works by selecting which of Kant's sentences to continue and by letting one word (*Entwurf*, *Überschritt*, "transcendental") carry its sense from *Being and Time* into Kant's text.

## Second Section: The Laying of the Ground of Metaphysics in Its Execution

**¶1, 19.** Every first incursion into a concealed field is determined only "little by little"; the direction of access firms up only during the advance, and an explicit marking-out of the field is at first lacking. Kant is quoted from the *Nachlass*: "Critique requires knowledge of the sources, and Reason must know itself"; and Kant first works out this self-knowledge through the *Critique*. The note is not in the corpus. The *Critique* says something close to it in its own preface, where the age demands that reason take up "the most difficult of all its tasks, namely, that of self-knowledge" (A xi). Kant presents that self-knowledge as a court of justice and as an inventory from which "nothing here can escape" (A xx). Heidegger presents it as an advance whose route Kant did not yet see. The difference sets up what follows: if Kant was feeling his way, the interpreter may map the route Kant took better than Kant did.

**¶2–¶3, 19–20.** The later interpretation, "not yet and no longer in possession of the original directive force of the projecting", must secure the guiding view in advance and anticipate the main stages of the whole; hence the Section's two parts, the dimension of the regress and the stages of the execution. No Kant locus. The interpreter's position is defined against Kant's: Kant had the force of a first projection without its map, the interpreter has the map without the force. Kant's own Doctrine of Method distinguishes "historical" cognition, which knows a system only as it was given, from "rational" cognition drawn "from the universal sources of reason, from which critique, indeed even the rejection of what has been learned, can also arise" (A 836–837/B 864–865). Heidegger claims the second kind of cognition of Kant's own book.

### A. The Characterization of the Dimension of Regress for the Execution of the Laying of the Ground of Metaphysics

**¶1, 20.** The task is to determine the essence of ontological knowledge by illuminating its origin "from the seeds that make it possible" (working rendering), and so to clarify the essence of knowing and the place and kind of the field of origin. Previous interpretation neglected or misread this field and therefore failed to appropriate the work's basic tendency. The "seeds" carry over from the *Prolegomena* quotation at the end of §3. The charge against earlier readers, that they left the field of origin uncharacterized, will be made good in §4 by a thesis Kant does not state: that the field is finitude.

#### I. The Essential Characters of the Field of Origin

##### §4. The Essence of Knowledge in General

**¶1, 20–21.** Kant does not discuss the essential characters of the field of origin thematically but takes them for granted as "self-evident" presuppositions, whose function the interpretation must not overlook. They can be summed up in a thesis: "The ground for the source for laying the ground for metaphysics is human pure reason, so that it is precisely the humanness of reason, i.e., its finitude, which will be essential ..." Finitude lies "in the essential structure of knowledge itself", not in its defects. Heidegger admits that the thesis is not Kant's: it sums up "presuppositions". The admission is honest and leaves the key word to him. "Human reason" is Kant's, from the first sentence of the A preface (A vii), and "finite" is Kant's at one place Heidegger will cite, where sensible intuition may be shared by "all finite thinking beings" (B 72). There Kant attaches finitude to a kind of intuition and adds "(though we cannot decide this)". The step from the human to finitude, and from finitude to "the essential structure of knowledge", is Heidegger's. A marginal note asks for the point to be developed from the difference between knowledge as representing and as knowing, as the lecture course of 1935/36 did; it is retrospective.

**¶2, 21.** What Kant says in the first sentence of the Aesthetic is "usually appraised much too lightly": "In whatever manner and by whatever means a knowing may relate to objects, intuition is that through which it relates itself immediately to them, and upon which all thought as a means is directed." One must "hammer in" that "knowing is primarily intuiting". The quotation is exact, and so is the note that Kant spaced the word "intuition". The inference is Heidegger's. Kant's sentence gives intuition two privileges: it is the one immediate relation to objects, and thought is directed at it "as a means ... as an end" (Guyer and Wood's rendering of *abzweckt*). It does not say that cognition is intuition. In the Logic Kant names the two sources cognition needs and insists on their unification (A 50–51/B 74–76). "Primarily" is the hammer Heidegger supplies. The marginal notes in his copy concede what it costs: "primarily" means first in the order of the essence's construction, and "precisely because knowing is *primarily* intuition, an intuiting *alone* is never for us knowledge". The notes are later than the text.

**¶3, 22.** The reinterpretation of knowledge as judging "violates the decisive sense of the Kantian problem", since thinking serves intuition; for their union both must share a genus, "representation in general (*repraesentatio*)" (A 320/B 376). The polemic is aimed at the Marburg logic of pure cognition, and Kant's text gives it less support than Heidegger needs. Kant defines the understanding through judgment: "We can, however, trace all actions of the understanding back to judgments, so that the **understanding** in general can be represented as a **faculty for judging**" (A 69/B 94). What Heidegger contests is the identification of cognition with that one faculty, and on that point Kant's "Neither of these properties is to be preferred to the other" (A 51/B 75), which Heidegger meets in §6, tells against the Marburg reading and against his own primacy alike. The citation of the ladder of representations is correct as to genus. Its context is the Dialectic, where Kant lists kinds of representation to protect the word "idea" from careless use (A 319–320/B 376–377). Heidegger takes it as the place where Kant shows the kinship of intuition and thought.

**¶4, 22–23.** "Representation" has the broad formal sense "according to which one thing indicates, announces, presents another"; representing "with consciousness" is *perceptio*; where what is represented is represented as such, representing is a relating to what presents itself, "objective perception", and knowledge is representing in this sense. Kant's ladder runs: the genus is representation, "under it stands the representation with consciousness (*perceptio*)", a perception referred to the subject is sensation, and "an objective perception is a **cognition** (*cognitio*)" (A 320/B 376). The formal sense of representation as indicating and announcing is Heidegger's gloss on a word Kant leaves undefined. It places representing in the vocabulary of showing that §5 will need for appearance. On the order of the ladder the paraphrase is faithful.

**¶5, 23.** Knowing representation is intuition or concept: "The former relates immediately to the object and is single; the latter refers to it mediately by means of a feature which several things can have in common" (A 320/B 377, quoted exactly). Thinking, representing "in general", serves to make the particular accessible "and indeed for everyone" (working rendering; Taft's copy garbles the clause); "Each of these two ... is indeed representation, but is not yet knowledge" (from the essay on the progress of metaphysics, not in the corpus). "For everyone" is Heidegger's addition. In the *Critique*, what is "valid for everyone" is the mark of conviction, whose ground is objectively sufficient (A 820/B 848), and the objective unity of apperception is what separates a judgment from a merely subjective association of representations (B 140–142); neither is said of the concept as such. Heidegger moves universal validity from the judgment, where Kant puts it, into the function of the concept, and §5's piece of chalk builds on the move.

**¶6, 23.** One might conclude that intuiting and thinking are related reciprocally and in full balance, so that knowing is intuitive thinking "and thus at bottom ... judgment". Heidegger states the view in order to reject it. It is the view Kant's own sentence of parity supports (A 51/B 75).

**¶7, 23–24.** Against it: intuition "constitutes the authentic essence of knowledge" and carries the weight, because only so can the finitude of knowledge be grasped; "That first sentence of the *Critique of Pure Reason* is already no longer a definition of knowing in general, but rather is already the determination of the essence of human knowledge"; as regards the human being, "every cognition consists of concept and intuition" (essay on progress, not in the corpus). The argument runs from the conclusion: intuition must carry the weight because only then is finitude visible. Kant's first sentence is general in its wording ("In whatever way and through whatever means a cognition may relate to objects"). When Kant revised the passage for B, he put the restriction to humans into the second sentence: "this in turn, <at least for us humans,> is possible only if it affects the mind in a certain way" (B 33). The placing suggests that intuition's immediacy is meant generally and its dependence on affection humanly. Heidegger reads the restriction back into the general sentence, and in §5 he cites the same insertion as evidence for his reading.

**¶8, 24.** Finite knowledge is set off against divine knowledge, *intuitus originarius*. Infinite intuition "first brings this being into its Being, helps it to its coming-into-being (*origo*)", and needs no thinking, for divine knowing is "intuition (for all its knowledge must be intuition and not thinking, which always shows itself to have limits)" (B 71). The contrast is Kant's, and Kant hedges it on every side. The B 71 sentence reports what "one" does "in natural theology", where "one is careful to remove the conditions of time and space from all of its intuition (for all of its cognition must be intuition and not **thinking,** which is always proof of limitations)". Kant uses the remark to argue that space and time cannot be forms of things in themselves. Original intuition, "one through which the existence of the object of intuition is itself given", "so far as we can have insight, can only pertain to the original being", and the whole contrast "must be counted only as an illustration of our aesthetic theory and not as a ground of its proof" (B 72). What Kant offers as an illustration Heidegger makes the measure of the essence of finitude. The B-Deduction passages he cites with it frame the contrast differently again: the other cognition is "an understanding that itself intuited (as, say, a divine understanding ...)" (B 145). All the passages are B-only.

**¶9, 24–25.** The decisive difference does not lie in divine knowing being only intuiting while human knowing is thinking intuition; it lies "primarily in intuiting itself", and that a finite knower must "also" think is a consequence of the finitude of its intuition. Here Heidegger contests the shape Kant gives the contrast. Kant twice puts it in the understanding: "An understanding, in which through self-consciousness all of the manifold would at the same time be given, would **intuit;** ours can only **think** and must seek the intuition in the senses" (B 135), and "the human understanding ... merely thinks, but does not intuit" (B 139). Kant's figure for divine cognition is an intuiting understanding, so that our finitude appears as much in the discursiveness of our understanding as in the receptivity of our intuition. Heidegger needs finitude placed in intuition, because the Third Section will show a pure intuition that is "formative" and can carry an a priori synthesis. Commentary R gives the reason the book needs this placing. Kant's B 135 and B 145 show that the placing is not Kant's.

##### §5. The Essence of the Finitude of Knowledge

**¶1, 25–26.** Finite knowledge is "noncreative intuition"; finite intuition is "*intuitus derivativus*" (B 72); it cannot give itself the object but must let it be given; it is "intuition that 'takes things in stride'", so "the character of the finitude of intuition is found in its receptivity"; it must be "solicited or affected" by what it intuits. A reconstruction with one re-inscription. Kant defines receptivity as "the capacity ... to acquire representations through the way in which we are affected by objects" (A 19/B 33), and *intuitus derivativus* is his word (B 72). "Taking-in-stride" (*Hinnahme*) is Heidegger's word for receptivity, and it has a weight Kant's capacity to be affected lacks: §§28–34 will speak of a pure taking-in-stride that gives itself what it takes. A marginal note warns against reading "derivative" as "derived from" the absolute. It is later than the text and agrees with B 72, where *derivativus* means dependent on the existence of the object.

**¶2, 26.** Kant continues: "This {intuition} takes place, however, only insofar as the object is given to us; but this, in turn, is only possible, to us human beings at least, insofar as the mind is affected in a certain way" (A 19/B 33); the phrase "to us human beings at least" was added in the second edition, which "only makes it more obvious that in the first edition finite knowledge is the theme from the outset". The textual fact is right: the 1781 text lacks the phrase and the 1787 text has it. The use is selective. Here a B addition is read as making A's intention explicit. In §31, B's changes will be read as Kant's retreat from what A had seen. Heidegger reads B as clarifying A when it narrows the subject to the human being, and as retreating from A when it narrows the imagination to the understanding. The criterion for telling the two apart is his thesis. Commentary R notices the asymmetry and accepts Heidegger's account of it; it deserves to be held to the later verdict.

**¶3, 26–27.** Human intuition is not sensible because its affection uses sense organs, but the reverse: because "our Dasein is finite—existing in the midst of beings that already are, beings to which it has been delivered over", it must take beings in stride, and organs serve the relaying. "With this, Kant for the first time attains a concept of sensibility which is ontological rather than sensualistic", and a "nonempirical sensibility" remains open: "Sensible intuition is either pure intuition (space and time) or empirical intuition" (B 146–147, quoted exactly). Kant's definitions support the order Heidegger draws. Sensibility is a capacity to be affected (A 19/B 33), not the possession of organs, and pure intuition is a form of sensibility present "even without an actual object of the senses or sensation" (A 20–21/B 35). Kant claims a novelty too, but a different one: the difference between sensibility and understanding is not one of distinctness, as the Leibnizians held, but of "origin and content" (A 44/B 61–62). Heidegger's "for the first time" names another achievement, an ontological concept, and grounds it in Dasein's being delivered over to beings. Kant grounds the difference in two kinds of representation. "Dasein" is the one word in the paragraph where Kant's capacity to be affected has been replaced by a mode of being.

**¶4, 27.** Finite intuition is knowledge only if the being can be made accessible "for everyone at all times"; "this piece of chalk—must allow itself to be determined as chalk, or rather as a body. In this way, we are able jointly to know this being as the same for all of us." The chalk is Heidegger's, the body Kant's. In the Analytic Kant illustrates the concept's mediate relation to objects with "All bodies are divisible": the concept of the divisible is related to that of body, and this "in turn is related to certain appearances that come before us" (A 68/B 93). The communicative frame, knowing "jointly" and "as the same for all of us", is Heidegger's. He derives the need for the concept from the sharing of intuition among finite knowers. Kant derives it from the mediateness of every relation to objects other than intuition's (A 68/B 93). The two derivations reach the same function by different routes.

**¶5, 27–28.** Determining representing takes "a look at the general" only to direct itself to the particular; this representing "in general" (*repraesentatio per notas communes*) is "representing in concepts", the "Representation {concept} of a representation" (intuition), and so predication: "Judgment is therefore the mediate knowledge of an object, that is, the representation of a representation of it" (A 68/B 93); the understanding, the faculty of judging, makes intuition "understandable". The Kant sentence is quoted exactly. Kant's reason for it, that "no representation pertains to the object immediately except intuition alone" (A 68/B 93), supports the serving role of thought that Heidegger maintains. The closing pun has a Kantian origin Heidegger does not cite: one must "make its intuitions understandable (i.e., to bring them under concepts)" (A 51/B 75). The "look" (*Hinblick*) at the general is the first appearance of the vocabulary of looking that will carry the doctrine of the schema (§20).

**¶6, 28–29.** Thinking unites with intuition in serving it, and through this union (synthesis) the object becomes "evident (true)"; Heidegger calls this synthesis "veritative", and it coincides with the "bringing-forward" of the being's determinateness. A re-inscription by coinage. Kant's syntheses are combinations of a manifold, in imagination, in apprehension, in judgment (A 77–78/B 103; B 130). Kant does not call the union of concept and intuition a synthesis that makes true. Truth in Kant is "the agreement of cognition with its object", granted as a name (A 58/B 82). The "veritative synthesis" locates truth in the unification of the two sources and identifies it with manifestness, the term of *Being and Time* §44. The predicative and apophantic syntheses that follow are measured against it.

**¶7–¶10, 29.** Judging is a unifying in another sense: "A judgment is the representation of a unity of the consciousness of various representations, or the representation of the relationship between the same, insofar as they constitute a concept" (Jäsche *Logic* §17, not in the corpus); judgments are "functions of unity", hence "predicative synthesis"; the joining of subject and predicate is the "apophantic"; the veritative synthesis contains both; and the thesis that knowledge is "synthesis" for Kant says nothing while the word stays ambiguous. "Functions of unity" is from the *Critique*: "All judgments are accordingly functions of unity among our representations" (A 69/B 94). The three-layer structure is Heidegger's analysis, and its last term is Aristotle's, not Kant's. The final sentence turns against the Marburg thesis that synthesis is the essence of cognition. Heidegger's distinctions are not Kant's, but nothing in Kant forbids them. Kant uses "synthesis" for operations at several levels without sorting them (A 77–79/B 102–105), and Heidegger's earlier remark that Kant uses the word "in a multitude of senses" (§3, 15) holds.

**¶11, 29–30.** The understanding is "still more finite" than intuition, since it lacks immediacy; its "circuitousness (discursiveness)" is "the sharpest index of its finitude". A reconstruction. Kant says that "cognition through concepts" is "discursive" (A 68/B 93) and contrasts our understanding, which "can only **think**", with one that would intuit (B 135). "Index of finitude" turns Kant's description into Heidegger's measure, and it qualifies §4's thesis that finitude lies "primarily" in intuition. Discursiveness is now finitude's "sharpest index", and Heidegger has finitude in both places, as Kant's B 135 and B 139 do, while keeping the priority for intuition.

**¶12, 30.** Finite intuition keeps the general character of intuition, that it is "giving"; likewise the understanding shows something of absolute knowledge, "an 'original intuition {an intuition which lets something spring forth}'"; it never produces the being but is "a type of bringing-forth" of the form in which a content "applies to many". The braces mark Heidegger's insertion into Kant's term (Taft's convention), and the insertion turns Kant's *ursprünglich*, original, into *entspringenlassend*, letting spring forth. "Spring forth" (*entspringen*) is Kant's verb for the origin of cognition (A 50/B 74) and for the two stems' growth from a root (A 15/B 29); in Heidegger it becomes the act of the root. The claim that the understanding is a "type of bringing-forth" has Kant behind it: the understanding is "the faculty for bringing forth representations itself, or the **spontaneity** of cognition" (A 51/B 75). The caution that it brings forth only the concept's form, the content being drawn from intuition, agrees with Kant on empirical concepts. It leaves open the a priori content of the categories, which §§11–12 have to face.

**¶13, 30.** In producing the form of the concept the understanding helps "set forth" the content of the object; in this "setting" the "re-presenting [*Vor-stellen*] of thinking reveals itself"; the essence of the understanding is "co-determined" by its character of "from out of itself" (spontaneity), "but without getting to the heart of the matter". Heidegger here contests a definition Kant gives repeatedly. For Kant spontaneity is what the understanding is: "the spontaneity of cognition" (A 51/B 75), "spontaneity of concepts" (A 50/B 74), and the "I think" is "an act of spontaneity" (B 132). Heidegger grants it as true and secondary. The core will be the understanding's dependence on the imagination and on time (§§29, 34). The hyphenated *Vor-stellen* prepares that answer: representing as setting-before becomes the letting-stand-against of §16. Kant's chief defining mark of the understanding is demoted in a subordinate clause.

**¶14–¶15, 30–31.** From the structure of knowing to the knowable: if finite knowledge takes things in stride, the knowable must show itself, as "appearance"; "only for finite knowledge is there anything at all like an object"; infinite knowing lets the being stand forth and has it "as a thing which stands forth [*als Ent-stand*]", "the being as being in itself, i.e., not as object". The coinage *Ent-stand* sets against Kant's *Gegenstand* a correlate of infinite intuition for which Kant has no name. Kant's usage runs the other way: he speaks of the object of an intellectual intuition. A noumenon "in a **positive** sense" would be "an object of a non-sensible intuition" (B 307), and the divine understanding is one "through whose representation the objects would themselves at the same time be given, or produced" (B 145). Heidegger corrects Kant's vocabulary, as he says outright three paragraphs later: "Strictly speaking, one should not speak of 'Object'". The correction presents itself as clarification. It removes from Kant the possibility that the thing in itself is a determinate object we lack the means to know. A marginal note writes "Objectivity is Being [*Seyn*]! in the empirical sense"; it belongs to the same retrospective voice as the 1930s note before the book.

**¶16, 31.** The being "in the appearance" is "the same being as the being in itself, and this alone", revealed according to the manner and scope of finite taking-in and determining. A later marginal note narrows the claim: the sameness is that of "the That of the X", not of the What. No Kant locus is cited. Kant's text supports and resists the claim in roughly equal measure. B xxvii supports it (unit ¶19). Elsewhere Kant speaks of a "non-sensible cause" of appearances (A 494/B 522) and of a "transcendental object ... which might be the ground of this appearance that we call matter" (A 277/B 333). Those formulas suggest a relation of ground between two terms, and Heidegger reads them all in the light of B xxvii.

**¶17, 31–32.** "Appearance" has a wider sense, phenomena as a kind of object (A 235 heading, A 249), and a narrower one, the content of affection stripped of determination: "The undetermined object of an empirical intuition is called appearance" (A 20/B 34); to appear is "to be an Object of empirical intuition" (A 89/B 121). The quotations are exact. The A 89/B 121 phrase stands in Kant's argument that "an object can appear to us only by means of such pure forms of sensibility, i.e., be an object of empirical intuition", a sentence about the forms of sensibility, not about finitude. A 249 belongs to a part of the chapter on phenomena and noumena that Kant cut in B. Heidegger cites it without saying so, and it gives him the wider sense he wants.

**¶18, 32.** Appearances "are not mere illusion [*Schein*]" but the being itself, not something other than the thing in itself; the double characterization corresponds to the two relations, "the being in the standing-forth [*Entstand*] and the same being as object [*Gegenstand*]". The first half is Kant's: to say that objects appear "is not to say that these objects would be a mere **illusion**", and "this object as **appearance** is to be distinguished from itself as object **in itself**" (B 69, a passage added in B). The second clause also gives Heidegger his "same being". The second half grounds the distinction in two kinds of intuition. Kant grounds it in the ideality of space and time as forms of our sensibility, from which the restriction of cognition to appearances follows (A 26/B 42; B xxv–xxvi). For Kant the infinite intuition is the illustration of B 72; for Heidegger it is the ground. That is the most consequential re-inscription in §5, and it explains why the Aesthetic's own proof of ideality plays no part in Heidegger's account of the thing in itself.

**¶19, 32–33.** Kant says the *Critique* teaches that "the Object is to be taken in a *twofold sense*, namely, as appearance and as thing in itself" (B xxvii); strictly, absolute knowledge has no objects; the *Opus postumum* says the thing in itself "is not another Object, but is rather another aspect (*respectus*) of the representation of *the same Object*" (Adickes's edition, not in the corpus; Heidegger's emphasis). B xxvii is quoted correctly. Kant's sentence goes on to apply the double meaning to the freedom of the will (B xxvii–xxviii), which is its purpose in the B preface. The *Opus postumum* is the late Kant of the 1790s, and Heidegger uses it to fix the sense of the *Critique*: Kant's development is treated as one position, and a late formula decides a disputed point. A marginal note quoting another *Opus postumum* passage, found through Jancke's critique of Heidegger, reinforces the reading; it is later than the text. The one-being reading has since become a standard position in Kant scholarship, and B xxvii supports it. What is not Kant's is the ground Heidegger gives it, the two intuitions of the previous unit.

**¶20, 33–34.** "Behind the appearance" means that finite knowledge "necessarily conceals at the same time", in advance, so that the thing in itself is "absolutely inaccessible to such knowledge by its very essence"; "According to the *Critique*, everything in an appearance is itself again appearance" (reply to Eberhard, not in the corpus). Concealment is Heidegger's term, and it anticipates the doctrine of untruth (§35). Kant's corresponding claim is epistemic and has no vocabulary of concealing: we have "nothing to do with anything except appearances anywhere (in the world of sense), even in the deepest research into its objects" (A 45/B 62–63), and the thing in itself is "uncognized by us" (B xx). Heidegger turns Kant's restriction of cognition into a structure of finite knowing that hides as it reveals.

**¶21, 34.** To prove by "positivist critique" that things in themselves are unknowable misunderstands the concept, since such proofs treat the thing in itself as an object of finite knowledge that happens to be inaccessible; "mere appearance" denies only that the being is known infinitely; Kant is quoted from A 45/B 62–63, exactly. Kant's own sentence supports the reading: we believe we cognize things in themselves only when "our transcendental distinction is lost" (B 62–63), so the inaccessibility is not a fact to be shown by experience. Heidegger reconstructs faithfully here. The target, "positivist critique", is Kant's nineteenth-century reception, not Kant.

**¶22, 34–35.** The double meaning of "outside us" (A 373) shows the difference: as thing in itself the being is outside us because we lack infinite intuition, as appearance because we are not it and yet have access to it; appearance and thing in itself are not "two classifications of objects arranged one behind the other". The citation is to the fourth paralogism of the first edition, which Kant replaced in B by the Refutation of Idealism (B 274–279). Heidegger cites the A Paralogisms as Kant's view without noting that B dropped them. In Kant the double meaning serves an argument against the empirical idealist, who confuses things outside us in the transcendental sense with things found in space (A 373). Heidegger reads a distinction made to refute the idealist as a distinction between two relations to one being.

**¶23, 35.** The characterization of finitude has indicated the dimension of the laying of the ground and the direction of the regress. A transition. In §5 the finitude of §4's thesis has absorbed Kant's distinction of appearance and thing in itself, which now rests on finitude rather than on the forms of sensibility.

##### §6. The Source-Ground of the Laying of the Ground for Metaphysics

**¶1, 35–36.** The understanding depends on intuition: "For we can understand nothing except what one of our words brings with it corresponding to something in intuition" (A 277/B 333). When Kant says "Neither of these qualities {sensibility and understanding} is to be preferred to the other" (A 51/B 75), this seems to contradict the primacy of intuition; but the belonging-together "does not exclude but rather includes an order of precedence in the structural grounding of thinking in intuition", which must not be levelled into "an indifferent correlation of content and form". Heidegger confronts the passage that tells against him and reads it so that his primacy stands. Kant's reasons for parity are both claims of indispensability: "Without sensibility no object would be given to us, and without understanding none would be thought. Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind" (A 51/B 75). Commentary R judges parity of indispensability and precedence in grounding compatible, and on Kant's stated reasons they are. The A 277/B 333 sentence is used out of its setting: Kant says it of the "transcendental object" behind matter, "a mere something, about which we would not understand what it is even if someone could tell us". It is a sentence about the limits of understanding, enlisted here for the understanding's dependence on intuition, which it also implies.

**¶2, 36.** One might stop at the duality of elements, the more so since Kant orients the "springing forth" of cognition on "two basic sources of the mind", the receptivity of impressions and the spontaneity of concepts (A 50/B 74), and says that "other than these two sources of knowledge" we have "no others" (A 294/B 350). Both quotations are accurate. The second stands in Kant's theory of error: "because we have no other sources of cognition besides these two, it follows that error is effected only through the unnoticed influence of sensibility on understanding" (A 294/B 350). The Third Section will add a third source, the imagination, which Kant himself counts among the sources in the A-Deduction (A 94, A 115). A 294/B 350 is therefore among the strongest Kantian texts against the book's later thesis, and Heidegger quotes it here, before the thesis is in play.

**¶3, 36.** The duality is no juxtaposition: "Only through their union can knowledge spring forth" (A 51/B 75–76); the unity of their union is no later result, but "what unites them, this 'synthesis,' must let the elements in their belonging-together and their oneness spring forth"; hence naming the two sources already points to their source-ground, "an original unity". The quotation stops at the end of Kant's sentence, and the next sentence goes the other way: "But on this account one must not mix up their roles, rather one has great cause to separate them carefully from each other and distinguish them" (A 51–52/B 76). From the necessity of union Kant draws the duty of separation, and on it he rests the division of aesthetic from logic. Heidegger draws from it the priority of a unifying ground. The inference that what unites must precede and generate what it unites is Heidegger's; at this point Kant's text supports only that cognition needs both. The omitted sentence is the conclusion Kant drew from the premise Heidegger quotes.

**¶4, 37.** At the beginning and the end of the *Critique* Kant goes beyond enumeration: the "two stems of human knowledge ... which perhaps spring forth from a common, but to us unknown, root" (A 15/B 29), and the point "where the general root of our cognitive power divides and branches out into two stems, one of which is **reason**", reason meaning "the entire higher faculty of cognition", so that "I therefore contrast the rational to the empirical" (A 835/B 863). The empirical here, Heidegger adds, is "the experiencing taking of things in stride: receptivity, sensibility as such". Both quotations are exact in Heidegger's German; Taft's copy renders the second as "common root", Kemp Smith's phrase, where Kant wrote "general root" (*allgemeine Wurzel*), and Heidegger's next paragraph turns on the difference. The gloss on "the empirical" is Heidegger's. Kant's next paragraph explains his contrast as one between kinds of cognition, "historical" cognition *ex datis* and "rational" cognition *ex principiis* (A 836/B 864). The architectonic's two stems are kinds of cognition, not sensibility and understanding as faculties. The two pairs line up loosely, and Heidegger's identification of the empirical with receptivity makes them line up exactly.

**¶5, 37.** In the first passage the root is qualified by "perhaps", in the second it "is reputed to exist"; in both it is only pointed to; Kant does not pursue it and calls it "unknown to us"; hence his laying of the ground "goes into and points consciously toward the unknown. It is a philosophizing laying of the ground for Philosophy." The observation about "perhaps" is exact: A 835 has no "perhaps". The inference changes the role of the unknown. Kant mentions the root in the Introduction to justify an order of exposition, sensibility before understanding, "since the conditions under which alone the objects of human cognition are given precede those under which those objects are thought" (A 15–16/B 29–30). "Unknown to us" marks a limit Kant does not propose to cross. Heidegger turns the limit into a direction. That is an extension in form and a contestation in substance, since the Third Section will name what Kant called unknown: Kant's "unknown" becomes Heidegger's "not yet unveiled".

#### II. The Manner of the Unveiling of the Origin

##### §7. The Outline of the Stages in the Laying of the Ground for Ontology

**¶1, 38.** The grounding of metaphysics is the projection of the inner possibility of a priori synthesis; the account of finite knowledge and of the basic sources has given the question "more precision" and made it "more complex". A transition. "Projection of the inner possibility" now stands where Kant's question of how synthetic judgments a priori are possible stood in §3.

**¶2, 38.** Since knowledge of beings rests on a prior knowledge of their constitution of being, and finite knowledge is receptive, finite knowledge "requires a knowing which does not take things in stride (and which is apparently nonfinite), such as a 'creative' intuiting". The paradox is Heidegger's formulation of a problem Kant poses in other terms. Kant asks how an intuition can precede its objects: "how can an outer intuition inhabit the mind that precedes the objects themselves, and in which the concept of the latter can be determined *a priori*?", and answers, "Obviously not otherwise than insofar as it has its seat merely in the subject, as its formal constitution for being affected by objects" (B 41). Kant's answer makes the a priori a form of receptivity, and no creative intuition is needed. Heidegger's statement of the problem brings back the creative intuition Kant's answer had made unnecessary, so that §§28–33 can show how a finite being is formative without being creative.

**¶3, 38–39.** "How can a finite creature ... know, i.e., intuit, prior to all [instances of] taking the being in stride, without being its 'creator?' In other words: how must this finite creature be with respect to the constitution of its own Being so that such a bringing-forward of the constitution of the Being of beings which is free from experience, i.e., an ontological synthesis, is possible?" The second question rewrites Kant's answer at B 41. Kant's "formal constitution" of the subject becomes "the constitution of its own Being" (*Seinsverfassung*), and the question is no longer what forms the subject has but what kind of being the subject is. This is the hinge from Kant's question to fundamental ontology, and it is set as a question.

**¶4, 39.** A complication: since all finite knowledge is itself a synthesis, the a priori synthesis "is not identical with the above-named veritative synthesis, which is concerned solely with ontic knowledge". No Kant locus. The distinctions of §5 are now applied to Kant's a priori synthesis, which must be a synthesis of pure elements.

**¶5, 39.** Hence the order: the pure elements, pure intuition and pure thinking; their essential unity, "the pure veritative synthesis", which must also determine pure intuition a priori, so that the concepts belonging to it spring forth prior to all experience in content as well as form; hence "the question concerning the essence of the 'ontological predicate' must shift to the center". The order follows the *Critique*'s: Aesthetic, then the clue to the categories, then their deduction. "Ontological predicate" is Heidegger's name for the category; Kant reserves "predicables" for the pure derivative concepts (A 82/B 108). The requirement that the categories' content be a priori is Kant's: the understanding "brings a transcendental content into its representations", which are therefore called pure concepts "that pertain to objects *a priori*; this can never be accomplished by general logic" (A 79/B 105).

**¶6, 39.** The question presses further back to the ground of the pure synthesis; only then does it appear how ontological knowledge conditions ontic knowledge, and "the full essence of ontological truth is circumscribed". No Kant locus. The "full essence of ontological truth" will be §24's reading of the highest principle of synthetic judgments (A 154–158/B 193–197) together with transcendental truth (A 146/B 185).

**¶7, 39–40.** Five stages: the essential elements of pure knowledge; their essential unity; the inner possibility of that unity, the ontological synthesis; the ground of that possibility; the full determination of the essence of ontological knowledge. The five stages are not Kant's divisions; they redistribute the Analytic. The first takes the Aesthetic and the clue to the categories, the second §10 of the clue (A 76–79/B 102–105), the third the transcendental deduction, chiefly in A, the fourth the Schematism, and the fifth the highest principle of synthetic judgments. The Analytic of Principles beyond its highest principle, the chapter on phenomena and noumena and the whole Dialectic fall outside them. Kant's architectonic puts the Schematism at the threshold of the "transcendental doctrine of the power of judgment" (A 136–137/B 175–176). Heidegger makes it the ground. The selection is announced: the stages are introduced as the "inner movement" behind Kant's arrangement (43). It is the book's largest editorial act on Kant's text.

##### §8. The Method for Revealing the Origin

**¶1, 40.** The preliminary account showed a wealth of syntheses; the pure veritative synthesis contains the idea of a knowledge that "appears to be nonfinite"; and the indication of the source-ground "leads to the unknown". A recapitulation. "Leads to the unknown" restates §6's reading of Kant's "unknown to us" as a direction, and the method is built on it.

**¶2, 40.** The method remains undetermined at first; the region is the human "mind" (*mens sive animus*), whose opening would fall to "psychology" and, since knowledge is taken as judging, to "logic"; the two divide the task or fight for preeminence. *Gemüt*, mind, is Kant's word (A 19/B 33; A 50/B 74). The division of labour between logic and psychology is the dispute of Kant's neo-Kantian reception, and Kant himself banishes empirical psychology from metaphysics (A 848–849/B 876–877). Heidegger states a problem of the reception in Kant's vocabulary.

**¶3, 40–41.** Given the originality of what Kant sought and the unfitness of the traditional disciplines, it is "hopeless" to grasp the essential by a "logical" or "psychological" questioning; "'Transcendental Psychology' merely expresses a perplexity". Kant's own use of the label is pointed. In the first-edition Paralogisms "transcendental psychology" is one of three "putative sciences of pure reason", with cosmology and theology (A 396–397, first edition only): the illusory rational psychology the Dialectic takes apart. Heidegger turns the label against interpreters who read the *Critique*'s analysis of the faculties as transcendental psychology. Kant also distinguishes his deduction from Locke's "physiological derivation" (A 86–87/B 118–119) and says that the subjective side of the deduction, though "of great importance in respect of my chief end", "does not belong essentially to it" (A xvi–xvii). That last passage matters in §31, where Heidegger argues that the subjective deduction is the essential one. Here he joins Kant in refusing psychology; in §31 he will refuse the reason Kant gave for keeping the subjective side subordinate.

**¶4, 41.** The method is left open, mindful of what Kant wrote to Marcus Herz after finishing the *Critique*: "This sort of inquiry will always remain difficult" (letter of 1781, not in the corpus). Taft's note supplies the rest of Kant's sentence: "for it includes the metaphysics of metaphysics". Heidegger stops before the clause that would have given his reading its strongest Kantian support, the *Critique* as a metaphysics of metaphysics. For once the cut removes support rather than resistance.

**¶5, 41.** The procedure can be called "analytic" in the broadest sense; Kant calls the *Critique* "a 'study of our inner nature'" (A 703/B 731), and "This revealing of the essence of human Dasein is 'to the philosophers, however, even a duty.'" Kant's sentence says what the study is: "the resolution of all our transcendental cognition into its elements (as a study of our inner nature) not only has in itself no small value, but is even a duty for a philosopher" (A 703/B 731; the German of both editions in the corpus reads "transcendent", where Guyer and Wood print "transcendental"). Three shifts occur. Kant's study of inner nature is a resolution into elements, which Heidegger's next paragraph denies analytic is. It stands at the end of the Dialectic, where it serves to expose the illusions of reason, not to ground ontology. And "our inner nature" becomes "the essence of human Dasein". A later marginal note points the method toward transcendental reflection (A 262–263/B 319), the Amphiboly once more.

**¶6, 41–42.** "Analytic" does not mean breaking finite pure reason into elements, "but rather the reverse: an 'unknotting' as a freeing which loosens the seeds [*Keime*] of ontology"; in Kant's words, it brings "to light through reason" "what reason brings forth entirely from out of itself" (A xx); analytic becomes "a letting-be-seen [*Sehenlassen*] of the genesis of the essence of finite pure reason from its proper ground". The word in scare quotes, *Auflösen*, is Kant's word from the sentence just quoted, *Auflösung ... in ihre Elemente* (A 703/B 731), and Heidegger reverses its sense: a resolution into elements becomes a loosening that lets a genesis be seen. The A xx quotation comes from Kant's claim of completeness: the *Critique* is "nothing but the **inventory** of all we possess through **pure reason,** ordered systematically", from which nothing can escape, "because what reason brings forth entirely out of itself cannot be hidden" (A xx). Kant's sentence guarantees an inventory; Heidegger's makes it a genesis. Kant does describe his Analytic as "the much less frequently attempted **analysis of the faculty of understanding** itself" rather than of concepts (A 65/B 90), the best support Heidegger has for an analytic that does not break things into elements, and he does not cite it. "Letting-be-seen" is *Being and Time*'s definition of phenomenology (§7). The *Critique*'s method has been renamed phenomenology.

**¶7, 42.** Such analytic is "the projecting anticipation of the entire inner essence of finite pure reason", a construction of its foundations that brings metaphysics to the ground in which it is rooted as "a 'haunting' of human nature" (*Heimsuchung*, from B xv). Kant's verb at B xv is *heimgesucht*: "Why then has nature afflicted our reason with the restless striving for such a path, as if it were one of reason's most important occupations?" Kant asks it as a question about metaphysics' failure to become a science. Heidegger makes the affliction the soil in which metaphysics is rooted. "Construction" names his own method, which §43 defends against the charge of arbitrariness. Kant's own answer to the B xv question is the doctrine of the ideas of reason in the Dialectic, and it plays no part here.

The first part of the Second Section makes three Kantian texts load-bearing: the first sentence of the Aesthetic (A 19/B 33), the contrast of derivative and original intuition (B 71–72, B 135–145), and the root of the two stems (A 15/B 29, A 835/B 863). The reading holds against Kant's text on four points: the receptive definition of sensibility (A 19/B 33; B 146–147), the mediateness of judgment (A 68/B 93), the inaccessibility of things in themselves as a consequence of the transcendental distinction rather than an empirical fact (B 62–63), and the one-being reading of appearance and thing in itself (B xxvii). It does not hold where Heidegger turns Kant's illustration into a ground. The intuitus originarius, which Kant counts "only as an illustration ... and not as a ground of its proof" (B 72), becomes the measure of finitude and the ground of the distinction between appearance and thing in itself. Kant's contrast of an intuiting understanding (B 135, B 145) becomes a difference located "primarily" in intuition. The selection of the First Section continues and sharpens. Heidegger omits the sentence after "Only from their unification can cognition arise", which commands separation (A 51–52/B 76). He omits the definition of the "study of our inner nature" as resolution into elements (A 703/B 731) and then inverts its word. He restates Kant's ground for the a priori, the formal constitution of the subject (B 41), as the constitution of being of a finite creature. And he reads the B text two ways, as clarifying A at B 33 and, as §31 will show, as Kant's retreat. Much here is announced as Heidegger's own: the thesis of finitude is a summary of "presuppositions", the five stages an "inner movement" behind Kant's arrangement, the unknown root a direction. The quotations are again exact.

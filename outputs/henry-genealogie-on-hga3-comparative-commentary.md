# Michel Henry, *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*, Chapters III and IV, read against Heidegger's *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* (GA 3)

Written for: readers of the two existing commentaries who want to know what the *Généalogie* owes to, and does with, the Kantbuch.

Henry is cited by paragraph of the chapter in Commentary R (R ¶7, 129) and by his printed page. Chapter IV is read from the converted text in the sources repo, whose running heads give the page (the last page marker before a passage). Chapter III is not converted; it is read from the page images of the scan in the sources repo's pdf-input (printed page = PDF index (0-based) plus 2). Commentary R's page numbers for Chapter IV are off by one at four places, and this commentary gives the corrected page: ¶1 (126, not 125), ¶8 (130, not 129), ¶24 (139, not 138) and the auto-affection sentence in ¶53 (157, not 156). The Kantbuch is cited by GA 3 section and page, read from the German running heads. Henry quotes Kant in the Tremesaygues and Pacaud translation (PUF 1963), by page and without A/B numbers. No Kant text is on disk, so Henry's readings of Kant are tested only where the Kantbuch quotes the same passage.

The decisive fact about the pair is in Henry's own text. He nowhere cites the Kantbuch. Chapter IV names Heidegger once in the chapter and its thirty-five notes (R ¶25, 139: space is in time and, "as Heidegger will say, 'in the world'", with no source). Chapter III cites Heidegger's *Nietzsche II*, the *Brief über den Humanismus*, *Sein und Zeit*, *Der Satz vom Grund* and *Identität und Differenz*, all in French, and no passage of GA 3. Commentary R mentions the Kantbuch five times in Chapter IV (R ¶2, ¶4, ¶6, ¶19, ¶29). Those references, and its remark that the note on Nabert and Lachièze-Rey places Henry "against Heidegger", are the commentary's own and are not in Henry. The relation is therefore one of presupposition and not of take-up, and where the commentary writes that Henry "follows" or "inverts" the Kantbuch, it means that the two texts stand in that relation, not that Henry says so.

The two chapters stand differently to it. Chapter III attacks a later Heidegger, that of the history of being and of the *Brief*, and the comparison asks whether the doctrine attacked is already in the Kantbuch of 1929, whether the Kantbuch holds what Henry says it lacks, and where the later Heidegger has moved. Chapter IV is a second statement of the Kant argument of *L'essence de la manifestation*, Section II, whose relation to GA 3 has been run in full, and this commentary does not repeat that run. It points to it where a thesis carries over and says where Henry has changed it.

Five traps govern the pair. Henry's "representation" (the ecstatic horizon of objectness) is not Kant's *Vorstellung*, which GA 3 §4, 22 reports as the genus of intuition and concept alike. Kant's *Realität* is whatness, not actuality (GA 3 §18, 86-87). The Kantbuch's "nothing" is a nothing that is "something" (GA 3 §25, 121-123). Henry works on the B edition, the Kantbuch prefers the A edition and reads B's account of inner sense as Kant's recoil (GA 3 §31, 163-165). And *Selbstheit* (selfhood, a temporal circuit) is not Henry's *ipséité* (ipseity, auto-affection as pathos), though the 1953 French renders one by the other. Two more concern Chapter III. The Heideggers differ: the Kantbuch's thrownness is delivery to beings already there (§§41, 43), the *Brief*'s is a throw by being. And Henry's "Heidegger" is made of translations, so a term such as "ek-sistence" belongs to the *Brief*, where the Kantbuch has "ecstatic" once (§24, 119).

## Chapter III. The Insertion of the "Ego Cogito" into the "History of Western Metaphysics"

Chapter III reads no passage of the Kantbuch. Henry's Heidegger here is the Heidegger of *Nietzsche II* (read in Klossowski's French, Gallimard 1971, as "N II"), of the *Brief über den Humanismus* (Munier's French, in *Questions III*, 1966), of *Sein und Zeit* (the French page cited), of *Der Satz vom Grund* (Préau, 1962) and of *Identität und Differenz*. The chapter is therefore not a take-up of GA 3 and cannot be graded as one. It states what Henry attributes to "Heidegger" in later works, and the comparison asks whether the doctrine he attacks is already in the Kantbuch of 1929, whether the Kantbuch holds what Henry says it lacks, and where the later Heidegger has moved. Units that touch no Kantbuch locus say so briefly and name what in the Kantbuch bears on them. Page numbers are Henry's, from the printed page; the unit numbers are those of Commentary R's Chapter III (64 paragraphs). Commentary R's own page marks are sound here except where the unit is split across a page, and this commentary gives the span.

R ¶1, 87. Henry opens by saying that with Heidegger the "denaturing of the *cogito* is immediate and complete", "I think" meaning "I represent", and that what matters is the thesis that the history of metaphysics is the history of being. The Kantbuch has no history of being and no reading of Descartes: Descartes appears in it only in a note on the *Regulae* (§17, 85 n. 120) and in a later marginal note (Anhang I, 253). Its Kant, however, already holds the equation Henry names. "The 'standing' I ... because it holds before itself, as 'I think', that is 'I represent' (*ich stelle vor*), something like stand and constancy" (GA 3 §34, 193). The denaturing Henry attacks is a feature of the Kantbuch's Kant (1929), not only of the later Descartes. What the Kantbuch lacks is the framing Henry rejects: the equation is there a thesis about the finite subject of the *Critique*, with no claim that being sends itself as representedness.

R ¶2, 88. Heidegger's thesis, as Henry reports it from N II 115, 119-120, is that the *hypokeimenon*, "that which already lies before from itself" (*das von sich aus schon Vorliegende*), is now interpreted as man (the *subjectum*), and that each epoch of being sends itself in the withdrawal of being. The Kantbuch treats *subjectum* as presence: the schema of substance brings "constant presence" into view (GA 3 §31, 165). The epochal sending, being's withdrawal, is later: the Kantbuch has forgetting as belonging to the understanding of being (§42, 233), and no sending. The thesis Henry reports is of the later Heidegger.

R ¶3, 88-89. Henry says the Cartesian *cogito* "excludes man rather than placing him at the centre", since the reduction leaves no eyes, ears or body, and that the idea of man appears only later, when the regard has slid "from *cogito* to *cogitatum*". The Kantbuch has the opposite emphasis for Kant: it begins from "human pure reason" and its finitude (GA 3 §4, 21) and ends in the question what man is (§§37-38, 208-217). Its transcendental subject is human by name. Henry's remark that man enters Descartes late is a statement about Descartes, and the Kantbuch cannot test it.

R ¶4, 89. Henry asks how, "by Heidegger's own account", man claims the role: not as man but as *cogito*, which is not a being but "a structure of pure phenomenality that claims to constitute the *fundamentum inconcussum veritatis*", and he charges the Heideggerian commentary with confusing "man" and "subject". The Kantbuch has a position on this which Henry does not weigh. It says that "more originally than man is the finitude of Dasein in him", and that if man is man only on the ground of Dasein in him, the question of what is more original "can in principle be no anthropological one" (GA 3 §41, 229-230). So the 1929 Heidegger himself denies that the transcendental structure is man, and Henry's charge that "man" is a front for a structure (R ¶35) is a point the Kantbuch makes first. It also says "all anthropology, even philosophical anthropology, has posited man already as man" (230). Henry's target in this unit is the later Heidegger's slide of *cogito* to man in N II; the Kantbuch does not slide in that way.

R ¶5, 89. Heidegger's gloss, in Henry's report, makes *cogitare* and *percipere* "taking possession ... disposing it toward oneself", "setting before oneself", the same as *Vorstellung* in its double sense of *vorstellen* and *vorgestellt*, with the represented "put at one's disposal" and assured. The Kantbuch's *Vorstellung* is wider and weaker: "representation" has "the wide formal sense that something indicates, announces, presents another"; with consciousness it is *perceptio*, "a knowing of the announcing and of being announced" (GA 3 §4, 22). The "taking possession" gloss is not there. The Kantbuch's representing is a letting-stand-over-against that receives what announces itself (§16, 70-71), not a disposal of it. The gloss Henry attacks is the later reading, and Henry's attack on "taking possession" does not reach the Kantbuch's broad sense, which is the one Kant's genus (*repraesentatio*) demands.

R ¶6, 89-91. A decisive question has been passed over by Descartes and the tradition: that of the *ego*, of the particular one who says "I think". Heidegger, Henry says, does not ask it: the "I" is evacuated, as in Husserl, Sartre and Merleau-Ponty, by the sub-thoughts grouped as "structuralism", and "as we shall see, Kant himself" was incapable of grounding the proposition "I am" (91). The Kantbuch does not ask the question of the particular I either: its selfhood is a structure of finite subjectivity (§34, 190-191) and the individual does not appear. That is true of the Kantbuch and supports Henry's claim, as does Chapter IV's. The remark about Kant is the preview of R ¶31 and R ¶40 of Chapter IV. The Kantbuch does not treat the "I am" (B 422 note), as the Chapter IV comparison found.

R ¶7, 90-91. Nietzsche II's interest, Henry says, is that it takes the question of the ego "head on", linking it, not to an ontic plane, but to the essence of phenomenality. The ego is to be established in "what is proper to it", in its power of exhibition and legitimation, and, for Descartes, the *cogito* is reduced to "I represent me" under the demand to be "posed in all certainty". The quotation from N II 124 ("every *ego cogito* is *cogito me cogitare*") is the thesis. The Kantbuch has a close analogue for Kant: the "I" is manifest "in the 'self' that is moved out with" the turning-toward, "not in a side act of knowing directed at thinking" (GA 3 §29, 150). The Kantbuch reads the I of the "I think" as accompanying all representing and as the representing's own selfhood, which is N II's thesis without the word *me cogitare*. The difference lies in the second claim of §29: the Kantbuch adds that the consciousness of the self "can be clarified only from the being of the self, not conversely" (150). That claim already contradicts Henry's reading (R ¶17) that Heidegger derives the ego from representing.

R ¶8, 91-92. Henry denies that in every representation the self presents itself as a correlate, as if, representing Freiburg cathedral, I had to represent myself beside it. It is by an essential necessity that the I that represents is "pro-jected and implied in its own representation", and so is presupposed a priori as belonging to the constitution of the field. The Kantbuch's account of Kant matches this closely and also avoids the objection Henry makes against the straw version. The I "accompanies" all representing, but "it is not a side act" (§29, 150). Henry's argument and the Kantbuch's say the same thing about the structure: the I is neither a second object nor an inference, but belongs to the field. What differs is the verdict. Henry takes this belonging as presupposition of an ipseity that representation cannot produce, the Kantbuch as the sign that the I is the horizon's own constancy (§34, 193).

R ¶9, 92. Because Heidegger "confuses the self with man", Henry says, he can write (N II 125-126) that in every representing it is man who represents himself, and that the self of man constitutes the *subjectum*. The Kantbuch has no *subjectum* thesis of that form for the self, and it denies the confusion in advance (§41, 229-230, quoted at ¶4). Where the Kantbuch speaks of "man" in the I's place, it speaks of "human subjectivity" as the finitude that the transcendental structure exhibits (§38, 214-218). The charge is a charge against N II.

R ¶10, 92-93. A theory of the ego and of the "I am" is formed as the explanation of *ego cogito ergo sum* and springs from the presupposition that *cogito* means "I represent me". There is no inference from *cogito* to *sum*, since the disposing-before-oneself of the representer, which is his being, is the same as the representing. Henry replies that Descartes has no such problematic. The Kantbuch has no reading of *sum*, and holds the same identity for Kant by a different path: its time is the I (§34, 191), so that for the Kantbuch the I's being is not inferred but is its temporal self-affection. The "no inference" point is thus the Kantbuch's too. Henry's rejection concerns the grounding of the I in representing; the Kantbuch grounds it in time.

R ¶11, 93-94. Nowhere in Descartes, Henry says, is there any hint of a problematic in which ipseity depends on representation; the *ego* appears at the extreme of the reduction, where doubt is alone in the world and "there is no longer any representation". That is a reading of Descartes, tested here against nothing in the Kantbuch. The Kantbuch's only Cartesian remark is later and undated: its notes link Kant's "I think" as "the most original a priori" to a presupposition "that Kant holds with Descartes and Leibniz", the primacy of logic grounded in it (GA 3 Anhang I, 253; marginal, retrospective). Heidegger there puts Descartes' premise inside Kant, and not the reverse.

R ¶12, 94. For the first demonstration, art. 26 of the *Passions* is enough: all that is represented is doubtful and uncertain, while the passion of the soul is certain by immanence. The Kantbuch has nothing on the *Passions*. Its own treatment of feeling in §30 (157-160), where the feeling subject feels itself in respect, is the nearest non-Cartesian locus, and it says that in respect the acting self is manifest in an unobjectifying and unthematic way (160). Henry's use of the *Passions* is for ipseity in affective immanence. The Kantbuch's feeling is rooted in the imagination, and the comparison cannot be pushed further than the shared claim that feeling is a way the self is manifest to itself without being represented.

R ¶13, 95. The Cartesian critique of secondary qualities has an "abyssal" meaning, a division between what is dead and what is living: sensations are of life, where there is no ek-stasis. Henry opposes this to the quality "stretched on the thing", which is only the unreal representation of an impression. The Kantbuch's counterpart is the sensible content that "announces itself" as being itself, within a horizon (GA 3 §5, 26, 32; §34, 189). The Kantbuch has no theory of qualities, and the contrast with Chapter IV's "representative sensation" (R ¶37 there) is Henry's own.

R ¶14, 95-96. Henry says Heidegger's claim, in N II 346, that representing (*percipere*, *co-agitare*, *cogitare*, *repraesentare in uno*) is a "fundamental trait of all human comportment, even non-cognitive", "must be not only contested but reversed", because the comportments the claim lists (feelings, passions, will) are heterogeneous to representing and so make the original dimension of the *cogito*. The Kantbuch is on the other side of that dispute only in part. It reads feeling as an unthematic self-disclosure (§30, 157-160) and says of the sensible that the Kantbuch's *Vorstellung* genus includes sensation (§4, 22). So the Kantbuch does not say, as N II does in Henry's report, that all comportment is representing in the sense of setting-before: it says that representation in Kant's wide sense includes what Henry wants to exclude. Henry's reversal therefore attacks a thesis the Kantbuch does not make in that form.

R ¶15, 96-97. If one cannot go back further than this "principial immediacy", it must also ground the essence of ipseity: Descartes did not elucidate it, but he placed in it "not only thought, being, 'there is', but a 'I think, I am'", and the original phenomenalization of phenomenality is accomplished as ipseity "inasmuch as appearing appears to itself in an immediate and distanceless auto-affection, independently of ek-stasis and representation". The Kantbuch's selfhood is the opposite pole: the self comes from "the from-itself-toward and back-to-itself" of time (§34, 191), which is not distanceless. This is the place where Henry's non-ecstatic self-appearing is set against a structure the Kantbuch has already named. The Kantbuch cannot confirm or refute a distanceless auto-affection, since it claims none, and its own "pure self-affection" is the case Henry will say is no auto-affection (Chapter IV, R ¶35).

R ¶16, 97. Because ipseity lies in the original essence of thought, in the *videor* that precedes the *videre*, the claim to ground the *ego* on the seeing of representation is a paralogism, "all the more deceptive as it can invoke an 'appearance' which must be read and deconstructed". Every act of representing implies that the representer pro-jects himself to the horizon of his act: but it is because he is a Self and already "has the Self in him" that he can represent. The Kantbuch states a close relative: the consciousness of the self can be clarified only from the being of the self (GA 3 §29, 150). It also gives a version of the "already has" in its own terms: the finite self is constituted by the "from-itself-toward ... and back to itself" before any act of reflection (§34, 191). The two agree that the self is not derived from the representing. They differ on what the being of the self is.

R ¶17, 97. Every proposition in which Heidegger ties the *ego* to representation and derives it from it must be reversed: the *ego* is not "the self on which the representing, as such, essentially returns and so posits itself as what it is", but the reverse. The Kantbuch does not derive the I from the representing. It makes the I the structure of the representing's own horizon, and it denies that the I's consciousness grounds its being (§29, 150). Where N II, in Henry's report, derives the *ego* from the representing, the Kantbuch gives priority to the I's being. Henry's reversal here is then already partly in the Kantbuch, and the part it does not contain is the claim that this being is life.

R ¶18, 97-98. The statement that "because ... the relation to the one who represents belongs to representing, the one who represents ... can be called 'I' ..." (N II 133, 131) is inverted: he can be named "I" only if he already is it, "in and by himself, on the ground of ipseity". The Kantbuch's "I" accompanies all representing and is "manifest" in the "self" taken out into the turning (§29, 150). It does not say that the I is called an I only because of the relation. It says the "I" of this "self" "is necessarily manifest". Henry's inversion states what the Kantbuch says about order (being before consciousness), though not what it says about the manner of the manifesting, which is the going-along and not the self-feeling.

R ¶19, 98-99. Henry answers the objection that a tree reflected in the river "represents" its image: it does not transcend toward it, because to represent is to pro-ject oneself, and one can do so only as a Self, so that ipseity is immanent to representation as its condition and unexplained by it. The Kantbuch has the notion that Henry here uses for the structure: the "I" is "carried out" in the turning-toward (§29, 150). It has none of Henry's mirror example. 

R ¶20, 99. The paralogism "is more easily recognized when referred to its Cartesian context": Descartes sought an unshakable foundation of truth and found it in the *ego cogito*, the "consistency of myself as *res cogitans*" lying in the "sure and stable determination of the representing" (N II 132). Heidegger's thesis has two interlocking claims, the ego as representing and certainty as the stability of the represented. The Kantbuch has no theory of certainty and no foundation for truth in the *ego*: its "ground" is the ground-laying of metaphysics in the finite subject (GA 3 §§1-3). The Kantbuch also says that the *ego's* "standing and abiding" is a transcendental determination and "no ontic statement" (§34, 193). So the Kantbuch does not link the I to certainty at all. The second claim is again the later Heidegger's, and Henry's charge of paralogism is not a charge the Kantbuch can answer for.

R ¶21, 99-100. The two theses ("I am insofar as I represent me"; certainty from the stable determining of the representer) are Heidegger's. Henry takes the first as the definition of the *ego* as representing and the second as the grounding of certainty in the stability of the represented. The Kantbuch has the first for Kant (§34, 193) and has not the second. Henry's treatment here separates them more sharply than the German does, but the Kantbuch fits only the first.

R ¶22, 100. In the Cartesianism of the Beginning, everything represented, valid only as set upon the *sub-jectum* of representation, is swept away by the reduction, "struck with nullity". The Kantbuch has no Beginning and no reduction in that sense, and its representing is not swept away by anything. This is a reading of Descartes with a phenomenological premise (the reduction) the Kantbuch does not share.

R ¶23, 100-101. If the *ego* is to ground representation, it must not be the *ego* of representation but one whose ipseity does not depend on it. The Kantbuch's I is exactly the I of representation, its horizon-forming constancy (GA 3 §34, 193), and so falls on the wrong side of Henry's condition. The Kantbuch's gain is that it asks the question Henry's condition poses (what is the being of the I that grounds), and its answer is time.

R ¶24, 101. Henry says that for Descartes representation never presents formal reality but only the objective reality of the idea, an image or double. The Kantbuch's "objective reality" is something else: transcendence gives the a priori its "objective reality", and the Kantbuch warns against taking Kant's *Realität* as actuality (GA 3 §24, 117; §18, 86-87). So Henry's Cartesian sense of "objective reality" (the content of the idea) and Kant's (whatness, given by transcendence) differ, and the Kantbuch is the place where the difference is drawn.

R ¶25, 101. The represented Self is possible only from the real Self, as its mere representation; "a Self that is represented to itself and represents itself in the re-presentation is only a self-represented, not the real Self", an unreal Self "co-objected to the horizon of representation". The Kantbuch does not distinguish a real Self from a represented one: its I is not an object and is not represented as one (§29, 150). Henry's real/represented distinction is thus not in the Kantbuch, and the Kantbuch has only the "unthematic going-along". It has no unreal Self, which is the thing Henry says any representation of the Self must be.

R ¶26, 102. Representation is never the ground in the Cartesianism of the Beginning: it cannot define the essence of certainty and truth any more than of ipseity, because a being becomes true and certain only if it is already so. In the Kantbuch, truth is "transcendental truth", the transcendence itself as original truth, with the horizon as its condition (GA 3 §25, 123-124). Henry's thesis that representation cannot ground truth is, for the Kantbuch, a thesis about the representation of beings (ontic truth), which the horizon makes possible; the horizon is not a representation of beings. So the Kantbuch and Henry agree that the representing of an object presupposes a prior truth, and they differ on what that prior truth is: horizon (the Kantbuch) or the self-revealing of life (Henry).

R ¶27, 102-103. Descartes' doubt strikes not only the unstable representing that "posits here and there" but all representing, including the stable one that holds the represented firmly, so that the "sureness of representing" has none by itself and must be established. The Kantbuch has no doubt and no establishment of the representing's sureness. It has the problem of the legitimacy of the categories, the *quaestio juris* of the deduction, which it reads as the question of the possibility of transcendence (GA 3 §§16-18, 70-89). The Kantbuch's analogue to "establishing the sureness" is the deduction's proof that transcendence is possible, and Henry's remark has no place in it.

R ¶28, 103-104. Heidegger's interpretation "is not innocent": from its failings it draws the benefit of inserting the *ego* into "the history of Western metaphysics", through the *Umschlag* (reversal) of the *idea* into *perceptio* which Heidegger calls "decisive" (N II 183). The Kantbuch has no *Umschlag* of *idea* into *perceptio* and no insertion. Its history is the history of the ground-laying of metaphysics from Aristotle's question (§40, 222-224) to Kant's (§§1-3), and it makes Kant a place at which the ground becomes visible. This differs from Henry's target in kind: the Kantbuch inserts Kant into a history of the question of being, and says that Kant recoiled from what he found (§31, 168), whereas the insertion Henry attacks makes the *cogito* an epoch of being.

R ¶29, 104. How does the Platonic *idea* already make beings possible? By presenting itself beforehand to man's sight as the aspect of a being; the philosophy that begins here is the "apriori condition of possibility" of the being, and Platonism is "already a metaphysics" inasmuch as it no longer thinks the truth of being as such, "paving the way for Aristotle and Kant" for whom it becomes "category". The Kantbuch has no Plato reading beyond the traditional concept of metaphysics (§1, 5-10) and says that the categories are ontological predicates and "conditions of the possibility" of the encounter with beings (§12, 55-58; §24, 117). It reads the categories as horizon-forming and is thereby close to what Henry reports for the *idea*: a condition that lets the being be seen. It does not read the *idea* in this way.

R ¶30, 104. The turn of *idea* into *perceptio*: the *idea* holds from itself the visibility that will be the being's presence, and presents itself as *hypokeimenon*, the underlying, as what is from itself. Henry's report is of N II. The Kantbuch's counterpart is the contrast between a being that encounters "from itself" (GA 3 §16, 70) and the horizon that the finite subject forms to let it do so. This is the contrast Henry draws between Greek and modern, and the Kantbuch has it inside Kant: the being encounters from itself, and the subject forms only the horizon of the encounter.

R ¶31, 104-105. When Descartes interprets being as "I think", that is, in Heidegger's terms "I represent", being is grasped as beingness, an a priori condition of beings, posited in being only insofar as it is represented. The Kantbuch has the corresponding claim in its Kant: the horizon of objectness is the condition of the encounter (§24, 117-119; §25, 121-123), and being, for finite knowledge, is not apprehended as a being (§25, 123). It also has a limit the report omits: for the Kantbuch beings in their own appearing are not reduced to the horizon, since "the appearances are not mere semblance but the being itself" (§5, 32). The "being is representedness" thesis in Henry's report is not the Kantbuch's, which keeps horizon and being apart.

R ¶32, 105. Because the *ego* of man throws itself before itself, it produces representedness as the essence of visibility, and so the a priori condition of beings, and man posits himself as *sub-jectum* and absolute ground. The Kantbuch's finite subject does not produce beings or the "absolute" ground. It produces only the horizon, and "does not create beings" (GA 3 §25, 120-121, 124). The Kantbuch is therefore insistent on the point at which Henry's report of N II claims an absolute: the finite subject is never absolute, and its creativity is "only ontological and never ontic" (124). The subject's self-positing as ground is not a Kantbuch thesis.

R ¶33, 105-106. Modernity begins with the definition of man as Subject: everything is only through him, and the being of beings, their capacity to be represented, is secured by man's self-securing, the "decisive" being "that man be certain and sure of himself" (N II 136, 184, 136, 137). The Kantbuch has no modern age as an epoch of self-securing. Its theme, finite reason, is the opposite of self-securing, and its finite subject has no sureness except in the horizon it must hold open (§25, 123). What it has in common with this report is the claim that for the finite subject objects exist only within the horizon the subject forms. The self-securing of the subject is N II's.

R ¶34, 106-107. It is easy to set against these positions those of Greek thought, of Protagoras, for whom man does not recover the world by representing but is defined by his prior belonging to the unconcealed; Henry adds that these Heideggerian theses lose their pertinence when referred to the original *cogito*, which "put out of play" man and his power of representing. The Kantbuch has only the Aristotelian line (§40, 222-224). It has no Protagoras, and nothing for or against the contrast of Greek belonging and modern mastery. Henry's argument here depends on his reading of Descartes, which the Kantbuch can neither support nor challenge.

R ¶35, 107. "Man" is only "a front, a mask" for a problem of another order, an ontological one: the human subject, even reduced to the subject of representation, designates nothing but an ontological structure, "everything except a being". As in ¶4, this is the Kantbuch's own position: the question of what is more original than man "can in principle be no anthropological one" (GA 3 §41, 229-230), and the finitude it studies is that "of Dasein in him". Henry's insight is that the interpretation of the modern subject as man hides the structure. The Kantbuch says the same of Kant: the question "What is man?" is to be taken back to Dasein (§§37-39, 208-222). Henry's formula against Heidegger is Heidegger's own in 1929.

R ¶36, 107-108. In the Greek world man occupies a modest place, since Being is understood as *hypokeimenon*, as *physis*: "the blossoming from itself, ... the fact of opening into the open" (N II 173; *Brief*, Munier, 101); the *idea* is only the putting-in-view of *physis*, with the *Umschlag* to *perceptio* following when the putting-in-view is no longer thought from *physis*. The Kantbuch touches *physis* only in noting Kant's use of "physiology of pure reason" (GA 3 §40, 222 n. 288) and has no account of Greek unconcealment as blossoming. This is not a Kantbuch matter.

R ¶37, 108. However modest man's place, does he not play an essential role in the Greek as the one who can contemplate the *idea*? More originally, the Greek man immersed in *physis* has the capacity to accord with the self-opening that gives presence. The Kantbuch's counterpart is the finite being's "letting beings be" (*sein-lassen*) and its "breaking into" beings as a whole (GA 3 §41, 228). In it Dasein's essence consists in letting beings be and in thereby having an understanding of being: "Existence means dependence on beings as such" (228). That is the Kantbuch's analogue to Henry's "accord" with presence, and it rests on beings' prior presence, not on an opening of being. Whichever way the Greek question is decided, the Kantbuch does not have the *Brief*'s "thrown by being".

R ¶38, 108-109. Man does not intervene in the question of being in his relation to beings, since he can relate to beings only by first relating to being, in "the light of being": the opening of man to being is prior to the opening to beings. This is the Kantbuch's structure: only because of a prior pre-ontological knowledge of being can a being encounter a finite knower (GA 3 §16, 70-71). It differs on who opens. In the Kantbuch the finite subject "forms" the horizon (§§24-25); in Henry's report of the Brief, being opens itself to man. The step Henry takes from the Brief (the opening of man to being is prior) is thus half Kantbuch, half Brief.

R ¶39, 109. Man does not intervene as a being: thinking "accomplishes the relation of being to the essence of man" (*Brief*, Munier 73-74), and the rejection of metaphysical humanism removes the conception of man as *homo animalis*, "rational animal", for a humanism in which man is nothing but being's "moment of pure appearing". The Kantbuch has the rejection of the anthropological conception (GA 3 §41, 229-230), and with Kant it has the "humanity" of reason as finitude (§4, 21). It does not have the *Brief*'s thesis that thinking accomplishes the relation of being to man's essence. This is the first unit in which the Kantbuch and the late Heidegger part on the role of being.

R ¶40, 110. Henry asks how such a problematic could criticize the *cogito*, which had done nothing but put man and the beings out of play and promote a new essence of the *humanitas* as *eidos* of appearing, so that the question is what the "ultimate difference of the non-metaphysical positions" of Descartes and Heidegger is. The Kantbuch has no such Descartes. Its Kant, however, does not put man out of play (§§36-38), and is thereby not in the position Henry says the Cartesian *cogito* shares with Heidegger. The Kantbuch's Heidegger and Henry's Descartes stand apart.

R ¶41, 110-111. Behind Heidegger's "man", Henry says, is a conception of being, and as thinking man is "nothing but ek-sistence": ek-sisting is "to throw oneself into exteriority and to hold oneself in it", "the place of all holding, the place of being". Being is the *there* as what holds itself before, poses itself and offers itself. Between thinking and being there is no reciprocity (*Brief*, Munier 74, 104, 106, 112): "thinking accomplishes the relation of being to man's essence" but "neither constitutes nor produces this relation". Heidegger's texts after *Sein und Zeit* repeat, Henry says, that the ultimate possibility of transcendental truth lies not in man but in being, since "being is the transcendent pure and simple" (the sentence is from *Sein und Zeit*, 1927, which precedes the Kantbuch). The Kantbuch does not say it in that form. Its transcendence is the finite subject's, and Dasein's (GA 3 §§16, 24, 43), and being-in-the-world is what "makes the subject-object relation possible" (§43, 235). The Kantbuch thus holds the structure and not the *Brief*'s assignment of it to being, and Henry's attack on that assignment is not an attack on the Kantbuch's position.

R ¶42, 111-112. The ek-sisting regard rests on being's truth and its prior opening, Henry says, and asks whether there are two openings or one eye. Heidegger writes (*Brief*, Munier 107, 111) that "the gift of itself in the open by means of this open is being itself": man holds himself in it not through himself but through being's gift, and "being is transmitted to man only insofar as the clearing occurs". The Kantbuch's reply to the "two openings or one" is that there are two moments (horizon-forming and encounter), and one structure: transcendence is "ecstatic-horizontal", the going-out and the horizon it forms (GA 3 §24, 119). The Kantbuch does not say that being gives itself to man and does not have a "gift of being". It does say that the horizon is formed by the finite subject, and that the encounter with beings is the other half. Henry's "one eye" has thus a Kantbuch form: horizon and encounter belong to one structure.

R ¶43, 112-113. The question is how the clearing is destined. Does its coming, being's destiny, not include man as its moment of accomplishment? Being is transmitted to man only insofar as its clearing occurs, and the clearing occurs only if man transmits it. Henry notes the circularity of the texts that affirm the belonging of ek-sistence to the truth of being and add a finality: being throws man into ek-sistence so that he guard its truth. The Kantbuch does not have this circle, because its thrownness has a different subject. Man is "thrown" there by being delivered over to beings that are already there: thrownness is the dependence of Dasein on beings already there, of which Dasein is not master (GA 3 §43, 235; §41, 228). No being sends man into ek-sistence in the Kantbuch, and Henry's circle is built on the Brief. It cannot be laid against the 1929 text.

R ¶44, 113-114. "Why should man, or rather ek-sisting, belong essentially to the truth of being, to its innermost possibility?" Henry asks, as Heidegger demands, for a phenomenological answer: in what does the phenomenality of the clearing consist? "Man belongs to the essence of being" (N II 234; *Brief* 101, 144, 124, 119, 142). The Kantbuch gives a different kind of answer: the finite subject belongs to the ground-laying because finitude is the subject's essence, and the ground is the finite subject's own transcendence (GA 3 §4, 21; §41, 229). The Kantbuch's response to a demand of this kind is the schematism, where the horizon is formed in time (§§19-23, 90-113), and its method claims to unveil the horizon from its origin (§8, 40-42). Henry's question is aimed at the *Brief*, but the Kantbuch has a different answer to it.

R ¶45, 114. If ek-sisting stands in the exteriority of being's ecstatic dimension and takes its phenomenality from it, how does the exteriority come about? "Dimension" is "being itself" (*Brief* 106, 131-132): "Being-in-the-world names the essence of ek-sistence in view of the cleared dimension, from which the 'ek-' of ek-sistence essences." The Kantbuch is the earlier statement of the term. It calls transcendence "ecstatic-horizontal" and "standing-out toward" (*Hinausstehen*, *Ekstasis*) with a horizon (GA 3 §24, 119). The Kantbuch has no "dimension" and does not identify the ecstatic horizon with being. In it, the horizon is formed by the pure synthesis, and Henry's question ("how does the exteriority come about?") is answered in the Kantbuch: it comes about by the formative activity of pure imagination, that is, original time (§§32-35, 173-201). The answer Henry will give in R ¶46 against the *Brief* ("the transcendental process of exteriorization") is, in a sense, the Kantbuch's own: the horizon is not given but formed.

R ¶46, 114. Exteriority is not simply there like a stone or a tree; it exteriorizes itself in the transcendental process that throws out and pro-jects what it throws before, as the "naturing-natured" of the Ob-jectified. All phenomenality opens the path to itself, and the Open leads to it. The Kantbuch's "forming" of the horizon (*bilden*) is the same insight in the German of 1929: ontological knowledge "forms" transcendence, and this forming is nothing other than the holding-open of the horizon (GA 3 §25, 123). Henry's "naturing-natured" and "process" are his own vocabulary; the Kantbuch has "bilden" (to form) and "zeitigen" (to temporalize, §35, 196).

R ¶47, 115. But the Open presupposes its prior Opening: not the opening it accomplishes in opening to man, but the transcendental process that throws the Open itself, so that "ek-sisting in the truth of being" is possible only for an ek-sister "far more primitive", the one who threw the Open. That is a claim about what grounds the horizon. The Kantbuch's answer is time as pure self-affection: the horizon is formed in the "from-itself-toward ... and back to itself" of time (GA 3 §34, 189-191; §35, 196, 200). It therefore provides an account of the prior forming. Henry will deny that the forming is non-ecstatic, but he cannot claim that the Kantbuch has none.

R ¶48, 115. The exteriority subsists only as long as the process producing it continues, and exteriorization "retains what it goes off to" so that the horizon is held in the unity and coherence of the stable dimension: "the receptivity of the ecstatic horizon is the condition without which it could not be seen". Here the argument of *L'essence de la manifestation* on receptivity returns, applied to the *Brief*. The Kantbuch has both halves of it: the horizon's holding-together (§33, 183-187, the three syntheses that hold the horizon) and its taking-in, "pure intuition gives itself in the taking-in what can be taken in" (§32, 173). It says in particular that this taking-in is not the receiving of something present: "this narrow concept of taking-in must be kept from pure intuition" (173-174). So the Kantbuch says that the horizon is received, and says how. Henry's claim that the *Brief* lacks this holds for the *Brief*. It does not hold for the Kantbuch.

R ¶49, 115-116. That man belongs to Heideggerian being, thrown by it into its truth "for the truthful guarding of its truth", "so that he watches over the truth of being", means that the truth of being does not illuminate itself and does not subsist by itself, since the exteriority that constitutes the Dimensional is phenomenological only if it is received and held in its act of exteriorizing. The reception of the ecstatic horizon is the transcendental condition of the truth of being. The Kantbuch has a thrownness without the guarding, and it has the reception of the horizon (GA 3 §32, 173). In the Kantbuch the reception is in the horizon's own forming; Henry's remark that it must be a prior reception is a demand the Kantbuch meets by identifying the reception with the self-affection that forms the horizon (§34, 191).

R ¶50, 116-117. How does man guard being? Not as a being but by his essence, ek-sisting; but being itself reaches the truth only in ek-sisting in it, by the work of ek-sistence. "Man is not the guardian of being. Being guards itself first, and keeps the Open in the throw by which it opens it", and man ek-sists only on the ground of the process of being. The Kantbuch has no guardian and no throw by being. Its nearest thing to Henry's "process" is the pure synthesis (GA 3 §§14, 26-27, 33). The "guardian" thesis is from the *Brief* and is not a Kantbuch claim.

R ¶51, 117. How can man open to his own ground, join the transcendental process, and ek-sist with it into the Open? Henry says to ask being itself: does the process that throws the Open reach itself in the Ek-sister? The Kantbuch does not ask the question of the ground of the process in these terms. Its transcendental process is original time and imagination, and the question what grounds these is the one the Kantbuch says leads to an abyss (GA 3 §31, 167-168). That is the one place where the Kantbuch states the difficulty Henry presses: the ground of the transcendental process is "the unknown", and Kant recoiled (168).

R ¶52, 117. Or is not the coming of Ek-sisting into itself, as auto-affection, "identity of affecting and affected", the essence of ipseity and of man himself? "For man is the one who says I" (Henry's own sentence, 117). Man speaks only because he can say I, on the ground of the essence of ipseity in him. The formula "identity of affecting and affected" is Henry's definition of auto-affection, which Chapter IV sets against the Kantbuch's self-affection. The Kantbuch also makes a self-affecting the essence of selfhood: "time as pure self-affection forms ... the essential structure of subjectivity" (§34, 189). What it does not have is Henry's claim that this identity is non-ecstatic. The reading Henry proposes (the Ek-sister's coming into itself is auto-affection) is, in the Kantbuch's terms, the claim that self-affection is selfhood. Henry's proposal is a radicalization of the Kantbuch's thesis, not a counter to it, and the difference remains the one marked in Chapter IV.

R ¶53, 117-118. Here the crucial intuitions of the Cartesianism of the Beginning call to be meditated once more: the one that, ignorant of man and having in its night only the self-appearing of appearing identical to being, says *ego cogito, ego sum*. The identity of the three words ("ego", "cogito", "sum") is the identity of being accomplished as thought and as ipseity. The Kantbuch has no such identity and no such night. What it shares with Henry's reading is the thesis that the *ego* and the thinking are one in the self's being (GA 3 §29, 150: "the being-conscious of the self can be clarified only from the being of the self"). That is far short of a reading of the Beginning, and the comparison stops there.

R ¶54, 118. It is also to be pondered that Descartes only said that nothing is more evident than the original connection of thought and ego, so that the connection is made at a time when evidence is not yet born in its own production, within the transcendental process of exteriorization: Schelling will call it unconscious. Henry distinguishes this "unconscious" from that of the horizon of ek-stasis, and ends with a material phenomenology. The Kantbuch has no unconscious and nothing on Schelling. Its own account of the nothing at the centre of transcendence (§25, 121-123) is not an unconscious, though it does say that what forms the horizon is not thematically known. The nearest the Kantbuch comes to "the unconscious" is the unthematic character of the horizon, and the comparison goes no further.

R ¶55, 119. To this amphibology (the double meaning of Cartesian key concepts) the monotony of Heidegger's concepts of phenomenality is opposed: being keeps man in its guard so that he may become its guardian; there is one Phenomenon, one space of light traversed in both senses, and being is itself this relation (*Brief*, Munier 103; *Identität und Differenz* 270: *Ereignis*). Man's essence is ek-sistence in the openness of being, and "what is innermost in man and his *humanitas* is a radical exteriority, the 'subjectivity' of this 'subject', that of the 'world', 'more objective than any possible object'" (*Brief* 132; *Sein und Zeit*, French p. 366). The Kantbuch has "Dasein in man" and "being-in-the-world", but its relation between man and being is not "the relation that being itself bears". It has a relation of the finite subject to beings, formed by the horizon. The "more objective than any object" has an echo: in the Kantbuch the horizon is "the nothing" that is not an object but the condition of every object (§25, 121-123). On the substance of this unit, the Kantbuch is the earlier version of what Henry calls monotony: one structure for man's and being's phenomenality, with the horizon as its centre.

R ¶56, 119-120. Because man's phenomenological essence is exteriority, that of being, and there is one essence and one accomplishment of phenomenality, one light, the concepts that formulate it are univocal. Henry lists the families of Heidegger's metaphors: sheltering and concealing, jointure, belonging, provenance, coming, destiny, light, glance, opening, sight, being and *there*, position and station (*Setzen, Stehen, hinaus-stehen, Stand, Bestand, Gegenstand, stellen, her-stellen, vor-stellen, zu-stellen*), truth and guard, world, throw, essence, relation, abode, house. Several of the terms in the "position and station" family are in the Kantbuch, at the places where the Kantbuch does its work: *Hinausstehen* for ecstasis (GA 3 §24, 119), *Stand* and *Bestand* for the I's constancy (§34, 193), *Gegenstand* and *Gegenstehenlassen* throughout (§§5, 16-17, 24), *vorstellen* for the I's representing (§29, 150; §34, 193), and *Blick* and *Anblick* for the pure image (§§20-22), with *Horizont* and *Offenbarkeit* for the opening. So the list is attested in 1929 for a large share of its entries. The univocity Henry charges to the late Heidegger is a feature of the Kantbuch's vocabulary, which uses one family of terms (*stehen*, *stellen*, *Blick*, *Horizont*) for the structure of transcendence and for Kant's own subject. That is the strongest textual support the Kantbuch gives to this part of Henry's critique. The "guarding" and "destiny" terms are not in it, and *Fuge* and *Gefüge* occur in it for the joining of transcendence (§16, 71; §18, 86).

R ¶57, 120. The monotony makes possible a method, what Marx against Stirner called the method of "appositions": imperceptible slides of meaning by which, gradually leaving one concept, one easily reaches another; from ethics to ontology there is only a step, as one notes that *ēthos* means "abode", "open domain to the presence of the god", and *nomos* is "assignment" hidden in being's decree (*Brief*, Munier 138, 141, 148). The Kantbuch does not use the method. Its steps are argued transitions from one Kantian doctrine to the next, and where it identifies two things, such as time and the "I think", it argues the identity and does not slide (GA 3 §34, 191-194: "they are the same", with a page of reasons). The charge of slides is not made good against the Kantbuch, and where it applies, to the *Brief*, the Kantbuch is not implicated.

R ¶58, 120-121. The univocity proves equivocal if the appearing to which it refers divides structurally, by the phenomenological materiality of its effectuation, into two heterogeneous modes. "Opening" in the clearing of being cannot indifferently designate the ek-sister and the process; and the *ego cogito*, arbitrarily reduced to "I represent", is inserted into history because the phenomenality representation borrows can be only that of ecstasis, and the representer is only an ek-sister of the improper kind. Henry's two modes, ecstatic and non-ecstatic, are not in the Kantbuch, and the Kantbuch cannot be said to be equivocal on them. It does say that truth splits into two, "the unconcealedness of being and the manifestness of beings" (GA 3 §25, 123-124, *sich gabeln*), and this is a division the Kantbuch makes within the one horizon, and not Henry's. The translators' 1953 French renders *sich gabeln* "doit être entendue à la fois comme", as the run on *L'essence de la manifestation* found.

R ¶59, 121. In what does the "improper way of thinking the ek-sister" consist? What alteration does the Cartesian *cogito* make to Platonic and Greek truth? It gives the initiative to man: the *ego* represents, throws before itself and brings back to itself the horizon of representedness, in which each thing is also defined by what it can represent. Whether the initiative comes from being or from man, "the phenomenality that it pro-jects or in which it is thrown is the same, that of the *ek-stasis*". The Kantbuch has the initiative in the finite subject, which forms the horizon, and also has the being's encounter "from itself": it divides the initiative, as ¶42 notes. Henry's point, that the phenomenality is the same whoever holds the initiative, is what the Kantbuch's single horizon says: the horizon is the same, and the question of who throws it is not asked in the Kantbuch with the *Brief*'s sharpness.

R ¶60, 121. Heidegger opposed Cartesian and Greek truth as *Gegenstand* and *Gegenüber*: in *Gegenstand* the *Gegen* is the casting-against of the representing subject, in *Gegenüber* the *Gegen* is disclosed in what comes upon the perceiving man, who listens and looks (*Satz vom Grund*, Préau, 185). Henry will say there is one *Gegen* that determines both. The Kantbuch has no opposition of this kind, and its *Gegen* already combines the two. For the Kantbuch the being "encounters from itself" (GA 3 §16, 70-71), and "the against" (*Dawider*) is not a resistance in beings but "the prior resistance of being" (§17, 73-74), which finite subjects let stand over against (*Gegenstehenlassen*). So the Kantbuch reads the Kantian *Gegenstand* itself as a structure of encounter, with the *Gegen* belonging to the horizon, and Henry's thesis that the *Gegen* is common to both appears in it unopposed. The later contrast of *Gegenstand* and *Gegenüber* is a later Heidegger's correction of the modern reading that the Kantbuch did not make.

R ¶61, 121-122. The decisive point, more than the attitude of Greeks or moderns toward what comes, is the structure of that coming, the essence of the truth of being. As long as that essence is thought through a *Gegen* that determines both *Gegenüber* and *Gegenstand*, the difference that separates them is a "secondary differentiation", for it is the same clearing within the opening opened by the *Gegen*, which constitutes the *Be-gegnen* (encountering). The Kantbuch has the unitary structure in *Begegnenlassen* (GA 3 §16, 70; §24, 115; §25, 122: "within which a self-standing being can encounter"), and it is where its Kantian horizon is defined. Henry's claim is in effect the Kantbuch's. The Kantbuch makes it about Kant and does not extend it to the Greeks.

R ¶62, 122. Reduced to "I represent", the *cogito* is homogeneous with what precedes it, with the most original truth of *physis*, since this too is constituted by the *Gegen*; the history of being is the same being, the same truth that sends itself as *physis*, as *idea*, and as *cogito*. The identity of the essence of appearing founds the secret affinity of the epochs. The Kantbuch has no such history, and its Kant is not in a series with Greek *physis*. It does have the same structure in each: horizon and encounter. What the Kantbuch offers to the claim is the evidence of a transcendence that does not change. Henry's inference, that insertion goes without saying once the *cogito* is reduced, is about N II.

R ¶63, 123. Because "man" is only a front for the transcendental condition of being interpreted from the *Gegen*, the history of being is not as absurd as it seems; it unrolls its prescriptions one after another. This is why man intervenes in it, already in Greece, "as the receptivity of the *ek-stasis* and as its design, but ultimately as the *ego*": not the *ego* that represents itself, but the ipseity that, presupposed in every representation, is insurmountably excluded from it. Henry here makes his claim on the Kantbuch's ground: man appears as the receptivity of the ecstatic horizon, which is the Kantbuch's *Hinnahme* of pure intuition (GA 3 §32, 173), and as the one who forms the horizon. Henry's final step, that this receptivity is ultimately the *ego*, is the thesis of Chapter IV, where he reads the receptivity in Kant as the self's passivity (R ¶26 there).

R ¶64, 123. But this *ego* is not itself inserted into the history of Western metaphysics, nor into that of being: it does not arrive at the time of Descartes, it is not a declension of the *ek-stasis*, it is before it and before the Difference; it is the Beginning that begins from the beginning. The Kantbuch's I is exactly the I that the history, for Henry, inserts, and it falls within the horizon. It is not a beginning before the difference, and the Kantbuch has nothing to say about the Difference (it does have the difference of being and beings, which in the existential analytic "remains hidden as such", §43, 235). Henry's last sentence is not addressed to the Kantbuch and cannot be tested by it.

Across Chapter III the Kantbuch is a witness more than an interlocutor. Henry's target is a Heidegger of the 1940s and 1960s, and most of what he attacks (the epochal sending of being, the self-securing of the modern subject, the *Brief*'s throw by being and the guarding of its truth, the contrast of *Gegenstand* and *Gegenüber*) is later than GA 3 and absent from it. Three things in the Kantbuch bear on his argument. First, it already holds, for Kant, the identification of the "I think" with representing (§34, 193) that Chapter III calls a paralogism, and it also holds the two things Henry sets against it: the consciousness of the self is clarified from its being (§29, 150), and the transcendental structure is not man (§41, 229-230). Second, it has the vocabulary of the "monotony" Henry lists, *Hinausstehen*, *Stand*, *Bestand*, *Gegenstand*, *vorstellen*, with a single horizon of encounter (§§16-17, 24, 34), so the univocity Henry charges is native to 1929. Third, its thrownness is delivery to beings, not a throw by being (§§41, 43), so that the circle Henry draws from the *Brief* (R ¶43) does not reach it, and its account of the reception of the horizon (§32, 173-174; §34, 191) meets in the Kantbuch the demand that Henry makes of the *Brief* (R ¶¶48-49). Henry's argument fails against the Kantbuch where it rests on being's gift or throw, and holds against it where it says that the self's being is not shown by the horizon-forming structure it is identified with. What the chapter does not do is cite the Kantbuch, and every comparison here is therefore by presupposition and not by take-up.

## Chapter IV. Empty Subjectivity and Lost Life: The Kantian Critique of the "Soul"

R ¶1, 126. Henry reads Kant's critique as a metaphysics of representativity: being is being represented, knowledge and object are identical, and the Critique is the search for the conditions of experience "as the possibility of the objects of experience". Henry does not name the highest principle of synthetic judgments; Commentary R does, and the sentence is that principle's content. The Kantbuch reads the same principle ontologically (GA 3 §24, 117-119: the conditions of experience are the conditions of its objects "at once", which expresses the structure of transcendence, "ecstatic-horizontal"), so the departure lies in what the principle is said to cover. In GA 3 it covers the horizon of objectness (*Gegenständlichkeit*) and nothing more. Beings themselves, when they show themselves to finite knowledge, are not representations: "the appearances are not mere semblance but the being itself" (GA 3 §5, 32), and only for finite knowledge is there such a thing as an object (§5, 31). Henry extends to being what GA 3 says of the horizon and to "representation" what GA 3 says of objectness. The extension is defensible as a thesis about what the Kantian horizon is, and it is the thesis the chapter sets out to undo. It is not a reading of the Kantbuch, which keeps the horizon and the being apart. "Ipseity of the ego" here already means Henry's term, not the Kantbuch's *Selbstheit*.

R ¶2. The primacy of intuition, the finitude of understanding as resting on that of intuition, and the contrast with the *intuitus originarius* reproduce the structure of GA 3 §4, 21-24 and §5, 25-26. Heidegger quotes the same opening sentence of the Aesthetic (A 19/B 33), wants it "hammered in" that knowing is primarily intuiting, and places the finitude of human knowledge in the intuiting before the thinking (24-25). Commentary R says Henry "follows" the Kantbuch here. The agreement is real, and it is an agreement about Kant: Henry gives the primacy to Kant's Aesthetic, which is where GA 3 puts it too. This is a reconstruction. What the Kantbuch adds, and R ¶2 drops, is the reason for finitude: finite intuition depends on beings that are already there and must announce themselves (*sich melden*), because Dasein is "delivered over" to them (§5, 26). Henry keeps the dependence and removes the beings, which prepares ¶3.

R ¶3. Henry's text has the receptivity argument in a typography that is the 1953 translators'. He writes "s'ob-jecte", "ob-jet", "pro-jection", "se pro-pose", "dis-jetée" and "pro-duit" with hyphens (¶¶4, 13, 25, 26), as the translators write "ob-jectivation" and "se pro-poser" for the Kantbuch. In *L'essence de la manifestation* Henry's own prose had dropped the hyphen. Its return here is mediation by the translation's typography, and it keeps the "against" and the "putting forth" visible where the German has *Gegenstehenlassen*. Henry makes receptivity a phenomenological category, "to receive is to let something appear as object", not defined from outside by the contrast with the *intuitus originarius*. The Kantbuch makes the same move before him: sensibility is "the ontological, non-sensualist concept" Kant first won, which does not depend on sense organs (GA 3 §5, 26-27), and pure intuition "gives itself, in the taking-in, what can be taken in" (§32, 173), with the narrow concept of taking-in as receiving something present to be kept away from it (173-174). The departure is smaller than the register suggests, and it is a selection. Henry's definition fits pure intuition only. For the Kantbuch, finite intuition as such is also affection by beings that are already there (26), and it is this second element that Henry's internalized "receiving" leaves out. Henry's definition of receptivity as phenomenality and nothing else is the point at which the warning against the narrow concept of taking-in (GA 3 §32, 173-174) is most easily lost. The contrast with the *intuitus originarius* is also not wholly "external" in GA 3: Heidegger uses it to show that the difference lies in the intuiting itself, as having to be given what it takes (25).

R ¶4, 127. Henry proposes "one essence of intuition" (127), the original distancing, with pure and empirical intuition as one and space and time as modes of the ecstatic horizon, and he gives priority to time, which he identifies with ecstasis. This is re-inscription. GA 3 keeps the difference Henry removes: sensible intuition is "pure intuition (space and time) or empirical intuition of what is represented as actual in space and time by sensation" (B 146 f., quoted at §5, 27 n. 35), and pure intuition is the condition of the encounter with beings, not the same intuition over again (§§9-10, 44-51). On time, GA 3 doubts Kant's own ground for the priority (§10, 50: whether it holds "must remain open") and lets it stand only if time dwells in the subject more originally than space, and only if the subjectivity of the subject consists in openness for beings. Henry's ground is the horizon: the time of inner sense is "the projection of the primordial horizon of objectivity". That is GA 3's "ecstatic-horizontal" transcendence (§24, 119), with the horizon taken as the ground of the time and not the other way about. "Distance" is Henry's word and not the Kantbuch's.

R ¶5. Thought as the unity of representation, the categories as ways of binding the manifold, and apperception as defined wholly by the unifying function of ecstasis: this is a faithful and sharper form of GA 3 §29, 149-151, where the pure understanding is the "I think" as a "faculty of rules", an advance holding-before of unities, "a representing turning-toward" that is at bottom pure imagining (151). The departure is the clause "no other content". For the Kantbuch the pure "I" has a being, which it takes from selfhood: "this being-conscious of the self can be clarified only from the being of the self, not the reverse" (150). That being is time (§34, 191). Henry's claim that apperception has no content beyond ecstasis is true only if the Kantbuch's selfhood is not allowed to count as content. R ¶40-¶53 will have to decide that.

R ¶6, 128. The question of primacy between thought and intuition, answered by a "common essence" of both in ecstasis, is the Kantbuch's question of the common root, asked of the transcendental imagination (§§26-28, 127-146) and pursued to original time (§§32-35, 173-201). Commentary R says Henry's substitution "amounts to the same for the argument". It does not. The Kantbuch's root is not a bare ecstasis of exteriority but a threefold unifying formation of future, having-been and present, which is as such self-affection with its "toward ... and back to itself" (§34, 189-191; §35, 196, 200). Dropping the return makes "ecstasis" a one-way going-out, and what is exteriorized is then all there is. This selection is the premise of ¶35. Henry also credits Kant with "labouring" to ground the unity of the process. The Kantbuch says the opposite: Kant drew back from the imagination as from an abyss (§31, 167-168).

R ¶7, 129. The critique is double: it thinks the condition of all experience, and it shows that this condition "contains no existence and cannot produce it" (129). Here Henry reconstructs the Kantbuch and revalues it. GA 3 §25, 120-124 says that ontological knowledge creates no beings and "does not relate thematically and directly to a being at all"; what it knows is a "nothing", the X, pure horizon; and it has "only empirical use", serving the experience of beings that show themselves (124). Where Henry reads a deficiency and an appeal to a heterogeneous element, GA 3 reads the structure of finite transcendence, which has to leave the being its own encounter. The Kantbuch has a place for what Henry needs: affection by what announces itself (§5, 26; §34, 189). It does not ask for the being of that announcing. "Abyssal" also echoes GA 3's own word, since the Kantbuch speaks of the abyss into which the laying of the ground leads (§31, 167-168), but the Kantbuch's abyss is the imagination and Henry's is sensation. Contestation by displacement, then: Henry accepts the Kantbuch's thesis and draws from it the conclusion the Kantbuch has no reason to draw.

R ¶8, 130. Henry's "empty form" and the sentence "sensation is what designates a reality" (A 175/B 217), with the postulate of perception (A 225/B 272), make sensation being itself. The Kantbuch gives three reasons to check this step. "Possibility of experience" means essence, whatness, *possibilitas*, and "gives all our a priori knowledge objective reality" (§24, 117, quoting A 156/B 195); transcendence itself provides the a priori "objective reality". Heidegger also warns against reading Kant's *Realität* as actuality, since it is *Sachheit* (§18, 86-87). And "the condition of experience" has two senses of "possible", neither of which is the possible as opposed to the actual (§24, 116-117). Henry's thesis, that reality in the sense of actuality is given only by sensation, therefore does not contradict the Kantbuch. It changes the register: the Kantbuch speaks of the possibility that makes actual experience possible, and Henry of what actualizes. Kant's texts are the only evidence for how A 175 and A 225 relate, and no Kant text is available here, so this commentary can decide only that the Kantbuch gives Henry no support for equating the two uses.

R ¶9, 130. Henry's step from sensation to "the original self-impression in which every impression impresses itself" has no counterpart in the Kantbuch's treatment of sensation, which ends at affection and the announcing. The nearest passage is elsewhere: in §30 the Kantbuch analyses feeling as "having a feeling for ... and at the same time the feeling subject feeling itself" (157), and takes respect as "feeling of my existence" which makes the acting self manifest in an "unobjectifying and unthematic" way and as unreflected (158-160). That is a non-objectifying self-disclosure in feeling, and Henry does not cite it. The silence matters: it means the claim that the Kantbuch can think self-givenness only as ecstatic is not shown by its text. The Kantbuch also roots this feeling in the transcendental imagination, "pure receptivity" and "pure spontaneity" together (159), so it is not self-impression of a sensation but a mode of transcendence. The thesis that sensation requires a self-impression is Henry's own.

R ¶10, 131. Kant, Henry says, brings impression back to ecstasis through inner sense and passes over affectivity; sensation becomes a "dead, opaque, blind content" that borrows light from representation. The reading of the structure is accurate to GA 3: for finite knowing the sensed content shows itself within the horizon that transcendence forms, not by itself (§25, 122-123), and "intuition without concepts is blind" (A 51/B 75) is cited at §6, 35-36 to establish that sensibility and understanding need each other. What the Kantbuch does not say is "dead". For it the empirical content is the being itself announcing itself (§5, 32, "the indeterminate object of an empirical intuition is called appearance"), and the dependence on the horizon is how a finite being encounters it. "Dead" is Henry's valuation of a structure the Kantbuch describes without it.

R ¶11, 131. "In representation no sensation is possible" holds, in the Kantbuch, only if "representation" means the horizon of objectness (§25, 124: ontological knowledge has "only empirical use" and relates to no being). For Kant the sensation is itself a *Vorstellung*: the genus covers intuition and concept (§4, 22, quoting A 320/B 376). Henry's formula is accurate only in the sense that he gave the word in ¶1. This is the second place where re-inscription decides the force of the argument: a reader who takes "representation" in Kant's and the Kantbuch's sense will find the sentence false. The parallel with Chapter II ("representation is the dimension of unreality") depends on one sense of the word and is applied to another.

R ¶12. Henry's reversal of pure and empirical intuition (empirical intuition "makes pure intuition effective and secretly grounds it") is his sharpest move against the Kantbuch, and it is a change of register. The Kantbuch's priority of the pure horizon is a priority of possibility: the condition of the possibility of experience is a condition of its possible and actual objects alike (§24, 117-119). Henry's priority is one of actualization, and the Kantbuch has no interest in denying it. On actual experience the Kantbuch says only that the truth of ontological knowledge lies in letting beings be encountered within the horizon (§25, 124). The claim that time as "empty intuition" yields no self-knowledge also meets nothing in the Kantbuch, which never says that time is perceived, and says that the self is time (§34, 191). The paragraph's second half, on the empirical self's need of a support in space, is the point Commentary R ties to the Refutation of Idealism (Henry does not name it here). GA 3 does not treat B 274-279; the Kantbuch's only remark on space is that it too belongs to the finite self (§35, 200).

R ¶13. The General Note on the System of Principles (B 288 f.) is quoted by Henry as proof that every use of the categories needs empirical intuition. GA 3 reads the same passage, and the opposition is exact. At §35, 199 Heidegger quotes B 288 ("we can see the possibility of no thing through the mere category but must always have an intuition at hand") and says it states the necessity of pure sensification, the presentation of the notions in a "pure image", though "it is not said that this pure image is pure intuition as time". The next section of Kant's note requires external intuitions, and the Kantbuch takes this to mean that space enters the pure schematism (199-200). Henry's quotation from B 289, "as the ground of the possibility of experience, and so of knowledge of an object given in empirical intuition", is not among the lines the Kantbuch cites. This is selection on the Kantbuch's side: it reads for the pure image what Henry reads for the empirical datum. Both readings can appeal to Kant, and the verdict on Kant is outside what these sources allow. The Kantbuch's use of the passage, to say that space is also transcendental, is a use Henry's argument has to meet and does not.

R ¶14. Two senses of synthesis, the binding act and the addition of an empirical datum, correspond to a distinction the Kantbuch draws in §4: predicative and apophantic synthesis on one side, "veritative" synthesis, which unites thinking with intuition and so makes the being manifest, on the other (GA 3 §4, 28-29; §24, 115, "synthesis ... the relation to the entirely other"). The reconstruction is accurate and the re-inscription small. In the Kantbuch the "entirely other" that thought has to reach is the being itself (115); in Henry it is sensation. The Kantbuch's version of the veritative synthesis includes pure knowledge, since ontological knowledge is veritative as well (§25, 123, "transcendental truth"), while Henry reserves the term for the empirical.

R ¶15. The concept that "turns around itself" and needs intuition for any synthetic solution (A 731/B 759) restates the Kantbuch's thesis that all thinking has "only a serving position" to intuition (§4, 22) and that "mere thinking is no knowing" (§24, 115). Reconstruction, and R's own preparation of the "perpetual circle" of the paralogisms, which GA 3 does not take up.

R ¶16, 134. Henry says the concept cannot know itself: its self-knowledge exhibits no reality and is "an empty and formal consciousness" (134). The Kantbuch also holds that the pure "I" is not a knowing of an object. What follows differs. For GA 3 the self-consciousness of the "I think" is not a reflective act: the "I" "goes along" in the turning-toward, manifest in the "self" that is taken out into the turning, and "not a side act of a knowing directed at thinking itself" (§29, 150). Henry reads the lack of an object as an emptiness requiring ground, the Kantbuch as the mark of an unthematic selfhood that is the being of the self.

R ¶17, 135. Henry says finitude is not "an unexplained and fatal property of the human condition" but the consequence of reducing subjectivity to representation, and he inverts Heidegger: finitude is not the ground of transcendence; it follows from a subjectivity that "is not a life". The target needs care. For the Kantbuch, finitude is the problem-basis of the laying of the ground (§5, 32; §4, 21), and transcendence is the essence of finitude (§25, 120); finitude is not left unexplained. It is explained through selfhood: pure self-affection "forms the essential structure of subjectivity", and only on the ground of this selfhood can the finite being be what it must be, "referred to taking-in" (§34, 189). Henry's inversion contests a priority the Kantbuch does not state in that form, but the dispute is real: whether the need to receive is explained by a selfhood that is temporal (P) or by a subjectivity that lacks life (R).

R ¶18, 135. "It is still nothing" (135): ecstasis exhibits only the emptiness of its horizon, so reality must lie in sensation, and Kant "seeks outside himself nothing but his own essence". The Kantbuch's X is "a nothing", and the same paragraph insists that it is also "something" ("not a being, yet nevertheless a something"; it "serves only as correlate", pure horizon, GA 3 §25, 122). Henry takes the privative sense only and leaves "something" out, and this is the omission on which "empty" depends. The reversal of a priori and a posteriori ("the true a priori") is a re-inscription of the term: in the Kantbuch the a priori is possibility, *possibilitas* (§24, 117), and Henry gives it the sense of affective self-impression. It is a thesis of Henry's and is nowhere in the Kantbuch.

R ¶19. Henry says ecstasis, emptied of reality, reaches for it as empirical intuition, and the critique of the paralogisms shows it: rational psychology claims an a priori science of ipseity, which is possible only if subjectivity's essence is self-revelation. The Kantbuch has almost nothing on the paralogisms. One footnote sentence says Kant "worked out the paralogism of substantiality on the basis of his own ground-laying of ontology" (GA 3 §34, 193 n. 270). The two readings therefore agree that the paralogisms follow from the ontology, and then part. The Kantbuch draws from it that the "standing and abiding I" is no soul-substance but a transcendental determination of the finite self (193). Henry draws from it that rational psychology had the right object, the self-revealing soul, and the wrong phenomenality. Chapter IV's central argument has no extended counterpart in GA 3; its relation to the Kantbuch is by presupposition of the account of time, selfhood and the "I think" in §§29 and 34.

R ¶20, 136. The transcendental predicates (substantiality, simplicity, identity) are "predicates of a formal problematic unity", analytic doublings of it, and no real predicates of a self. On what they are not, the Kantbuch agrees: "standing" and "abiding" "are no ontic statements about the unchangingness of the I but transcendental determinations" (§34, 193). It then gives them a content Henry denies them. They mean that the I, as it holds before itself constancy in general, "forms the horizon of sameness within which the objective can be experienced as the same in change" (193). In the Kantbuch the predicates are constitutive of the horizon, so they are not empty; in Henry they are empty because they belong to a being that does not appear. Henry's description fits the Kantbuch's account if "empty" means "not a predicate of a being". It does not fit if the predicates are constitutive of the horizon, as they are in GA 3.

R ¶21, 137. "One possesses neither concept nor intuition of a self" (137): Henry's double lack. The Kantbuch agrees with the negative claim, since the "I think" is not a concept of an object, and it names a third way in which the I is manifest: in the turning toward, "in the 'self' that is moved out with it, the 'I' of this 'self' is necessarily manifest" (§29, 150). The sentence has a verb, "geäußert" ("exteriorized"), that belongs to a Hegelian vocabulary the earlier run flagged for the Kantbuch. Henry's remedy for the double lack is auto-affection; the Kantbuch's is the unthematic going-along. That the lack is "double" is his reconstruction. The Kantbuch says only that the I is neither.

R ¶22, 138. Inner experience as the positive counterpart of the paralogisms: the self must be received in intuition and then subsumed under the categories. The Kantbuch contains no treatment of the empirical self or of inner experience in this sense. It treats the pure self and uses "inner sense" only for the form of intuiting oneself (§10, 48). Henry builds here on Kant alone, and on the Kantbuch only through the premise that time is the form of inner sense.

R ¶23. Henry's summary of the categories' need for a permanent object, which he names only in ¶24 as the refutation of problematic idealism, is likewise independent of the Kantbuch, which does not discuss it. It prepares ¶24.

R ¶24, 139. The debate between "realist" and "phenomenalist" readings of the Refutation is set aside, since both take inner life to be a series of representations received in inner sense, and "nothing is more repugnant to the essence of ipseity ... than being-represented as such" (139). The Kantbuch has a sentence on which Henry's charge could rest, but Commentary R does not report that he uses it: "The 'standing' I ... because it holds before itself as an 'I think', that is 'I represent' (*ich stelle vor*), something like standing and constancy" (§34, 193). The identification of the "I think" with representing, which Henry's Chapter III calls a paralogism when Heidegger applies it to Descartes, is thus already stated in the Kantbuch's account of Kant. Henry's objection in this unit therefore reaches the Kantbuch, though he names it elsewhere.

R ¶25, 139-140. Inner sense is a misnomer, since its essence makes it a sense of exteriority; the exteriority of space rests on that of time, "so that space itself is in time and, as Heidegger will say, 'in the world'"; the ego is by principle "what cannot be intuited" (139), and the intuiting power could not seek or recognize a self in the intuited "if it did not first possess it in itself" (140). The one explicit Heidegger reference in the chapter is unsourced, and it is not to the Kantbuch: GA 3 does not call space "in the world", and says only that space also belongs to the finite self (§35, 200). The phrase is the idiom of the later analysis of Dasein's spatiality, which this run does not check. On the substance, the Kantbuch is nearer to Henry than the reference suggests. It questions whether Kant's ground for making time the universal intuition holds (§10, 50), denies that time is "in the subject like cells in the brain" (§33, 188), and says that inner sense "receives not 'from outside' but from the self" (§34, 191). Henry's second step, that recognizing oneself presupposes a prior possession, has its Kantbuch form in the structure "from-itself-toward ... and back to itself", which "first constitutes the character of the mind as a finite self" (191). The dispute is whether that prior self-relation is ecstatic. The Kantbuch says it is the same circuit as time; Henry says it is not ecstatic at all.

R ¶26, 140. Henry grants Kant "the profound insight" that the self does not produce itself and so must be received, passively, and faults him only for modelling that passivity on sensible receptivity. The Kantbuch states both halves. Its Kant denies self-positing in so many words: "it is not at all the case that a mind exists, which among other things also relates something to itself and carries out self-positings" (§34, 191), and it makes the finite being's essence "referred to taking-in" (189). Henry's praise of Kant is a statement of the Kantbuch's thesis about self-affection. His fault-finding assumes that Kant knows no mode of receptivity other than intuition, whereas the Kantbuch's Kant has pure taking-in, which gives itself what it takes in (§32, 173). R's reconstruction fits Kant's sensible receptivity and misses the pure one on which the Kantbuch's account of the self depends.

R ¶27, 141. What the inner sense has to receive, if the self is to be given, is no longer the exteriority that the transcendental power produced, but the matter of inner sense; Henry asks whether that matter "can exhibit the being of a self" (141). The step from form to matter is Henry's. The Kantbuch speaks only of pure inner sense, "the pure self-affection, that is, original time" (§35, 198), and never of its impressional matter. Henry's statement that the exteriority is "only a secondary receptivity relative to thought", since the power produces its own content, re-states the Kantbuch's pure intuition, which gives itself what it takes, and reverses the valuation: for the Kantbuch this is what makes finite pure knowledge possible, for Henry it marks a power with no reality of its own.

R ¶28. Inner sense has no matter of its own, so a pure time can lend itself to the schematism, and the demand arises for a manifold "intrinsically mine". The schematism is the Kantbuch's centre (§§19-23, 90-113), and time as transcendental determination is what it works with (§35, 198). The demand for a manifold that is mine is not in the Kantbuch, whose self is the form of the time circuit. This unit is Henry's argument on Kant, with only the schematism taken from the Kantbuch.

R ¶29, 142. Impressions received in inner sense are "transcendent", the same as outer ones, "deprived ... of the original bond that unites this impression to a self" (142). Mineness, Henry holds, is a mode of givenness, lost once givenness is ecstatic. The Kantbuch meets this with selfhood, not with mineness. For it the self is constituted by the circuit of time, and "this from-itself-toward and back-to-itself first constitutes the mind's character as a finite self" (§34, 191). The objection lands where the Kantbuch is least equipped: it has an account of selfhood for the pure self and none of how a given sensation is *mine*. §30's pure feeling does not supply it, since it concerns respect for the law (157-159). Henry's claim here is a real gap in the Kantbuch's account, though a gap in what it sets out to explain.

R ¶30. Henry reads the second-edition self-affection (B 153-157) as "the affection of inner sense by the understanding": the transcendental act "shocks" inner sense and produces its impression. Henry dates it to "the second edition" himself and cites neither B 153-157 nor the Kantbuch. Commentary R adds both, and says it is the doctrine the Kantbuch (§34) "made the essence of time". It is not. The Kantbuch's self-affection is pure and is not "an effective affection that strikes a present self" (§34, 189); and it cites the B-edition texts on the understanding's "synthetic influence on inner sense" (B 154) as the sign of Kant's recoil, where the function of the imagination is handed over to the understanding (§31, 163-165). Henry's "stopgap" and the Kantbuch's "recoil" therefore agree on the valuation and differ on what the Kantbuch took to be essential: the Kantbuch's source for self-affection is the passage it cites from the Aesthetic (B 67 f.), which it says Kant had not first introduced in B (190). Whether B 67 f. is itself a B-edition text cannot be settled here without the Critique.

R ¶31, 143. Existence and self, two conditions Kant's own theory requires, are said to be met by an impression that is "the empirical trace of this pure act of determination", and the B 422 f. note's "indeterminate empirical intuition" is "the most important Kantian text" for Henry. The Kantbuch does not discuss this note or the "I am" in it. Its nearest text is the "feeling of my existence" of respect (§30, 159) and the identity of time and "I think" (§34, 191). Henry's finding, that Kant admits an existence given before any categorial objectification, is a result on Kant and has no Kantbuch counterpart.

R ¶32. The impression would ground the self's existence only if it contained a self; Henry asks whether it does, as the "immediate resonance" of the "I think", and answers in ¶33. A transition unit, with nothing to compare.

R ¶33, 144. The circle: the impression is mine because it comes from the I, and the I exists only as the impression (144). The circle arises on a reading that wants the I's existence from a given impression. The Kantbuch's account avoids it by making the I's being its temporal self-affection, with no empirical impression needed: "time and the 'I think' ... are the same" (§34, 191). The Kantbuch also says that the question of the I's timelessness or eternity is not asked at all within the transcendental problematic (193). Henry's circle is thus the Kantbuch's reason for making the I pure, and Henry does not meet that move: he reads it as a further ecstatic structure (¶35).

R ¶34, 144-145. Henry then gives his own account of self-affection: the transcendental subject affects inner sense, and this affecting is "the naturing and first accomplishment of all phenomenality" (144), auto-affection in which affecting and affected are identical, so that "inner sense does not receive impressions coming from elsewhere ... it is the place where they are formed" (145). The Kantbuch's sentence is closer than any in Henry's chapter: "The inner sense receives not 'from outside' but from the self. The inner affection must come in pure taking-in from the pure self, that is, form itself in the essence of selfhood as such" (§34, 191). Henry's "not from elsewhere ... formed" is the same structure, re-inscribed (his sentence goes on, "and so 'given to it'", with "se donnent à lui" in quotation marks and no source): *bilden* (to form) becomes the affective forming of impression, and selfhood becomes ipseity. The translators' introduction to the 1953 French had glossed time as what "forms itself" (K 40-41), and Henry's wording could come from either that or the German, but nothing here shows it does. This is the closest convergence of the chapter, and where the claim most needs the distinction between the two senses of the word: the Kantbuch's self-affection is formal, the circuit of horizon and return; Henry's is material, a pathos.

R ¶35, 146. Kant's inner sense is ecstasis, so the affecting and the affected are external to each other; the doctrine "affects itself" only as tautological doubling of the same, "not its inner essence as that of ipseity" (146). A long note (R n. 16) sets out the French reflexive reading (Nabert, Lachièze-Rey: the mind posits itself as passive in order to receive the impressions of its own acts) and calls it ecstatic too, because "the self makes itself object". The note does not mention Heidegger. Commentary R's remark that it places Henry against the Kantbuch is the commentary's inference. The characterization Commentary R gives of the Kantbuch is accurate as to what it makes of self-affection, pure time (GA 3 §34, 189-191, §35, 200), and its "ecstatic" label is licensed for transcendence (§24, 119). It is not licensed for the selfhood the Kantbuch derives from the circuit, because that selfhood has no further self before it, and the circuit includes the return to itself that Henry's ecstasis leaves out (¶6). Henry's verdict that this is affection by a horizon is his thesis. The objection that the Same affects the Same presupposes an existing Same, which §34 denies (189, 191). The note's rejection of self-positing agrees in kind with GA 3 §34, 191, though Henry's target there is the French commentators and not the Kantbuch.

R ¶36. Existence needs sensation, and the condition of receiving an impression is self-impression. Henry finds his thesis implicit in Kant's two requirements. The Kantbuch has no counterpart; its sole text on the existence of the self is the feeling of respect (§30, 159).

R ¶37, 147. The impression in inner sense is "representative sensation", "like the humus of the world, the mysterious outside of an inside that never shows itself there as it is in itself" (147). The Kantbuch's concept of the sensible is of affection and announcing by beings (§5, 26; §34, 189). The coinage "representative sensation" is Henry's and cannot be checked against it.

R ¶38. The impression failing, the search turns to the I of the "I think", and Henry rehabilitates rational psychology at its Cartesian root: thought contains the "being" and the "knowledge" of the self. The Kantbuch meets this at the level of principle, since "the consciousness of the self can be clarified only from the being of the self" (§29, 150). The being it names is selfhood as time. Henry's rehabilitation and the Kantbuch's priority of being over consciousness thus agree on the order and disagree on what the being is.

R ¶39. B 428 f.: thought "in no way presents the subject of consciousness as appearance", and the I represents itself "neither as I am nor as I appear to myself". For Henry this is the self-refutation of the metaphysics of representation, since the condition of all appearing is said not to appear. The Kantbuch says the same about the horizon and counts it a positive structure: it must be kept "unthematic, but nevertheless in view" (§25, 123). R reconstructs the Kantbuch's thesis that the condition of objectness is not an object and reads it as failure, where the Kantbuch reads it as how a horizon works. The dispute can be stated exactly: whether what stays unthematic in view is for that reason a manifestation in itself (Henry: it must be, if it is to be anything) or a manifestation constituted only in the going-along with the objects (the Kantbuch). The passage in B 428 f. has no counterpart in GA 3.

R ¶40, 148-149. The "enormous" concession: Kant grants the I phenomenality and ipseity (B 157: "that I am"; "the I is in all thoughts"), and Henry asks "what then of the I of the 'I think'?" (149). The question is the Kantbuch's question and the Kantbuch has an answer, which Henry does not discuss here: the I is original time as self-affection (§34, 191-192: "time and the 'I think' ... are the same"). The silence matters most at this point in the chapter, since it is the answer that Henry has to refuse in order to arrive at his own. The text at §34, 192-194 gives the reason why the Kantbuch sees no inconsistency in Kant's ascribing the same predicates to time and to the I.

R ¶41, 150. The names Kant gives the "I think" (concept, judgment, expression, formal proposition, mere consciousness) show an unplaceable phenomenon, and the question is "if the being of the 'I think' is that of the power that intuits and thinks, that of ecstasis". The catalogue is Henry's symptom reading and is not in the Kantbuch. On the Kantbuch's terms the conditional is answered in the affirmative, with the qualification of ¶6: the being of the pure I is original time, whose circuit includes the return.

R ¶42, 150. "From the outset Kant substituted for the *cogito* a representation of it" (150), the mark of the paralogism being Kant's, through the phrase "intellectual representation" (B 423 note). Here the Kantbuch contradicts the charge as it applies to itself. For the Kantbuch the I is not represented to itself in a second act: "the 'I' 'goes along' in the pure turning-toward" and it is "not a side act of a knowing directed at thinking itself" (§29, 150). The "I think" therefore accompanies representing without being represented. Henry's dispute with this has to be that going-along is already ecstatic, and §29 does not say what ground the going-along has. As for the phrase from B 423, the Kantbuch has no comment.

R ¶43. Kant's habit of calling the "I think" an expression or statement shows the substitution; the condition of the representation collapses when the expression is taken for the thing. This is Henry's reading of Kant's wording. The Kantbuch treats the "I think" as an act of the finite subject and not as a sentence, and nothing in it responds to the charge about wording.

R ¶44, 151. A metaphysics of representativity pushed to its end "tips into nothingness and self-destructs" (151), and with it the "essence of life" is lost. The figure has a Kantbuch counterpart: the laying of the ground leads "before an abyss", Kant "saw the unknown" and "had to recoil" (§31, 167-168), and the Kantbuch speaks of a nothing at the centre (§25, 121-123). Both readings see Kant arrive at an edge and retreat. They differ on the diagnosis. The Kantbuch says Kant recoiled from the imagination because pure reason held him more strongly (168); Henry says the ontology destroys itself. For the Kantbuch the edge is a cause of the retreat; for Henry it is the system's own result.

R ¶45, 152. The form of apperception "is inherent in all experience" but "is not itself an experience" (A 354, Henry in a note), so self-consciousness could be only a representation of the condition of unity ("consciousness of self in general is the representation of that which is the condition of all unity", 152). The Kantbuch reads the corresponding "standing and abiding I" (A 123) as the correlate of all representations and as an "I" that forms the horizon of sameness (§34, 192-193). A 354 is not discussed in it. The Kantbuch's interpretation does not make self-consciousness the representation of a condition. It makes it the condition's own unthematic going-along, as ¶42 notes, so that the "representation of the condition" is Henry's reading of Kant's formula and not the Kantbuch's.

R ¶46, 152. The proposition must be reversed: the condition of representation cannot itself be represented, because "immanence cannot show itself in the phenomenological medium of exteriority". The logical half, that the horizon of objectness cannot be an object, is the Kantbuch's thesis (§25, 122-123: the X "is not an object but a nothing"; the horizon "must remain unthematic"). The phenomenological half is Henry's own. The Kantbuch has no concept of immanence for it to meet, and it would answer that what cannot be an object can still be manifest, as the horizon is.

R ¶47, 153. Henry preserves the critique of the paralogisms as true of its object and changes the object: Kant describes "the poorest of all" representations, not the self. The Kantbuch's reading of the paralogisms is a footnote (§34, 193 n. 270), so this reinterpretation has nothing in it to confirm or resist. The step is Henry's and rests on his earlier claim (¶42) that the paralogism is Kant's substitution of a representation for the *cogito*.

R ¶48. The poverty of the representation "I think" measured against the conditions of real objects, and the sterility explained by its lacking a manifold or being a "vehicle" of concepts: a direct reading of the Critique with no Kantbuch counterpart. Kant's "vehicle of all concepts" appears in the Kantbuch at §29, 150 ("the I is the 'vehicle' of the categories"), where Heidegger takes it as a constitutive role and not as a poverty.

R ¶49. The two senses of simplicity (A 355) are not in the Kantbuch. Henry's use is of Kant against the paralogism and of the amphibology between property of the self and property of its representation, which he found in Descartes.

R ¶50, 153-154. A 381: "everything is in continual flux ... except (if one insists) the I, which is so simple only because this representation has no content". Henry sees the Heraclitean flux and the emptiness of the I as two faces of one reduction of subjectivity to time. The Kantbuch reads the "standing and abiding" in the same pair of Kantian predicates the other way: they are horizon-forming characters of the I as it forms original time, so that the I's constancy is the constancy of the horizon (§34, 193-194). Henry takes the same stability to be emptiness. The difference is the one already seen at ¶20.

R ¶51, 155. The transcendental subject "= X" can take on every determination because it has none, "like the clouds in the sky"; "the transcendent ego of which contemporary phenomenologies will speak in their turn, is an accommodating phantom" (155). The Kantbuch's X is the object-X, the correlate of the unity of apperception (A 250, quoted at §25, 122: "a correlate of the unity of apperception for the unity of the manifold in sensible intuition"), and it treats the X as pure horizon, not as phantom. Henry's X is the subject-X of A 346/B 404, which the Kantbuch does not quote; the subject-X and object-X meet in the Kantbuch's identification of the I with the horizon-forming time (§34, 193). The jibe at "contemporary phenomenologies" does not touch it, because the Kantbuch's I is not a transcendent ego but a structure of transcendence.

R ¶52, 156. The representation "I" is still too rich, since every specification it supports "bears this hidden ipseity which they borrow from the mere representation 'I'" (156). Here Henry arrives at the Kantbuch's thing under his own name. What he finds hidden in the "I" is what the Kantbuch calls selfhood, and it too is hidden in the sense that it forms the horizon and is not thematic. The word is shared and the sense is not (see the traps above): for the Kantbuch selfhood is the temporal circuit, and this is what constitutes the specifications in Henry's list (subject of knowledge, noumenal self, idea of the soul). Henry's claim is that this hidden positivity cannot come from representation, and so is life. That is the claim the Kantbuch would have to refuse, and the chapter's own materials do not decide between them.

R ¶53, 156-157. The representation "I am" is singular and individual, so auto-affection "can be accomplished phenomenologically only as a determinate affection" (157), and a note answers the objection from "not a representation of a particular object" (137). The Kantbuch's selfhood is a structure of finite subjectivity as such, and it contains nothing like individuation. §41 says only that in every mood "our Dasein becomes manifest to us" (227), and does not elaborate singularity. Henry's insistence that ipseity is always this ipseity is the strongest ground against the Kantbuch's selfhood as an account of what a self is, and it is an objection from outside the Kantbuch's problem.

R ¶54, 157. Henry moves to thinkers who, "without Kant's extraordinary analytic apparatus", point "more surely toward the Essential". This is a transition, with no Kantbuch content. What the chapter hands to Chapter V is the reading of the "I think" as the point where a metaphysics of representativity self-destructs, and the claim that life, lost there, can be regained.

Across Chapter IV Henry takes over from the Kantbuch more than he states and removes more than he shows. He takes over the primacy of intuition and the location of finitude in intuiting (¶2), the extension of receptivity beyond the sensory (¶3), the horizon as ecstatic (¶4), the root of both stems and the nothing at the centre (¶¶6-7, 18), the I as unthematic going-along and the identity of time and "I think" as the structure of selfhood (¶¶25-26, 34, 40), and the recoil at the edge (¶44). The thesis that the condition of experience contains no existence, which he calls Kant's self-critique of the metaphysics of representativity, is the Kantbuch's own claim about ontological knowledge (§25). Where he departs it is for three reasons. He reads the Kantbuch's "nothing" privatively and not as horizon (¶¶7, 18). He changes the register from possibility to actualization, so that his "reversal" of the pure and the empirical (¶12) fights a priority the Kantbuch never asserts. And he makes "representation" a word for the ecstatic horizon alone (¶¶1, 11), which lets Kant's own genus (sensation is a representation) turn into the claim that sensation is not. The argument that does hold against the Kantbuch is narrower and stronger: the Kantbuch has an account of the pure self as temporal selfhood and none of the mineness of a given impression (¶29), and its single non-objectifying self-disclosure, in respect (§30), is not drawn on by Henry and does not close the gap. The reading of the Kantbuch's self-affection as affection by a horizon (¶35) remains Henry's thesis. It is not refuted by §34, which denies the existing self that the objection needs, and it is not supported by it either. The Kantbuch's silence on the paralogisms (¶19) means the chapter's central argument does not engage it directly: the claim that Kant's critique describes the empty represented I and leaves the self-revealing I untouched (¶¶47, 53) is a reading of Kant and meets the Kantbuch only through the account of the I as time. That verdict is local to this chapter. One caution applies to every judgment above: no Kant text was available, so Henry's readings of Kant are tested only where the Kantbuch quotes the same passage. Henry's explicit debt is also smaller than Commentary R makes it look. The Kantbuch is never named and Heidegger is named once, so the relation drawn here is between the texts.


## 4. The person and what motivates

### 4.1 An attitude that forgets itself

The third section opens with a change of attitude, and it describes the order of the first two sections from the new standpoint. In the naturalistic attitude, Husserl says, "what is experienced at the bottom is material (physical) nature, and founded in it is the experience of bodily and psychic being" (211/220–221); he even proposes to turn the usual word round and say "physio-psychic", since the reversed expression "indicates quite fittingly the order of the founding" (183/190–193). The layered reading can cite both sentences. Each of them, however, describes how things stand in one attitude, and the same pages say what rank that attitude has. The naturalistic and the personalistic attitude are not on a par: the first "subordinates itself" to the second and gains a certain independence only "through an abstraction, or rather through a kind of self-forgetfulness of the personal ego" (183–84/190–193).

The word had occurred before. The thing of the first section was the thing for a subject that remained "self-forgotten", and the forgetting was undone by bringing in the body. Now a second forgetting is named. What the natural scientist leaves out of account is himself as a person, who looks, measures, values the knowledge he gains and speaks of it with others, and for whom there are things and bodies at all. The founding order is the order of what such a person finds when he attends to nature alone.

Husserl sees that this makes the argument of the book look circular, and says so. At the beginning nature was posited outright, as the natural scientist posits it, and persons were "subordinate objects of nature"; once the essence of personality was pursued, nature appeared as "something that constitutes itself in the intersubjective association of persons and thus presupposes it". "We seem to fall here into a vicious circle" (210/218–221). One half of the circle is the received view; the other is its reversal. The distinctions of parts 2 and 3 allow us to say, before following Husserl further, why the two halves do not close into a circle of the vicious kind. That the soul is founded on the body means that some of the circumstances of its states lie in the body. That nature is constituted among persons means that there are things only as unities of appearances, and appearances only for subjects who move, perceive and understand one another. The first is a dependence between two realities, under the rule of conditionality. The second concerns the manifold in which any reality is given. A can depend on B in the first way while B depends on A in the second, since the two relations are different. This way of dissolving the circle is not stated in the text. But the text supplies its elements, and the third section adds the one still missing: the rule under which persons are what they are.

### 4.2 The surrounding world as circumstance

"As person I am what I am (and every other person is what he is) as *subject of a surrounding world*" (185/192–195). With this sentence the form of circumstances is taken up a third time, and its second term is named first. A person has circumstances, and they are called the surrounding world (*Umwelt*). The definition that follows separates it at once from nature. The surrounding world is the world perceived, remembered, believed and valued by the person; it is "not a world 'in itself' but a world 'for me'" (186/194–195). Physical reality belongs to it only so far as the person knows of it. Someone who knows nothing of the discoveries of physics does not have the world of physics for surroundings.

Two consequences are drawn. The first concerns the content of this world. In its core it is a world of sensuously appearing things, but on this core the person's valuing and practical acts have built: the thing that burns is fuel, the fuel is a useful good, the good a commodity. "All these are founded objects, which have constituted themselves for the ego through founded acts" (188/196–197). Here the spontaneous constitution of part 1 enters, and with it a third sense of "level", to which we shall return. The second consequence concerns the rule. The natural scientist says that stimuli go out from things and excite the nerves. For the person the word has another sense.

> In place of the causal relation between things and human beings as natural realities there enters the *relation of motivation* between persons and things, and these things are not the things of nature existing in themselves … but things experienced, thought or otherwise posited as such, intentional objectivities of personal consciousness. (189/196–199)

A later paragraph gives the criterion by which the two relations can be told apart. Between me and a thing there may be a real relation: light travels from it to my eye. There is also the intentional relation: I see it, it attracts me, I reach for it. "The real relation falls away if the thing does not exist; the intentional relation remains" (215/224–227). What attracts me, attracts me as it is given to me, and it would do so if I were deceived. Husserl calls the relation "motivational causality" and insists that it is "no real causality" (216/226–227).

The circumstances of the person are therefore of a different kind from the circumstances of the thing, of the body and of the soul. Those were realities. These are objects as meant. And the unity that stands in relation to them is a subject for whom they are there. The form is the same, a unity that comports itself under circumstances; each of its terms has changed once more.

### 4.3 Motivation, wide and narrow

"Motivation is the lawfulness of spiritual life" (220/230–231). The sentence stands at the head of § 56, and the paragraph that it opens is the most carefully divided of the book. It is also the place where the concept reaches down into the first section.

Husserl begins with motivation in the narrowest sense, in which I am the one motivated: I draw the conclusion because I have judged the premisses, I decide because I value. This is the motivation of position-takings by position-takings, and it stands under norms of reason. He then widens. The whole realm of association and habit belongs to motivation too, though no position is taken there: one thought recalls another, a present situation recalls a similar one and I expect the like. What makes these cases of motivation is that one can ask of them: how do I come to this, what has brought me to it? "That one can ask in this way characterizes all motivation whatsoever" (222/232–233). The motives may lie deep and may never have been noticed; they are still of the kind that could be brought to light by asking.

The third extension is the one that concerns us. The experience of things is itself motivated through and through. "The apprehensions of things and of thingly nexuses are 'webs of motivation'" (224–25/234–237): each partial intention points on to others, the given side to the sides not given, the present look to the looks that would follow. And then the relation described in § 18 returns under its new title. In the unity of the consciousness of a thing we have "'conditionalities', a 'because' and 'so'" to unfold in different directions: "if I turn my eyes so, if I set the optical series of experience going so, then I must see this and that" (226/236–237). The "if–then" that tied appearances to my movements was, as we saw, called motivation in passing in the first section. It is here counted expressly among the kinds of motivation, and the next sub-paragraph draws a conclusion of the widest reach.

> If we consider the structure of the consciousness that constitutes things, we see that all nature, with space, time, causality and so on, is completely dissolved into a web of immanent motivations. (226/236–237)

Three pages later we read that "the 'because–so' of motivation has a wholly different sense from causation in the sense of nature" (229/238–241). The two statements seem to pull against each other. If causality is something wholly different from motivation, how can nature, causality included, be dissolved into motivations? One receives the impression that Husserl gives two answers to the question of how the two rules are related.

On the one hand, they are two rules for two kinds of unity, and they exclude each other. Causal dependence holds between realities as realities; it holds whether or not anyone knows of it; its ideal is unambiguous determination by law. Motivation holds between what a subject is conscious of and what it then believes, feels or does; "what I do not 'know'", what does not stand before me as perceived, remembered or thought, "does not determine me spiritually" (231/240–243). The physiological processes in my nerves condition my sensations and do not motivate me. On the other hand, causality as something experienced is itself a unity that shows itself in motivated series. That the spring is elastic is seen in the answering of its swings to my flicks; and that I see this depends on appearances following my movements and one another in the motivated order that makes them appearances of one thing. The form of circumstances that constitutes the thing is embedded in a form of circumstances of another kind.

The text lets both answers stand, and I think it is right to do so, since they answer different questions: what rule holds among things, and under what rule there are things for someone. But the second answer has a consequence that Husserl formulates only in § 61, where sensibility with its associations and tendencies is called "a stratum of hidden reason, at least so far as the constitution of nature reaches", on the ground that all the complicated relations of the "if–then", "all causalities", can become clues for explicit theoretical unfolding (276/286–289). Motivation, which was to be the law of spirit as opposed to nature, turns out to lie beneath nature as well. The series of rules runs from the motivation of appearances through causality and conditionality to the motivation of persons. Its first and its last term bear the same name, and the two terms that concern nature stand between them.

### 4.4 The servant and his master

Among Husserl's examples of the "because" of motivation is this one: "The servant meets his master, and because he recognizes him as his master he greets him with deference" (230/240–241). I shall again go through the example and vary it, as a description of my own that tests what the text says.

I walk down a corridor; someone comes towards me; I see that it is the head of the house in which I serve; I step aside and greet him. If I am asked why I greeted, I say: because it was he. The "because" names what I saw and whom I took him to be. It does not name the light reflected from his face or the process in my retina, of which I know nothing and which I could not cite as my reason.

First variation: it was his brother, whom I took for him. I greeted in the same way and for the same reason; the reason was that I recognized my master, and I was mistaken. The motivating circumstance was the man as I took him to be. This is Husserl's criterion at work. The real relation to my master has fallen away, since he was not there, and the motivation is untouched. He makes the point with a stronger case: "I am afraid of the ghost" although I know that what I see is nothing real (232/242–243).

Second variation: it was my master, and I was reading a letter as he passed. Light and sound reached me as before. I did not greet. Nothing that belongs to the causal circumstances is missing, and everything that belongs to the motivating ones is. What does not stand before me does not move me.

Third variation: as he steps out of the shadow into the light of the window, my pupils contract. This too answers regularly to a circumstance, and it is something that happens in me. But the question "how did you come to that?" finds no purchase. I did not come to it; it does not manifest my deference or anything else about me as a person, and I learn of it, if at all, as I learn of the elasticity of a spring. The variant breaks the concept. A comportment that answers to circumstances of which the subject is in no way conscious is not motivated, however lawful it is.

Fourth variation: I have served in this house for twenty years and greet without thinking, as I would not have done in my first week. The greeting is still motivated, in Husserl's wide sense, and the question how I came to it now has an answer that leads into my past: I have always done so. Here the circumstance to which the act answers includes what I have become. We met this structure in the soul under the name of the idiopsychic dependence, the dependence of consciousness "on itself". In the person it is no longer one kind of dependence beside another. Every motive finds a subject already formed by its earlier motives.

The description was carried out for a person among persons, in a world of roles and customs, and its result holds within that scope. In motivation the circumstance is something as it is meant, it is there for the one it moves, and it moves him as the one he has come to be. What shows itself in such comportment is the person's own manner: in this case a certain deference, which the servant has and which another man in his place might lack.

### 4.5 Expression, and a third sense of "level"

How is another person given? The answer of the second section was empathy, and its result was the human being as a two-sided natural object. The third section describes the same experience in the personal attitude and reaches a different unity. When I listen to someone speak or watch him work, I do not find a body and, joined to it, a soul. I find a human being, and his body is "as body, through and through a body full of soul" (240/250–251). Husserl compares this with reading. I see the printed page, but I do not attend to the marks; I "live in the sense". The sense is not a second thing attached to the first: the spiritual "is nothing second, nothing appended, but precisely animating" (239/248–251). He calls a unity of this kind comprehensive, a unity of expression and what is expressed, and he places persons, books, tools, buildings, states and churches under the one title.

The relation of this unity to the founded reality of part 3 is fixed in two sentences. The belonging of the expressed spirit to the appearing body has "anything but the sense of the founded unity of nature 'human being'", the animal being that is an object of zoology; "rather, it precedes the constitution of every such unity" (244–45/254–257). And: "the unity of expression is a presupposition for the constitution of the founded reality", since through expression the other person "is there at all" for the one who experiences, and must be there in order to enter as one level into a real unity of two (245/254–257). The human being of the naturalistic attitude, the soul founded on a body, is thus a later constitution than the person who is understood in the body. First there is someone, and then this someone can be taken together with the body as a psychophysical object.

And yet Husserl speaks of founding here as well. The apprehension of the physical body is the "founding substrate" for the comprehending apprehension of sense (240/250–251); in a supplement he writes that "one can speak here too of a founded unity", in so far as the spirit is comprehended on the basis of the perception of the body (325/336–337). This is a third relation, different from the two we have met, and it is time to set the three side by side.

(i) A level of manifestation is a unity that serves as the manifold in which a higher unity shows itself. The schema is in this sense a lower level of the real thing, and the property of first order a lower level of the property of higher order. Lower and higher are here the same object under less and more apprehension. (ii) A founding by circumstances is the dependence of the states of one reality on another reality. The body founds the soul in this sense. Lower and higher are here two realities, each real in its own manifold, and the dependence is what makes the higher one a reality in the world. (iii) A founding of sense is the relation in which an apprehension presupposes another whose object it values, uses or understands. The mere thing founds the tool in this sense, the perceived body the person who expresses himself in it, the page the text. Lower and higher are here one object, to which a stratum of sense has accrued, and in living experience the lower is not attended to: the apprehension of the human being, Husserl says, "goes through" the appearance of the body and does not stop at it (240/250–251).

The ladder of the received view, thing, body, soul, spirit, takes one step of each kind and reads all three as steps of the same kind. From the schema to the material thing it climbs by (i). From the body to the soul it climbs by (ii). From the human animal to the person it would have to climb by (iii); and here the text says that the step leads in the other direction, since the unity of expression comes before the founded unity. I do not mean that Husserl never speaks as the received view does. He does, and part 6 will collect the places. But where he says what "founded" means in each case, the meanings are these three.

### 4.6 The form a third time

We can now put to the personal ego the question that was put to the thing and the soul: how is it given as what it is? Husserl's answer in § 57 uses, deliberately, the terms of the earlier analyses. In reflecting on my acts, he says, "I experience how I 'comport' myself under different subjective circumstances", that is, with respect to what is pregiven to me in my surrounding world; and as I enter into the interweaving of my motivations I experience how I am motivated by them and am wont to be motivated, what manner I have as the subject of these motivating circumstances, "what kind of personal subject I am" (248–49/258–261). What shows itself in this experience he calls personal properties or properties of character. A note to the passage draws the parallel to its end: observing others and myself under like circumstances, "I come to know myself as a personal 'reality'" (249 n./258–261).

Everything is there. A unity, the person; properties, the traits of character; a manifold of states, the acts and affections; circumstances; and a rule, expressed in the words "am wont". The parallel with the thing is stated outright. Once the apperception has developed, I am "pregiven to myself just as the thing is pregiven to me once the apperception of things has developed" (250/260–261), and I can set out to know myself better as I can set out to observe a thing. The person is constituted as a reality, under the form of circumstances, with the surrounding world in the place of real circumstances and motivation in the place of causality. Heidegger's first criticism, that the inquiry into the person remains an inquiry into the constitution of a reality, describes § 57 exactly.

### 4.7 "Not from experience but from life"

The next paragraph bears the title "Constitution of the personal ego before reflection", and it takes back the completeness of what has just been said. In reflection I always find myself as a personal ego. "Originally, however, it constitutes itself in the genesis that pervades the flow of lived experience" (251/260–261). The question is then asked whether the unity found in reflection is the original one, and the answer is no.

> The ego is originally not from experience, in the sense of associative apperception in which unities of manifolds of a nexus constitute themselves, but from life (it is what it is not for the ego, but itself the ego). (252/264–265)

The sentence denies of the ego precisely what defined a reality. A reality is a unity of a manifold under circumstances, and it is such a unity for someone who apprehends the manifold so. The ego that lives is not for itself in this way: "it does not primarily experience itself, but constitutes natural objects, things of value, tools" (252/264–265). It develops in doing so; what it does and undergoes has after-effects; it acquires abilities and habits. All this happens before, and whether or not, it becomes an object for itself. Husserl marks the difference from the thing in one line: "In the thing there occurs no apperception 'thing'; that occurs only in subjects" (253/264–265). A subject is the one being in which the apprehension of itself as a unity under circumstances occurs, and for that very reason it is not exhausted by what this apprehension grasps. Someone "does not know what he is; he comes to know himself" (252/264–265). It should not be concealed that the comparison with the thing accompanies even these sentences. The ego can have hidden dispositions, Husserl says in the same place, "just as a thing has properties that have not yet been drawn into the apperception of the thing" (252/264–265). The thing remains the term of comparison at the very point where its form is said not to suffice.

There are, then, two answers in §§ 57 and 58 to the question whether the person is a constituted reality. According to the first the person is a reality like the thing and the soul, a unity manifested in comportment under circumstances, given to itself in a developed self-apperception. According to the second this reality is the person as an object for itself and for others, and the person is in the first place the life in which that object, like every other, comes about. Husserl does not withdraw the first answer; the note to § 57 only confesses that "the analysis of personality is here very incomplete" (250 n./260–261). The use of the word "constitutes itself" for what happens in the genesis of the ego is the seventh of the uses listed in part 1, and we can now say what sets it apart. In every other use something is constituted for a subject. Here the subject is said to constitute itself, and what is meant is that it becomes what it is by living.

### 4.8 The hardest case: the underground

The thesis that the form of reality reaches its limit in the person has to face the last paragraphs of the section, in which the person seems to be given a lower layer after all. The spiritual ego, Husserl writes in § 61, "finds itself dependent on a dark underground of dispositions of character, of original and hidden dispositions, and on the other hand dependent on nature" (276/286–289). There are "two levels" in the person, a lower one that might also occur alone, as "pure animality", and the higher, specifically spiritual one of free acts. And § 62 states the chain in the order of the received view: the spirit in its acts depends on the soul, and the soul on the body, "hence it is conditioned by nature" (281/290–291). Is this not founding by circumstances, carried one storey higher?

In one respect it is, and the concession should be made without reserve. Husserl's example is a man crippled by a fall: the injury "has consequences for his spiritual life", and "certain groups of motivation fall away from now on" (276/286–289). What a person can be motivated by depends on what is pregiven to him, and what is pregiven depends on his body. A blind man has no motives that come from colours.

But the same sentence of § 62 continues: "yet it does not on that account stand in a relation of causality to nature. It has an underground that is conditionally dependent" (281/290–291). The dependence is placed in what the person has, and what the person has is distinguished from what the person does. The distinction was prepared at the beginning of the chapter, where everything that is pregiven to the ego, from the sensations up to the constituted things, is called its "possession" (*Habe*); whatever constitutes itself in acts of the ego becomes, "as something constituted, a 'possession' of the ego and something pregiven for new acts" (214/224–225). Conditionality reaches this possession. It does not reach the answer the ego gives to what it has, which is motivated by it and is not its effect. The cripple's motives are fewer. What he makes of those he has is not settled by his injury.

A second feature of the underground points the same way. § 62 explains its own vocabulary: the reality "body" expresses a regulation, the reality "soul" expresses "a second regulation" related to the first, and spirit is the index of a regulation of its own, for which "another cut, so to speak, is made through absolute consciousness" (281/290–291). The realities are here described as what they were in the analysis of the spring: titles for rules. And the lower level of the person, which § 61 calls its "natural side", is described in the same paragraph as "the site of the constitution of a world of appearances" (279/290–291), that is, of nature. The underground of the person is at once what depends on nature and what nature is constituted in. This is the circle of § 53 again, now inside the person, and it shows that the lower level of § 61 is no fourth storey of the building. It is the place where the first of the rules, the motivation of appearances, has its seat. What the text does not settle is whether this lower life is itself to be understood as soul in the sense of the second section, as a reality under conditionality, or as part of the life from which the ego is. The paragraphs speak both ways, and I leave the matter there.

The third section thus leaves the question of the title half answered. The person can be constituted as a reality and is so constituted, under a rule that is not nature's. The person is also that for which there are realities. What it means to call it, as the last paragraph of the book does, absolute, and nature relative, must now be asked in the light of the form.

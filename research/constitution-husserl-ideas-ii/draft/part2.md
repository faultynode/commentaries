
## 2. The thing and its circumstances

### 2.1 What a thing has over a phantom

Husserl comes to the concept of reality by a question that looks narrower: what does a material thing have that a mere filled shape lacks? His method, stated at the head of § 15, is to refuse the inherited answers and to follow the perception of a thing in the directions it itself indicates, running through in fact or in phantasy the series of further perceptions in which the same thing would show more of what it is. Carried out on a thing at rest and taken by itself, the method yields a surprise. If we hold to the thing "apart from the nexus of things", we have "no means at all of distinguishing the essence of the thing from the essence of an empty phantom" (36/38–41).

The example is the stereoscope. Two flat pictures fuse and I see a body in space, about which meaningful questions can be asked and truly answered: "this is a red, rough pyramid" (36/38–41). But whether it is heavy or light, elastic, magnetic, are questions for which the perception gives no foothold. The properties of this group are not left undetermined, as the colour of the unseen back of a thing is left undetermined; they are not represented in the apprehension at all. "In this way", Husserl adds, "we also see a rainbow, the blue sky, the sun" (37/40–41). He calls what is seen in such cases a phantom, and the spatial shape with its sensuous filling, which the phantom shares with the thing, the schema.

The point of the comparison lies in what it shows about the thing. A thing that rests and does not change shows us no more than its schema. It is apprehended as material, and yet in what is properly given "nothing would change if the whole stratum of materiality were struck out of the apperception" (37/40–41). The same holds when the thing moves or alters: what comes to actual perception is a continuous run of schemata, and a phantom can move, deform and change colour as well. Materiality, then, is not one more content among the sensuous contents. However long I look at the thing alone, I shall not see it.

### 2.2 The relation to circumstances

The answer is given in one step. "Up to now we have taken the thing in isolation. But the thing is what it is in relation to 'circumstances'" (41/44–45). Husserl's first case is illumination. The same unchanged thing looks different in daylight, in coloured light, at dusk, and it does so in a determinate way: to each change in the source of light there belongs a change in the schema. Here, he says, lies what the phantom lacks.

> Reality in the proper sense, which is here called materiality, does not lie in the mere sensuous schema, in what could belong to the perceived if there were for it nothing like a relation to "circumstances" … rather, it lies precisely in this relation and in the manner of apprehension that corresponds to it. (41/44–45)

Two things are said in this sentence, and both matter for everything that follows. The first is that reality is a relation. It is not found in the thing taken alone, and no closer inspection of the schema could find it there. The second is that the relation belongs to a "manner of apprehension". The series of schemata is experienced differently when the schemata are taken as running off in dependence on something else: they are then "experienced as manifestations (*Bekundungen*) of one and the same" (41/44–45). What is one and the same through the changing looks of the thing under changing light is its objective colour, the colour it has, as Husserl puts it, whether it hangs in sunshine or "in the dark of the wardrobe" (42/44–47). No single look is this colour, and the sum of the looks is not it either. The colour is the unity that holds through them according to the circumstances.

### 2.3 The steel spring

Husserl's second example allows the structure to be seen more exactly, and I shall go through it once in my own person and vary it. The stretch that follows is a description carried out on his example. It tests what his sentences say.

"A steel spring, when struck, performs certain oscillations" (42/44–47). I hold the strip of steel at one end and flick the other. It swings, quickly and narrowly, and comes to rest. I flick it harder: it swings more widely and for longer. I bend it slowly and let go: it returns. In each case I see a course of changing shapes and positions, a series of schemata, and in each case the series answers to what I did. What I call the elasticity of the spring is not any one of these series. It is that the spring answers in this way, each time, to that. Husserl's formula for this is "under like circumstances, like consequences" (42/44–47), and he at once guards the formula against a misunderstanding: the general rule "is not conscious as such; it expresses a form that belongs to the apperception of the real property" (42/44–47). I do not see oscillations and then infer a law. I see the spring as elastic, and seeing it so already contains the articulation into what happens to it and how it responds.

Now the variations. First, I leave the spring alone. Nothing happens. Husserl counts this case in: absence of change is the limiting case of change and falls under the same rule. But it has a consequence. The spring at rest shows me its shape and its grey sheen and nothing of its elasticity; the property is meant, and it is not given. It would be given only if I brought about the circumstances. A real property is therefore, from the start, one that can be absent from what is seen while the thing is fully in view.

Second, I transfer the spring into the stereoscope. I now see a strip of the same shape and colour, and it may even be seen to swing if the pictures are made to change. But there is nothing I could do to it, and nothing that happens in its surroundings could be that to which its swinging answers. The question whether it is elastic has lost its sense. This variant breaks the concept: with the circumstances removed, the same series of schemata no longer manifests anything.

Third, I hold the real spring in a flame until it glows, and let it cool. Flicked again, it bends and stays bent. Husserl uses this very case: the spring "is annealed, it loses its elasticity" (47/50–51). The property has changed. But it has changed under circumstances, and under the same circumstances another spring of the same steel would change in the same way. Hence a new unity appears, of which elasticity and its loss are in turn the states. Husserl draws the conclusion in general form: "the constitution of real properties can also take place at higher levels", such that "in unities of attestation higher unities in their turn attest themselves" (46/48–51).

Fourth, I try to imagine a spring that goes slack from one moment to the next with nothing whatever having happened to it or around it. Husserl asks this question himself, for elasticity, for colour that fades and for water that dries up, and he does not answer that it is inconceivable. That nothing changes without a cause is, he says, "presumably no a priori necessity" (49/52–53). What he holds is that the apprehension of a thing as real prescribes the search for circumstances, and that natural science has made of this prescription a strict idea. I note the hesitation because it shows where the necessity lies. It does not lie in the things, as a law that they could not break. It lies in the sense of "real property": a change that answered to nothing would manifest no property.

The scope of this description should be stated. It was carried out on a solid body, by one perceiver, with a body that functions as usual, and it took the circumstances from the surroundings of the thing. Husserl makes the first restriction himself and justifies it: solid bodies are the things given first, on which the others are understood (53/56–59). The second and third are the ones he lifts in § 18.

### 2.4 Unity, state, circumstance

We can now name the terms. There is, first, a manifold of what Husserl calls states: the momentary looks and positions of the spring, each of which, taken by itself, is a schema. In the apprehension of the thing the schema "is perceived as 'attestation' (originary manifestation) of a real property" and thereby as a state of the thing at that moment (43/46–47). I shall render *Beurkundung* by "attestation" and *Bekundung* by "manifestation"; Husserl uses the first for the case in which the manifesting series actually runs off and the property is thus given in the original, and the second more widely (122–23/128–131). There are, secondly, the circumstances, themselves real, to which the states belong. And there is the unity that holds through the states in their dependence on the circumstances: the property, and through the properties the thing. Where the dependences actually come to givenness, Husserl does not hesitate to say that causality is perceived: the causalities are then "not merely supposed but 'seen', 'perceived'" (43/46–47).

The thing itself is nothing behind this. A note says that "substance signifies here nothing further than the material thing as such", considered as the identical of real properties that realize themselves "in regulated manifolds of states in regulated dependence on pertinent circumstances" (44 n./46–49). And at the end of the chapter Husserl says what the whole analysis was for. He had "on purpose" let "the general of thinghood, 'reality'", stand out more strongly than what belongs to materiality in particular; and "that general something, which would surely best be designated as reality, is called substance" (54/58–59). The material thing has been analysed for the sake of a concept wider than materiality.

I shall call the structure so described the form of circumstances: a unity, a manifold of states in which it shows itself, and circumstances on which the states depend according to a rule. The label is mine. Husserl's own name for it, which we shall meet in § 31, is the "formal-general concept of reality", and his shortest statement of it is that realities "are what they are only in relation to other actual and possible realities" (126/132–135). It should be noticed how much this already contains. A thing is real through its relation to other things; these are real through theirs; no member of the nexus has its reality apart from the nexus. The last paragraph of the book will call nature a field of thoroughgoing relativities. So far as things among things are concerned, the phrase only restates § 15.

### 2.5 A first sense of "level"

The schema, which is a state in relation to the real property, is itself a unity in relation to something else. A shape is given in many aspects as I move my eyes and head, a colour in many shadings; and Husserl is careful to keep this relation apart from the one just described. The attestation of a shape through adumbrations, for all it has formally in common with any constitution of unities in manifolds, "is something in principle other than the manifestation of real properties through states", and "at the level of the schema there is no talk of substantial reality and causality" (131/138–139). Presentation and realization are two operations.

They are nevertheless joined in a definite way, and the way they are joined gives the word "level" its first precise sense in the book. What is a unity in one relation serves as manifold in the next. Aspects are unities of sensation data and manifolds for the schema; schemata are unities of aspects and states of the real property; properties of the first order are states of properties of a higher order. Husserl describes the constitutive make-up of the thing as a "remarkable stratification" which is "only a kind of continuation of another, analogous stratification in which the sensuous schema is already constituted" (53/56–59), and elsewhere he describes the regress: going back step by step from the unities to the manifolds that constitute them, we come at last "to the data of the lowest level, the sensation data in immanent time" (130/136–139). The summary in § 18 says the same in the forward direction. The real world "constitutes itself in levels", with the manifold of sense-things as lower stratum, and "realization is completed in such a way that the sense-things become states of real things" (65/68–71).

A level in this sense is a unity that functions as the manifesting manifold of a higher unity. It is what *Ideas I* called a "necessary intermediate link", and it is what Mattens has in view when he observes that the phantom can be contrasted with a lower and with a higher level. He is right to make it exemplary: within the constitution of the thing, analysis does proceed by taking away an apprehension and seeing what unity is left. But two features of such levels should be marked before the word is applied elsewhere. The lower level is not another reality on which the higher one rests. The schema is not a second object beneath the spring; it is the spring as it looks now. And the lower level is not independent of the higher in the way a foundation is independent of a house: in the perception of a thing the schema is from the start taken as a state, and it is isolated as a mere schema only by abstraction or in the stereoscope.

### 2.6 The body among the circumstances

The third chapter of the first section opens by confessing a restriction. The thing described so far is the thing for a single subject thought of in isolation, and thought of in such a way that this subject "remains, as it were, self-forgotten, and forgotten also by the one who analyses" (55/58–61). The forgetting has to be undone, because the qualities of things as they stand before me in intuition prove "dependent on my own make-up, that of the experiencing subject, related to my body and my 'normal sensibility'" (56/60–63). The thing has circumstances that were left out, and they lie on my side.

Husserl distinguishes two kinds of sensation that take part in every perception of a spatial thing. Some are apprehended as features of the thing, as its colour or its roughness. Others, the sensations of my own movement, receive no such apprehension and yet are indispensable, because the first kind run off in dependence on them: if I turn my eyes so, the image shifts so. It is in describing this dependence that he writes the sentence quoted at the beginning.

> In all constitution and at all levels we necessarily have "circumstances" related to one another and what "belongs" to the circumstances; everywhere we find the "if–then" or "because–therefore". (57/62–63)

The sentence is said of the constitution of spatial things, at all its levels, and it reaches further down than § 15 did. Even the phantom has its circumstances. A coloured shape seen in the stereoscope is, as Husserl had already noted, "an 'appearance' that refers back to kinaesthetic 'circumstances' to which it belongs" (22/22–25). The form of circumstances therefore holds for presentation too. But the "if–then" at this lowest level is of a particular kind, and Husserl names it without emphasis. The sensations that present the thing are "apperceptively related to motivating series, to systems of kinaesthetic sensations" (57/62–63); the sense-things are constantly intended as "motivating for the pertinent aspects as motivated" (65/68–71). That my moving and the changing look of the thing belong together is, in his vocabulary, a relation of motivation. It is not a causal relation between two things. The word had been used in passing before, for the way in which one partial apprehension of a thing points on to others (19/20–21; 40/42–45). It will return in the third section as the name of the fundamental law of spiritual life. Here it names the rule under which there are appearances of things at all.

A second kind of dependence on my side is of another sort again. If I take santonin, things look yellow, as through yellow spectacles; if an organ is injured or diseased, the things perceived by means of it appear altered (73/76–79). Such dependences Husserl calls psychophysical and prefers to describe by the word "conditionality" (*Konditionalität*), on the ground that a cause in the proper sense is something real, whereas "the subjective is, over against reality, an irreality" (64/68–69). They are regular, and they are bound up with causality at one distinguished place: "my body" is the thing at which "a system of subjective conditionality is interwoven with this system of causality" (64/68–69). But they do not enter into what the thing is. The world remains the one that shows itself to normal perception, the altered looks are accounted for by the altered organ, and the conclusion is drawn sharply: "to the constitution of the thing anomalies can contribute nothing"; what they contribute "is only the rule of my subjectivity" (74/78–79).

### 2.7 The form preserved in the thing of physics

The first section does not end with the thing of intuition, and its last step shows once more that what Husserl is following is a form. The dependence of the looks of things on the state of my body leaves a problem that a single subject cannot always resolve. If one of my organs gives testimony that conflicts with the rest, I can usually discount it as abnormal; but Husserl notes that the case may remain wholly undecided "so long as we take the experiencing subject as solipsistic" (73/76–79). The thing of intuition is relative to a normal sensibility, and what counts as normal is settled among several subjects who understand one another. With them comes the demand for a determination of the thing that holds for everyone, whatever his senses: the thing as correlate of any rational subject, determined in logical and mathematical form, of which the intuited qualities are then appearances.

It would be natural to suppose that this highest level of the constitution of the thing replaces the structure found at the lower one. Husserl says the opposite. The higher constitution ascribes to the thing a stock of persisting mathematical properties, "but in such a way that the general structure of the thing, the form of reality-causality, is preserved" (132/138–141). The states too are mathematized, and the causality of the thing of physics indicates the manifold of sensuous causalities. The physicist's thing is again a unity of properties that show themselves in states under circumstances according to laws; only the manifold has changed, which now consists of the things of intuition as they are given to the several subjects. The step from the seen thing to the thing of physics is one more level of manifestation, and what is kept across it is the three-term form and nothing else. This is the place where the text itself uses the word "form" for what the thing hands on, and it is used there to introduce the contrast with the soul, to which, as we shall see, the mathematical version of the form does not apply at all.

By the end of the first section, then, Husserl has three kinds of "if–then" in hand, although he has announced only one concept of reality. There is the motivation that ties appearances to my movements; the causality that ties the states of a thing to other things; and the conditionality that ties what I sense to the state of my body. Only the second constitutes real properties of things. The first lies beneath it, as the rule under which there are schemata to be realized. The third lies beside it, and constitutes, as we shall see, properties of something other than the thing. The received view counts one basis, material nature. The text has so far given us a thing that is real only in a causal nexus, a nexus that is given only in appearances motivated by a moving body, and a body at which causality turns into something else. What that something else constitutes is the question of the second section.

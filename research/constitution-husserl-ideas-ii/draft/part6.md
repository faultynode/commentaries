
## 6. The layered reading at full strength

### 6.1 Heidegger's three points

The strongest statement of the layered reading is also the earliest. In his Marburg course of the summer of 1925 Heidegger reported on the manuscript of *Ideas II*, which Husserl had communicated to him, and his report is neither hasty nor ungenerous. He knows that the book contains a personal attitude beside the naturalistic one and that the two are not of equal rank: "the naturalistic is subordinated to the personalistic" (GA 20, 169). He knows the underground of § 61, which he renders as the "natural side" that every ego has "as the underground of subjectivity" (169). He quotes the decisive sentences of § 64 one after another: that subjects cannot be absorbed into nature, that without spirits there is no nature, that spirits are not unities of appearances, that nature is the X and spirit is not (170). Whatever his criticism is, it does not rest on having overlooked the passages on which this study has leaned.

His judgment is that they change nothing. The reasoning of § 64 is "the same consideration" as the one that, in *Ideas I*, left pure consciousness as the residue of the annihilation of the world; Husserl "only comes back to his original division of being under another title. Ontologically everything remains as it was" (171). Three reasons are given, and Heidegger numbers them.

First, the inquiry into the person "remains within the question of the constitution of reality and objectivity" (171). What is asked is "how a being manifests itself as real in its reality" (172), and the question is put "in the order in which the things of the real themselves stand". Then comes the sentence that has fixed the received view.

> The *fundamental layer* remains the *actuality of nature*; on it the psychic is built, and on this the spiritual. (172)

The naturalness of the personal attitude is stressed, Heidegger grants, but the execution gives the investigation of nature the precedence. "*The being of the person is not experienced primarily as such*" (172).

Second, the access to the person is nothing other than the immanent reflection on lived experiences, the *inspectio sui*, from which the theses about absolute givenness derive. It is said that the nexus of experiences has its individuation in itself; "but the mode of being of the acts remains undetermined" (172).

Third, and for Heidegger most important, the whole division follows a traditional definition as its guiding clue (*Leitfaden*): the human being as *animal rationale*, a living thing that "has reason with it" (172, 174). From this comes the question that he puts to the layers: can the being of the concrete human being be "built together" out of the being of the material underground, of the body, of the soul and of spirit? "Is the being of the person the product of the modes of being of these layers of being?" Or does it show itself that in this way of first dividing and then putting together the person is taken, for all the tendency towards the personal, as "a many-layered thing of the world", whose being no determination of the reality of its directing-itself, however far it is carried, will ever reach (173)? The personal attitude does not take the human being as a reality of nature, "but still as a *reality of the world* which constitutes itself as transcendence in absolute consciousness" (173).

### 6.2 What speaks for him

The reading can call on a good deal of the text, and before answering it I shall set out what it can call on, including what the earlier parts have had to concede.

There is the programme, with its "lowest level". There is the order of the book. There is § 35, which announces the constitution of the human being as that of "a material body on which new strata of being, the bodily-psychic ones, are built" (143/148–149). There are the two sentences on the order of founding from the opening of the third section, and the chain of § 62, in which the spirit depends on the soul and the soul on the body. There is the first supplement, which sums up the ascent of the first two sections by saying that the soul constitutes itself "as a founded and therefore non-self-sufficient reality on the underground of the body" (310/322–323). There is the privilege of the body that we found in supplement XII: everything real in the world is corporeal, and the soul is a worldly reality only through the body that founds it. And there is Husserl's own vocabulary in § 61, the "two levels" of the person, of which the lower could occur alone as "pure animality": the *animal rationale* could hardly be described more plainly.

The list is longer than the passages already met. The first section introduces animal nature as "a complex consisting of a lower stratum of material nature" and an upper stratum inseparable from it (28–29/30–33), and animal realities as founded realities with material ones as their lower level (33/34–37). The third section opens with a retrospect in which Husserl says of the fundamental kinds of apprehension that they "were built on one another: physical experience as the basic one; resting on it and including it, the experience of the body", to which the experience of the soul belongs "as a constitutive stratum" (174/182); for natural science the soul is "a mere stratum of real occurrences in bodies" (175/182–185). The first supplement begins its ascent with the words: "Following the constitutive order, we have to begin with the constitution … of material nature" (305/314–315). And in § 56 itself the unity of body and spirit "constitutes itself as a higher unity of two real unities" and is perceptually present "as a formation of two levels" (245/254–257).

More important than these places is a consideration that turns the thesis of this study into an argument for Heidegger. I have maintained that the concept of constitution in *Ideas II* is a form, taken from the thing and carried over to body, soul and person. But a form taken from the thing is the thing's form. If the soul is real "in a similar sense" as the material thing, if psychic properties are unities of manifestation as elasticity is, if I am pregiven to myself "just as the thing is pregiven", then the person is understood from the thing, however carefully the terms are altered on the way. Heidegger says exactly this in his first point, and he says it in Husserl's own word: the question is how something "manifests itself as real in its reality". On this showing the form of circumstances does not refute the reproach of the many-layered thing. It is the more precise description of what is reproached. And it would explain the fact noted at the end of the last part. Where the form no longer applies, Husserl has only negations left, because the form was his only positive concept of being.

### 6.3 Replies, in his order

To the first point. It should be granted that the constitution of the person in § 57 is the constitution of a reality, and that Husserl's concept of reality has its origin in the thing; the supplement says so in those words. It should also be granted that between body and soul there is a founding, with a privilege of the body. What does not follow is the sentence about the fundamental layer. That sentence takes "built on" in one sense throughout, and we have found three. The material thing is not the lower level of the soul as the schema is the lower level of the thing: the soul shows itself in lived experiences and not in bodily states. The body founds the soul in the sense that circumstances of the soul's states lie in it; this founding is the condition of the soul's being a reality in nature, it holds in the naturalistic attitude, and the text calls that attitude an abstraction that forgets the person. And the person is not built on the psychophysical unity: the unity of expression in which a person is there "precedes the constitution of every such unity", even though Husserl is willing to call this unity too, in its own sense, a founded one (325/336–337). Husserl does call the ascent from material nature "the constitutive order", and he does say that the kinds of apprehension are built on one another. But the first is said, in the supplement, of strata distinguished "ideally" for an ego thought of in isolation, and the second of "the whole system of naturalistic experiences" (174/182). The building is a building of apprehensions within one attitude. At the place where the exposition reaches the person, Husserl says that the unity in which a person is there comes before the founded one. And the one passage that describes without restriction to an attitude how "the levels of the constitution of reality are built on one another" begins the building with the single ego, which constitutes its outer world and then, together with other egos, objective nature and the social world (324/334–337).

There is a further point about the bottom of the building. The lowest level that *Ideas II* reaches by its own regress is sensation, and beneath the causal nexus of nature it finds the motivated order of appearances and movements. If one insists on asking what, in this book, everything else rests on, the answer the text gives is not material nature. Nature is in the middle.

To the second point. The access to the person in § 57 is reflective, and Heidegger is right that the being of the acts receives no determination of its own there. But § 58 exists in order to say that the person given in reflection is not the original one. The ego is "not from experience", it "does not primarily experience itself", someone "comes to know himself". These sentences deny that the *inspectio sui* gives the person as it is, and in denying it they say something, however little, about how it is: it is by living, and it is in advance of what it knows of itself. Heidegger does not mention the paragraph. I cannot tell whether it stood, in this form, in the manuscript he read; the printed sentences contain words that the apparatus assigns to Husserl's later revision.

To the third point. Here least can be said in reply, and I do not think the text allows more. § 61 does divide the person into a lower level, sensibility, and a higher, the *intellectus agens*, and it does consider the lower as something that might exist by itself. One can point out that Husserl describes this lower level as the site of the constitution of nature and as "hidden reason", which is not what the tradition meant by the animal in us. But the scheme of the division is the traditional one, and Heidegger identified it correctly.

### 6.4 The objection turned

The question remains whether the form of circumstances, being the thing's form, makes the person a thing. On this the text contains a resource that Heidegger's report passes over, and it is the very objection he raises.

His question was whether the being of the person can be put together out of layers. Husserl asks the same question about the unity of body and spirit in § 56, and what he says there cuts both ways. He does describe the human being as a higher unity of two real unities, a formation of two levels. But he denies, three times over, that such a unity is a putting together. The spiritual is "nothing second, nothing appended"; body and sense are not two things joined, of which each could also be by itself. A merely parallel connection of two realities "creates no reality of a higher level": a sum of layers is not a unity. And where there is a founded unity, it is founded by the fact that each side is a circumstance for the other, so that the unity lies in a rule and not in a stacking. The programme had already said that what is new in a founded unity "can never be reduced to mere sums of other realities". What was meant as an objection to Husserl proves to be a thought of Husserl's own. He keeps the levels and rejects the sum, and he rejects it for the reason Heidegger gives.

The same holds, with a difference, for the form itself. The form of circumstances was read off the thing. But what Husserl kept of it when he carried it over was, at each step, less of the thing. Of the three terms the thing supplied the pattern of all and the content of none: no schemata for the soul, no outer circumstances for the person, no causality for either. And the feature in which the form was first strained, that a unity can be among its own circumstances, is the feature by which § 64 distinguishes spirit from every thing. Husserl did not think the person from the thing in the sense that he ascribed to it the thing's manner of being. He used the thing to learn what it is to be a unity under circumstances, and then described the person by what in it will not fit.

That is also the measure of what Heidegger saw rightly, and I do not want to diminish it. A description by what will not fit is a description by negation. Husserl has a concept of reality that he can state with complete clarity, and he has, for that which is not reality in this sense, the words "absolute", "irreal", "from life" and a note saying "too quick". Heidegger's sentence that the being of the person "is not experienced primarily as such" is unjust to § 58 and just to the book as a whole: the person comes into it at the point where a form developed elsewhere gives out. One may add that Husserl was the first to say so. Heidegger himself told his students that Husserl, on communicating the manuscripts, had written to him that he would now have to carry the whole out anew, "with in part completely changed content", and that the characterization given in the lecture was therefore "in a certain way already somewhat antiquated" (168).

### 6.5 What it is to be constituted

The title asked what it is, in *Ideas II*, to be constituted, and the answer can now be given without the metaphor of storeys. To be constituted as a reality is to be the unity of a rule that binds a manifold of states to circumstances. This holds of the thing, of the body and of the soul, and it holds of the person in so far as the person is given to itself and to others as someone of such and such a character. What differs from region to region is the manifold, the kind of circumstance and the kind of rule; and the "levels" of which the book speaks are three different relations that this form makes possible, the level of manifestation, the founding of one reality by another as its circumstance, and the founding of sense. The question of the received view, what is constituted on what, has therefore no single answer, because "on" has no single meaning. The question the text itself puts is: in relation to which circumstances, and under which rule?

Put in this way, the question has a result that the received view could only register as a reversal. The rules form a series, and the series does not begin with nature. It begins with the motivation that ties appearances to a body that moves; causality and conditionality, the two rules of nature, come second and third; the motivation of persons comes last and takes up the first again. Nature is a field of relativities because the form of reality applies to it without remainder, and spirit is called absolute on two grounds, of which the analysis of reality supplies one: a unity that has a history is among its own circumstances, and what is wholly determined from outside has nothing in itself. So far, and no further, § 64 draws a consequence of § 15. Its other ground, the individuation of consciousness, it brings from the first book.

The result has its limits, and I have tried to mark them as they arose. I have followed the concept through the printed text and have not asked when its several formulations were written; whether the connection I find between § 33 and § 64 was one Husserl saw, or one that Edith Stein's and Ludwig Landgrebe's arrangement of his manuscripts produced, cannot be decided on the basis used here. I have left the constitution of time and the constitution of a common world where the book leaves them. I have not read Ricœur, Merleau-Ponty or Sokolowski on these matters, and what is said here may in part have been said by them. And the tension that runs through the third section remains. The person is a reality constituted under circumstances, and the person is not a unity of manifolds at all but that which lives. The text holds both, and the concept of constitution that it possesses can state only the first.

Is to be constituted, then, to be founded on something lower? For the realities of nature the answer is: sometimes, and in a sense that has to be specified each time. For the person the answer is no. What Husserl achieved in *Ideas II* is more than a building of strata, though he builds. It is the discovery that reality has a form, the patient demonstration of how far the form reaches, and, in the sentence that the ego is not from experience but from life, the admission that the one who constitutes cannot be wholly given in the form in which everything else is. Heidegger asked what the being of that life is. That he could put the question to this book, and find in it the passages from which to put it, is not the least of the book's merits.

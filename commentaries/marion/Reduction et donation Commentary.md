# Commentary on Jean-Luc Marion, *Réduction et donation: Recherches sur Husserl, Heidegger et la phénoménologie*

References are given by Marion's own divisions (chapter numeral and section number), since the source used here carries no critical pagination.

Certain confusions dog this book and are worth naming in advance. Givenness (*donation*) is not a gift and carries no donor; Marion's argument here is strictly phenomenological, and reading it backward from the later theological work inverts its order. The correlation "the more reduction, the more givenness" is not a rule of yield but a claim that whoever reduces is measured by what gives itself. The critique of *Sein und Zeit* does not charge Heidegger with failing to raise the question of being; it charges him with never producing ontological difference as a phenomenon, which is a phenomenological objection, not a doctrinal one. Marion's *objectité* (objectness, Husserl's *Gegenständlichkeit*) is wider than objectivity and must not be collapsed into it. The call that governs the third reduction is not the call of being, and the one it summons is neither a fourth ego nor Levinas's ethical subject. Finally, Marion neither endorses Heidegger's reading of the Sixth Investigation nor Derrida's reading of the First; he shows that both rest on the same unexamined equation of presence with intuition.

## Foreword

Marion states the thesis in a single line: the research to follow places givenness at the centre of the reduction, and therefore at the centre of phenomenology. Two claims are packed into "therefore." Phenomenology is defined by the reduction rather than by intentionality or by description, and the reduction is in turn defined by what it delivers rather than by what it brackets. The link to his earlier Descartes studies is called indirect but probably necessary — those books tracked the metaphysical determination of the ego and of the divine names, and they presupposed, without saying so, a doctrine of what gives itself. The closing phrase names the destination: to determine what, without being it, comes after the subject. The French *sans l'être* holds two senses at once — without being the subject, and without being at all — and both are meant. Whatever succeeds the ego and Dasein will not be a subject, and will not be reached through being. The 1988 debate on who comes after the subject supplies the occasion; Marion's answer, *l'interloqué*, arrives only in chapter VI.

The acknowledgements situate the book institutionally and, more importantly, philosophically. Courtine and Franck are credited with a practice of phenomenology older and better than his own — Marion is announcing that he writes from within the French Husserl scholarship of the reconstituted Paris Archives rather than against it. Ijsseling and Bernet at Leuven supply the Husserl-Archiv's confirmation, which matters because the argument of chapter I turns on unpublished and late Husserlian self-interpretations. The last name, Michel Henry, is the philosophically loaded one: Henry's material phenomenology had already detached manifestation from intuition and from the world's horizon, and Marion's eventual attempt to reduce beyond the question of being borrows that gesture even where it departs from Henry's answer. The date, February 1989, places the book after the publication of the early Freiburg and Marburg lecture courses on which chapters II and VI depend.

## Phenomenology as Such

The epigraph is drawn from Heidegger's earliest Freiburg lectures: what does "given," "givenness" mean — phenomenology's magic word and the stumbling block for everyone else? Marion is not decorating his introduction. He is claiming that the question his book raises was already raised, and dropped, at the very beginning of Heidegger's phenomenological career, before the question of being had taken command. The word Heidegger uses for the outsiders' difficulty, "stumbling block," registers that givenness is not a technical convenience but the point at which phenomenology becomes unintelligible to those outside it. That givenness could be both the magic word and the scandal already implies that no one has thought it.

Phenomenology takes over the office of philosophy in this century because Nietzsche exhausted metaphysics, including its inversions. The claim is not that phenomenology succeeds Nietzsche chronologically but that a new beginning became possible only once every metaphysical possibility, and every reversal of one, had been played out. Marion's periodisation is Heidegger's: the end of metaphysics is an accomplishment, not a collapse. What follows in the chapter will show that the new beginning inherits more of what it succeeds than it knows.

The 1887 and 1900 coincidences are not anecdote. Nietzsche's last god and Husserl's inaugural lecture on the aims of metaphysics fall in the same year; Nietzsche's disappearance and the first volume of the *Logical Investigations* fall in the same year again. What binds them is one question: can the conditions of presence be stretched until every entity reaches presence, past the limits set by earlier metaphysics or by any possible metaphysics? Can each thing's coming-into-presence occur without condition or reserve? The question is Nietzsche's final advance and Husserl's first arrival, and around it philosophy tips from a metaphysics completed both positively and negatively toward something perhaps already post-metaphysical. Phenomenology can claim to satisfy what Husserl called modern philosophy's secret longing at the very moment it oversteps it. Hence the exact position Marion assigns it: on the watershed line. By liberating presence it fulfils metaphysics' expectation; by taking away the object of metaphysics' quarrel it abolishes it. Fulfilment and abolition are one act, which is why the book will not be able to decide against phenomenology from outside.

The claim to abolish metaphysics is only a claim. Presence given without the old reserves imposes demands of its own. Husserl's breakthrough of 1900–1901 is therefore ambiguous twice over: it extends the field of presence past all limits, to the point of dissolving the very notion of a limited presence, and precisely thereby it reproduces presence's constitutively metaphysical determination — objectivity. Marion's target is not the familiar quarrels about realism and the transcendental turn but something that cuts beneath them: if the aim of phenomenology is the return to the matters at issue, and if those matters turn out to give themselves only as constituted objects, then the ideal of objectivity contradicts phenomenology's own objective. Nothing guarantees that the things in question show up only in the figure of their objectivation.

Heidegger's starting point is exactly that non-coincidence between phenomenology's aim and objectivity. The recently published courses from the first Freiburg period, from Marburg, and from the second Freiburg period allow a point to be established firmly: being became the stake of phenomenology for Heidegger first and definitively within a critique of Husserl's ideal of objectivation. Marion is staking the book's method on the *Gesamtausgabe* rather than on *Sein und Zeit* alone, and the point is decisive because it makes the Husserl–Heidegger conflict internal to phenomenology rather than a change of subject. The conflict is exemplary because perfect mutual comprehension coexists with total mutual incomprehension, as in every great philosophical confrontation. The question now reads: does the return to the matters at issue lead back to their objectivity or to their being? And does whatever performs that leading-back operate as transcendental I or as Dasein? Marion's parenthetical *re(con)duction* keeps both the leading-back of the reduction and the conducting agency in view. What is at stake is neither a mutation of method nor a fresh posing of the question of being, but whether phenomenology opens a new beginning at all.

Heidegger too fails to accomplish what he attempted, and Marion states the three failures that will structure the book. Dasein remains haunted by the I — chapter III. The phenomenon of being, even in the attenuated figure of ontological difference, never exposes itself — chapters IV and VI. And the phenomenology of the inapparent, once required, never gets past its programmatic status and its contradictory formulation. If the horizon of being in its temporality thereby becomes questionable, as Heidegger himself conceded in the closing section of *Sein und Zeit*, phenomenology faces a dilemma: disappear as a philosophical discipline, or grant that its method must no more depend on the question of being than it could earlier confine itself to objectivity. Levinas, Ricœur, Derrida and Henry have all in fact taken the second route, letting phenomenological procedure rest on its own protocols alone, on the far side or the near side of both the constituted object and the entity in its being. Marion is not proposing a novelty; he is naming a route already taken and offering to justify it.

What follows attempts nothing more than to disengage phenomenological procedure as such from the successive and provisional aims it has been confused with. The methodological warrant is Heidegger's own: in phenomenology, unlike metaphysics, possibility stands higher than actuality. Marion pushes that principle to its limit and turns it against phenomenology as actually carried out. A genuine thought is not surpassed by refutation but by repetition, by borrowing from it the means of thinking with it beyond it. The closing sentence — then even failure succeeds — sets the standard by which chapter IV will judge *Sein und Zeit*: the incompletion of that book will count as an essential accomplishment rather than an accident.

## I. The Breakthrough and the Enlargement

### 1 - Two Interpretations and an Enlargement

Husserl's own salute to the *Investigations* as a work of breakthrough, offered thirteen years after their publication, barely conceals a demotion beneath the praise. In the same year he brings out the first volume of the *Ideas*, and the salute is the kind a traveller gives from a departing ship to a shore left for good; his own gloss confirms it — a work of breakthrough, and so less an end than a beginning. Every subsequent reading has been caught in that paradox: the breakthrough is acknowledged only in order to serve at once as the starting point of a later phenomenology, so that 1900–1901 gets understood by reference to what it does not yet say. Whether one judges the drift regrettable, as Ingarden's school does, or correct, as Husserl does, changes nothing. Marion's counter-demand is that the breakthrough be read on the sole discourse of the *Investigations* themselves. That does not mean doxography: excellent studies have shown that Husserl's opponents remain largely unaware of the metaphysical situation determining them, so identifying targets yields nothing beyond a catalogue. Only an interpretation already awake to the essence of metaphysics can give the breakthrough its metaphysical dignity, and just two such interpretations exist. Heidegger's finds in the Sixth Investigation the categorial intuition of being, hence a rupture with neo-Kantian dissimulation and an opening onto a givenness of being; his 1973 formulation is that the question of the sense of being could be unfolded only if being were first given, and Husserl's *tour de force* consisted in setting being in presence, phenomenally present in the category — which gave Heidegger at last a ground, since "being" is then not a mere concept won by abstraction. On that reading the breakthrough anticipates the destruction of ontology and completes the end of metaphysics; it pierces beyond metaphysics. Derrida's reading inverts the perspective entirely. The breakthrough does disengage signification in its a priori ideality, but Husserl never draws the consequence that signification signifies by itself; he keeps leading it back to a fulfilling intuition that secures it in evident presence. Phenomenology thus perpetuates the primacy of presence against its own intention, and its belonging to classical ontology is betrayed not merely by the recourse to intuition but by the uninterrogated primacy of presence in and over signification. Overcoming the metaphysics of presence would then require overcoming phenomenology, by playing against it the indication (*Anzeichen*) censored in the First Investigation — the sign foreign to all presence — against the meaningful sign awaiting intuitive fulfilment. On that reading the breakthrough does not overstep metaphysics; it leads back into it.

The two interpretations pull the *Investigations* in opposite directions and generate opposite sequels. Read from the Sixth, they yield the categorial givenness of being and permit the passage to ontological difference by way of the destruction of the history of ontology. Read from the First, they yield a primacy of presence made all the sharper by the fact that the intentionality of the a priori had made it contestable, and they require the passage to *différance* and to deconstructions without beginning or end. Marion extracts two questions rather than choosing a side. First: in what, for Husserl himself and setting aside the prolongation in the *Ideas*, does the breakthrough consist, and does its consciously Husserlian motive bear on metaphysics? Second: Heidegger takes the categorial givenness of being from the Sixth Investigation, Derrida stigmatises presentifying intuition in the First; if that distribution holds textually, might it have enough conceptual reach for the two readings to be organised rather than opposed? The second question carries the book's decisive wedge, stated here almost in passing: does the characteristic of givenness amount to the characteristic of presence by intuition? Everything after depends on the answer being no.

Husserl calls the *Investigations* a breakthrough only in 1913, but already in 1900 he identifies the difficulty of pure phenomenological analysis and its motive — an anti-natural orientation of thought and intuition, an anti-natural habit of reflection. The habit consists in refusing to treat objects as what is actual and treating instead the acts that underlie them. The matters at issue are not what we would naturally count as actual but what we normally pass over: the acts. To get back from things (*Dinge*), which may be nothing but words, to the matter at stake each time (*Sache*), reflection must each time be led back to its own acts, and conceptions led back to the intuition that answers to them or fails to. Marion assembles the textual chain: logical concepts must draw their origin from intuition; the return to the things themselves proceeds by fully developed intuitions; assertions are won purely from inner seeing and from an intuitive ascent to general necessities, so that unconditioned universality itself comes to be intuited. The return to the things themselves has force only where it restores possible evidence to what, on the natural orientation, precisely offers no intuition — which is why the difficulty is admitted at all: Husserl demands intuition in logic, and does not shrink from speaking of the intuitive performance of abstraction. The 1913 draft preface makes it explicit, calling the method strictly intuitive in the enlarged sense Husserl gives the term and locating there the deep difference between his rationalism and all earlier ones, including scholastic ontology. The self-interpretation does not deviate from the original intention: already in 1901 Husserl claims to have found a cornerstone of all future phenomenology in a fundamental and hitherto unperformed enlargement of the concepts of perception and intuition. Marion's conclusion: the breakthrough consists in raising intuition, as the agent of evidence, to the rank of adequate phenomenological justification for every statement. Opening and close answer to each other — the breakthrough is accomplished by leading everything that claims to constitute itself as phenomenon back to intuition.

The first confirmation removes the standard objection that the principle of all principles of 1913 sets phenomenology on an unforeseen course, or contradicts the original injunction. The unpublished draft preface, written in 1913 but written *for* the *Investigations*, credits them with radically professing that principle: the right of what is clearly seen, which as such is the originary, prior to all theory, the ultimate norm. The principle is fulfilled when, following the evidence of experience, we simply speak of things that are and accept what is immediately given as something that is. Far from contradicting the return to the things themselves, the principle comprehends it: turning back from objects to acts already implies that originarily giving intuition is a source of right for knowledge. Against the objection that this reconciliation dates from 1913 and is contaminated by the turn of the *Ideas*, Marion cites two anticipations from the *Investigations* themselves. The Fifth argues that whoever will not concede the specificity of intentional lived experiences — that being-object consists phenomenologically in certain acts in which something appears or is thought as object — will not understand how being-object can itself become objective. Marion reads this as an unrefutable argument in the strict sense: the objector excludes himself from the terrain of givenness on which argument is possible at all. The second anticipation is the principle of presuppositionlessness closing the introduction to volume two, which requires that the meditation on the sense of knowledge be carried out as pure eidetic intuition on the exemplary basis of given lived experiences of thought. Intuition itself cannot count as a last presupposition, since it is neither presupposed nor posited nor given but originarily giving. Marion's aside is precisely calibrated: intuition sees what theories presuppose about their objects, and because it gives without ground or condition it precedes theories of the given, as a theory of theories — in something like the sense in which Aristotle, denying that there is a science of science, must admit *nous* at the principle of the principles. The regress-stopping function is what makes intuition principial, and it is exactly that function which will later pass from intuition to givenness.

The second confirmation is that intuition can hold this office only by undergoing the phenomenological reform first, submitting to a fundamental enlargement. Announced in the Second Investigation, the redeployment is accomplished only in the Sixth, whose introduction promises an absolutely unavoidable enlargement of the originally sensuous concepts of intuition and perception, permitting talk of categorial and universal intuition. Marion notes the delay with which the decisive move arrives: the Sixth keeps its promise only in its second part, and §45, explicitly titled the enlargement of the concept of intuition, is where Husserl finally dares to admit that before the categorial object we cannot do without the words "intuition," "perception," "object" in an undeniably enlarged sense. Follow intuition as the guiding thread and the unity of the First with the Sixth becomes visible: the First posits the intuitive and universal return to the things themselves as principle, but does not yet possess the concept of intuition it purports to employ, and so has, in Husserl's own words, a merely preparatory character; the Sixth, the cornerstone of all phenomenology, is the most extended, ripest and most fruitful of the book. Marion turns the apparent anomaly — a starting point presupposing results still to come — into Husserl's own methodological warning that phenomenology must use nearly all the concepts it aims to clarify, so that research moves in a zigzag, forever returning to earlier analyses and confirming each by the other. This licenses the interpretive rule the whole chapter follows: the *Investigations* must be read backward from their end. The answer to the first question can now be given. The breakthrough leads concepts and objects back to intuition and thereby enlarges intuition's own reach radically, so that intuition gives more than it appears to give — more, at least, than it appears to a non-phenomenological gaze. Because intuition is enlarged, more appears than appears to appear.

### 2 - The Domains of Intuition

What does the enlarged intuition give to be seen? The draft preface answers with a second aspect of the one breakthrough: we evidently know something objective which nevertheless is not in the manner of a thing (*Reales*); ideas themselves are, and count as objects. In 1900–1901 this means that ideal objects genuinely exist — the seven regular solids are as much objects as the seven Sages, the parallelogram of forces as much as the city of Paris. The anti-natural orientation requires treating as objects the acts hitherto held to be non-objective: precisely these acts, until now devoid of all objectivity, must become objects of apprehension and theoretical positing, to be considered in new acts of intuition and thought, analysed, described, made into objects. Objectivity therefore oversteps the mode of being of the thing, frees itself from reality, and is itself enlarged — which is why Husserl prefers the vaguer term *objectness* (*Gegenständlichkeit*), covering not only objects in the narrow sense but states of affairs, properties, and dependent real or categorial forms. Commenting on the *Investigations* in 1925 he will say flatly that there are irreal objectnesses and irreal truths belonging to them. Marion draws two points, the second the load-bearing one. Objectness extends past real objectivity in parallel with intuition's extension past the sensuous; and both extensions terminate on the same horizon — the categorial. The note on objectness in the First Investigation already concerns categorial forms. So the breakthrough works from the start for the sake of the categorial, through the enlargement of intuition and of objectness alike.

The enlargement of intuition is directed against the Kantian finitude for which our nature entails that intuition can only ever be sensuous; it requires subjecting intuitivity itself to an essential enlargement against its Kantian sense, so that the concept in its categorial figure affects us and is given. Marion sets out three moments, each deploying a new sense of the categorial, from which he draws the surprising conclusion that for Husserl every intuition is categorial to some degree. (a) Kant made sensuous intuition blind without a concept subsuming it; Husserl takes this over to hold that the goal is not simple intuition but adequate intuition that has taken on a categorial form and so fits thought perfectly. The most elementary intuition would have no validity without a signification whose fulfilment it secures, and it can only presuppose that categorial form if the form is itself given. (b) That givenness occurs intuitively, in the evident ideation where the universal is given in person. One and the same intuition delivers two irreducible objects, because on the same intuitive substrate two acts are performed rather than one. In one direction intuition founds an individual intention — this house, this shade of red, this little patch of yellow wall so unsubstitutable that reaching it means going to the motif and risking, like Bergotte, dying of it. In the other direction the first intuition serves as mere support for aiming at the house as the essence of any possible house, or at the universal red that no worldly shade can exhaust or approach, since that essential red never shines among them although every particular red shines from it. On the sensuous intuition an apprehension that brings forth the species is built up, a new mode of apprehension constitutive for the intuitive givenness of the idea. The categorial as universal essence is given, and given intuitively: the first intuition is diverted from the individual — hence from itself as given here and now as a "this" — under the possessive fascination of the categorial. Ideal objects truly exist, and we evidently grasp categorical truths relating to them; evidence (*Einsicht*) accomplishes the intuition of essences in ideation. Marion's interest lies not in this well-known result but in what makes it possible. Eidetic intuition already mobilises the pairing of founding and founded acts, anticipating the Sixth Investigation, and above all it mobilises the instance of the categorial: the difference between particular and universal acts is itself called categorial, non-sensuous acts fall under categorial thinking, and the First Investigation traces the different significations attributable to one intuition to variations of a single categorial point of view. The orientation of intuition toward universal categorial forms therefore rests entirely on a categorially determined interpretation of intuition itself. The decisive step of enlargement is taken, from the outset, on the authority of the categorial — hence from the Sixth Investigation. The whole is unintelligible unless read from its terminus. This reversal of reading order answers not only to the zigzag but to the phenomenological paradox Heidegger stated in 1925: acts of ideation are categorial acts that give objects, and what they give is characterised as idea, *species*, translating *eidos*, the look under which something shows itself; walking through my surroundings and seeing houses, I do not first and expressly see particular distinct houses but first see universally that here is a house. I see the house *as* house before, and in order to, see a house; the "as" precedes any given house and makes it appear as such. Eidetic intuition does not extend sensuous intuition by a fragile addition but precedes it and makes it phenomenologically possible.

The last step is categorial intuition in the strict sense, and Marion registers surprise at the economy and speed of §45. A principle: the essential homogeneity of the function of fulfilment, so that whatever the signifying intentions, all in principle demand at least the possibility of intuitive fulfilment. A question: in an expression like *gold is yellow*, can all the categorial forms receive their corresponding fulfilling intuition if we hold to the two types of intuition already recognised? Sensuous intuition plainly cannot fulfil the categorial forms *gold* and *yellow*, which take their fulfilment from eidetic intuition. What remains is the bare *is*. And the *is*, Husserl says, is itself set before our eyes, not merely thought but intuited, perceived. For we do not aim only at gold and then at yellow but at their connection, and we assert their unity; more, the predicative unity is doubled by an existential ascertainment — not only gold-yellow but gold-yellow *is*. Strictly categorial intentions therefore call for a corresponding fulfilling intuition. Confirmation comes from the negative case: as the signification *gold-yellow* does not coincide with *gold-yellow is*, so the absence of the fulfilment of *is* is clearly distinguished from its presence, which would give us gold-yellow being in person. Positively and negatively, the last categorial form puts the interplay of intention and intuition to work, so that all significations, even categorial ones, unavoidably run up against an intuition, a perception, an object. Marion strips the doctrine of its mystique: categorial intuition opens no third eye but results from the plain return to the things themselves, which finds that the *is* too offers a signification and therefore demands an intuition. It is a particular, if polemically striking, case of the great class of acts in which something appears as actual and indeed as given in person. Then comes the sentence that reverses the whole received account: categorial intuition marks the determination of all intuition by the categorial requirement of the phenomenon's givenness in person. Intuition results from givenness without exception. The oddly analogical status Husserl assigns categorial intuition confirms the dependence — the analogy holds with every other intuition with respect to a requirement of universal givenness of the phenomenon.

The Kantian limit is transgressed in several ways for a single motive: the phenomenological requirement that every phenomenon come into presence without remainder or reserve, the categorial included. That yields the last corollary of the first question, and the transition to §3: does the breakthrough of 1900–1901, now identified with the enlargement of intuition, bear on metaphysics, itself definable by the primacy of presence?

### 3 - The Accomplishment of Presence

If metaphysics is defined by the absolute and uninterrogated primacy of bringing entities into presence, and if that bringing is only ever accomplished by intuition, then metaphysics is never accomplished so perfectly as in the breakthrough of 1900–1901, which ends by opening the domain of universal intuition — an expression, as Husserl concedes, that will sound to many like wooden iron. Presence covers a domain without limit or remainder because evidential intuition becomes universal. Marion insists the audacity be measured: nothing makes an exception to intuition and so escapes being led back into the full light of presence — not the sensuous, not the essence, not the categorial form itself; nothing will remain invisible, since some mode of intuition hunts down each of these objects as so many modes of presence. Fewer than seven years after the breakthrough, the Göttingen lectures enumerate the various modes of authentic givenness, and the list runs so long that no residue of shadow or withdrawal survives — givenness of the *cogitatio*, of near recollection, of phenomenal unity in the flux, of that unity's variation, of the thing external to the flux, of recollection and imagination, of logical entities, of the universal, and even of logical absurdity and nonsense. Hence the forced conclusion: givenness is everywhere. Givenness floods every entity and every thought like a tidal wave, once the invisible par excellence — the intelligible as *logos* and *idea* — lets itself be staged by an intuition without limit in the Kantian sense, without condition in the Leibnizian sense, and without reserve. Without reserve means twice over: keeping nothing back in the invisibility of withdrawal, and keeping no restraint. Intuition inspects everything and respects nothing; it accomplishes the theoretical demand with a strange barbarism — the surge of presence. Marion then blocks the usual misreading of Husserl's increasingly programmatic tone: the programmes signal no indecision about aim or means. The aim is bringing into presence, the sole means is universalised intuition, and the work becomes programmatic only because the sheer scale of givenness delivers so much material to constitution that *Sinngebung* faces a nearly infinite task, whence the insistent and almost anxious appeals to teams, generations, regions. Phenomenology thus reaches, in a very sober sense, a kind of *hybris* before presence superabundantly given by intuition. Two texts confirm it: the Second Investigation's invocation of a divine intuition of everything, and a 1925 passage defining the method of intuitive generalisation — unveiling what is experienced as world only within narrow limits and with an ill-defined horizon, so that by proceeding from possible evidence to possible evidence we form an overall picture, an overall intuition of the world, filling the open indeterminate horizons with appropriate experiential possibilities. Marion presses the German: intuition brings into presence not merely objects in the world but the world itself; it does not merely fill the world but superimposes itself on it, covering total worldhood with its presentification. It covers the world only by totalising it in a *Gesamtanschauung* that shows the world's total figure by forming it (*bilden*) — it fills the world with presence only insofar as it constitutes itself as a world. It makes the world only by making itself world. The world becomes world through intuition, which must therefore be called universal in the literal sense. The metaphysics of presence is accomplished in absolute appearance: the world as overall intuition, overall intuition as worldmaking.

The breakthrough therefore opens only onto the metaphysical accomplishment of presence, and Marion rejoins Derrida's conclusion by another road. That road takes him through Nietzsche, and specifically through a late fragment titled, oddly, "How I recognise my own kind." Nietzsche there claims to disclose philosophy's hidden history, not unlike Husserl setting up phenomenology as modern philosophy's secret longing; in both cases the last metaphysician brings to evidence what metaphysics could not and dared not make appear. Nietzsche's terms are quasi-phenomenological: philosophy as he has understood and lived it is the deliberate search for even the most cursed and infamous sides of existence. Husserlian phenomenology likewise searches out the most hidden sides in order to submit to the evidence of presentifying intuition what no earlier philosophy could see face to face. Nietzsche's enterprise passes for negating, and in a first sense rightly: an experimental philosophy anticipates the possibilities of radical nihilism without stopping at a no. Correspondingly phenomenology, in order to see, must from 1900–1901 deny every presupposition, soon practising the reduction of thought to the evidence of the given in all its figures. In both cases plenary presence first requires destroying the shadows that bound and obscure it. And as the reduction — that other, first negative nihilism — issues in constitution and *Sinngebung*, so Nietzsche completes the no with a yes, the great Amen: a Dionysian saying-yes to the world as it is, without subtraction, exception, or selection; the eternal cycle; the highest state a philosopher can reach, standing Dionysian before existence, formulated as *amor fati*. Phenomenology's breakthrough wants, for its part, the universal intuition that brings the totality into presence in an overall intuition of the world. Husserlian evidence performs its own sunrise, leaving only the shortest shadow, eliminating every non-evidence of the world. To Nietzsche's noon answers phenomenology's intuition without remainder; to Zarathustra's monstrous unlimited Yes and Amen answers the originary givenness of the principle of principles. For the principle that fixes the highest state a phenomenologist can attain requires bearing that whatever offers itself originarily in intuition, in its bodily actuality as it were, be accepted simply as what it gives itself to be — but also only within the limits in which it gives itself there. Receiving what gives itself in the noon of intuition as it gives itself, neither more (Husserl) nor less (Nietzsche), on the sole source of right, intuition, which in both is without limit, remainder or withdrawal. A further fragment on the highest will to power thinks the being as *Schein*, and Marion sets beside it Husserl's claim that under the stroke of the reduction all ontologies fall, so that pure phenomenology seems to harbour all ontologies within itself. Entities find their source of right only by letting themselves be reduced to intuition, hence brought into presence without remainder or withdrawal, in the exact measure in which they give themselves — that is, by appearing as phenomenon. The ambivalence of *Schein*, mere seeming and appearing at once, carries both destruction and showing. Nothing is that does not appear; nothing appears that is not. Enlarged intuition and the unlimited yes each normalise the being of entities by securing their perfect presence. To accomplish the metaphysics of presence, Nietzsche can therefore recognise as his strangest and most unavoidable own kind — Husserl.

The answer to the first question stands, at least in outline: the breakthrough of the *Investigations* accomplishes the metaphysics of presence by enlarging intuition to the point where, echoing Nietzsche's great Amen, it brings the totality of entities into presence.

### 4 - A Misconstrual of Signification?

The second question follows: does that accomplishment of the primacy of intuition as universal presentification suffice to fold the breakthrough back, once and for all, into metaphysics understood as a metaphysics of presence? Marion notes he has pressed the primacy harder than Derrida did, and that it affiliates incipient phenomenology with Nietzsche ending — which makes the negative answer he will give harder-won. Two prior remarks. (a) Determining metaphysics by the primacy of presence, and claiming metaphysics as the unifying figure of all philosophy, comes from Heidegger, without whom these notions would be unusable; using them therefore commits one on the matter of their originator. Derrida consigns the breakthrough to metaphysics using Heideggerian notions without justifying the tactical borrowing or acknowledging their intrinsic pertinence. Should one speak of a metaphysics of presence at all? Of one in Husserl? Of one already in the *Investigations*? These preliminary questions go unasked. They would matter less if Derrida took over the whole Heideggerian strategy, but he thinks too originally to do so without reserve: he accepts the notion of a metaphysics of presence while radically rejecting Heidegger's understanding of the *Investigations*, finding in them not the first signs of a new beginning but the plain accomplishment of metaphysics as the unveiling of entities in presence. Marion states the paradox without pretending to resolve it: how can one read the *Investigations* by playing one Heideggerian thesis, the metaphysics of presence, against another, categorial intuition of being as the sketch of a new beginning? He will let the paradox light his own path — and in fact the rest of the book takes both Heideggerian theses apart in turn. (b) Derrida's reading is less simple than that. The *Investigations* fall back into metaphysics only because they had first reached the extreme edge of the field of presence; they return because they came close to transgressing it, since in one and the same movement Husserl describes and effaces the emancipation of discourse as non-knowledge. What that names is the status of signification, valid without confirmation by an intuition and hence without the ground of a bringing into presence. Husserl was probably the first — a point Derrida does not stress — to understand that it belongs to the original structure of expression to be able to dispense with the full presence of the intuited object, and that the absence of intuition is not merely tolerated by discourse but required by the structure of signification in general. The step forward frees signification from presence; the step back, taken within the *Investigations* themselves, restores an obligatory intuitive confirmation and censors signification's autonomy. To reconstruct that simultaneous advance and retreat, Derrida must introduce two decisions. First, he reduces the determination of signification to that of the sign or of discourse — an equivalence that governs his reading far more deeply than the foregrounded distinction between meaningful sign and mere indication, since before deciding whether indication is the truth of the meaningful sign or the reverse, one must justify that the essence of signification is played out entirely in the figures of the sign. Obvious for Derrida; not for Husserl. Second, he must suppose that intuition governs the metaphysics of presence through and through, so that intuition alone accomplishes the bringing into presence. Marion's question, which opens the rest of the chapter: is intuition, for Husserl, the last word on presence?

Those two decisions carry Derrida's whole reading. Marion sets the first aside, without concealing that it remains questionable, since it determines Derrida's own itinerary rather than the assignment of the Husserlian breakthrough — the parenthetical joke about "propriety" sounding here like impropriety itself registers that Derrida's path is precisely one that disowns having a proper starting point. The second decision generates a real difficulty for locating signification within phenomenology at all. What status can Husserl grant signification when he establishes it apart from any confirming intuition, while intuition, enlarged to an overall intuition, becomes universal bringing-into-presence? If signification signifies without intuition, which is Derrida's version of the breakthrough, and if presence gives itself universally through intuition, which is Marion's reading of Husserl, what remainder of presence, what mode of being, what place is left for signification? The step back would then follow almost necessarily from an aporia Derrida makes unavoidable and Heidegger does not even suspect. Marion sharpens it into a question put to the *Investigations*: does the enlargement of intuition to the dimensions of a world contradict the ideality of signification? What presence can intentional and significative inexistence still receive once intuition exhausts all presence? Signification dispenses with presence, hence with being, and so could dispense with intuition only by dispensing with itself. Derrida alone leads us there — but answers too elegantly and too quickly. On his answer Husserl encounters *différance*, always older than presence, and refuses its consequences as one refuses a jump; the intuitionist imperative, metaphysics' genuine categorical imperative, holds the breakthrough at the threshold, and the originality of meaning-to-say is limited by the *telos* of vision, so that the difference separating intention from intuition, radical as it is, is nonetheless provisional. Marion asks whether self-contradiction and half-conscious repentance are the only available figure. They are — but only if intuition is the last word on presence, in which case signification must either turn into *différance* or fall back under the intuitionist yoke. That hypothesis, which Derrida presupposes as the horizon of the aporia he builds, is exactly what characterises metaphysics as metaphysics of presence: to be is to be present, and presence comes down to intuition. Two interpretive routes remain. Either Husserl contradicts himself, conceding that the metaphysical hypothesis of presence-by-intuition is insuperable. Or Husserl confronts the status of signification only because he has already transgressed the primacy of intuition where presence is concerned — the more decisively in that he frees signification from intuition only after carrying out intuition's most metaphysical enlargement. On that route the irreducibility of signification to intuition does not contradict the universality of the intuitive mode of presence but attests, by transgressing it, that intuition, however universal, is not the ultimate name of presence. Signification could be without being seen. The question then becomes whether being can manifest itself, already in the *Investigations*, otherwise than by intuition, on a mode attested above all in signification. Since no one has taken this route, not even the one who makes it possible *a contrario*, Marion takes it.

### 5 - Presence without Intuition

However universal intuition may be, it admits an exteriority, made all the more irreducible by a phenomenologically irreducible difference that leads, through a total separation, to the opposition between intuition and signification. Among the oppositions the *Investigations* reach, this one comes first: sensuous against categorial intuition, adequate against inadequate, individual against general all concern figures of intuition alone and, far from putting signification's isolation in question, presuppose and exhibit it. Signification's exterritoriality with respect to intuition is marked at the very end, just where categorial intuition has been won — for categorial intuition, extending to the most extreme significations, does not bring signification back under the intuitionist yoke but underscores that it only ever fills and covers significations by granting them the a priori right to delimit the spaces to be saturated with presence. The phenomenological possibilities of error, disappointment, adequate or partial fulfilment all require that signification precede intuition and that even when filled with an intuitive given it first receive from that given a confirmation of itself, hence of its sense. Marion then blocks a natural misreading of the convenient formula i + s = 1 with its limit cases: signification does not vary inversely with intuition. Intuition can give only what it has, and can give it only to what it is not — the always prior signification. The point is that the two are not commensurable quantities on one scale, which is why an increase in intuition cannot encroach on signification's domain. The irreducibility is asserted from the First Investigation onward, which announces important differences concerning the possible relations between signification and the intuition that illustrates and perhaps renders it evident, and which recognises an act of signification supported by the intuitive content of the word-representation yet essentially different from the intuitive intention directed at the word itself. From beginning to end signification is opposed irreducibly to intuition and thereby determines itself, by that negation, as intuition's other. How was that decision thinkable?

Because an act of signification is constituted without the least fulfilling or illustrating intuition having to intervene, signification imposes itself in an autonomy for which intuition offers only an eventual complement. In expression or perception signification is always already accomplished; contrary to natural conviction, intuition either fails entirely or falls partly short, and in any case comes as a surplus. Husserl's formulation is unambiguous: every statement, whether or not it exercises a knowledge-function, has its intention and constitutes in that intention, as its specific character of unity, the signification. Intention depends so little on intuition for its accomplishment that it precedes fulfilment; it dispenses with intuition and can therefore make its addition eventually possible. The clearest confirmation of the inadequacy of intuitive presentation even in coherent significations comes from geometry: no mathematical ideality can find adequate fulfilment in actually lived space. Inadequacy — the overflow of intuition by intention, signification's overhang — is not an exception but an absolute rule. Straight line, the curve of an equation, even triangle will never meet adequate fulfilment in lived intuitions; the equation constantly finds fulfilling intuitions, but each time only for particular values, never for its universal essence as such; and no empirical drawing will ever intuitively fill the signification of a figure, while imaginative presentification soon fails outright, as with the chiliagon Descartes can no longer imagine and can only understand. Mathematical intelligence is characterised precisely by its capacity to think significations irreducible to any intuition. The paradox deepens: these idealities are not merely thinkable as significations exceeding all intuitive fulfilment, but thinkable *only* without adequate intuition, and thinkable then in full evidence. Marion quotes the passage where Husserl invokes evidence as the last authority in all questions of knowledge and repeats four times that he *sees evidently* — that in reiterated acts he means identically the same concept or proposition; that with the proposition "π is a transcendental number" he has before his eyes nothing less than anyone's individual lived experience; that the reflective formulation has for its object what constitutes the signification of the direct one; and that what he means in the proposition, or grasps as its signification when he hears it, is identically what it is whether or not he thinks, whether or not he is, whether or not acts or persons exist to think it. Phenomenological evidence is accomplished here without fulfilling intuition and without any lived experience of intuition, because the ideality of the object — in fact and by right the ideality of every signification — depends neither on an intuition nor on an intuiting agent. From the First Investigation signification wins a strictly autonomous evidence. Marion then turns the tables: Husserl's own stigmatising of the confusion between signification and fulfilling intuition in Erdmann and Sigwart anticipates, more summarily, the very diagnosis Derrida believes he can bring against Husserl — that the originality of meaning-to-say is limited by the *telos* of vision. Husserl criticises as non-phenomenological the confusion Derrida attributes to him, in an anachronism as exemplary as it is ideal.

Signification's autonomy can even be confirmed from the very text Derrida invokes to denounce the subtle displacement by which, for Husserl, true and authentic meaning-to-say would be the meaning-to-say-truly. The passage is §11 of the First Investigation, tellingly titled "The ideal distinctions: first between expression and signification as ideal unities." Marion's claim is that the cited sequence contradicts the ideal status it is supposed to illustrate — namely: if possibility or truth is lacking, the intention of the statement is accomplished only symbolically, since it cannot then draw from intuition and the categorial functions founded on it the fullness constituting its knowledge-value, so that it lacks, *as one is accustomed to say*, the true or authentic *Bedeutung*. Derrida concludes at once that for Husserl signification takes its authentic truth from intuitive fullness serving as ground. Marion's four counter-points are textual and decisive. (a) The thesis stated is not the phenomenologist's but natural consciousness's, explicitly flagged by the central interpolation "as one is accustomed to say." (b) The terms Derrida singles out — true, authentic, symbolic, possibility, truth — appear only in quotation marks; they are used improperly, by those who do not follow the ideal distinctions, and Husserl's own thesis will be the refusal to deny a signification its truth on the grounds that it lacks fulfilling intuition. (c) The continuation, omitted from Derrida's citation, does exactly that: it announces the later treatment of the difference between intentional and fulfilling signification, a cross-reference to the Sixth Investigation's first chapter, and it then dogmatically states Husserl's own thesis, that it is *certain* that every statement, whether or not it exercises a knowledge-function, has its intention and constitutes in that intention the signification. Unless Husserl contradicts himself within a few lines, the earlier opinion in deliberately vague vocabulary must be the error he criticises. (d) The passage is framed by formulations that leave no doubt: a few lines below, the ideal unity grasped with evidence, not to be confused with the real judgement; a few lines above, the ideal content, the signification of the statement as unity in multiplicity, with the decisive addition that we do not arbitrarily place it in statements but *find* it there. If there is a displacement in §11, it is not subtle but rather crude, as befits a position Husserl explicitly rejects.

Since the misreading cannot be inadvertence, Marion locates its source in a prior interpretation of a genuinely difficult text: §26 of the First Investigation, on essentially occasional and objective expressions. Husserl there holds that any expression containing a personal pronoun or demonstrative ceases to mean a signification; the meaningful sign is replaced by a non-meaningful one whose indicative function, a universally efficient indication, indicates an infinity of possible significations precisely because it signifies none of them identically. Here signification fails the sign. Husserl adds the sequence Derrida privileges: the word *I* names a different person case by case, and does so by means of an ever new *Bedeutung*. Where does that ever-surging signification come from, never given in the statement? Husserl's answer, cited at once by Derrida: what constitutes its *Bedeutung* each time can be drawn only from living speech and the intuitive circumstances belonging to it; reading the word without knowing who wrote it, we have a word not devoid of *Bedeutung* but at least foreign to its normal one. Signification does indeed depend on intuition here, and Derrida concludes that Husserl's premises should have authorised the exact opposite. Marion's objection has three parts. (a) The failure of signification and the recourse to intuition concern a very particular case, the indeterminacy of the This and the I that Hegel had already pointed out. Such expressions are characterised not by a reduction of signification to intuition but by the absolute and radical absence of signification itself — an absence Husserl calls essential, making it not a privileged case of all expression, as the generalisation supposes, but an exception to the normal regime, indeed a genuine non-expression. Since the essence of expression lies exclusively in signification, essentially occasional expressions must be understood as essentially non-expressive, and so cannot bear on the doctrine of expression at all. (b) It is unsurprising that *I* has no signification of its own, since in the *Investigations* the I, reduced to a mere complex of lived experiences, has no ideal signification as a transcendental I, and of the I as meeting point of accomplished acts there is, in Husserl's words, nothing to notice. Against the alleged paradox Marion sets another: Husserl's premises about the I should force us to recognise the essentially non-expressive character of the occasional expressions in which it figures. (c) The failure of the I's signification is not Husserl's last word in 1901. The addendum to §5 of the Sixth Investigation explicitly resumes §26 and tries to reintroduce signification into indication at least partly. It opposes the hearer of *I*, who receives only a universal indeterminate thought and depends on intuition to reconstitute the signification, to the speaker, who receives the indication as given and possesses from the outset the *indicated* signification: not the indication of a non-given signification but the givenness of a signification on the mode of indication alone. Indication does not always exclude signification, in the guise of the indicated. Husserl then sketches a phenomenology of indicated intention in mathematics and in the deictic This. Marion adds a further note: the mathematical case, where the letters of a formula function as indications referring within the global signification to categorial elements that are significant because given, suggests that the missing *Gestaltqualität* of indication is destined to be at least tangentially reduced, so that indication is not an insuperable structure exclusive of all signification. Even at the point of the oldest and most formidable objection, Husserl warns before conceding anything that this is in no case a word without signification.

Marion holds to a single thesis without claiming to exhaust the arguments: in the *Logical Investigations*, signification gives itself evidently without depending on fulfilling intuition. Even without intuition, we find it there, in the First Investigation's phrase. It owes nothing either to the phonic complex, in the Sixth Investigation's phrase, since signification cannot hang in the air, and the sign of which we say it is the signification is wholly indifferent to what it signifies. So signification, lacking intuition, does not thereby disseminate at the anomic rhythm of the signifier's differences. It offers a content — ideal content, intentional sense, theoretical or logical content — and in every case the essence of signification lies not in the lived experience that confers it but in its content, which presents an identical intentional unity. Marion's wordplay on *tenir* is doing real work here: signification has a content, holds it from itself alone, and holds that content as its own holding only insofar as it holds *to* it, holding in and to itself alone. Self-maintenance, without intuition, without indication, without an act of utterance, suffices for signification to propose itself in presence. Two questions close the section, the second reframing the first. Is it legitimate to speak of presence here, when no presentation institutes it? Or rather: what *sui generis* mode of presence unfolds when signification, from itself and by itself alone, presents itself?

### 6 - The Evidence of Givenness

The mode of presence remains undetermined, which is why "presence" may not name it exactly. Marion assembles the Sixth Investigation's statements that every signification can be thought completely without the least correlative intuition; that the act in which signification resides is not perception, at least not perception alone; that signifying is not accomplished first by means of intuition, since on the contrary the signifying intention is given first and given for itself, and only then does the corresponding intuition intervene. Signification's priority, secured by its independence, alone explains the First Investigation's last word: there are innumerable significations that will never reach expression, whatever the enlargement of intuition. The signification we find there constitutes itself, advancing without condition, and although untouched by intuition it accedes to the last authority in questions of knowledge, evidence. The decisive point: signification, as an effective though non-real object, ultimately appears in full evidence, as an ideal unity evidently grasped, standing before us with evidence as one and the same object. Evidence can phenomenologically permit itself anything, as last authority, and so permit anything, as the universal stage-manager of the visible. It permits itself to have significations necessarily exist, that is, present a unitary sense. The autonomy in which unity of sense culminates rests on its sufficiency for entering visibility; and since phenomenologically being reduces to evidence, signification exists insofar as it signifies itself — significations actually being, being *as* significations. Husserl therefore reaches being before the Sixth Investigation and before categorial intuition, as soon as the Fourth distinguishes independent significations in their specificity: by virtue of its independence, the signification itself in person exists. Phenomenological evidencing reaches being as existence as soon as it reaches signification as independent, precisely because that independence qualifies signification as an effective entity. Signification is enlarged not only to sense offered without intuition but to its acceptance as having the standing of an entity: sense as such has the validity of an entity, which consecrates signification's absolute independence from every presupposition — above all from intuition. Marion's closing question turns the whole doctrine of intuition inside out: must intuition itself be held to be a presupposition to be reduced, so that the terminal given of signification may appear?

The question opens onto the intelligence of the breakthrough. If it is settled that the breakthrough accomplishes the universal enlargement of intuition, one must also envisage that signification too — perhaps signification above all — is enlarged to the point of actually existing as a strictly autonomous entity. Everything happens as if intuition freed itself from sensibility only in order, by a paradox still to be considered, to let signification free itself in turn from intuition. Natural consciousness resists this second enlargement as much as the first, but a text compels the surrender: the domain of signification is far more extensive than that of intuition. The difficulty of such a thought results not from a lack of evidence but from an excess of it. First the excess of intuition's evidence over the limits of sensibility, up to an overall intuition. Then the excess of signification, which exists beyond the intuition it essentially dispenses with — the bill drawn on intuition is honoured irregularly by signification all the more since signification is not in principle obliged to draw it. Then, above all, the excess of the second excess over the first: incomprehensibly, signification oversteps by far a field that was already overall. How does one transgress a field already enlarged and already total? How can the enlargement of intuition and the enlargement of signification fail to contradict each other? The tension between the purity of formalism and the radicality of intuitionism becomes insuperable once intuition, by right if not in fact, completes metaphysics by tolerating no remainder — no fringe left over on which to mark signification's vastness. Where would that domain extend if intuition in advance covers and uncovers everything, the categorial included, in a single evidence? Evidence would then seem to well up from two sources, intuition and signification, autonomous to the point of competing for primacy. Husserl's own last self-interpretation lends itself to the contradiction by its very indeterminacy: what was decisively new in the *Investigations* lay not in the merely ontological researches but in the subjectively oriented ones, above all the Fifth and Sixth, where the *cogitata qua cogitata* first received their right and came to govern the whole method of intentional analysis, and where evidence — that rigid logical idol — was first turned into a problem, freed from the pre-eminence of scientific evidence and enlarged into universal original self-givenness. Marion grants both halves and then presses the ambiguity: must the ultimate enlargement of evidence be understood as the enlargement of intuition to the categorial, as the mention of the Fifth and Sixth Investigations suggests, or as the enlargement of the domain of signification, far vaster than intuition's? The enlargement seems so broad — an unconditional amnesty freeing indiscriminately everything metaphysics had shackled — that it does not want to know what it frees, or at least does not identify or declare it. To what exactly does the enlargement give free rein? Mentioning a universal *Selbstgebung* does not answer; the indeterminacy of its universality only underscores the unresolved ambiguity of evidence. Would the breakthrough remain obscure by excess of evidence? Would phenomenology, far from resolving the conflict of formalism and intuitionism, merely exacerbate their irreducibility by confounding them in an obscure evidence, universal but undecided?

The same late self-interpretation that confirms the aporia also opens the way out. A note in the *Crisis* dates the *Durchbruch* to around 1898 and identifies it as the first breakthrough of the universal correlational a priori of the object of experience and the modes of givenness — a discovery that struck Husserl so deeply that his life's work was thereafter dominated by the task of elaborating it. The body of the paragraph confirms it: never before that breakthrough had the correlation of the world and its subjective modes of givenness provoked philosophical astonishment. The *Investigations* accomplish their breakthrough neither by enlarging intuition nor by recognising signification's autonomy but by being astonished, as at a marvel of marvels, at a correlation. Which one? Not the noema/noesis correlation dominant in the *Ideas*, nor the intuition/intention correlation prevailing in the *Investigations*; both are made possible by a more essential relation that Husserl belatedly discovered had governed the breakthrough from the start — the correlation between appearing and what appears as such. Appearing no longer counts as a datum for a conscious subject alone but as the givenness of what thereby appears. The appearing gives what appears. Or: what appears, nothing less than an effective entity, appears in person in the appearance because, by an essential necessity, it gives itself there. Phenomenology begins in 1900–1901 because for the first time thought sees what appears appear in the appearance; and it manages this only by conceiving appearing itself no longer as a datum of consciousness but as the givenness to consciousness, even by consciousness, of the thing itself, given on the mode of appearing and in all its dimensions — intuition, intention, and their variations. Whatever its concrete or abstract, real or ideal sense, an entity has its own modes of self-givenness in person. So the phenomenological breakthrough consists neither in the enlargement of intuition nor in the autonomy of signification but in the sole unconditioned primacy of the phenomenon's givenness. Intuition and intention, however liberated, are liberated only by the givenness they illustrate, or rather that never stops illuminating them; they deliver only its modes. They would give nothing, and would not themselves need to be given, if everything did not have, by the principle of correlation, to be given in order to appear. Givenness precedes intuition and intention because these have sense only for and through an appearance, which counts as the appearing of something appearing only by virtue of correlation, hence of givenness. Seeming no longer falls under mere semblance, since it comes about as an advent to appearing, from something that appears. That appearing thing, correlated to its appearance by appearing itself, does not deceive in the appearance, however thin — partial intuition, empty intention — because it gives itself there. Only givenness charges the appearance with the seriousness of an appearance: never an appearing without something appearing, never something appearing without an appearance. Marion then marshals the confirmations. In 1907: absolute givenness is the last word; and reciprocally, seeing and grasping what is given in person, where this is actual seeing and self-givenness in the strictest sense and not some other givenness aiming at a non-given, is the last word and what is absolutely self-understood; the reduction would exercise no primacy if it did not lead the phenomenon back to its last givenness, and the givenness of a reduced phenomenon in general is absolute and indubitable. In 1913 the principle of principles privileges intuition only insofar as it first interprets it as originarily giving, and admits a formulation directly in terms of givenness — that every theory could draw its truth only from originary *Gegebenheiten*, a word Marion pointedly notes may be read as "data" or as "givennesses." And already in 1900–1901 givenness preceded both eventual intuition and signification, since for consciousness the given remains essentially the same whether the represented object exists or is fabricated and even absurd. Both must be reinterpreted as two modes of the single originary givenness.

Intuition opens to its enlargement only insofar as it first gives itself as a mode of givenness. Marion's proof text is grammatical: when a signifying intention is fulfilled on the basis of a corresponding intuition — when the expression in the actual operation of naming is related to the *given* object — the object is then constituted as "given" in certain acts and is indeed given to us in them, on the same mode on which it is meant by the signification. The subtle play of quotation marks alone marks the gap between two givennesses, as discreetly as the dative character of originary phenomenality requires. The object at once meant and "given," the signified objectness "given" to us in evident knowledge, remains given in the fulfilling intuition because it was already given in the signification. The enlargement of intuition therefore does not contradict signification's autonomy but implies it: in both cases one and the same originary givenness is at stake, and it can increase one of its modes only by increasing the other, which conditions the first. Intuition can extend only by extending its fulfilment, hence by depending on the signified spaces to be filled. If intuition is to give, significations must already be disengaged, hence already given in full autonomy without intuition — as Husserl says, the signifying intention is given first and given for itself, and only afterward does the corresponding intuition come in addition; and: there are, then, significations. Nothing precedes givenness, which modulates itself in every mode of the phenomenon whatever. Wider than intuition, more autonomous than signification, givenness gives the phenomenon through both, because through and through it falls to givenness to deal out the thing in person. In their last advance the *Investigations* set self-presentation and self-appearing of the object as equivalent to its self-givenness, where something appears as actual and as given in person; and the phrase *aktuelles Gegebensein* is to be taken strictly — what is accomplished, as bringing into presence on the metaphysically insuperable mode of act, is being-given. To the question about a mode of presence irreducible to intuition, and about whether "presence" is even the right word, Marion can now sketch an answer: Husserl determines presence by going back beneath intuition to attribute it to signification only because he oversteps both in favour of givenness. Whatever shows itself as given appears, as already given, and because it is given to appear, it *is*. To be — to be in presence, since in metaphysics one stands for the other — comes down to the givenness that gives the given to appear. Two qualifications follow. (a) This is indeed a last figure of the metaphysics of presence, and Derrida's conclusion is ratified; but presence is not thereby reduced to intuition at signification's expense, since presence triumphs in signification as much as in intuition, and the *Investigations* fall under metaphysics in their totality. Derrida's reading is paradoxically not radical enough, since by supposing signification to break with presence he exempts it from metaphysics, and he does so because his understanding of presence is too narrow to reach its properly Husserlian deepening as givenness — though without his reading that deepening would have remained inaccessible. Confirm Derrida's conclusion, contest his principal argument. (b) But if presence culminates in a givenness where the given appears, and if the metaphysics of presence is thereby completed without remainder in the *Investigations*, how could Heidegger have recognised there his own point of departure, at Marburg and again in his last seminar in 1973? Did he miss givenness as the completion of the primacy of presence? Did he take for a rupture with metaphysics what, with Derrida, seems its unreserved accomplishment?

### 7 - Givenness as Question

Heidegger twice acknowledged in the breakthrough the necessary if insufficient condition of the new beginning: in the summer semester course of 1925, where what is decisive in the discovery of categorial intuition is that it gives a ground; and in the 1973 Zähringen seminar, where Husserl's *tour de force* at last gave him a ground. It seems obvious to Heidegger and his interpreters alike that categorial intuition, by fixing being at work in the expression, forms the slender but stubborn hyphen between the last metaphysician and the first thinker. If so, Derrida's critique wins even against Heidegger: presence, reassured by the primacy of intuition, would uncover the being proper to every entity and thereby cover over *différance*. But the received interpretation, however accurate to the letter, may hide the essential. The essential does not lie in an intuition of categorial being, which neither text ever formulates in those terms. Categorial being is never said there to be intuited or intuitable, but only, and more radically, *given* according to categorial intuition. In 1973 categorial intuition is thought explicitly by analogy with sensuous intuition, an analogy made unavoidable by the fact that being gives itself as well as the sensuous does: for Husserl the categorial is given just as much as the sensuous, so there is categorial intuition, and since categorial intuition is like sensuous intuition in being giving, Husserl arrives at it along the path of analogy. Marion reconstructs the order: givenness oversteps the bounds of sensuous intuition, so by analogy a non-sensuous giving intuition must be admitted. The decision leading to categorial intuition therefore does not arise from intuition but from the excess of givenness over the sensuous. If intuition becomes categorial, that is because being gives itself — not the reverse. Categorial intuition does not give being; being compels the admission of something like categorial intuition as a consequence of its own givenness. The 1973 passage confirms it, and Marion now quotes the sentence he had truncated in §1: the point Husserl does not cross is that, having virtually obtained being as given, he does not interrogate further. The decisive step of the *Investigations* reaches being as given; Husserl gets there, at least virtually. Where is the weakness? In contenting himself, too cheaply, with naming that givenness instead of thinking it — naming it by the finally very crude analogy of a categorial intuition, which by a new or rather very old syntagm conceals the abyss of the givenness of being. Marion risks the formulation: without saying so, Heidegger opposes categorial intuition to givenness. The breakthrough goes as far as the givenness of being but recoils before its abyss, closing it over with the uninterrogated because problematic, but structurally traditional, concept of categorial intuition. If categorial intuition results from givenness rather than provoking it, then thought that rests content with it flees the enigma of givenness. Heidegger, unlike Husserl, will set himself to think givenness, and will therefore destroy categorial intuition — which in fact he never mentions again, in *Sein und Zeit* or after.

The 1925 course confirms the paradoxical conclusion. Analysed as one of the fundamental discoveries of phenomenology's fundamental book, categorial intuition is thought from the start as an intuition, hence as the plain grasp of what is given bodily as it shows itself, with the proviso that bodily presence is a distinguished mode of an entity's self-givenness. It imposes itself in addition to sensuous intuition as soon as one observes that categories like "totality," "and," "but" in fact attain originary self-givenness on the same footing as significations fulfilled by sensuous intuition. Categorial intuition can be admitted only in answer to a categorial givenness, hence only by being thought first as giving. Categorial acts, considered in their character as giving acts, are intuitions: they give objectness; the categorial act brings the entity in this new objectness to givenness, precisely because that objectness is defined by self-givenness, as a self-giving objectness. Never, already in 1925, is categorial intuition directly related to entities, still less to being, as any sort of intuition of being; always it mediates its relation to entities through givenness, which originarily determines both. Here too the breakthrough consists not in the enlargement of intuition alone but in the enlargement of the concept of reality or objectness to the dimensions of givenness: on the path of understanding what presents itself in categorial intuition one can learn to see that an entity's objectivity is by no means exhausted in reality in the narrow sense, that objectivity in the widest sense is much richer than a thing's reality, and indeed that a thing's reality in its structuration becomes comprehensible only from the total objectivity of the entity plainly experienced. What is at stake in the *Investigations*, especially the Sixth, lies less in categorial intuition than in what it indicates without itself realising — the enlargement of presence, understood as objectivity, on the excessive measure of givenness. Enlargement must be understood not only as an extension of intuition and signification by givenness but above all as the requirement of a freeing of the ground. Givenness enlarges presence by freeing it from all limits of the faculties, to the point of letting entities play freely — possibly entities in their being. Only an enlargement that liberates in this way could claim to overstep the metaphysics of presence, which in fact never stops restricting the present and holding back its givenness.

Heidegger's privileging of givenness over categorial intuition shields his hermeneutic of the *Investigations* from Derrida's objection, and shields Husserl too, at least to a degree Marion says he will merely indicate — the aside about having already advanced too far in open ground registers that the claim outruns the consensus. Heidegger does Husserl justice on two decisive points. (a) Husserl never declares being or entities intuited by any intuition of being. What he says instead is that the evidence of the given object on the mode of the intended implies that here being in the sense of truth is realised, that truth in person is here given, directly to be seen and grasped, so that even the little word *is* is itself given, or at least presumed given, in fulfilment; and that each correlate of representation, even non-sensuous, requires that its actual "being-given" or its appearing as "given" be named. The completed appearance gives what appears in it in person; expression claims to give even the copula and the positing; so the *is* must give itself in person, since it appears as such. Categorial intuition neither gives nor even sees the *is*: it marks — the paradox is exact — the in fact anonymous givenness of the *is*. Categorial intuition functions as an indication of a givenness it does not itself perform. (b) Husserl always infers categorial intuition from sensuous intuition by analogy, and does so in order to respect the advances of givenness: if we consider being as predicative being, some state of affairs must be given to us, and naturally by means of an act that gives it — the analogue of ordinary sensuous intuition. Categorial intuition remains a need *of* givenness, far from givenness needing it in order to accomplish itself as a givenness of the *is*, hence of the entity in its entity-ness. Categorial intuition only allows the measure — henceforth measureless — of givenness to be taken. It marks the opened abyss without covering it, at least for Heidegger, if not for Husserl. Marion's closing reversal is sharp: before the fascination of superabundant unconditioned presence, the more sober of the two is not the one expected. Husserl, dazzled by limitless givenness, seems not to notice the strangeness of such excess and merely administers it without interrogating it — unless the dazzlement betrays, by covering it, a fright before the enlargement of presence by givenness.

Here surges the question Husserl could not answer, because he perhaps never heard it as a genuine question: what then gives itself? Not merely "what is that which gives itself?" but, more essentially, what does *giving* mean; what is at play in the fact that everything is given; how are we to think that whatever is, is only insofar as it is given? Husserl, as though submerged by the imperative — at once threatening and jubilant — of managing the superabundance of givens in presence, at no point in the *Investigations* asks after the status, the reach, or even the identity of that givenness. His silence amounts to conceding Derrida's thesis: leaving uninterrogated the givenness whose enlargement he has nevertheless carried out, Husserl does not free it from the bolt of presence and so keeps it in metaphysical detention. Heidegger, seeing at once with extraordinary lucidity that the breakthrough consists wholly in the enlargement of givenness beyond sensuous intuition, assumes the Husserlian inheritance exactly by bearing his whole interrogation on what such givenness means, and by guarding against reducing it too soon to presence, even under the figure of categorial intuition. What must be understood, far beyond, is how and why whatever is, is only as given: to be comes down to being given, by a givenness accomplished only in the phenomenon's play with itself, of appearing with what appears. Two prerequisites follow. First, a step back before givenness itself, in order to come to regard it as worthy of question instead of forgetting it by dwelling in its evidence. Second, the recognition that in universal givenness what is at issue is the being of what appears, in the very measure in which it gives itself. Restated as questions: does a phenomenological reduction accomplish the step back that allows givenness to be considered as such; and does the breakthrough of givenness not lead phenomenology at once and inevitably toward the question of being? The two amount to one demand, and it sets the agenda of chapter II: does the reduction lead phenomenology to see being as a phenomenon?

## II. The Entity and the Phenomenon

### 1 - Putting Ontology Out of Play

Marion opens with two flatly opposed declarations. Heidegger in 1925: there is no ontology alongside a phenomenology; scientific ontology is nothing other than phenomenology. Husserl in 1912: in itself, ontology is not phenomenology. The opposition must not be blunted, and the reasons are cumulative. Other texts confirm it; it made Heidegger's divergence from Husserl possible and necessary well before 1927; it underlies, at least apparently, the quarrels that divided the post-Husserlian schools. But what matters most is that with the relation between phenomenology and ontology it puts in play the turn by which the century's principal — arguably its only — attempt at a radical grounding of philosophy both abandons and recovers the question of being. Marion's programme for the chapter is stated as a sequence of three definitions: phenomenology, then phenomenon, then entity, each tested for whether and how far it reaches an understanding of being or depends on one. The order matters, because the third definition is where the two thinkers finally part: Heidegger will insist that the title "phenomenon" says nothing about the being of what is encountered but characterises only the mode of access to it.

Phenomenology's overstepping of ontology follows from its terminal determination as transcendental idealism — the *Cartesian Meditations* make phenomenology *ipso facto* a transcendental idealism, and the afterword to the *Ideas* insists that transcendental-phenomenological idealism is no particular thesis or theory but transcendental phenomenology itself as concrete science, universal idealism even where the word idealism is never used. Marion glosses the two adjectives strictly: universal and transcendental mean non-subjective because non-empirical. Idealism can call itself universal (*katholou*) and transcendental (unhypothetical, *anhypotheton*) only insofar as, prior to any other condition, it determines everything that presents itself to be seen — idealism, because only the *idea* can be seen, and to be seen means to be undergone as a lived experience. Hence Husserl can say retrospectively that the *Investigations* were purely phenomenological just insofar as their themes were not ontological. Why should phenomenology begin authentically where ontological themes cease? The last section of *Ideas I* answers: the connections between constitutive phenomenologies and the corresponding formal and material ontologies do not mean the former ground the latter, and the phenomenologist passes no ontological judgement when he recognises in an ontological concept the index of a constitutive eidetic connection. Marion presses the concession: an ontological concept's right and validity can indeed be recognised phenomenologically, but in that very measure they belong to phenomenology and never redound to ontology. The encounter stays a crossing and never becomes an alliance. An a priori ontology of the real world is unavoidable and yet one-sided and finally unphilosophical, because the factual world remains an ontic fact devoid of philosophical — that is, transcendental — intelligibility. Ontology tries, and must try, to bring the world's a priori to light, and cannot in principle succeed, since it does not reach full intelligibility of the world or of its fact. That Husserl takes "ontology" in a narrow sense does not weaken the objection: the sense will not widen later, and the demand for phenomenological intelligibility of whatever ontology claims to treat will press all the harder on any subsequent attempt to restore classical ontology — even by destroying it. Marion is already aiming that remark at *Sein und Zeit*. The 1907 Göttingen lectures take the step, or rather indicate the step to be taken beyond ontology. What universal and transcendental idealism reproaches classical ontology with is not that it tries to think the being of the world but that it does not think it radically, that is, does not think it in its origin: before administering being under the a priori one must first secure it, and certainty about being is obtained only by phenomenology, which assigns itself absolute givenness under intuition's gaze as its sole object. Only the *epochē* reaches entities as such, that is, as absolutely given in and to a transcendental gaze; the demand for entities becomes a special case of the search for absolute givenness. That givenness does not take over ontology's field at a more secure level but powerfully displaces it, since some entities pass through the reduction into absolute givenness and others do not, while many immanent absolute givennesses overstep what ontology recognised as entities. Not only do all ontologies fall under the reduction and vanish there; the *epochē* means putting belief in being out of operation. Marion measures the destitution exactly: what Husserl calls the setting-out-of-performance of belief in being forbids not only the old governance of the world by ontology and any restoration of ontology out of absolute givenness, but belief in being itself. Under the reduction — which never stops being performed and deepened — it is neither useful nor permitted to appeal to being or to grant it the least theoretical trust. Being is no longer at issue, for three reasons Marion lists: being plays no role in making absolute givenness possible; entities either vanish or reduce to that same givenness as lived experiences and essences; and the world henceforth unfolds as world of experience, since only the experience the reduction opens makes a world. If one still wants to say that phenomenology seems to harbour all ontologies within it, that restoration stays legitimate only so long as one sees that everything the sciences of entities supply resolves into the phenomenological — an image, Husserl adds, that must not be misunderstood and whose precise sense remains to be established. Ontologies no more rise to phenomenology through the reduction than entities coincide, within it, with the immanent noematic given. Another world appears, absolutely other, with no vestige or restoration of the old. In itself ontology is not phenomenology, and no less in itself phenomenology is not, need not be, and could not be ontology.

### 2 - The Method of Ontology

Contesting that prohibition was Heidegger's original design and thereby his first turn — and contesting always means attesting by subversion, hence conceding. Even in its parricide, Heidegger's first turn stays faithfully occupied with the last word of the *Ideas*: the articulation of phenomenology and ontology. In 1927 §7 of *Sein und Zeit* sets a reciprocal relation: ontology is possible only as phenomenology, yet phenomenology does not include ontology as one of its regions or possibilities, since taken at the level of the matter itself phenomenology is the science of the being of entities. The summer semester course of the same year lets the equivalence run at full strength: phenomenology names the method of ontology, that is, of scientific philosophy, and correctly understood it is the concept of a method. Marion insists that three terms are being articulated here and not two — phenomenology, ontology, philosophy. At the ground it is philosophy that is at stake, seeking its own scientificity in a sense parallel to Husserl's rigour; that scientificity consists in unfolding as and within an ontology, since philosophy must justify itself out of itself as universal ontology. But ontology becomes universally accessible only if philosophy has the method for reaching it — method meaning both the path traced and the strength to travel it. The name of that method is phenomenology, and *Sein und Zeit* condenses the relation by speaking of philosophy as universal phenomenological ontology. Phenomenology's task is not to constitute itself, even by subsuming philosophy, as it is for Husserl, for whom philosophy wants nothing more than to satisfy the hidden longing phenomenology names. Phenomenology's task is the strange one of conducting philosophy to what completes it as philosophy — ontology. Two inversions coincide: phenomenology loses the rank of autonomous and final science of itself for ontology's benefit, and that demotion to the ancillary status of method is what serves ontology's restoration. Phenomenology, entered into philosophy's service and not its own, works as method toward ontology.

The violence done to Husserl's definition should not distract from a prior question. Phenomenology becomes method for philosophy only because it first becomes method toward ontology. Where and when does Heidegger decide, or rather see decided under thought's gaze, phenomenology's ontological charge? The answer, and the question's sharpest formulation, are already in the 1925 course: phenomenological research is interpretation of entities toward their being; a particular entity or determinate region of being is set in advance as the theme of that interpretation, and it is questioned toward its being, toward that in view of which what has been set in advance is questioned; being is what is and must be looked toward, being is what is to be read off the entity, being is what phenomenological interpretation places in its fore-sight. Marion asks us to slow down here: phenomenology does set an entity as the goal of its advance, but the advance no longer stops at that entity, since it interprets the entity in view of what it sees as standing in relation to it and, for that very reason, as beyond it — being. What *Sein und Zeit* states in 1927 is already sketched in 1925: taken at the level of the matter itself, phenomenology is the science of the being of entities. The matter itself to which phenomenology makes philosophy return is no longer called the entity, nor the essence, category or noema, but radically the entity in view of being. Phenomenology becomes method in the strict sense precisely by displacing the matter beyond the entity to being — becomes, thanks to Heidegger, a path beyond itself, a kind of methodical self-overcoming. Marion proposes applying to this inaugural transition what Heidegger said only at the end of his path, in 1962: if phenomenology means letting the ownmost matter of thinking be said to us, then the title should be a way through phenomenology into the thinking of being, the genitive saying that being as such shows itself as what is to be thought and needs a thinking that answers it. Like the a priori, which in 1925 is one of phenomenology's three principal traits alongside intentionality and categorial intuition, phenomenology itself bears the character of the being of entities and not of the entity. Ontology names — inadequately — that displacement of phenomenology from entity to being. Where for Husserl phenomenology renders ontology obsolete because it deals with entities in ontology's place and better, for Heidegger phenomenology takes over ontology's title because it displaces itself from entities to being. The regard has changed: mere sight (*Sicht*), which can only fix on permanent available evidence and hence on entities, gives way to a looking-toward (*Hinsicht*) that sees entities only in relation to their being and for the sake of that being. The debate about agreement or antagonism between phenomenology and ontology therefore deepens into a debate about ontology's own status: does it concern entities (Husserl) or the being of entities and its mode of encounter (Heidegger)? The phenomenological method itself is not in dispute; only its point of application is. Between Husserl and Heidegger the difference is from the start played out with the difference between entity and being. But how does this second sight become possible, which sees the entity otherwise than as a pure entity? Marion finds Heidegger's criterion: phenomenological means everything belonging to the mode of exhibiting phenomena and phenomenal structures, and non-phenomenological whatever fails that mode of research; whence the strange but unavoidable consequence that phenomenology has never and at no time to do with phenomena, still less with mere phenomena. The paradox is the paradox of appearing itself: phenomenology treats not phenomena that have appeared but their mode of exhibition — not phenomena but, through them though directly, their very phenomenality. Two questions follow. Does phenomenology's displacement from entity to being coincide with its displacement from mere phenomena to their phenomenality? And does Husserlian phenomenology satisfy the definition of phenomenology by phenomenality, given that it ignores the first displacement? The first must wait; the second can be answered now. What the 1925 course identifies as a missing of the sense of being originates in a missing of the being of the intentional. Husserl never stops leading transcendent objects back to immanent acts along the thread of intentionality, and puts the world's supposed being in question by the *epochē*; but in that regression he aims first at the result of the acts, the disengaging of an absolute givenness in the immanence of lived experiences given perfectly to and as consciousness. The acts serve as means to that immanent givenness without themselves becoming the stake of a genuine questioning, so that the manner of being of the acts remains undetermined. Marion presses the fairness objection himself: why charge Husserl with missing a question that was not his priority, and what legitimacy has a reproach whose object did not matter to the accused? The answer lies in why it did not matter. Husserl's priority question is not the being-character of consciousness at all but how consciousness can become the possible object of an absolute science; what guides him is the idea of an absolute science, and that idea is no discovery but the very idea modern philosophy has pursued since Descartes. The elaboration of pure consciousness as phenomenology's thematic field is therefore not won phenomenologically in a step back to the things themselves but in a step back to the traditional idea of philosophy. Husserl does not follow phenomenology's guiding thread to its terminus, the question of being, because having won intentionality and the reduction — for which Heidegger always acknowledges himself his disciple — he diverts them to accomplish the philosophical ideal of an absolute science of and through consciousness. Marion is careful about what is not being alleged. Heidegger is not deploring the famous passage from the supposed realism of the *Investigations* to the attested idealism of the *Ideas*; he contests the tacit basis common to both works — first that consciousness's mode of being poses no specific or prior question, then that this mode of being can be assimilated to the mode of being of worldly things, so that what is always at stake is only the being of an object given in advance, being as objectivity in the sense of being-an-object for a taking-into-view. Against the obvious objection — Husserl's unbridgeable essential difference between the phenomenal being of the transcendent and the absolute being of the immanent in *Ideas I* §§42–44 — Marion answers for now that the being of consciousness is nonetheless defined, precisely in order to oppose it to the being of the world, as the sphere of absolute position, hence from position, permanent presence, objective subsistence: exactly what allowed Kant to define the being of the world. A distinction of regions within being does not suffice to think a difference of manners of being; on the contrary, establishing consciousness as a region, even an original one, implies that it too occupies a region, hence a position, hence an objectivity. And it is precisely treating the being of consciousness as a region that forecloses the question of the being of consciousness as non-object, since a quick and therefore operative answer lets one get on with cataloguing intentional acts, that is, phenomena as noemata, and neglect the unscientific question of the mode of being of consciousness as the seat of the intentional. Husserlian phenomenology goes back to the things themselves only up to a point, and the point is called the being of consciousness as such. An authority imposes the stopping point: the traditional idea of philosophy, which Heidegger will later call metaphysics. Measured against the criterion of the phenomenological — considering not phenomena but the mode of their exhibition — the conclusion, however violent the paradox, is that Husserl's phenomenology remains non-phenomenological, phenomenological only in intention and not yet in a fundamental and principial sense. Phenomenology has a long way to go to become itself, that is, to return to the things themselves. It must become method radically, not method for science but method for itself toward what is at issue — hence transgression of itself, up to its own intention, which is called the being of the intentional.

The turn taken by the idea of phenomenology from Husserl to Heidegger, already before 1927, shows up in one index: the inversion of its relation to ontology, which it now tries to reach by making itself method instead of abolishing by substitution. That index refers to an underlying displacement: the phenomenological no longer concerns knowledge of phenomena but knowledge of their mode of exhibition, and so no longer aims at grounding the sciences but at thinking phenomenality. The turn becomes really conceivable only once we have shown how thought can transgress the phenomenon toward its phenomenality — which is to ask how phenomenality differs from the phenomenon otherwise than verbally. Even reaching that question requires settling accounts with the competing definitions of the phenomenon on which Husserl and Heidegger divide.

### 3 - The "Reduced Phenomenon," Reduced to Present Objectivity

Determining the phenomenality of the phenomenon should present no insuperable difficulty, since phenomenology as science of phenomena must reach it as soon as it defines its own principle. Heidegger repeats that the principle is the return to the things themselves, or rather orienting thought by nothing but what is at issue each time. Husserl does not respect it — not merely because Heidegger says so, but because in the *Ideas* that principle competes with another, exceptionally titled the principle of all principles: before the return to the things themselves and before the reduction, it is laid down that every originarily giving intuition is a source of right for knowledge, that whatever offers itself originarily in intuition, in its bodily actuality as it were, is to be taken simply as what it gives itself to be, and only within the limits in which it gives itself there. The negative formulation shows the stake better: the norm we must follow as phenomenologists is to lay claim to nothing that we cannot make essentially evident to consciousness itself in pure immanence. Nothing can exercise a claim on consciousness except what gives itself to it in full evidence, and reciprocally whatever gives itself to it in full evidence can press its claim absolutely. Return to the things themselves therefore means return to the evidence given by intuition to consciousness, and the certainty of that givenness anticipates the reduction itself, understood as the exemplary case of an intuitive because immanent givenness. Heidegger's 1962 comment draws the conclusion: in its negative as in its positive sense, the appeal to the matter itself counts as the consolidation and elaboration of method, the procedure by which alone the matter itself arrives at an attested givenness — the principle of all principles concerns not the content but is a methodological principle; and if one asks where it gets its unshakeable legitimacy, the answer must be: from the transcendental subjectivity already presupposed as philosophy's matter. Marion registers what Heidegger is stigmatising: phenomenology's displacement from science of phenomena to science of and through consciousness, resuming the metaphysical project of a certain science and absolute knowledge. But he immediately raises the counter-objection, and it is a strong one. Does Husserl not, by that very principle, secure the phenomenon's right and primacy, letting it at last be taken as it gives itself? Did the 1911 *Logos* article Heidegger constantly names as Husserl's deviation not already require that phenomena be taken as they give themselves? Does the appendix to the Sixth Investigation — the text Heidegger himself privileges — not declare that the descriptive character of phenomena as we live them is alone determinative? Marion concedes the point entirely: the principle of all principles must indeed be read as Husserl's highest determination of the phenomenality of phenomena, since it states their absolute mode of givenness to consciousness. And precisely for that reason Heidegger's objection lands. For here phenomenality is interpreted as givenness, but givenness is in turn interpreted as the givenness of an effective presence for consciousness and for the sake of certainty. Phenomenality is at stake, but understood from consciousness, presupposed as prevalent. Consciousness determines phenomenality by reducing every phenomenon to the certainty of an effective presence, rather than phenomenality forcing consciousness to let itself be determined by the conditions and modes of a givenness that is always multiple and disconcerting. Marion notes in passing, with real scholarly scruple, that for Husserl there is simply no distinction between the two principles — at most two formulations of one demand — and that the split between them becomes visible only from an already Heideggerian standpoint. The point about Heidegger's 1962 title is also worth holding: *Zur Sache des Denkens* answers Husserl's *zur Sache selbst* in its very wording, accomplishing the return to the matter at issue only at the cost of a fresh break with Husserl.

Three remarks establish the domination. (a) The givenness of phenomena taken as they give themselves presents them in the present under the figure of bodily actuality, as a rigorous, that is rigorously certain, science demands. It belongs to the mode of being of lived experience that an intuitive perceptual gaze can be directed quite immediately upon every actual living lived experience as an originary present. The mode of being of the lived experience — that in which the thing appears — must turn into an originary, effective and therefore certain present, since it is to appear to a gaze that seeks only certainty there. And the pre-eminence of the present holds uncontested sway over lived experience only because it first characterises, or rather comes from, the mode of being of the intuiting consciousness: my empathy and my consciousness in general is, as a streaming present, given originarily and absolutely, according to essence and according to existence alike. The interpretation of the phenomena's mode of being as actuality follows from the actuality necessarily induced by consciousness's presence in the present. The transition between the two terms in one and the same present evidence does not contradict the opposition of immanence and transcendence but stages it: the objectivity of the transcendent object can only result from the acts of a consciousness immanent to itself, along intentionality. Since the *Investigations*, what counts for us as the most certain of all is that being-object in the phenomenological sense resides in certain acts in which something appears or is thought as object. Objectivity, hence the effective presence imposed on every phenomenon, follows from consciousness's intentional acts; consciousness therefore determines in advance, in conformity with the prevalence of pure presence within it, the mode of being of phenomena, which give themselves as they appear only on condition of appearing in the mode consciousness silently imposes — satisfying the actuality of presence. Which reigns, uninterrogated. (b) What appears is admitted as such not because it appears but because it appears to an authority established from the outset as originary; the appearance could always be suspected of being mere semblance if the authority it satisfied were not itself held to be originary. That authority is called intuition. That intuition is originary means that the origin of the givenness of phenomena lies less in their appearing than in the intuition that claims originarily to be giving. Marion notes the standing difficulty over that adjective — which gives, intuition or appearing? Intuition, presumably, because it legitimates appearing, since no other faculty or authority of consciousness can judge appearing behind its back. Seeing in general can be posited as originarily giving consciousness because it is the ultimate source of right for all rational assertions, hence for all presence; one must speak, as of a single thing, of originarily giving intuition and its own originary right. At the origin of all appearing stands intuition, which gives the appearance originarily not by substituting for it but by authenticating in law that its appearing is no semblance, since intuition, instituted at the principle of all vision, sees nothing there to disappoint it. Intuition sets itself up as the tribunal of appearance — a tribunal more originary than reason's — before which appearance stands as effective presence of what gives itself. Phenomena give themselves absolutely only to the absolute of intuition. Which reigns, uninterrogated. (c) The clearest index of phenomenality's subjection to consciousness comes from the definition of the phenomenon out of lived experience. Husserl firmly denounces the equivocation that allows the name *Erscheinung* to be given both to the lived experience in which the object's appearing consists and to the appearing object as such; the appendix to the Sixth Investigation concedes at the very end that the term is loaded with equivocations extremely damaging here, and lists them: appearance means, first, the concrete lived experience of intuition, having a certain object intuitively present or presentified; second, the intuited (appearing) object insofar as it is what appears here and now. The warning demands that the phenomenologist not confuse lived experience with the appearing thing — sensations are lived but do not appear objectively; objects appear, are perceived, but are not lived. Marion then asks the question the warning invites: why keep a duality that on Husserl's own admission breeds equivocation and confusion? Why not hold to what the same appendix calls the originary concept of appearance, the second one, that of what appears or can appear, the intuitable as such? The equivocation must absolutely remain, because the duality of the term is paradoxically Husserlian phenomenology's fundamental acquisition: phenomenon is said first and not only of the object that appears but of the lived experience in and according to which it appears, and this duality alone will license thinking absolute givenness, intentionality, and the noesis/noema pair. Appearance is approached, even and especially where intentionality is taken into account, from the immanence of lived experience, hence never from the appearing of the object itself, which is by definition conditioned. Nor does the passage just cited tell against this: even where the concept of what appears is expressly held to be the originary concept of appearance, the same text continues immediately that lived experiences of any kind can become objects of internal reflective intuitions, so that the phenomenality of lived experiences becomes itself that of objects and phenomenology accordingly means the doctrine of lived experiences in general. Phenomenology could not equivalently be called the doctrine of appearing phenomena and the doctrine of lived experiences unless the phenomenality of phenomena were thought from lived experiences. The equivocation results purely and simply from adding to the traditional sense (phenomenon: appearing object) the properly Husserlian one (phenomenon: the lived experience in and as which the object appears). Marion then unpacks the French *vécu* and, through it, *Erlebnis*: one should hear in it the affective charge ordinary speech keeps when it says someone has lived a great deal; lived experience implies an undergoing, in the sense of a proof of the actual world encountered, and also in the sense a photographer or printer gives the word — a print or plate. For the mind, *Erlebnis* means making itself the print of phenomenality; and conversely, print means that the phenomenality of the appearing object is inscribed and attested first in the fabric and along the flux of consciousness. The appearing object sketches and shades itself off — adumbration — on the sensitive plate of consciousness, which thereby becomes the first and only print of the phenomenon. The governance of the phenomenon by lived experience is confirmed beyond the equivocal doubling by its grip on the definition of truth: evidence is the lived experience of truth. Truth, hence the accomplishment of phenomenality as full manifestation, blossoms on the ground of lived experience, shows through it as through a filter, is recorded on it as on a film, is finally traced in it as in the filaments of a prior network. The phenomenon appears only to and as the print of its consciousness. Which reigns, uninterrogated.

Consciousness therefore determines phenomenality radically by imposing on it the reflectivity of presence, the absolute of intuition, and the ordeal of lived experience. The return to the things themselves shrinks to a return to the sources of intuition. The phenomenon so disengaged receives its limit along with its purity — Husserl's frequent "reduced phenomenon," which Marion hears in two senses at once: the phenomenon obtained by the reduction, and the phenomenon whose mode of being the reduction reduces to what the primacy of consciousness imposes. For the fundamental point is that evidence is that consciousness which in fact sees and grasps itself directly and adequately in person, and that this is nothing other than adequate self-givenness. Self-givenness becomes the phenomenon's only correct mode of being because it alone satisfies the demands of direct grasp, permanent presence, and immediate evidence that consciousness imposes on whatever claims to appear before it. From the phenomenon consciousness expects only one service — presence — since every actually living lived experience is one that is present. By the constraining grace of the reduction as the authority of bringing-into-presence, the reduced phenomenon is reduced to the entity present here and now. If absolute givenness is a last term, then absolute presence becomes a first term, short of which we could speak neither of phenomenon nor of lived experience. The primacy of presence unfolds as a horizon all the less surpassable for not giving itself as a horizon, holding itself back and dissimulating itself in its neutral evidence. So the gap between the appearing transcendent thing and the immanent lived experience in which it would appear — an opposition Husserl calls the high point of his meditation — opens only by understanding both terms according to bodily givenness in person, hence presence: every thing given in person can, despite that givenness, also not be, while no lived experience given in person can also not be. More essential than this essential law separating transcendent from immanent is the constraint of presence itself: nothing enters even the field of a merely possible phenomenality without first subscribing to givenness in person, and that givenness has no function but to offer consciousness's gaze the present object required by reduced phenomenality. The primacy of the immanent over the transcendent follows only from a better and more immediate satisfaction of consciousness's demands, since a perceptual gaze can be directed quite immediately upon every actual lived experience as originary present. Lived experience prevails as object par excellence because originarily present; it earns the privilege of accomplishing before all the transcendent world the perfection of presence — enduring, immediate, intuitable, available. And why call it immanent? Because consciousness, which lets itself be brought back to the Cartesian definition of substance — Husserl deliberately cites and deliberately modifies the *Principia* — knowing itself permanently present, apprehends in the lived experience of the region consciousness an originary presence of its own type. It has only to remain within itself to discover a presence of equal permanence. Marion is here seeding chapter III: it is precisely this Cartesian determination of consciousness as permanent presence that Heidegger's destruction of the *ego* will target. The reign of presence as permanence thus extends over every region — immanent or absolute being and transcendent being are both called entity and object, and both have their objective content of determination. But is it self-evident that objectivity offers the only face of the entity? That objectivity can be attributed equally to the immanent entity of consciousness and the transcendent entity of the world? That the phenomenon must reduce its phenomenality to objectivity understood as assured permanence? The reduction of the phenomenon to a phenomenality of objectivity shows itself fully in Husserl's inability to consider the non-present: thing-perception does not presentify something non-present as memory or imagination would; it renders present, grasps a self in its bodily present, and does so in accordance with its own sense, which would be violated by supposing anything else of it. Perception can unfold only as a presentification that seizes a presence in person, objectively awaiting that seizure. Any approach to a thing insofar as it withdraws from permanent presence must therefore be abandoned to imagination and memory, since it does not fall under the grasp of presence at all. In short, since the phenomenality of the reduced phenomenon is reduced to objective and permanent presence, any phenomenon that does not reduce to that presence excludes itself from phenomenality. Reduced to the remainderless evidence of presence, the Husserlian phenomenon eliminates the distortion of appearing — a distortion that all the adequations, between intentionality and fulfilment, between evidence and truth, exist only to bring back to strict equality. Since no remainder disturbs the final equation, the Husserlian phenomenon, as perfect appearance of presence, may be called a flat phenomenon.

### 4 - From the Inapparent Phenomenon to the "Phenomenon of Being"

Can the phenomenon be understood otherwise than as permanent presence before consciousness's gaze? The decisive index is the authority granted to presence itself in matters of phenomenality — how far the phenomenality of the phenomenon is defined in terms of presence, or better, at presence's terminus. At first Heidegger seems to answer in strict conformity with Husserl, since the famous §7 retains as the meaning of the expression "phenomenon" that which shows itself in itself, the manifest, and treats the deviant cases — semblance, index, deceptive appearance — as confusions untangled only if the concept of phenomenon is understood from the start as the self-showing. Marion denies the conformity for two reasons. (a) What is at stake is not presence, even in person, but the phenomenon's showing itself on its own initiative. The phenomenon gives itself from itself and out of its own visibility, far from being reducible to presence for a consciousness. The possible deviances in fact attest an initiative proper to the phenomenon in entering visibility, along a route that harbours, in proportion, hiding places for possible dissimulations. Nor does Heidegger mention anything like consciousness — not because nothing is required to see what rises into its own visibility, but because in a sense that visibility, in whatever mode, is decided upstream of all evidence and hence of all consciousness. Visibility does not represent itself; it presents itself. And precisely because it presents itself, it can also absent itself. From the deviant senses of the self-showing, that is from the phenomenon's non-showings, Heidegger draws not the need for a drastic reduction to presence absolutely given for consciousness but the opposite conclusion: coveredness is the counter-concept to phenomenon. Marion presses the German: *Gegenbegriff* means less the contrary or the contradictory than the counter-play, the counter-fire, the buttress that inscribes manifestation within the very quarry of dissimulation, so that in coming to its own manifestation the phenomenon only recovers what was covered, taking up in the manifest mode what remained concealed in the covered one. The phenomenon manifests only insofar as it manifests what remained unmanifest before that manifestation and still obscurely governs its brilliance. (b) Consequently phenomenology oversteps the vulgar use of "phenomenon" only in that it renders manifest not the manifest but the non-manifest. What does phenomenology let be seen? What must be called phenomenon in a distinguished sense? What by its essence is the necessary theme of an explicit showing? Evidently what does not show itself at first, what remains hidden over against what does show itself at first, but which belongs essentially to what shows itself at first, constituting its sense and ground. The phenomenon has to come to manifestation only insofar as it is not at first apparent; the phenomenon is characterised first by its inapparence. Marion confirms the stupefying inversion with further texts: coveredness is the counter-concept to phenomenon, and coverings-over are the first theme of phenomenological consideration; what can be phenomenon is at first and for the most part covered over, that is, known in a provisional determination; some coverings are accidental and others necessary, given with the mode of being of uncovering and its possibilities; and every phenomenological proposition originally drawn is, as a communicated statement, in a position to dissimulate, so that this possibility of what was originally drawn becoming fossilised belongs to phenomenology's concrete work. Heidegger's own marginal note names what finally remains hidden: the truth of being. What requires the work of unveiling in the phenomenon, precisely because of itself it stays dissimulated, is nothing less than the always-veiled unveiling of truth.

Marion adds two further formulations. Critical reflection shows that phenomenological questioning can begin in what is most self-evident, that phenomena are not lying open in broad daylight, that the paths to the things themselves are not simply laid out, and that the constant danger of straying persists — which is precisely the sense of phenomenology as clearing research. And if the *phainomenon* is what shows itself, there remains the astonishing possibility that an entity show itself as something it is not, so that strictly the phenomenon is undergone as enigmatic. The contrast is now exact. The Husserlian phenomenon is defined by evidence and confined to it, so that every residue of non-evidence must disappear from the reduced phenomenon. The Heideggerian phenomenon, originating in the rise into visibility of the not-yet-visible, implies by right and in principle the inapparent within appearance. In one case evidence reduces appearance to presence, hence to objectivity for consciousness; in the other, appearance reveals as such the inapparent whose contrast haloes the apparent. Instead of the certain evidence of an object for consciousness, the phenomenon offers itself as the enigma of the forever non-objectifiable play of the apparent with the inapparent. Phenomenon no longer means the certain object but a certain play of the apparent within appearance. Phenomenology's work is accordingly to render apparent not only the inapparent but the play of apparent with inapparent in appearance: behind phenomenology's phenomena stands essentially nothing, but it may be that what is to become phenomenon is hidden, and it is just because phenomena are at first and for the most part not given that phenomenology is needed.

The question about the relation of phenomenology to ontology led to a question about the phenomenality of the phenomenon. At the end of the first, the cut between Husserl and Heidegger ran through the phenomenological pertinence of a project of ontology; at the end of the second it runs between reducing the phenomenon to presence and recognising the enigma of an appearance ringed with inapparence. What connects the two cuts, and how does thinking the phenomenon as enigma advance the thought of phenomenology as ontology? Marion offers an indication that confirms their intimate connection: the 1925 formula taken up in *Sein und Zeit* — as much semblance, so much being. (Marion's note traces it back through Husserl's own *Cartesian Meditations* to Herbart, which quietly denies Heidegger any coinage here and makes the formula a shared inheritance rather than a divide.) *Schein* stands here for the semblance that does not directly satisfy appearance, because it covers an inapparence which, if it appeared, would offer a wholly different appearance; so semblance attests the phenomenon's enigma as play of apparent and inapparent. Why should being show up just where that enigma does? First because the enigma is nothing illusory, since it puts in question everything that presents itself, hence possibly being too if being also presents itself. But above all because the phenomenon's play concerns being directly: what in the most proper sense remains hidden, or falls back into covering-over, or manifests itself only distortedly, is never this or that entity but the being of entities. The ultimate matter to which the return to the things themselves must come back is the phenomenality of being itself. Heidegger's retrieval and radicalisation of Husserl's watchword aimed at nothing but formulating that incomparably ambitious and paradoxical task — to come back to being as being and, in the same gesture, to come back to it as to a phenomenon. The phenomenon had to pass from evident presence to the enigma of the play of inapparent with apparent only so as to make possible the phenomenality of being, which par excellence lets itself be covered over in the very uncovering of the entity, since it makes room for it. On condition that the phenomenon open onto its own enigma, one may dare to qualify being itself as phenomenon — an audacity Heidegger commits at least once: this phenomenon, "being," is worth elaborating. Let us elaborate it, then, with Heidegger and without Husserl.

We have arrived where the 1973 Zähringen seminar says that this phenomenology is a phenomenology of the inapparent. Rather than retorting too quickly that one must choose between a phenomenology, hence the apparent, and the inapparent, hence the impossibility of phenomenology, Marion asks why the paradox — a phenomenology of the inapparent as such, and not merely of the not-yet-appearing — is required by a necessity of thought. The formulation is easy, whatever the difficulty of thinking it. Phenomenology must bear on the inapparent because being does not appear and is not perceivable; being is never perceived within the horizon of presence as a phenomenon of strict observance. Why? Because uncovered presence in evident permanence accommodates and suits only entities; only an entity can stay here and now to answer "present" to the summons of evidence. But this being itself is nothing that is, and what belongs to the being of an entity therefore remains in obscurity. Two texts, one of 1925 and one of summer 1927, frame *Sein und Zeit*'s founding thesis: the being of entities is not itself an entity. Only what the gaze sees and knows in full assurance *is* — is an entity; being itself does not show itself precisely because it is not, since we first and foremost experience entities and come to know being only afterward, or perhaps never at all. From the standpoint of the present phenomenon, hence of the evident entity, being is nothing of an entity and nothing visible. Nothing? In 1929 the lecture on metaphysics will no longer hesitate to radicalise what *Sein und Zeit* already senses, and will strictly identify being with the nothing. For the moment it is enough that since the phenomenon admits only entities, the most perfect uncovering into presence can present only the evidence of an entity — the most evident phenomenon in the world gives only what it has. Any inquiry toward the being of entities must therefore seek a goal other than uncovering, for being can never be found uncovered or in the open; only entities can and must be. Hence the terminological and factual distinction between the uncoveredness of an entity and the disclosedness of its being: an entity can be uncovered, by perception or any other access, only if the being of the entity is already disclosed — only if I understand it. One should not be surprised that Heidegger speaks of a disclosure in which being unfolds, as though aligning it with the entity's entry into presence; the surprise only shows that we still spontaneously think being on the model of the uncovering of entities. Being does not disclose itself as entities are uncovered, if only because its disclosure precedes and makes possible that uncovering. Which also means that disclosure does not disclose being as uncovering uncovers entities, within and according to presence. In disclosing itself, being opens the field for the uncovering of the present entity and, for that very reason, does not itself enter evident presence. If one wants to keep the title of phenomenon for being — at the risk of a dangerous ambiguity — one would have to think a phenomenon not exhausted in presence here and now, since it is defined only by being able to refuse presence. To render phenomenal not what, being invisible, could become visible and so become an entity, but paradoxically to render phenomenal what, invisible as such, could in no sense become visible in the mode of the present entity: can that task be undertaken, or even formulated?

### 5 - The Two Senses of the Reduction

The Husserlian phenomenon was concluded to be flat — remainderless, level, all surface, hence perfectly present. Since we now seek a mode of the phenomenon that dispenses it from the requirement of presence, we may suspect it would escape flatness too, and so must sound rather than uncover what may be called the depth of the Heideggerian phenomenon. Depth, because it is now to be envisaged in two layers rather than one. How? By interpreting the entity precisely in its being. Being offers itself to no direct reading for the gaze of evidence, which by definition uncovers only present entities. Yet those entities, beyond their presence, offer for reading what they themselves ignore or even dissimulate — the mode of their entry into presence, their phenomenality: being is to be read off the entity; what phenomenological interpretation places in its fore-sight is being. Marion works the two German words. *Ablesen*, badly rendered by "reading off the surface of," first indicates a gleaning, a non-systematic but selective picking; the point is not merely to read the phenomenon's evidence in its flatness, which Husserlian constitution already does perfectly, but in addition and on that occasion to read between the lines, between the flashes of evident presence, what that presence proceeds from — and the text's next word is *Interpretation*, whose German equivalent would be *Auslegung*. So the phenomenon is to be read in such a way that one gets out of it, or at least transgresses the uninterrogated evidence of its presence. Getting out does not mean forgetting it to pass to something else; it means passing from the thing to something other than the thing. To what? To what one places in fore-sight: *Vor-sicht* means in one word attention and precaution, but to pay attention one must look further than the end of one's nose, look ahead; the gaze that takes care looks, precisely in order to take care, beyond what it has before its eyes — not at another thing, since the point is to keep hold of this entity here and now, but at the horizon from which danger could come and where any new situation will occur. So looks the phenomenological gaze: it looks at the entity according to its horizon, in view of its being. Interpretation does not consist in seeing another entity but in seeing the entity otherwise — not otherwise than as this entity; on the contrary, in seeing it for the first time *as* an entity, that is, as uncovered in presence, in short as phenomenon. And the phenomenon appears: it comes from its own emergence out of inapparence into evident appearance. To see it as such is therefore to see it as never ceasing to appear, hence to disengage itself from inapparence and fix itself in presence. Before the phenomenon's appearance, phenomenological interpretation has something in the back of its mind — or rather, since it does not seek the reason of effects, it has an attention behind, or better within, its vision. Vision of the phenomenon's presence detects there, through its attention, a depth. Depth does not mean that behind the phenomenon something else waits to appear, but that the phenomenon's very appearing — as a manner of being and therefore as a non-entity — reveals a depth. Depth does not double the phenomenon in the cinematic or detective sense; it reveals the phenomenon to itself, manifesting that it is only insofar as being first opens as the phenomenality in which the phenomenon can subsequently be uncovered. The phenomenon's depth thereby relaunches phenomenology as knowledge not only of phenomena but of their phenomenality. Being can open, and become phenomenology's stake, only insofar as phenomena are seen as entities entering presence; being discloses itself from the uncovering of entities only in the sense in which phenomenality is detected from the enigma of the phenomenon. The redefinition of phenomenological method is therefore one and the same, for Heidegger, with instituting the question of the being of entities — a convergence announced by the identification of phenomenon with entity and accomplished by the accession to being as phenomenality. Hence Heidegger's phenomenological tendency to clarify and understand being as such, or the interpretation of entities toward their being. In both pairs — phenomenon/phenomenality and entity/being — and to allow their superposition, thought performs the same act: a transgression. Transgression means no arbitrary violence but the performance of the return to the things themselves. The things of thought do not coincide with the things of life; thought must not return only to entities and phenomena, that is, to evident and permanent presence, but must redouble its impetus to enter the immanent depth of that presence, opening immanence itself along phenomenality, hence being. The question of being as such can be won only when questioning is guided by a questioning-through-to-the-end, a questioning back into the beginning, that is, when it is determined by the radically grasped sense of the phenomenological principle of the matter itself: to let the entity be seen as the entity itself in its being. Transgression does not undo the entity or contest the phenomenon, since the point is to let the entity be seen as the entity itself. It consists in questioning to the end, boring through to the radical beginning, to what is at stake in the entity appearing as such: being as phenomenality. Transgression moves within the phenomenon-entity itself, because for Heidegger transcendence does not overstep the phenomenon's immanence; on the contrary, if the phenomenon counts as entity, then the being that offers the entity to itself becomes, as phenomenality, the transcendent pure and simple. Marion's supporting note is important: since transcending strictly reaches no entity at all, it concretely consists in a self-transcendence, hence in Dasein's transcendence by itself — which is why being's transcendence must be understood as Dasein's, the only way to avert the danger of landing on another entity. That already prepares §6. Being's transcendence does not leave the entity behind but pushes it to its limit; phenomenality's transcendence does not traverse the phenomenon's immanence but carries it to its term.

Being's transcendence pushes the entity to its limit so as really to return to the things themselves. The thing to which thought must return is nothing of an entity but the nothing of the entity, namely being. This first accomplishment of the phenomenological precept by transgression of the entity puts us on the way to a second, also obtained by transgression: the accomplishment of the reduction. Marion cites the 1927 course in full because it is the hinge of his whole book. For Husserl, phenomenological reduction is the method of leading the phenomenological gaze back from the natural attitude of the human being living immersed in the world of things and persons to transcendental conscious life and its noetic-noematic lived experiences, in which objects are constituted as correlates of consciousness. For us, it means leading the phenomenological gaze back from the apprehension of an entity as always determined to the understanding of the being of that entity. Heidegger therefore remains a phenomenologist, since he takes the reduction up again. The retrieval involves a deep displacement, yet the displacement still disengages a phenomenon in a certain sense: the reduction now leads not from the thesis of the world to immanence in a level presence but from the phenomenon as uncovering of an entity to its understanding in depth toward its being. Far from destituting belief in being, the reduction, relaunched by the radical return to the matter itself, disengages the sense of being of the entity. Bracketing the entity takes nothing away from the entity itself and does not make one accept that the entity is not; radically, this switching of the gaze has the sense of making the being-character of the entity present. The phenomenological switching-off of the transcendent thesis has the sole function of making the entity present in view of its being; the expression "bracketing" is therefore always liable to misunderstanding, insofar as one thinks that in bracketing the thesis of existence phenomenological meditation would have nothing more to do with the entity — on the contrary, it is concerned absolutely and radically with nothing but determining the being of the entity itself. Bracketing the positing of the world, hence of the entity, does not put the entity in question but puts in question whether the entity can be uncovered without the horizon of being first opening. Suspending the thesis of the entity does not eliminate the entity; it forbids seeing it otherwise than according to the matter itself, and so requires seeing it as it is — as entity according to being. Marion therefore rejects Husserl's verdict to Ingarden that Heidegger grasped neither this path nor the whole sense of the method of reduction, and the afterword's claim that the objections rest on misunderstandings, on interpreting his phenomenology at the level its whole sense consisted in overcoming, on failing to understand the principial innovation of the reduction and the ascent from worldly to transcendental subjectivity, on remaining bogged in an anthropology. (The scholarly quarrel is live: Tugendhat takes Husserl's side on Heidegger's abandonment of the reduction, while Granel and Courtine read the transgression as Marion does.) Heidegger's transgression does not depart from the Husserlian reduction by falling back short of it toward a naive positing of the world; it departs by passing beyond it, toward the sense of being of the entity. But if Husserl commits the misreading of imputing a misreading, a theoretical motive must be found, and Marion proposes one. Husserl envisages only two terms constituting the two sides of a single reduction — thesis of the world, reduced phenomenon — without suspecting that Heidegger sees three, arranged in two reductions: thesis of the world to phenomenon by the purely Husserlian phenomenological reduction, then entity to sense of being by phenomenological interpretation. Why does Husserl not see the relaunch, to the point of reading the interpretation of the entity toward its being as a regressive inversion of the first reduction? Because Husserl does not reach, or rather has from the outset forbidden himself to reach, the phenomenon as an entity. For Husserl the reduced phenomenon, in its most evident presence, offers only a noematic correlate to consciousness; it never appears as an entity capable in turn of referring not to consciousness alone but to the sense of being. So the failure to recognise the ontico-ontological status of the reduction follows directly from a failure to carry through the return to the matter itself, which Husserl was nonetheless the first to take as goal and method. Conversely, displacing the reduction into interpretation goes together with relaunching the principle of a return to the matter itself, since both amount to transgressing the entity toward its being — a transgression that first requires recognising the phenomenon as an entity. On that basis Heidegger's originally though originally phenomenological enterprise can unfold as a bringing to light of the entity toward its being, and phenomenality as the being of the phenomenon *qua* entity will be put in play by two distinct tactics — the analytic of Dasein on one side, ontological difference on the other. Those two tactics organise the rest of the book.

### 6 - The Redoubled Reduction — Dasein

The analytic of Dasein voids the suspicion of anthropology as soon as one sees that it answers the radical injunction that the question of being must be constructed. What is to be constructed is the apparatus of a question that oversteps every entity, so it cannot be a return to naive consciousness, to the positing of the world, a regression short of the phenomenological attitude. The apparatus to be set up — Marion notes that *Gestell* resonates in *gestellt* — leads back, hence reduces, everything given to what is precisely not immediately given and indeed can never be mediately givable because it is giving: being. So not only does the intervention of an apparatus exclude regression short of the reduction; the intention of the question that apparatus constructs — being, which by definition cannot give itself as an entity — requires that a certain bracketing be exercised to the full. From the very opening, and for two fundamental reasons, a certain manner of reduction is being practised.

The first reason concerns the question's tripartite structure. Questioning, as questioning after something, has what it asks about; but every questioning after something is in one way or another an addressing of a question to something. Besides what it questions after, questioning includes what it interrogates. In a research question, that is, a specifically theoretical one, what is asked about must be determined and brought to the concept, and within it lies what is properly intended, what one wants to find out, toward which the questioning advances. An authentic question therefore mobilises an apparatus that is not binary, as in the Husserlian reduction, but ternary: what one asks about; the one of whom one asks it; and what one finally wants to know through the asking. Marion offers a police analogy — putting a question about where, when, how, by what means to a witness or suspect, in order to discover the truth about an event. What matters above all is to hold apart what one asks of the one interrogated from what one ultimately wants to know. That gap is the same one already met in *Ablesung*: a second-order reading that gathers what it finds in view of something other than what it has just found. The ternary question is strictly speaking an investigation: it wants information, obtained from an entity, in view of what that entity itself ignores or dissimulates. The question asks of the entity far more than it can or will say; it wants to know not what can be known but what cannot at first, and perhaps never, be uncovered. The 1925 course, more explicit here than *Sein und Zeit*, names the three elements: what one seeks to know by questioning, the sense of being; what one asks about in questioning, the being of entities; and what or whom one interrogates in questioning, the entity itself. That formal result suffices for understanding Heidegger's own practice of the reduction, which sets itself to question to the bottom — that is, in two moments, relaunching the entity twice rather than once. First round: the entity is interrogated, asked to declare its identity and, if need be, produce its papers; and in that interrogation something other than itself, other than its immediate identity, is demanded of it — the entity identified as such, in an Aristotelian and metaphysical moment, is required to explain itself about its being. Explaining oneself about the being of the entity might even count as a recall of the Husserlian reduction. In any case the entity is summoned to assign its being: what, for it, is the case with its being? It is asked not what it is but what it is *of* — the genitive of the being of the entity. That is the first relaunch. But there is now a second, which the interrogated entity neither can nor should reach; it concerns the entity only indirectly and belongs properly to the questioning itself insofar as it is authentically exercised. What the questioning finally wants to know is in no way what the interrogated entity means or can know: the questioning has another idea in the back of its mind, seeking not only the being of entities but the sense of being itself. The gap, explicit from 1925, between the being of entities and the sense of being points from the very start — against any hasty account of the turn — toward what *Zeit und Sein* will call the attempt to think being without entities. The entity is referred out of the questioning, which continues so to speak without it, from the being of entities to the sense of being, in a strict passage from the ontic to the ontological that alone opens the horizon proper to the phenomenological intention of *Sein und Zeit* in its initial formalism. One might risk saying that from the origin, hence from the confrontation with the reduction in Husserl's sense, Heidegger has only one intention: to interrogate the entity by a question about its being such that in the end being alone comes into play. Marion insists on the genitive: this is not the sense *of* being, as though being still intervened indirectly, but being directly as sense, sense directly as being. That is the second relaunch — carrying the being of entities beyond the entity to the sense of being. The reduction redoubles: no longer content to manifest of what being the entity is (being as consciousness or being as world), it aims from that first result at disengaging being as such. Whether *Sein und Zeit* reaches that goal can be shown later; what matters here is the intention and structure of its questioning. The redoubled reduction tries to conduct the entity, by a strictly though originally phenomenological method, to the sense of being — to being without regard for the entity.

But which entity? Which entity could have such regard for being as to lead us, willing or not, to being without regard for the entity? Does the choice of the human entity, Dasein, not prove a regression toward anthropology and hence the concrete impossibility of the redoubled reduction? The only way to answer, even formally, is to look for the criteria that privilege Dasein. Heidegger asks it himself: this entity, of which we say questioning, taking into view toward, laying claim to as, relating to — as what is it given to us in advance? It is the entity that we ourselves are, the entity that I in each case am, which we call Dasein. Is that not an admission of anthropology, or worse, of existentialism? The preceding page had already warded off the misreading: the questioning is itself an entity, given along with the question about the being of an entity in the performance of the questioning, whether or not one sees it explicitly. Dasein is not one of the entities capable of supporting the redoubled reduction, one among others privileged by an unfortunate anthropological penchant; it does not come afterward to serve a questioning built in advance. It is itself, from the outset, what constitutes the questioning, as its flesh and body, its place and its call. Dasein as such is always already not merely ordered to the double reduction but performs it. Why? Because Dasein is decisive here not only ontically but ontologically, for us as phenomenologists. It is not enough to say, with *Sein und Zeit* — which Marion finds more reticent here — that understanding of being is itself a determination of Dasein's being, and that Dasein's ontic characteristic lies in its being ontological; it must be said phenomenologically. Dasein's privilege is strictly phenomenological, not anthropological. It is characterised ontically as having access to being, as having being for its stake, as having being for its depth. Of itself, as an entity, it refers, better refers itself, to being: the ecstatic-horizonal character goes together with the essential determination of Dasein, that it transcends within itself. It transcends, indeed transcends itself, unceasingly, not by an unassignable irrational grace but by its own phenomenological trait, intentionality: intentionality is the *ratio cognoscendi* of transcendence. Intentionality thought to its root implies a world, more exactly being-in-the-world — another line of fracture with Husserl — and so aims, in the entities it thereby transcends, at the being of the entity; *a fortiori*, in the entity it is first of all for itself, Dasein intentionally aims at the being of its own entity-ness. Dasein can therefore never stop at consideration of itself, even in inauthenticity, and must always aim by transcendence at the being of the entity. By nature its ontological identity makes it the chief witness, if not more, in the investigation into the sense of being. It must indeed be qualified as the entity in whose being that being is at issue for it — provided one hears the being of that entity as being *überhaupt* according to its sense, the sense of being. Later texts repeat it: to conceive understanding of being means first to understand the entity to whose constitution of being understanding of being belongs, Dasein; Dasein is the entity to whose existence understanding of being belongs; Dasein's characteristic trait is that it relates to entities understandingly of being. Dasein's privilege accrues to it only from its disposition to undergo a redoubled phenomenological reduction, which transits from entity to sense of being only by working on the entity determined par excellence by the being of the entity. By this first tactic the analytic of Dasein is accomplished as redoubled reduction and thereby permits the referral of the entity as phenomenon to being, as sense of being, as phenomenality.

### 7 - The Redoubled Reduction — the Nothing

The second tactic sets up another phenomenological apparatus, whose main lines are fixed by the 1929 lecture *What Is Metaphysics?*, with still greater sharpness once the corrections of later editions are included. Marion defers the full itinerary to chapter VI and extracts only what marks it as a manner of phenomenological reduction, indeed a metamorphosis of the redoubled reduction. First trait: as the analytic of Dasein followed from the three dimensions along which the question of being must be constructed, so here it is a matter of elaborating the question of the nothing. Elaborating a question means identifying the acts, and determining their conditions of possibility, capable of securing the givenness of the nothing as such. As *Sein und Zeit* aims to reach and thus to give the sense of being, so here the aim is to reach and thus to give the nothing — which the lecture in fact does: the nothing itself, as such, was there. Second trait: in the 1929 apparatus as in that of 1927, the reduction is accomplished on the occasion of anxiety, but the two employments differ notably. In 1927 anxiety affects Dasein so as to bring it to the full rigour of being-in-the-world, which qualifies it as Da-sein for all other entities; anxiety is thus circumscribed to Dasein alone, singularising it radically over against the totality of entities. In 1929, by contrast, anxiety operates without particular reference to Dasein, or more exactly exercises Dasein in the encounter with entities in general, on the basis of a result already obtained by boredom: this boredom manifests entities as a whole. How can anxiety's field of application widen to the point of no longer performing the reduction on one entity, Dasein, led back to its principial trait of being-in-the-world, but on all entities, led back to their originary nothing? Two readings offer themselves. Either Heidegger abandons in 1929 the supposedly narrow rigour of a strictly phenomenological reduction — but then why so ostentatiously keep one of *Sein und Zeit*'s most famous analyses? Or the widening of the ontic field becomes possible through an increased phenomenological power of the reduction, strong enough to operate the same reduction not only on Dasein but on entities in their totality. Marion offers a physical analogy: as atomic physicists try to pass from fission, where the chain reaction requires enriched uranium, to fusion, where it would occur for any material, so Heidegger's reduction tries between 1927 and 1929 to exercise itself on all entities and hence on any entity. It still proceeds from Dasein even in 1929, yet unfolds resolutely beyond Dasein's field — a field that, since it consists in being-in-the-world, already concerns entities in their totality by right from 1927. It remains to show that the generalised reduction of 1929 aims, by different stages, at the givenness and setting-in-phenomenon of the very term the 1927 reduction called the sense of being. The third trait meets that demand in outline. What does anxiety do to entities in totality as naively posited by natural — here scientific — consciousness? In anxiety there is a shrinking-back that is no longer flight but a fascinated repose; that shrinking-back takes its departure from the nothing, which does not draw toward itself but essentially repels; the repelling is as such the letting-slip referral toward entities in totality, which thereby sink away; this repelling referral toward entities slipping away in totality, such that the nothing encircles Dasein in anxiety, is the essence of the nothing: nihilation. Marion glosses the seemingly obscure description as limpid. Anxiety abolishes the distinction between entities: I can no longer specify which entity I fear, so I fear entities whatever they are, entities in their totality. Anxiety thus imposes repulsion toward entities in their undifferentiated totality and provokes their expulsion — an expulsion that at the same time designates them all the better as such, since it assigns them in their threatening entity-ness. It sees the entity and makes it slip slowly, finally founder in a long motionless movement, like a ship that, vertical at the moment of sinking, seems to freeze between sky and sea. The expulsion drives the entity out of the world — literally shows it the door, which is the ordinary schoolroom sense of *abweisen*. Heidegger's 1949 note glosses the phrase precisely: to repel concerns the entity taken for itself, without its relation to being; to refer means to refer into the being of the entity. So the expulsion of the entity from the world is only anxiety's first moment; its work continues in the referral — made possible by the entity's slipping and foundering — to being. Being does not appear once the shipwreck of entities in totality is consummated, as the supreme one among the wreckage; being is not discovered as the last piece of flotsam of the entity's sepulchral shipwreck, nor, in Husserl's idiom, as a residue. Being manifests itself here in a pure movement of referral that signals back toward it in the strict and inversely proportional measure in which the entity is assigned to expulsion. So being appears not as anxiety's terminus — result, residue, hence still an entity — but as the accomplishment of its movement. Anxiety produces being by putting itself forward as expulsion of the entity and, inseparably, referral to being. Anxiety thereby performs a phenomenological reduction by leading entities in totality back to being. And that reduction takes up some of the most fundamental traits of the 1927 reduction, as the use of referral in 1925 and 1927 to define the phenomenon itself confirms: the possibility of appearance as referral is founded in the phenomenon in the proper sense, in self-showing; appearance means a referral-relation, itself an entity, toward an entity.

An objection forces a fourth trait. The 1929 reduction diverges from the analytic of Dasein in the identity of its terminus: on the strength of Heidegger's later re-reading, the reduction of entities in totality by anxiety was assigned the being of the entity as terminus, yet what recurs more explicitly and more often as terminus is the nothing itself. What relation holds between them? And how can we recover in 1929 the essential triplicity of terms that alone seemed to permit the redoubled reduction? Marion retraces the stages. There are at least two, deploying three terms and not two. The first leads from entities in totality to the being of the entity by a referral that expels, or inversely an expulsion that refers. One must not object too quickly that this first stage leads only to the nothing, for the nothing itself constitutes the transgression of the entity toward the being of the entity — explicitly so in the first 1929 redaction: in the being of the entity the nihilation of the nothing occurs; the nothing does not remain the indeterminate opposite of the entity but discloses itself as belonging to the being of the entity. The nothing arrives first as the nothing of the entity, its ontic annihilation; but the nihilation of the nothing immediately inverts the relation, since annihilation would itself remain impossible and inconceivable if the nothing, as the nothing of the entity, did not reveal the first flash of another relation of the entity — the being of the entity. The nothing as such not only annihilates the entity but annuls it by a transcendence that manifests its role as spokesman for the being of the entity. So we do recover, transposed from Dasein as privileged entity to entities in totality, the first relaunch and retrieval of the reduction accomplished in 1925 and 1927 from entity to being of the entity. In 1929 what is interrogated is entities in totality; what is asked about keeps the same identity as in 1927, the being of the entity, unveiled in the nothing of entities in general. But what of what one wants to find out? To satisfy the third dimension of questioning one would have to disengage a second retrieval of the reduction and name what 1925 aimed at under the title sense of being. Marion notes first, in mitigation, that *Sein und Zeit* evidently does not always manage to decide the sense of being, still less to expose it concretely at the end of the unfinished analytic; the sense of being remains more the horizon of a project than the accomplishment of a route. That indeterminacy makes the difficulty of fixing the third term in 1929 more forgivable. As a simple hypothesis, Marion risks an equivalence: what *Sein und Zeit* designates without truly reaching it as sense of being, the 1929 lecture aims at — without expressly including it — under the name of ontological difference. So far as he knows, the lecture does not mention the phrase in the body of its first redaction; but from 1927 in the courses and from 1928 in the published texts, ontological difference determines the horizon of the research, so much so that in 1931 Heidegger could complete his 1929 text by introducing self-commentaries mentioning it. Marion lists them: what essential discord is unveiled here becomes ontological difference; the nothing differs radically from the entity is expounded by the difference; we should rather say that in anxiety the nothing is encountered at one stroke with the entity — what does at one stroke mean? takes the gloss the difference; and the closing description, that the essence of the originarily nihilating nothing lies in bringing Da-sein for the first time before the entity as such, is confirmed by properly before the being of the entity, before the difference. From 1928–29 the watchword and guiding thread of the analyses becomes, ever more expressly, bringing ontological difference to light, and it will play *ad nauseam* the canonical role of what one seeks to know through and beyond whatever one asks of any entity. It is unsurprising, then, that even in the 1929 text where it does not yet appear explicitly it already determines the whole question to be constructed. On that hypothesis — and Marion is careful that it is only a hypothesis — the third element of a phenomenologically deployed question would be secured, and with it the goal of the second retrieval of the reduction: to lead the being of the entity back to ontological difference, which is to say to manifest the gap between entity and being — the *of* — from the standpoint not of the entity, even already reduced to nothing, but of being as such.

The two retrievals of the phenomenological reduction converge on one goal: to receive being itself in pure givenness in person and as phenomenon. Heidegger tries to include in phenomenological evidencing not merely the entity, possibly led back to objectivity, but the nothing that transcends every entity — being. Being means appearing; not something relative that occasionally happens to being, but being unfolds precisely as appearing. It remains to determine whether either retrieval accomplishes its design, and above all to understand why Heidegger successively mobilises two implementations of a reduction extended to being; that these questions admit a coherent answer is not self-evident, and Marion defers them — to chapter III for the doubling by Dasein, chapter VI for the doubling by the nothing, and chapter IV for the relation between the two. But before that, one point must be underlined: at the very moment he rejects and claims to overstep Husserl's reduction as non-phenomenological, Heidegger can still legitimately call himself Husserl's disciple, since it is always by virtue of phenomenology's method and the principle of a return to the things themselves that the second and radical reduction — of all entities to the being of the entity — unfolds. Heidegger no more abandons Husserlian phenomenology than he refutes it; he relaunches an impetus temporarily slowed, because he dares to ask of its possibility what its actuality could no longer give — being as phenomenon. For what is essential about phenomenology is not that it is actual as a philosophical tendency: higher than actuality stands possibility. Heidegger held to that appraisal to the end, describing phenomenology not as a tendency but as the possibility of thinking that changes with the times and thereby remains, the possibility of corresponding to the claim of what is to be thought — a formulation in which the word *Anspruch*, claim, quietly anticipates the third reduction of chapter VI. That being should appear comes to phenomenology only in the mode of possibility. But can that possibility be accomplished in fact?

## III. The Ego and Dasein

### 1 - The Figure of Descartes in Heidegger's Itinerary

Heidegger's confrontations with Nietzsche, Hegel, Kant and Aristotle go without saying; his relation to Descartes can look secondary, and neither Heidegger's commentators nor Descartes scholars insist on it when they do not ignore it outright. Whatever the reasons for that neglect, Marion counters with a brute fact: chronologically, Descartes appears at the very beginning of Heidegger's career and occupies him almost to its end. On the texts available in the *Gesamtausgabe* as of 1985, the outer limits of the discussion run from 1921 to 1974. The point of this section is entirely evidentiary — Marion is establishing a fact whose conceptual motive §2 will supply.

The winter semester course of 1921/22, *Phenomenological Interpretations of Aristotle*, sketches a general introduction to phenomenology rather than treating Aristotle, and the philosopher it does address is Descartes. Examining the metaphysics and idealism of the I in its Kantian and phenomenological figures, Heidegger arrives at Descartes and already marks the limit: the *sum* is indeed first for Descartes, but the failing lies just here, since he does not stay with it and already has a pre-concept of the sense of being as mere ascertainment and even as the indubitable. That Descartes could deviate into an epistemological question, and even inaugurate it, only expresses that the *sum*, its being and its categorial structure, was never a problem for him, and that the word was understood in an indifferent, formally objective, uncritical and unclarified sense. Descartes, on this early reading, privileges the *ego* in its certainty and takes the *sum* over without mediation: the mode of being illustrated by the *sum* is taken in its supposedly obvious and common sense, and so is in fact thought from the sense of *esse* that suits objects. He privileges the question of the *ego*, whence the inauguration of a theory of knowledge, and passes over the question of the *sum* in silence, whence an objectifying interpretation of all *esse*. Marion presses the paradox: under the young Heidegger's gaze Descartes already poses the question of the mode of being of the *sum* precisely by passing it over in favour of a question about the *ego*'s status and power — the weight of the question is placed from the outset, without motive and following the traditional attitude, on the I, so that the sense of that I stays essentially indeterminate, instead of on the sense of the *am*. The essential is marked at once: within the "I think" of the "I think, therefore I am," the I must be determined from the sense of being and not from its own sense as I.

The confrontation broadens during the Marburg years, which open and close with a course explicitly on Descartes. The first, in winter 1923/24, introduced modern philosophy, and the last Marburg course of summer 1928 confirms that it took up Descartes and anticipated *Sein und Zeit* §§19–21. That last course also treats Descartes as prolonged in Leibniz, who like Descartes sees in the I, in the *ego cogito*, the dimension from which all fundamental metaphysical concepts must be drawn — the attempt to solve the problem of being as metaphysics' fundamental problem by returning to the subject, a return that stays ambiguous in Leibniz as in his predecessor and successors because the I is not grasped in its essential structure and specific mode of being. Marion draws the conclusion: Heidegger discerns from the origin of his path the determining importance of Descartes, but not where his contemporaries saw it, in the establishment of the *ego* as transcendental or quasi-transcendental principle. He locates it in what Descartes conceals behind the evidence and dignity of the *ego cogito* — the indeterminacy of the manner of being of that *ego*, whose *sum* stays so indeterminate that it falls under the mode of being of objects. Heidegger no longer interrogates the *ego cogito* about the cogitative origin of its primacy but about the ontological indeterminacy of the *esse* within it, hence about what it conceals of itself rather than what it proclaims of itself. That concealment, first located in the indeterminacy of the being of the I, calls for phenomenological examination, since phenomenology bears par excellence on what of itself does not manifest itself. Marion's point is structural, not biographical: the conversation with Descartes marks Heidegger's strictly phenomenological point of departure more than any other confrontation.

The late texts confirm it. In 1969 the second Le Thor seminar recalls Descartes' epochal position — what happened between Hegel and the Greeks is Descartes' thought; and with Fichte one witnesses the absolutisation of the Cartesian *cogito*, which is *cogito* only insofar as it is *cogito me cogitare*, into absolute knowing. In 1973 the Zähringen seminar carries the reading of the Cartesian *ego* to its peak from the question of being: subjectivity itself is not put in question as to its being, since from Descartes on it is the *fundamentum inconcussum*, so that in all modern thought subjectivity constitutes the barrier to setting the question of being in motion. In 1974 one of the last texts, *Der Fehl heiliger Namen*, still signals that barrier while taking up the theme of the first Marburg course: at the beginning of modern thought stand treatises on method, prior to any clarification of the matter of thinking — the *Discourse* and the *Regulae*. Chronologically at least, Heidegger's thought never stops meeting Descartes', in a confrontation at least as constant as those with Nietzsche or Aristotle. But Marion is careful about what the fact establishes: textual data confirmed by the abundance of Cartesian references in the mature works suffice to establish the encounter and demand its comprehension, not to clarify it. The abundance becomes intelligible only when concepts motivate it. What conceptually identifiable reason forces Heidegger, from beginning to end, to argue about and with Descartes?

### 2 - The Phenomenological Motive of the Original Confrontation

At the very moment Heidegger was expounding and criticising Descartes at Marburg, Husserl was expounding and approving him at Freiburg, in the winter 1923/24 course that became *First Philosophy*: even when he happened to hold a false theory, Descartes' philosophical genius led him to sow the seeds of transcendental philosophy. Husserl had not waited for that date, or *a fortiori* for the *Cartesian Meditations* of 1929, to put Descartes at the centre; the 1907 Göttingen lectures had already done so, and probably earlier texts too. Phenomenology in its Husserlian figure had thus bound its fate to its interpretation of Descartes before Heidegger arrived, so that nothing phenomenological could be decided in principle without a discussion of Descartes. Marion's formulation is exact and is the hinge of the whole chapter: the Descartes Heidegger encounters already has the status of a phenomenological motive, if not the rank of a phenomenologist, and he appears as a phenomenologist first positively, by way of Husserl. Husserl's authority, especially after the turn of 1907, has invested Descartes with such phenomenological dignity that any discussion of Descartes amounts to a discussion with Husserl — more exactly, any discussion of the Cartesian theses Husserl legitimated amounts to a theoretical discussion of Husserl himself. The equivalence can then be worked in two contrary directions: either Descartes is a phenomenologist because he anticipates Husserl, or Husserlian phenomenology is not fully phenomenological because it stays captive to uncriticised, indeed undiscerned, Cartesian decisions. Heidegger takes the second direction very early, and his distance from Husserl's interpretation of phenomenology is accomplished by criticising the Cartesian presuppositions within it. Descartes will undergo a critique that is aimed first and equally at Husserl, who is the less a phenomenologist the more he remains a Cartesian. So Descartes emerges as Husserl's non-phenomenological motive — which is why chapter III, apparently a detour into Descartes scholarship, is the direct continuation of chapter II's charge that Husserl's phenomenology is not phenomenological.

The 1925 *Prolegomena* accordingly attempt an immanent critique of phenomenological research by examining how it determines pure consciousness. The question is whether, in Husserl's elaboration of intentionality as phenomenology's thematic field, the question about the being of that region is constructed at all — what being means here when the sphere of consciousness is called a region of absolute being, what absolute being means, what being means when said of the transcendent world and the reality of things; whether within phenomenology the methodological ground has been won for making it possible to construct that question about the sense of being which every consideration must first traverse and which remains unquestioned there; whether and how the region consciousness is determined in its being. Marion notes the exact repetition: in 1925 Heidegger puts to Husserl and the region consciousness the same question and the same critique he had put in 1921 to Descartes and the *ego cogito* — establishing the epistemological priority of the *ego* and of consciousness is an achievement, but it does not dispense one from determining that first term's mode of being. Descartes repeats himself in Husserl, positively in bringing to light the condition of all certainty in knowledge, and negatively in the forgetful evasion of the mode of being proper to originary certainty. Husserl did indeed encounter and note an unbridgeable essential difference between consciousness and the reality of the world, a true abyss of sense. But can he for that reason simply see there the gap with a necessary and absolute being? Does it suffice, in order to think an epistemic gap, to name an ontico-ontological one, as though from consciousness's irreducibility to what it constitutes there followed the principial difference of manners of being, the most cardinal there is, between consciousness and reality? Husserl would have had to do more than repeat the epistemic terms of the opposition — consciousness absolutely certain because knowing, over against reality contingent and relative because known — and would have had to elaborate the two manners of being. Instead he reasons, in sketching them, within a pair, certainty and contingency, that belongs entirely to the mode of being of worldly reality alone and so falls wholly under being understood as permanent subsistence in the present. Like Descartes, Husserl confines himself to the being of a reality improper to consciousness and so evades the supposedly principial question of its manner of being; consciousness pays for its epistemic primacy with an implicit but total submission to the manner of being of reality, hence of the world. And Husserl performs that desertion by leaning explicitly on Descartes, citing him verbatim in order at once to define and to obscure consciousness's manner of being: immanent being is indubitably absolute being in that it in principle needs no "thing" in order to exist.

Marion pauses over that citation with philological care, and the five remarks are worth following because they turn a footnote into an argument. (a) Husserl does claim to define consciousness's manner of being, since he deduces absolute being from immanent being. (b) He cites Descartes' *Principia* I §51: by substance we can understand nothing but a thing that exists in such a way as to need no other thing in order to exist. The meeting is no accident, since the two thinkers, already agreed on the *ego*'s epistemic primacy, converge again on defining its manner of being by substantiality. (c) Husserl modifies the formula: he drops *alia* from *alia re* and accepts *res* only in quotation marks. Why? Because *alia res* would imply that consciousness is itself, and first, a *res*, whereas Husserl is precisely opposing consciousness to reality; so in defiance of philological probity he alters what in the citation would extend reality to the *res cogitans*, keeping only the application of substantiality to the *ego*. (d) The doctoring proves that Husserl is using an insufficient and unsuited Cartesian determination: for Descartes substantiality covers not only the *res cogitans* but, not without difficulty, the whole external thing, so that it contradicts rather than confirms Husserl's privileging of consciousness — corporeal substance and mind, or thinking substance. A second contradiction could be added: for Descartes every finite substance, thinking as much as extended, confesses a radical indigence toward God's ordinary concurrence, so that substantiality, which the *ego* must share with extension (a first disagreement with Husserl), has only relative and in no way absolute validity (a second). (e) These divergences do not put Husserl's intimate familiarity with Descartes in question; they prove that the underlying convergence outweighed any detailed divergence. Marion's own Descartes work is quietly in play here — the finite/infinite substance distinction that Heidegger and Husserl agree to pass over is exactly what *Sur le prisme métaphysique de Descartes* had made central.

Such an exemplary encounter — Husserl citing Descartes to determine consciousness's manner of being — could not escape Heidegger, and the 1925 course notes the formula and identifies it precisely as a borrowing. It can then stigmatise the ontological insufficiency: immanence, indubitability and absoluteness in no way permit thinking the being of consciousness, since this third determination does not determine the entity itself in its being but grasps the region consciousness within the order of constitution and assigns it there a being formally prior to all objectivity. The Cartesian definition cannot ground the difference of regions, which is ontological. Marion notes the reversal of roles with relish: Heidegger reduces to nothing the textual adjustments Husserl imposes on Descartes' formula, and here it is Heidegger who defends the orthodoxy of the Cartesian text, precisely because he opposes Husserl conceptually. And there is more. Not only does Husserl go astray by taking up and forcing an unsuited Cartesian answer, and not only does he evade the authentic determination of consciousness's manner of being by supposing the simple retrieval of Cartesian certainty enough; he strays more radically still by assuming a Cartesian question he has not phenomenologically legitimated. Marion here returns to the passage already used in chapter II, and its full weight now appears: Husserl's first question is not at all about the being-character of consciousness; what guides him is how consciousness can become the possible object of an absolute science; what leads him primordially is the idea of an absolute science, and that idea is not his discovery but the idea modern philosophy has pursued since Descartes; so the elaboration of pure consciousness as phenomenology's thematic ground is won not phenomenologically by return to the things themselves but by return to a traditional idea of philosophy. Marion measures the critique in three parts. (a) The question of consciousness's manner of being goes unanswered because Husserl stays dependent on Descartes. (b) Evading the authentically phenomenological difficulty of the being of consciousness, Husserl privileges the non-phenomenological ideal of a certain science of consciousness — which places us close to the parricidal declaration of the same course, that phenomenology, before the fundamental task of determining its own field, is non-phenomenological. (c) If Husserl departs from phenomenology, he owes it to the persistence in him of the Cartesian ideal of *mathesis universalis* and the most universal wisdom, defined already in the *Regulae*. So far from Descartes having guided Husserl onto the phenomenological path, as Husserl thinks, he played the signal role of holding him back from it: between Husserl and full phenomenology, hence between Husserl and Heidegger, Descartes stands as the sole obstacle and stumbling block. The affinity binding Husserl to Descartes — Heidegger's own word, and one he applies precisely at the point where Husserl says his meditation reaches its summit — designates a single phenomenological obstacle that phenomenology must surmount to remain itself. Henceforth, to advance along the phenomenological path Husserl abandons, Heidegger must not only leave Husserl but destroy the one who held Husserl back — Descartes himself.

We can now see why Descartes occupies Heidegger so much: the chronological weight of the debate follows from the phenomenological radicality of the question Descartes poses precisely by not posing it. To think Descartes means for Heidegger not to repeat the institution of the *ego*, as Hegel, Schelling and Husserl each did in their way, nor to invert it as Nietzsche did, but to destroy it, so as to make appear, as the phenomenon it had until then concealed, the mode of being of the *ego* — or of whatever must replace it — as distinguished from the mode of being of intraworldly entities. Destroying the *ego* does not reduce to abolishing it ontically; it undertakes to free its ontological dignity. Destroying the *ego* opens access to Dasein. In that sense Descartes has no privilege in Heidegger's thought other than that of the obstacle par excellence, which forbids phenomenology's ontological accomplishment by blocking it with the *ego* and thereby masking Dasein.

### 3 - The First Failure: The Indeterminacy of the "ego sum"

In 1927, in line with what had been sketched since 1921, Descartes appears in *Sein und Zeit* as an extreme counter-case. As extreme counter-case to the ontological problematic of worldhood, Descartes pushes phenomenology to its last extremity by misconstruing the manner of being of worldly entities; but in doing so he puts in question the manner of being of all entities, beginning with Dasein. Since the interpretation of the world first takes its departure from an intraworldly entity and thereafter loses sight of the phenomenon of world entirely, Heidegger proposes to clarify that starting point ontologically by considering the most extreme development it ever received — Cartesian ontology of the world. At that extremity what is also at stake is the phenomenological destruction of the *cogito sum*, announced as the third part of the debate with Descartes after §§19–20, barely sketched in §21, and finally deferred to the unpublished Part Two, Division Two. The reproach therefore bears on two failings, one toward the world and one toward the *ego*, whose two manners of being are equally though differently missed. Marion insists on a point of order that changes the standard picture: the reproach to Descartes precedes the famous analysis of the *res extensa* in §§18–21, where it finds only a first confirmation, and appears first about the *cogito sum*, already in the introduction — an introduction that covers the whole plan announced in §8, hence the unpublished part too. The principle that institutes subjectivity in all modern philosophy has two characters: it claims to state an absolutely certain beginning, and in the same stroke it misses the thought of being within it, masking the *esse* in the *sum*, itself always left unthought in the shadow cast by the *ego*, alone thought in evidence. Since in that history determinate and privileged sectors of being are taken into view and primarily guide the problematic — Descartes' *ego cogito*, the subject, the I, reason, spirit, the person — these sectors, in accordance with the complete failure of the question of being, remain unquestioned as to their being and the structure of their being. And again: Kant, taking over Descartes' ontological position, is led to another essential failure, that of an ontology of Dasein; the failure conforms to Descartes' ownmost tendency and is decisive, since with the *cogito sum* Descartes claims to procure a new and sure ground for philosophy while leaving indeterminate, in that radical beginning, the mode of being of the *res cogitans*, more exactly the sense of being of the *sum*. Elaborating the implicit ontological foundations of the *cogito sum* marks the second stage of the destructive return through the history of ontology, and the interpretation proves not only that Descartes had necessarily to miss the question of being in general but shows why he could form the opinion that the *cogito*'s absolute being-assured dispensed him from asking after that entity's sense of being. Marion draws three consequences. (a) §6 impugns Descartes first and foremost about the sense of being of the *sum*; the Cartesian failure of the sense of being in general is remarked first and foremost on the *ego cogito*, and only the order of Part One and the absence of Part Two can give readers the impression that Heidegger privileges the doctrine of the *res extensa*. That doctrine involves only a particular failing, to think the phenomenon of world, inscribed within the universal failing to think the manner of being of entities and first of Dasein. (b) Yet the *ego cogito* and the *res extensa* offer two comparable failures. Descartes misconstrues the *ego*'s manner of being because he holds to the certainty of its existence without distinguishing such an epistemic category from an ontologically determined existential; and if he holds to certainty here, it is because he transposes it into the *ego* from the domain where he first tested it epistemically — the object of methodical science, hence extension. Marion states the circular dependence precisely: if epistemically the object depends on the *ego* according to a tacit and undecided ontology, a grey ontology, the *ego* in turn borrows from the *res extensa* to accomplish its own interpretation by certainty. Both failures deploy the same insufficiency, indeterminacy of the sense of being. (c) The two dimensions of that single insufficiency anticipate exactly the two regions Husserl distinguishes, the absolute region of consciousness and the relative region of worldly things; and as Descartes fails to think them as such, so Husserl fails to think their respective senses of being. The two failings must therefore be taken up as integral parts of the destruction of the history of ontology, hence positively as a breakthrough past the phenomenological obstacle Descartes presents.

Usually taken as the thinker of the *cogito sum*, Descartes could more justly be characterised by a radical inability to think that very *cogito sum*, or at least to think the *sum* in it from the *esse*; on the contrary he reduces *sum* to *cogito* and *cogito* to *ego*. The *ego* itself is characterised only epistemically, as the absolutely certain first principle making certain knowledge of other entities possible. The extension of certainty, running back from the known entity to the knowing *ego*, satisfies the demands of method by generalising them, and does so only by leaving the question of the sense of being for the *ego* exactly proportionally indeterminate and in shadow. The more epistemic certainty invades ever wider sectors of entities so as to homogenise them as so many *cogitata*, the more the whole of entities betrays the basic indeterminacy in which forgetfulness of any question about what being means each time for each entity leaves them. This strikes the *ego* first, which by absorbing *esse* into *sum* and *sum* into itself certainly secures nothing in itself but its own ontological failing. That indeterminacy marks Descartes' first and radical failure: a total ontological indeterminacy of the *res cogitans sive mens sive animus*; Descartes, credited with discovering the *cogito sum* as the starting point of modern philosophical questioning, examined the *cogitare* of the *ego* within certain limits, but left the *sum* wholly unelucidated even though he posits it just as originally. Marion is careful to say what Heidegger is not doing. He never contests the certainty of the knowledge of the *ego* as *cogito*, and it is remarkable that he never enters the common, easy and idle debate about the certainty of the reasons demonstrating the *ego*'s first, indubitable and necessary existence. His point is entirely different: that epistemic certainty, which delivers the *ego* as the first certain object for the knowledge it finally is itself, does not suffice to determine ontologically its own manner of being. By his very silence Descartes postulates the univocity of certainty, which keeps the same sense and validity as it runs back from known objects to knowing subject; and that univocity, like the medieval univocity of being, rests only on a basic indeterminacy. More: certainty is not only ontologically indeterminate but indifferent to the question of manners of being. Descartes first claims that certainty applies in the same sense across the heterogeneous series *cogitatum*–*cogito*–*ego*; then he postulates that just as the *cogitatum* is supposed, since the grey ontology of the *Regulae*, to find the correct determination of its mode of being in certainty, so the *ego* requires no other determination of its sense of being than certainty again and only certainty. The certainty of the *ego cogito* therefore does not abolish the indeterminacy of the *sum* and the *esse* within it but reinforces it. The evident certainty of the *cogito* only permits Descartes to desert all questioning about the mode of being that certainty itself implies, and leads him to take its sense of being as self-evident — as he in fact answered Hobbes, that the distinction of essence from existence is known to everyone. So Descartes not only misses the question of the sense of being of the *sum*; he masks that miss itself, blinded by the epistemic evidence of the *cogito*. Marion's formula for it: Descartes' first failure is accomplished by failing itself.

That failure of the failure nevertheless decides the *ego*'s manner of being, precisely because it does not determine it explicitly. If Descartes does not think his *sum* as such, he will think it implicitly on the model of the intraworldly entity, following a back-radiation of the understanding of world onto the explication of Dasein, since Dasein inclines to fall onto the world in which it is and to interpret itself reflectively from it. The manner of being of the intraworldly entity becomes, by the very fact that any approach to the sense of being of the *ego* is missing, the pole of attraction and interpretation for the manner of being of the non-intraworldly entity. The Cartesian *ego* — like its substitutes and derivatives across the metaphysical tradition, its Husserlian avatar included — differs essentially from Dasein in this: it is not according to its own manner of being, hence is not thought according to its own manner, but from the outset and always runs aground on the intraworldly entity and imports from it an improper manner of being. It is certainly an *ego* only by not properly being according to its being — epistemic certainty, ontologically indeterminate. Marion's closing sentence turns the *cogito*'s triumph into its defeat: the Cartesian *ego* loses itself at the very moment it finds itself, and precisely because it finds itself on the mode of certainty.

### 4 - The Second Failure: The Permanence of the Intraworldly Entity

Impropriety redoubles here. Just as the Cartesian interpretation of the *ego* misses its manner of being and also fails to understand that first failure, and just as the absence of that interpretation delivers the *ego* to engulfment in the mode of being of intraworldly entities to which in principle it does not belong, so the interpretation of the mode of being of intraworldly entities misses the phenomenon of world and substitutes for it the univocal and minimal subsistence of the present-at-hand. On the famous, ambiguous and short-lived analysis of §§15–18, the worldhood of the world shows itself less in the subsistence of present-at-hand entities than in their play as handy, ready-to-hand equipment; in that play the entity is defined by what it can serve for, in a finality that under the various aspects of interest, utility, function and organisation depends ultimately on what it is about, hence on Dasein itself, which thereby opens the world in its worldhood. Presence-at-hand follows from readiness-to-hand only by reduction and impoverishment of the ready-to-hand entity to the sole demands of theory: the object required by the theoretical attitude has only to persist, isolated like an atom of evidence, permanent like a perfect subsistence, neutralising all finality as purely objective. The object of the theoretical attitude is obtained by reduction, abstraction and method; it does not precede the usable and ready-to-hand entity but follows from it by impoverishment and elimination. That operation, which also inverts the phenomenological pre-eminence of readiness-to-hand over presence-at-hand, derives from Descartes. The privilege method accords mathematical knowledge rests for him not on any intrinsic excellence of that science but on its capacity to attain the certainty and permanent subsistence of an object; the primacy of mathematics results from the privilege granted from the outset to permanent subsistence alone, to certain objectivity as the sole sense of being of the intraworldly entity. What has a mode of being such as to satisfy the being accessible to mathematical knowledge is in the proper sense; that entity is what is always what it is, so that what constitutes the proper being of the entity experienced in the world is what can be shown to have the character of constant remaining, as that which remains capable of changes. Far from letting the intraworldly entity give him in advance that entity's mode of being, Descartes prescribes to the world its true being on the basis of an idea of being — being equals constant presence-at-hand — that is neither legitimated in its right nor unveiled in its origin. The permanence of the entity as present-at-hand object fixes the sense of being of the intraworldly entity only by degrading it into a sense that certainty imposes, at the expense of the phenomenality of the world. The interpretation of entities in general as permanent presence-at-hand does not merely miss the sense of being of the *ego* by leaving the *sum* indeterminate within it; it also and first misses the sense of being of the intraworldly entity whose perfect knowledge it nonetheless claims to secure. The two failures rejoin in one more originary failing to think the being of any entity.

Marion then steps out of the exposition to assess it as a historian of philosophy — a rare and useful move, since it lets him back Heidegger where he is right and correct him where he is not. Three remarks. (a) Heidegger confirms that constant presence-at-hand occludes the sense of being by noting the Cartesian interpretation of *res extensa* as substance, itself reduced to what *remains* through every reduction. The reference is exact, but it masks another that attributes permanence first and directly to the *ego* before the *res extensa*: before asking whether the same wax remains and answering that it must be admitted to remain — before, that is, encountering the *res extensa*, which does not in fact figure in the wax analysis at all — Descartes had already reduced the *ego* to the *cogito* so that at last there should remain precisely only what is certain and unshaken. If permanence characterises certainty as a (missed) manner of being, it should intervene at the first certainty, and in fact it does, with the *ego*'s existence; so the diagnosis of permanent subsistence should have been conducted apropos the *ego*: each time it thinks, the *ego* remains. Marion finds the omission surprising in so precise a reader of Descartes, and all the more so because this first remanence confirms rather than weakens *Sein und Zeit*'s general thesis that presence-at-hand does not determine only intraworldly entities but flows back by reflection onto the *ego* itself and closes off all access to its true being. One may fairly reply that §§19–21, treating only worldhood, had no occasion to mention a text about the presence-at-hand of the *ego*. Even granting that, another question arises: should Heidegger have used the *ego*'s remanence in the unwritten Part Two, Division Two, on the ontological foundation of the *cogito sum*? On that hypothesis alone he would have drawn more from a text that confirms him at the very moment he seems to ignore it. (b) The failure of the sense of being in general shows in the Cartesian texts as the insufficiency of the doctrine of substance. On the one hand, as Heidegger pertinently notes, substance is held not to affect us directly, since it cannot be noticed first from the mere fact that it is an existing thing, that alone not affecting us of itself. The inquiry into substance therefore turns at once into an inquiry into its principal attribute, substance itself remaining in principle unknown in itself. A basic equivocation of the term follows, confusing its ontological with its ontic sense so as to evade the former all the more easily and take refuge in the latter. The debate Descartes foregrounds about the distinction of finite and infinite substance only reinforces the underlying orientation toward the ontic sense alone; in no way does the Cartesian treatise on substance in *Principia* I §§51–54 resume Aristotle's at least ontologically intended discussion of *ousia* in *Metaphysics* Z. Marion endorses this reproach essentially. A second and less visible but more important critique deepens it: in subordinating the ontological to the ontic in substance, Descartes necessarily confuses ontological difference — the ontic being substituted for the ontological, *substantia* functions now in an ontological, now in an ontic sense, most often in a confused ontico-ontological one, and what shelters in that imperceptible difference of signification is the inability to master the fundamental problem of being. To that fundamental problem of being Heidegger adds, in his own copy, a marginal note: ontological difference. Marion calls the addition decisive, and it does two things. It reveals that Descartes, by obscuring the ontological within substance, first produced the aporia in which Husserl would remain caught when he imagined he could distinguish substances or regions by ontic criteria alone without distinguishing their respective modes of being. And it reveals that Descartes failed to confront the difference between being and entities, which alone would have permitted an ontological fixing of the distinction between entities or substances. The convergence of the two failures follows finally from the original evasion of ontological difference. Reintegrating Descartes into the history of metaphysics by what *Sein und Zeit* still calls only destruction of the history of ontology had in the end to bring to light in him metaphysics' essential trait, the misconstrual of the difference between being and entities. Since that difference remains implicit in *Sein und Zeit*, though really at work, it stigmatises Descartes only in the form of the two failures of the sense of being of entities. That suffices to disengage the ontologically Cartesian genealogy of Husserl's phenomenological insufficiencies, which was the point to be proved — and it opens directly onto chapter IV, where ontological difference and the question of being will be set against each other. (c) Could one not object that Descartes does elaborate a thought of the world, since worldhood becomes an explicit problem when the *ego* asks whether it is not alone in the world, and again when the Sixth Meditation undertakes to prove the world's existence? Marion turns both references into arguments for *Sein und Zeit*. In the first case the *ego* accedes to other possible entities only from itself, that is, from the ideas it can have of them; representation therefore determines them in advance as certain objects, hence according to subsistent persistence, God being no exception to that determination and, symptomatically, other people finding no free place in it. In the second case the very fact that the existence of the external world must be proved constitutes the genuine phenomenological scandal — more than the absence of a convincing proof, which is what Kant deplored while taking over the Cartesian design. For the world can owe its existence to such a proof only insofar as it has first been reduced to the rank of a representation awaiting actuality, that is, presence-at-hand. To prove or fail to prove the world's existence presupposes that one has already missed the worldhood of the world, its appearance in the phenomenological horizon.

Descartes' two failures therefore make one: having grasped the being of Dasein only in the same mode as the being of the *res extensa*, as substance. And by that he determines Kant, for whom the consciousness of my existence means the consciousness of my presence-at-hand in Descartes' sense, so that the term *Dasein* designates the presence-at-hand of consciousness as much as that of things. Marion lets the point stand without comment, and it does the work: the word Heidegger will reserve for the entity that is not present-at-hand is precisely the word Kant used for presence-at-hand.

### 5 - Dasein as "Destruction" of the "ego"

Descartes' two failures to think the sense of being lead back to a single inability to think the being of entities without recourse to presence-at-hand, and that inability itself results from misconstruing ontological difference, understood at least in its negative formulation: being is never explicable from entities. (Heidegger's own marginal note on that sentence again reads: ontological difference.) Descartes, and after him Kant no less than Hegel, sets up the *ego* as a privileged entity to the point that it must account for other entities and stand in for every sense of being in them, guaranteeing them ontically and legitimating them ontologically. But at the same moment and to a growing degree its own sense of being remains, first of all, wholly indeterminate. The indeterminacy of the *ego cogito* in its mode of being invades all other entities and strips them of ontological ground — the ontological groundlessness of the problematic of the Self from Descartes' *res cogitans* to Hegel's concept of spirit; and if idealism means leading all entities back to a subject or a consciousness whose sole distinctive privilege is to remain indeterminate in their being and to be characterisable at best negatively as non-things, then such idealism is methodologically no less naive than the crudest realism. Consequently what separates Descartes and those he made possible from the question of the sense of being is exactly what separates the *ego cogito* from Dasein. Marion then reads the two terms against each other morphologically. Dasein keeps an echo of what the *ego cogito* already exhibits: the *Da*-, here itself, the unique place where everything else can subsequently take place. But with the *ego cogito* the rest has the status only of *cogitatum*, because as *ego* I confine myself to *cogitare*; whereas from Dasein the *Da*- apportions to the rest of entities nothing less than *sein*, nothing less than being. Where the *ego* gives to be thought, or rather gives itself to be thought and even to be mere thought, without ever giving being in a determined and determining sense, Dasein gives being by determining the manner of being of other entities, because it itself in advance determines itself to be according to its own manner of being. The *ego* is, certainly, but it is without thinking about it, since it thinks only about thinking its thinkables; it therefore no more fixes their respective manners of being than it has determined itself in its own. Thinking itself as being only through and for the exercise of thinking, it masks — first by the epistemic evidence of its own ontologically unstable existence, then by the certainty of other subsistent truths — the total absence of any decision about the being of entities, reduced to pure and simple *cogitata*. *Ego cogito*, not *ego sum*, and not Dasein: the very formula Descartes privileges betrays what indeterminacy disqualifies it ontologically and which two failures it accomplishes. The whole interpretation of Descartes in *Sein und Zeit* should therefore be thematisable in the single opposition of *ego cogito* to Dasein, in accordance with the declaration of principle that the *res cogitans* coincides with Dasein neither ontically nor ontologically.

The ontic opposition first. The *res cogitans* does not cover Dasein: it has only an ontic consciousness of itself from Dasein's standpoint, while Dasein does not identify itself, from the *res cogitans*'s standpoint, as being another *res cogitans*. Heidegger never sets this out explicitly, but Marion reconstructs it along three lines. (a) The *ego* is a *res* sharing the reality of intraworldly entities, whether present-at-hand or ready-to-hand, whereas the being of Dasein is delimited against the modes of being — readiness-to-hand, presence-at-hand, reality — that characterise entities not of Dasein's measure. The *res* of the *ego* leads to Husserl's inability effectively to distinguish the region consciousness from the region world; Dasein by contrast counts among no real terms and admits nothing real in itself, because it precedes and makes possible the mode of being of reality. (b) The *ego* is defined by the absolute primacy in it of the theoretical attitude; it is born of doubt, and doubt itself becomes practicable only once every immediate, pressing, useful and necessitated relation has disappeared — no conversation, no cares or passions, freed from all cares. By contrast, scientific research is neither the only nor the nearest possible mode of being of Dasein, which relates to the world by way of concern, handling and using entities as ready-to-hand, hence with no disinterestedness whatever; the theoretical attitude accrues to Dasein only afterward and as if by subtraction, since for knowing to become possible as a contemplative determination of the present-at-hand, a deficiency in concernful dealings with the world is first required. Dasein does not simply hold the theoretical attitude by rejecting the so-called natural one — which is in fact concern using entities as ready-to-hand — but sustains and oversteps both, because more radically it is Dasein that ontologically makes both possible in advance. (c) The *res cogitans* confines itself to thinking and relegates extension to other *res*, along an almost irremediable cut; consequently it escapes space, which it also lets escape. Dasein, by contrast, since it is not first defined by representation of the present-at-hand entity, does not exclude a fundamental spatiality. Dasein's spatiality lies in the de-distancing by which it abolishes an entity's remoteness from it; that annulment of remoteness modulates Dasein's original ecstasis, its being-in-the-world. Against the idealist subject issuing from the *ego cogito*, the ontologically well-understood subject, Dasein, is spatial. Marion's formulation is exact: Dasein is neither non-extended in the manner of the *ego cogito* nor extended in the manner of the material *res*; it is spatial, that is, not non-extended. So Dasein, in refusing the common title of *res*, does not restrict itself over against the *res cogitans* but oversteps it, being confined neither to the theoretical attitude nor to non-extension. Taken as an entity, Dasein does not coincide with the *res cogitans*.

But since Dasein's ontic characteristic consists in its being ontological, its ontic opposition to the *res cogitans* can only prepare the ontological opposition, drawn this time from Dasein itself. The *res cogitans* can claim, like Dasein, a manifold primacy, but not an ontological primacy, and on at least three points the opposition becomes irreducible. (a) In Dasein its being is at issue; this entity has as its own to have to decide its mode of being, and in that decision what is at issue is not only its own mode of being but being as such, hence the mode of being of other entities, which have to decide neither. Dasein maintains a surprising relation of uncertainty with itself: far from securing itself by knowing itself as such, it knows itself only by conceding what game is played out in it — the game of its being, or more exactly of being put in play, always to be decided on the occasion of this privileged entity. Dasein knows itself authentically only by recognising itself as an undecided and all the more uncertain stake, one that will never be made certain and never should be. Marion's image is worth keeping: Dasein plays in the sense in which wood plays — it affords a clearance, an articulation, a mobility, so that the everywhere-invisible fold of being may unfold, turning on this entity as a panel on a hinge. Such play, finally beyond both uncertainty and certainty, sets Dasein decidedly against the *ego cogito*. Heidegger is textually wrong to characterise the *ego* as *fundamentum inconcussum*; yet Descartes does aim in it at a foundation on which all certainty can rest, at firm enough foundations, and he does want it unshakeable — a minimum that is certain and unshaken. It is even remarkable that he thinks it according to the persistence of presence-at-hand, as something firm and lasting; more, the *ego* immediately takes the figure of a foundation, better of a self-sufficient holding, a stock entirely my own. In thinking itself, the *ego* seizes itself in full ownership; not only is uncertainty overcome there, but the certainty of that holding, henceforth definitive, will extend to every future *cogitatum*. The *ego* does decide about itself, but so as to abolish all play in self-certainty; and if it decides in future about other entities, it will be to reduce them, as so many *cogitata*, to its own certainty. So Dasein opens a play — that of the being of other entities — through its own, where the *ego* closes all uncertainty, first in itself and then in the *cogitata*. (b) Dasein exists, and existence is in turn defined as possibility: Dasein always understands itself from its existence, from a possibility of itself to be or not to be itself. To exist means to be outside oneself, so as to be only on the mode of being-able-to-be, in conformity with the stake that essentially institutes this entity in a basic play with its being, hence with being itself; existence implies Dasein's ecstasis outside itself into the play of the being about which it falls to it to decide. When the *res cogitans* seizes itself with certainty in saying *ego sum, ego existo*, it immediately interprets its *sum*, hence its being, as an existence. Is it the existence that characterises Dasein? Heidegger says the opposite: if we choose the character of existence to designate the being of this entity, that title does not and cannot have the ontological signification of the traditional term *existentia*, which ontologically means exactly presence-at-hand, a mode of being essentially foreign to the entity that has Dasein's character. Descartes does in fact understand *existentia* as the counterpart of mere possible essence, which it abolishes in certain and univocal permanence; he does not even define existence, taking it as self-evident — no one, he says, was ever so stupid as to need instruction in what existence is before being able to conclude and affirm that he exists. For the *ego cogito*, *existentia* says entry into presence-at-hand; for Dasein, existence means departure from self and transcendence over presence-at-hand, so as to enter the possibility that it definitively is. (c) Finally, to Dasein it belongs essentially to be in a world. Intentionality is not confined, as it is by its Husserlian limit, to the theoretical attitude, because the relation to world does not first depend on the constitution of things; intentionality widens and radicalises to the point of opening the I, from the outset and from itself, onto something like a world, which alone lets the being of other entities be at issue in an entity. That critique of Husserl, which in large part motivated the writing of *Sein und Zeit* and runs through the whole of it, holds against Descartes too, by virtue of the affinity binding them. Descartes reaches the *ego cogito* on the hypothesis of its independence from every possible world: the *ego* appears when and on condition that worldly entities disappear under hyperbolic doubt, and it is defined as a substance whose whole essence or nature is only to think and which needs no place in order to be and depends on no material thing. Heidegger is therefore perfectly entitled to speak, of Husserl and Kant and so of Descartes too, of a worldless I, a worldless subject. Marion adds that the classical difficulties about opening onto a world in Cartesianism need not be rehearsed, since they would confirm the diagnosis. Dasein finds nothing of itself in the *res cogitans*, since the *ego* could be defined from Dasein as its strict inverse: the entity for which its being is not at issue. And reciprocally Dasein could be defined from the *ego cogito* as its inverse: the entity that is not insofar as it thinks (itself). Dasein therefore stands to the *ego cogito* in a relation of destruction.

### 6 - Dasein as Confirmation of the "ego"

Such a relation of destruction would nonetheless make no sense unless an ontology were already at work in the *ego* as it confines itself to thinking, since destruction always bears on the history of ontology. One must therefore suppose the *ego* a metaphysical situation inscribing it in the history of the misconstrued ontological difference, whence a re-examination of the *ego cogito* as still deploying a figure of the being of entities, though in an obscure and forgetful mode. But that epochal supposition would in turn have no legitimacy unless the *ego cogito* could establish its ontological pertinence, however inauthentic and occluded, not merely within the history of ontology but within the new beginning; the *ego* must, if only to play its hermeneutic role toward and within metaphysics, retain in itself a reserve and potentiality of being. It remains to see whether *Sein und Zeit* does justice, even partially, to these two claims of the *ego cogito*. Marion is setting up a deliberate counter-movement: §5 gave the destruction its full force so that §6 could show what survives it.

From the dominant standpoint of its failure, the Cartesian *ego* is absolutely denied any manifestation of the sense of being, a property characterising Dasein alone. Marion proposes to balance rather than weaken that antagonism by noting another relation between the same antagonists. The *ego cogito* offers itself to Dasein as its strictest adversary; yet Dasein would not have so urgent a duty to destroy it if it did not find there, as in a wayward sketch, some of its own most proper traits. Dasein cannot fail to recognise itself in at least four characters of the *ego cogito*, in a rivalry made all the more troubling by the resemblances. (a) Dasein does not have an end at which it simply stops but exists finitely; finitude is not added from outside to an existence that would otherwise merely lack indefinite duration but essentially determines Dasein, which is only toward a term, its own death, according to a temporality of the future. Finitude, marking being-toward-death, opens Dasein's access to its own ecstatic temporality under the privilege of the future, as against the temporality of presence-at-hand, which privileges the present as remaining. Now the *ego cogito* too is characterised by finitude — *cum sim finitus*. And that finitude has not merely an anthropological function (the *ego* must die, it lacks perfections) but a quasi-ontological one, since finitude alone provokes doubt, hence disengages the *cogitatio*, which in turn institutes worldly entities as so many *cogitata* to be constituted. The *ego*'s finitude thus directly determines the sense of being of entities other than the *ego*. The pertinence of the comparison stays masked to and by Heidegger, who envisages the *ego*'s finitude only within the horizon of ancient-Christian anthropology and reduces the relation between finite and infinite substance to efficient production, so as to deny Cartesian finitude any originary validity. It remains that the *ego* can establish neither itself as *cogito* nor, inseparably, worldly entities as *cogitata*, except because it is according to an essential finitude — an implication Heidegger's later meditation on *cogitatio* as representation will keep developing. So Dasein confirms the *ego* according to finitude.

There is more. (b) Dasein is the entity in whose being that being is at issue only on the express condition that this being be its own, in person: its essence lies rather in this, that it has each time to be its being as its own; the being at issue for this entity is each time mine, and the addressing of Dasein must, in accordance with this entity's character of mineness, always include the personal pronoun — I am, you are. Dasein can be itself, namely the one whose own it is to put itself in play as an entity with being as the stake, only in the first person; no one can hold Dasein's role in anyone's stead; the office of Dasein admits no proxy. Even if it is "you are" that is Dasein, that *you* will have to say, in turn, "I am." Dasein, even and especially when played by another than myself, is played in the first person because it must be played in person. So even if Dasein does not first say *ego cogito*, it can say *-sein* only by saying *ich bin*, hence *ego sum*. Marion finds the convergence absolutely decisive, and the reason is Cartesian rather than Heideggerian: Descartes did not merely inaugurate the link between thinking and existence in a subject; he linked them in a subject always interpreted, in the theatrical sense, in the first person — better, in a *persona*, also theatrical, that must be performed in person by assuming the function of an I, by saying "I," *hoc pronuntiatum, Ego*. Descartes' successors tend on the contrary to eliminate that engagement to and of the *ego*, either by replacing the formula with one no one need perform in his own name — man thinks (Spinoza) — or by abolishing it, by subtraction (Malebranche) or by generalisation (Leibniz). Descartes is distinguished then not only by the necessary connection of two simple natures, thinking and existence, but above all by the performance of their necessary link through the unsubstitutable *ego*. Existence accrues to man only insofar as he thinks, but above all insofar as he thinks in the posture of the *ego*. So Descartes comes appreciably close to the unsubstitutability characterising Dasein. Dasein confirms the *ego* according to mineness. (Marion's note is worth folding in: that Cartesian egoity anticipates Heideggerian mineness finds confirmation in the parallel charge of injustice brought against them by Pascal against the *moi* and by Levinas against Dasein — the same objection twice, at three centuries' distance.) (c) Finitude and unsubstitutability accrue to Dasein as the entity in whose being its being is at issue, and this manner of being belongs to it in virtue of being-toward-death, since death is the ownmost, absolute and least surpassable possibility — death as the possibility of the pure and simple impossibility of Dasein. Before its death Dasein finds itself exposed to its own last impossibility, both because death remains ontically inconceivable and because death puts a term to the possibility that Dasein is, still more than to its possibility of doing this or that. The *ego cogito* knows a similar paradox, not about its death but about its freedom, since possibility is disengaged in Cartesian terms with free will, alone formally infinite in the finite *res cogitans*. That free will discloses its own impossibility when confronted with divine omniscience and omnipotence, which annihilate the very notion of the possible; in such an encounter the *ego cogito* faces not only the impossibility of (free) possibility, as theory nevertheless imposes it, but also the possibility of impossibility, since in the practical order it decides to act as if it could act freely, though it does not understand how it can. In every action the *ego cogito* behaves as if it were free and as if the impossible — an event not necessarily predetermined by God — became open again to the possible. The possibility of the impossible can therefore be said of freedom as of being-toward-death. So Dasein confirms the *ego* again, according to the possibility of impossibility.

Even granting indisputable textual bases, it will seem hazardous or specious to infer from these convergences an essential homogeneity between *ego* and Dasein. No formal resemblance seems to balance the critique of the ontological indeterminacy of the *ego cogito*: what Descartes left indeterminate in that radical beginning is the mode of being of the *res cogitans*, more exactly the sense of being of the *sum*; a complete ontological indeterminacy provokes a non-determination of the *res cogitans* and leaves the *cogitationes* ontologically indeterminate. If ontologically *ego* and Dasein differ as the indeterminate from the determinate, must one not conclude that from *Sein und Zeit*'s strictly ontological standpoint they differ absolutely? (d) But it is precisely that indeterminacy which, far from leading to an unmediated opposition, suggests a fourth convergence. Dasein itself — and this is exactly why the existential analytic is required — disengages itself only slowly from an unavoidable indeterminacy. When the existential question about the *who* of Dasein must be answered, the suspicion arises at once that the ontological horizon for determining the entity accessible in pure and simple givenness remains basically indeterminate; more, the being of Dasein itself remains ontologically indeterminate so long as the sole determining phenomena, anxiety and care, have not intervened. The indeterminacy denounced in the *ego cogito* therefore concerns Dasein too, at least provisionally, and tearing free of ontological indeterminacy remains a formidable task for either, to the point that the final §83 can leave one supposing that a sufficient determination of the horizon of givenness has not yet been won. And there is more: indeterminacy, objected to the *ego* and afflicting Dasein as an insufficiency, can also receive a phenomenologically positive qualification at decisive moments of Dasein's disclosure. Indeterminacy can become an ontological determination when it manifests the disappearance of every determination of Dasein by entities. Marion finds the reversal in at least three places. (i) In anxiety Dasein undergoes an absolutely indistinct attunement, since that before which anxiety is anxious is wholly indeterminate; that indeterminacy not only leaves factually undecided which intraworldly entity threatens, but signifies that intraworldly entities are not "relevant" at all. Anxiety therefore deploys an attunement wholly indeterminate — in the very terms first objected to the *ego* — by which Dasein faces not this or that entity but precisely the impossibility of identifying an entity to flee before; and that no determinate entity can any longer determine anxiety as a particular fear is what determines the nothing as such, so that the peculiar indeterminacy of that alongside which Dasein finds itself in anxiety is expressed as the nothing and nowhere. Through the ontic indeterminacy of anxiety Dasein arrives at its ontological determination; its transcendence over entities is accomplished only through radical ontic indeterminacy, the nothing, and only so can it determine itself in its being. (This anticipates chapter VI, where the same reversal will carry the whole third reduction.) (ii) In being-toward-death indeterminacy reappears in an unmistakably phenomenological function: with the certainty of death goes the indeterminacy of its when. It is exactly the conjunction of death's certainty with its indeterminacy that disengages it as Dasein's possibility — death as Dasein's end is its ownmost, absolute, certain and as such indeterminate, insurpassable possibility. That indeterminacy of dying is originarily disclosed in anxiety, because it amounts to the indeterminacy of being-able-to-be as it characterises and hence ontologically determines the entity that can resolve because it exists — the indeterminacy governing an existing entity. To be undetermined amounts, for Dasein, to being only on the mode of existence, by resoluteness and according to possibility, that is, to determining itself ontologically. (iii) In the analysis of conscience as the call of care, indeterminacy is explicitly granted its phenomenological positivity: the indeterminacy and indeterminability of the caller is not nothing but a positive character. In fact resoluteness itself, gathering and summing up all the prior existentials, imposes an essential indeterminacy, that of existence as such: to resoluteness necessarily belongs the indeterminacy characterising every factically thrown being-able-to-be of Dasein; resoluteness is sure of itself only as decision; and yet the existentiell indeterminacy of resoluteness, determining itself each time only in the decision, possesses its existential determinacy. It must therefore be granted that Dasein's ontic indeterminacy is precisely what secures its ontological determination, as the entity that resolves itself with nothing of an entity: Dasein determines itself by its own resoluteness only insofar as nothing of an entity determines it and it does not itself determine itself as an entity. Referred back to the initial objection to the *ego*, what does Dasein's positive indeterminacy mean? At the least that the debate is not between indeterminacy and determinacy but between an ontological indeterminacy (the *ego*, ontically determined) and an ontic indeterminacy (Dasein, ontologically determined by that very possibility). The opposition concerns two indeterminacies: one, ontic, positively secures Dasein's determination in its being; the other, ontological, negatively leads the *ego* not to determine itself in its being. But does that conflict suffice to disqualify the *ego* definitively? Nothing is less certain, once it belongs essentially to Dasein to give itself first as the "they" and to miss itself as such. Everything then happens as if the *ego*, even in its indeterminacy, mimed Dasein, in the way the "they" mimes, in the inauthentic mode, the authentic Dasein to which it essentially belongs.

*Ego* and Dasein therefore meet along finitude, mineness, the possibility of the impossible, and indeterminacy. That their resemblances stay separated, even opposed, along authenticity and inauthenticity does not suffice to make them strangers, since that ultimate opposition belongs entirely to Dasein's existence. Deciding phenomenologically between *ego* and Dasein as strict strangers does not look so easy. But what mimicry still joins them?

### 7 - The Repetition of the "ego"

What follows from these conditional confirmations? That the destruction of the *res cogitans* would never have shown such urgency, from the introduction of *Sein und Zeit* and throughout the work, if Dasein had not been able to recognise itself there so clearly. The *ego* appears to Dasein as a failing, but first as its own failing, hence above all as a danger whose fascination imposes its norms and which must be resisted better than Husserl resisted it. In its unremitting combat to mark Dasein off from the *ego cogito*, *Sein und Zeit* had to locate its insufficiencies step by step, register its decisions and invert its orientations — and such a confrontation, however warlike, cannot avoid a kind of mimetic rivalry in which the victor sometimes appears, in some respect, vanquished by his vanquished. Precisely because *Sein und Zeit* never stops rejecting the *ego cogito*, it appears there all the more enigmatic in itself and all the more intimately bound to Dasein. The analytic of the one, advancing only with the destruction of the other, confirms the other's undecided validity. Marion suggests the paradoxical conclusion may first have been Heidegger's own: for the *cogito sum* to serve as the starting point of the existential analytic, there would be need not only of an inversion but of a new ontologico-phenomenal confirmation of its content; the first statement would then be *sum*, in the sense of I-am-in-a-world, and being thus, I am in the possibility of being toward various attitudes, *cogitationes*, as modes of being alongside intraworldly entities — whereas Descartes says that *cogitationes* are present-at-hand and in them an *ego* is co-present-at-hand as a worldless *res cogitans*. Marion finds it stupefying that at the end of the preparatory analytic and after the essential of his destruction of Descartes, Heidegger still sketches the possibility of retranscribing the analytic of Dasein in the — displaced and reinterpreted — terms of the Cartesian *ego*. Descartes' epochal figure must have exercised a powerful fascination for it still to be invoked after surviving both its historical avatars and its phenomenological critique. The confirmation granted here to the *cogito sum* can be justified phenomenologically only if, in a way still to be determined, the protocol statement Descartes recorded can be made manifest under the aspect of another phenomenon than the one to which Descartes, and so also Kant and Husserl, confined themselves. What is in question with such a confirmation of what has just first undergone an inversion can only be a repetition, in a non-Cartesian mode, of Descartes' *ego cogito sum*. Strange as it seems, that design is no *hapax*: *Sein und Zeit* attempted it, and the last seminars still formulate it — the paragraphs devoted to Descartes in *Sein und Zeit* constitute the first attempt to get out of the prison of consciousness, or rather not to re-enter it; it is not at all a matter of restoring realism against idealism, since realism, in merely asserting that a world exists for the subject, stays a tributary of Cartesianism; it is rather a matter of managing to think the Greek sense of *egō*. Heidegger had undertaken to pass beyond the *ego* toward the *egō* topically, in commenting on Protagoras and stressing his irreducibility to Descartes. But had he not, earlier, accomplished it more radically through the analytic of Dasein — a non-Cartesian and perhaps already more-than-Greek *ego*?

In that case must one not finally recognise that in *Sein und Zeit* the *ego*, during the destruction of its Cartesian sense, not only does not definitively disappear but is born for the first time to its authentic phenomenological figure? Better still: would the new beginning not be inaugurated with the declension of the *ego* according to the non-metaphysical, existential requirements of the analytic of Dasein? So one must examine how the egoity of the *ego* can attain its phenomenological, that is non-Cartesian, legitimacy.

Take Dasein: how does it differ essentially from entities that are not on its mode? In that it is the entity for which its being is at issue, that is, for which being is each time its own. But since the being at issue for this entity in its being is each time mine, one must grant that the addressing of Dasein must, in accordance with this entity's character of mineness, always also say the personal pronoun — I am, you are. Dasein, because it puts being in play in itself, can only put itself in play, hence can say itself only in person, as it can put itself in play only as an I: the entity we call Dasein, I am it each time myself, and that as a being-able-to-be for which being this entity is at issue. Here the possibility of saying "I am," hence of declining being in the first person, results from Dasein's property of putting itself in person into the play of its own being. The I would have neither interest nor legitimacy if, as an existential determination of Dasein, it did not have to be and could not be interpreted existentially — if egoity and selfhood were not conceived existentially. But the two terms do not stay equivalent, as though one could be substituted for the other. On the contrary, their existential interpretation requires that the Self disclosed by the reticence of resolute existence be the originary phenomenal ground for the question of the being of the I; only phenomenal orientation on the sense of being of authentic being-able-to-be-a-Self puts the meditation in a position to clarify what ontological right substantiality, simplicity and personality can claim as characters of selfhood. Selfhood alone makes it possible, by its absolute coincidence with itself, for any personal pronoun to be said, and so secures for the I of a possible "I am" its authentic possibility; if the Self did not determine the I, no entity would be such that it could put itself in play in its own being — precisely because no *same* would then be accessible. Conversely, Dasein in its posture as the "they" claims to hold to the I, itself a mere semblance of a Self. The I can therefore say "I am" with perfect existential legitimacy only if it reduces itself to the essential phenomenon of the Self. And the Self becomes visible and given only in the phenomenality of care, since the expression "care of self" would be a tautology: in all care it is precisely of itself that Dasein takes care in relation to other entities; it cares for nothing but itself, or rather every care concerns other entities only in virtue of the care the Self thereby shows itself taking of itself. In that context "I am" finds a correct phenomenological site — it puts to work the Self's care of itself, care being the being of Dasein. "I am" therefore intervenes to mark Dasein's mineness; then, more precisely, to deploy the phenomenon of indebtedness, since the essential point is that being-in-debt arises as the predicate of the "I am"; and finally the whole disclosedness of Dasein is played out, through resoluteness, with and in the "I am": what is won with resoluteness is Dasein's most originary because authentic truth, and the disclosedness of the *Da* equiprimordially discloses the each time whole being-in-the-world — world, being-in, and the Self that this entity is as "I am." So not only does "I am" not always imply the ontological indeterminacy of the *sum* in which Descartes founders; it offers the most visible phenomenon for access to the being of Dasein, the care that establishes the Self. For the single I can deploy itself phenomenologically in two opposed ways, inscribed exactly in the two postures open to Dasein, authenticity and inauthenticity — whence two statures, since the ontological concept of substance characterises not the selfhood of the I *qua* Self but the sameness and constancy of something always already present-at-hand. Nothing could be clearer: the I can manifest itself either as the identical constancy of substance, hence on the mode of a worldly and even present-at-hand entity, persistent and subsistent, or on the contrary as and from the Self, hence from the mineness that puts Dasein in play in its being. The I turns from the status of a subsistent *res cogitans* to that of "I am" according as it falls under sameness or under selfhood. The single I bears resoluteness in the very sense in which Dasein never stops playing itself out there, so as to decide the manner of being of its being. How does the I accede to its non-Cartesian status? By opposing to ontological indeterminacy, hence also to the existentiell irresoluteness of inauthentic falling, a selfhood read existentially off authentic being-able-to-be-oneself, that is, off the authenticity of Dasein's being as care. Drawn from care, selfhood cannot persist as a *res*; if it offers a constancy of the Self, a self-constancy, that is not because the Self is a constantly present-at-hand ground of care but because the Self never stops resolving itself authentically according to and from its ownmost being: self-constancy signifies existentially nothing but anticipatory resoluteness. The conclusion follows: the I can as well have to be destroyed as be able to be confirmed, according as one or the other of Dasein's possible determinations repeats it — either inauthentically, in the Cartesian manner of the persistent and subsistent *res cogitans*, or authentically, in the manner of anticipatory resoluteness, the structure of care, Dasein's mineness. "I think" therefore no longer appears as a metaphysical thesis to be refuted among others in order to disengage the phenomenon of Dasein, but as the very terrain Dasein must conquer, since no other will ever be given it in which to manifest itself. *Ego cogito, sum* states less a counter-case to Dasein than a territory to occupy, a statement to reinterpret, a work to redo.

Between *ego* and Dasein, Descartes and Heidegger, what is at stake beyond the patent critique is a struggle for the interpretation of one and the same phenomenon — "I think," "I am." Putting the two interlocutors back on a level leads first to recognising them as interpreters of each other, more essentially than as interpreter and interpreted. But it also lets another question surge up. If the I is ontologically determined only to the measure of selfhood as put to work in care, two demands become licit. (a) Internal to *Sein und Zeit*: is the I of the "I am" in fact wholly determined by selfhood? Is selfhood in turn sufficiently and exclusively defined by the structure of care? Does that selfhood reach all entities or only entities of Dasein's measure — and in the latter case, what other determination relays it for the rest? (b) And one that exceeds *Sein und Zeit* altogether. Granting that the I attests itself more essentially as an "I am" than as an "I think," is it thereby exhausted in its status as the I of a *sum*? Does the I attest its ultimate footing and reach its last phenomenality in its function as "I am," phenomenologically accomplished as Da-sein? Is the putting of self in play by self that characterises the I devoted only to being? Or might there be at issue in the I that I certainly am also, or even first, another stake than being? Does what is put in play in, by and despite the I necessarily and exclusively exhaust itself in terms of being? In the I, is its being at issue first, or, further back, a more original putting in play? Marion's last sentence is the one the rest of the book answers: is it even permitted, despite *Sein und Zeit*'s silence, to ask the question?

## IV. The Question of Being or Ontological Difference

### 1 - The Breakthrough and the Difference: *Sein und Zeit*

Phenomenology accomplishes a first breakthrough with the *Logical Investigations*, that of intuition in play with intention so as to reach the things themselves. It accomplishes a second when Heidegger assigns intentionality to return, as to the matter itself, no longer to entities but to their being. Onto the distinction between intuition and intention is superimposed, if not substituted, the difference between being and entities. Ontological difference defines Heidegger's breakthrough entirely, for two reasons. It displaces phenomenology from knowledge of entities to the thinking of being, first as fundamental ontology and then as *Ereignis*. And it alone permits separating metaphysics — attached to being only as being of entities and for the sake of entities — from the thinking of being as such, that is, permits a destruction of the history of ontology that in fact requires rewriting the history of metaphysics as the history of the forgetting of being, as an unthought history of being. So ontological difference decides at once Heidegger's own phenomenological thought and the site of all prior metaphysics. Marion draws the consequence sharply: it would be enough for the notion to be poorly attested, or its emergence to remain confused, for both Heidegger's own breakthrough and his reinterpretation of the metaphysical history of ontology to weaken. And the order of dependence runs one way only — ontological difference permits a hermeneutic of the history of metaphysics because it alone undertakes a hermeneutic of entities in view of being; the second hermeneutic alone makes the first possible, not the reverse; the phenomenological breakthrough makes the historical destruction possible by rebound, not the reverse. That is why the question of where and when ontological difference appears in Heidegger's work is not a point of doctrine. Any imprecision, hesitation, or ambiguity here weakens not this or that concept but the radical phenomenological breakthrough on which the whole Heideggerian advance depends; and any gap between ontological difference and the course of the thinking, any delay between ontological difference and the performance of the breakthrough, would count as a symptom of a basic incoherence in the entire enterprise. The question decides whether Heidegger's thought has the character of a breakthrough at all.

The stakes are redoubled by a difficulty of almost equal size. Ontological difference must all the more emerge, and can all the less, because it remains latent — if not lethal — throughout the history of metaphysics. Metaphysics has as its own not to think the gap between being and entities except by leaving it unthought as such: the thinking of metaphysics remains engaged in the difference unthought as such. Ontological difference escapes all the more in that it is not absent but at work, latently: the being of entities is missed precisely because it operates only for the benefit of entities. We never think outside or before ontological difference, since even when we ignore it we still think within its dissimulation, under cover of its covering-over. So breaking through to ontological difference cannot mean reaching it at last from a territory or posture wholly foreign to it, but only passing from its latent to its patent state. The breakthrough breaks through from one state to another of a difference that was in any case already there. And what is at stake in that passage is nothing less than the step back out of metaphysics — as the non-thinking of ontological difference — toward the new beginning: we speak of the difference between being and entities; the step back goes from the unthought, from difference as such, to what is to be thought, which is the forgetting of difference; the forgetting here to be thought is the veiling, thought from *Lēthē*, of difference as such, a veiling that for its part has withdrawn from the origin; forgetting belongs to difference because it belongs to it. The step back out of metaphysics is therefore exactly equivalent, if it is to be accomplished at all, to the breakthrough into ontological difference. Consequently the movement by which Heidegger tears himself from metaphysics — that is, *Sein und Zeit* — must by definition accomplish a breakthrough into ontological difference, and into ontological difference thought as such. Destroying the history of ontology would have neither sense nor success unless access to explicit ontological difference were disengaged in the same movement. Can that requirement in fact be met? Two difficulties suggest doubt. (a) Since the breakthrough toward ontological difference takes its departure within the unthought of that same difference, its whole process unfolds within latency, within the original unthought, finally within the undecided. The impetus of the breakthrough must therefore be born within ontological indecision itself. By what undecided and provisional rupture could it emerge, and can a progressive emergence ever suit a breakthrough at all? (b) Ontological difference should emerge as soon as the programme of a destruction of the history of ontology is imposed. Since that programme is formulated explicitly in §6 of *Sein und Zeit*, ontological difference should be formulated there just as explicitly. But does *Sein und Zeit* not precisely ignore ontological difference?

### 2 - Emergence and Delay

Where and when does ontological difference appear? According to Heidegger himself — a first paradox — it remains unknown to *Sein und Zeit*, both as unthought and as thought. In a 1949 foreword to the third edition of *Vom Wesen des Grundes*, written in 1928 for the Husserl *Festschrift* of 1929, Heidegger states that the treatise saw the light in 1928, at the same time as the lecture *What Is Metaphysics?*; the lecture meditates the nothing, the treatise names ontological difference. Marion grants the judgement at first glance. The lecture does meditate the nothing, its genesis, appearance and status; and the treatise can name difference only from the nothing, since ontological difference is the nothing between entities and being, and the nothing as nihilating not-ness is not identical with but the Same as the nihilating not-ness of difference. In the text itself difference does appear expressly: ontic truth and ontological truth each concern differently the entity in its being and the being of the entity; they belong together essentially on the ground of their relation to the difference of being and entities — ontological difference; the resulting inevitable bifurcation of the essence of truth into ontic and ontological is possible only through the simultaneous eclosion of that difference. Truth here leads to ontological difference; or rather the duality of truth, of entities and of their being, takes its possibility from the prior difference of being from entities within the single being of entities. On Heidegger's word and on the evidence of the textual facts, one would conclude that ontological difference is named only in 1928/29, after *Sein und Zeit*, which would appear as the only one of Heidegger's major texts foreign to ontological difference.

That conclusion, however widely accepted, is on examination extremely fragile, and Marion dismantles it in three moves. (a) If the 1929 text names ontological difference, naming must not be confused with meditating: it may be only difference named but not yet thought. Does naming it suffice to think it as such, that is, to free it from the unthought that metaphysically characterises it? (b) One might answer that the 1929 text thinks as much as it names, since it wins the difference from a meditation on the ontico-ontological bifurcation of truth, where the disclosedness of being is counter-distinguished from the manifestness of entities — the disclosedness of being alone makes possible the manifestness of entities — and that disclosedness is named ontological truth. But such an alethiological origin of the difference goes back before 1928/29. The summer 1927 course establishes an almost identical distinction: we distinguish, not only terminologically but for reasons belonging to the matter itself, the uncoveredness of an entity from the disclosedness of its being. More: there the two figures of truth already go back explicitly to ontological difference — one must come to grasp conceptually, in its possibility and necessity, the difference between uncoveredness and disclosedness, and equally the possible unity of both, which implies the possibility of grasping the difference between the entity uncovered in uncovering and the being disclosed in disclosure, that is, of fixing the distinction between being and entities, ontological difference; so that by attending to the Kantian problem we arrive at the question of ontological difference. And *Sein und Zeit* itself thematises that same opposition strongly, to the point of suggesting a difference between its terms: being, as what is asked about, demands its own mode of exhibition, essentially differing from the uncovering of entities. The comparative analysis culminates in §44. So if ontological difference must emerge from the inquiry into the doubling of truth, it should appear as soon as truth splits into two irreducible senses — well before *Vom Wesen des Grundes*. (c) That conclusion so little contradicts Heidegger's 1949 declaration — which speaks of a naming, not of a first naming — that Heidegger himself, in a 1929 note to his own text, refers the reader to the first public communication on the subject in the summer 1927 course, §22, whose conclusion rejoins its beginning where Kant's thesis that being is not a real predicate is clarified, and this with the intention of taking ontological difference as such into view for the first time and once and for all, and this from ontology, itself experienced in the mode of fundamental ontology; adding that the whole course belongs to *Sein und Zeit*, Part One, Division Three, *Zeit und Sein*. So ontological difference, if it appears in immediately published texts only in 1928/29, emerges in the public courses from summer 1927, in the work immediately following *Sein und Zeit*. That authorised precision makes *Sein und Zeit* ever more sharply the text par excellence to have ignored ontological difference — at least in its published part. Yet the exclusion carries the marks of a double difficulty. First, *Sein und Zeit* does distinguish two modes of truth and differentiates them, like the other texts; how can it alone among them not infer ontological difference from that? Second, if the summer 1927 course, the first inventor of ontological difference, belongs to *Sein und Zeit* and its global project, how can the published part bear no trace of it, especially in the long introduction that holds for the whole, including the unpublished Division Three named in 1929? The clean break Heidegger proposes may look too clean to be receivable. Before debating the thesis, Marion checks how it is imposed for Heidegger himself, in two steps. (a) Where and how does the 1927 course expound ontological difference? At §22. Having provisionally concluded in §21 the analysis of the Kantian thesis on being as position, Heidegger notes in §22(a) that in ordinary usage being is taken for an entity in the question of what the entity as entity is. Dasein does understand being in a certain way — otherwise it would simply not be on Dasein's mode — but understands it only confusedly, in a latent state: the difference of being and entities is *there* latently in Dasein itself and its existence, even if not expressly known; the difference is there, that is, it has Dasein's mode of being and belongs to existence; existence means something like being in the performance of that distinction. But that achievement, which cannot be lost since it defines Dasein as the ontically ontological entity, in no way amounts to a declared thinking of difference as such; on the contrary Dasein dwells within it without knowing it. In that sense the difference of being and entities is there pre-ontologically, without an explicit concept of being, latent in Dasein's existence; but as such it can become an explicitly understood difference. To pass from implicit to explicit, difference must be understood as the *there* of the two differents, thanks to Dasein's temporality. Whence a modified name once it is expressly performed: we call the explicitly performed difference of being and entities *ontological difference*. Marion presses what the passage costs. Difference is designated first by its latency, a latency so deep that Heidegger is forced to use two terms according as difference dissimulates itself (*Unterschied*) or, more rarely, ends by appearing (*Differenz*, *ontologische Differenz*). One may even ask whether, named here, ontological difference is really won at all — whether this is a record of phenomenological victory or a programme for a breakthrough still to be made. The embarrassment is confirmed by the course's incompleteness: of its second part, which promised nothing less than to examine the fundamental ontological question of the sense of being in general, only a first chapter is communicated, titled the problem of ontological difference; and that chapter comprises only four paragraphs, of which only the last takes up difference, in a single section, and does so as first and for the most part *not* explicitly ontological. Such a narrowing of the discourse as it nears its declared goal suggests that a difficulty brakes it and an aporia blocks it, not that a breakthrough frees thought. Everything happens as if ontological difference were marked and named once and for all in summer 1927 only so as to have an unanswered question recognised as an aporia. (b) Does that text stand in a privileged relation to *Sein und Zeit* — continuity or rupture? Plainly continuity. First because the *Grundprobleme* fall within Division Three of *Sein und Zeit*, as *Vom Wesen des Grundes* and the overall plan of §8 confirm. Second because the transition from implicit difference to explicit and truly ontological difference depends here on Dasein's temporality: *Differenz* is *there* only if Dasein understands itself temporally to the point of unfolding within itself the being of the entity. So not only is the analytic of Dasein no obstacle, to be surmounted by the turn, in acceding to ontological difference; it alone, as the exercise of temporality from the structure of care, permits thinking explicitly the originarily temporal character of being in general. And if Dasein appears as its sole temporal workman, how could ontological difference in its first public communication surpass or reject it? Marion inverts the initial question: rather than asking how far from *Sein und Zeit* ontological difference appears, ask whether it can even be conceived that the analytic of Dasein does not serve, thoroughly and from the outset, the explication of ontological difference.

### 3 - The Occurrences and the Notes

Two kinds of answer are possible. Either one simply denies that *Sein und Zeit* ever names ontological difference and attributes its appearance to *Vom Wesen des Grundes*, as L. M. Vail does in holding that the term does not appear as such in *Sein und Zeit* and that the 1928 essay first makes use of it. Or one maintains that if the word does not appear the thing already works the 1927 text, as John Salus argues in saying that even Heidegger's first great work already moves within the framework of ontological difference, and as Granel does in granting that what is properly thought by Heidegger already in 1927 is *die Differenz*. The extreme paradox — an ontological difference all the more at work in *Sein und Zeit* for never appearing there — finds its most perfect expression in Jean Beaufret, for whom *Sein und Zeit* would be the book of the difference of being and entities in such a way that the word "difference" does not yet come to the fore, the locution appearing in Heidegger's teaching only in the months following publication, in the Marburg course of summer 1927. Marion refuses the formula as an explanation demanding an explanation: how is one to understand that the book of the difference of being and entities is precisely the one that keeps completely silent about its locution and its word? How can the second phenomenological breakthrough have failed to say or understand its own lexicon? Such an interpretation, paradoxical to the point of incoherence, would not have imposed itself on most readers had Beaufret's authority not underwritten it. Marion separates two theses in order to contest both: (a) *Sein und Zeit* never uses the locution "ontological difference" — an assertion of fact; (b) *Sein und Zeit* moves within ontological difference thought as such — a theoretical assertion. Neither survives examination, and both conceal the true situation of ontological difference in *Sein und Zeit* — or rather the true situation of *Sein und Zeit* within ontological difference.

First point: Heidegger does use the phrase "ontological difference" in *Sein und Zeit*, contrary to the commentators' opinion, so far as Marion knows without exception. (a) §12: what matters at first is to catch sight of the ontological difference between being-in as an existential and the reciprocal "insideness" of present-at-hand entities as a category. A similar formula, without the adjective, returns at §40 in determining Dasein as dwelling alongside, being familiar with, once the existential sense of being-in has been clarified in its difference from the categorial signification of insideness. Between being-in, properly existential because proper to Dasein's manner of being, and the insideness of one present-at-hand entity in another, there is not only a difference but a difference of ontological status: it separates two manners of being, concerning finally Dasein on one side and presence-at-hand on the other. No pretext permits weakening either the difference or its ontological character. That is a first factual occurrence. (b) §63: the delimitation of the structure of care has furnished the basis for a first ontological difference between existence and reality, leading to the thesis that the substance of man is existence. This second occurrence must likewise be heard as an "ontological difference," separating Dasein's manner of being (existence, existential) from that of entities not of Dasein's measure (reality, categorial). The same difference reappears significantly on the last page of *Sein und Zeit*, where the adjective is missing but the differentiated terms remain the same: the difference of the being of existing Dasein from the being of the entity not of Dasein's measure; and the "difference" of "consciousness" and "thing." A clear topography emerges: between the being of Dasein and the being of other entities a relation is drawn that is in fact called ontological difference. Marion adds a shrewd diagnosis of why translators and commentators balk at naming *ontologischer Unterschied* and *ontologische Unterscheidung* by the name that belongs to them: a scruple about acknowledging a fact whose status — precisely, ontological — remains indeterminate. The reluctance can be overcome only if the assertion of fact is legitimated by the theoretical assertion that the difference named here can in a certain sense be thought as ontological. To try that legitimation one must track the difference more subtly, since alongside its occurrences as a noun it appears in verbal form, more discreetly, in the working of the matters themselves. Two such occurrences are decisive.

The first comes at §2, in the inaugural and solemn construction of the question of being, which calls strangely for three terms rather than two: what is interrogated, a role held by entities and more exactly by the peerless entity Dasein; what it is asked to answer about, the being of that entity; and finally what one wants to know in posing the question, which does not coincide with what is asked about — the sense of being. Precisely in distinguishing the three terms Heidegger is led to state a point of importance: being, as what is asked about, demands its own manner of showing itself, essentially differing from the uncovering of entities. An essential difference intervenes here, and phenomenologically it opposes being in its own showing to entities in common uncovering. Unless one forgets that phenomenology alone deserves the name of ontology, one must conclude from that difference of modes of presence to an equally essential difference between being and entities. More: that essential difference is disengaged on the occasion and at the instigation of the construction of the question of being as such, so as to separate its first two terms, and thereby perfectly earns the title "ontological" — a difference established by and for the question of being is exactly an ontological difference. The ontological work of difference is all the more remarkable for intervening at the first repetition of the question of being, in a place — the introduction — that governs all of *Sein und Zeit*, unpublished and unwritten divisions included.

The second occurrence stands in an equally sensitive place: the last page of Division One, where a recapitulation and a transition to Division Two are accomplished at once. Again, exactly as at §2, what is at issue is the question of being and the relation of its three terms: being — not entities — "is given" only insofar as truth is, and truth is only insofar and as long as Dasein is; being and truth "are" equiprimordially; what it means that being "is," where it must nonetheless be differentiated from all entities, can be concretely put in question only once the sense of being and the reach of the understanding of being are clarified. Marion calls the declaration fundamental, since entities, the being of entities, and finally the sense of being explicitly repeat the question of being posed at the outset. It is not yet a matter of claiming to answer it, since the third term has not been understood; but the now almost completed analytic of Dasein permits marking, with full phenomenological exactness and from the ontologically remarkable entity, the gap and relation between being and entities within the being of entities. And to characterise that inseparably identical gap Heidegger here uses, as at §2, the verb *unterscheiden*. If being differentiates itself from entities, how not conclude that this is indeed an ontological difference? Nor can one object that the interpreter forces the letter, since in his own copy, exactly after the phrase in question, Heidegger wrote the note: ontological difference. The difference does work, from *Sein und Zeit* onward, the inseparable gap of being from entities — the text supplies occurrences and Heidegger himself confirms their sense.

The frequency and convergence of the marginal notes that gloss *Sein und Zeit* with the locution deserve emphasis, and Marion cites four beyond §44. (a) In establishing phenomenology as the only possible ontology, Heidegger repeats the threefold dimension of the question of being: the necessity of a fundamental ontology taking as its theme the ontologically-ontically remarkable entity, Dasein, so as to face the cardinal problem, the question of the sense of being in general. The three terms are grouped in two couples — the remarkable entity and its being, then the sense of being facing the first couple. And Heidegger glosses that sense of being in general in a note: being — not a genus, not being for entities in general; the "in general" equals *katholou* equals in totality of: being of the entity; sense of the difference. So the sense of being attains its radicality only insofar as *being*, even in "being of the entity," is already accented for being's benefit and not the entity's, so that with the being–entity couple an ontological and not an ontic difference is already in play. Here again the question of being shelters ontological difference from its first construction — or rather shelters itself within it. (b) Stigmatising at §20 the exemplary insufficiency of the Cartesian determination of ontology, Heidegger marks the ambiguity of *substantia*, now ontic and now ontological, and concludes that behind that insignificant difference of signification hides the inability to master the fundamental problem of being. The *Unterschied* of signification is anything but negligible, since what is at stake is an ontico-ontological signification, hence the play of being and entities; and Heidegger glosses that very sequence in a note: ontological difference. What Cartesian metaphysics leaves unthought, through the indeterminacy of the concept of substance, is ontological difference itself. So the destruction of the history of ontology is accomplished in *Sein und Zeit*, as much as the construction of the question of being, from ontological difference — which retroactively confirms chapter III's reading of the Descartes chapters. (c) At the threshold of the analysis, so as no longer to leave the being of Dasein indeterminate, Heidegger recalls the opposition of being and entities: entities are independently of the experience, knowledge and grasp by which they are disclosed, uncovered and determined, but being "is" only in the understanding of the entity to whose being something like understanding of being belongs. The point is to differentiate the uncovering of entities from the detection of being; and Heidegger insists in a note that this understanding is to be heard as a listening, and that this never means being is only "subjective" but means being (*qua* being of entities) *qua* difference "in" Da-sein as the having-been-thrown of the throw. Dasein, remarkable in that as an entity it is ontological, plays at the hinge and fold of being and entities, and better is in person that fold and hinge. (d) Finally, taking up again the canonical statement that being is never explicable from entities, Heidegger glosses once more: ontological difference. Marion concedes the methodological objections — a text should not be read from marginalia, especially much later ones, and Heidegger's retroactive self-interpretation often breeds more obscurity than clarity, as this very chapter has shown. But granting the prudence, one cannot deny the evidence: in these occurrences Heidegger did not have to over-interpret his own texts to read an ontological difference in them, implicit in some places (the question of being, the destruction, Dasein's remarkable trait) all the better for being explicit in others. No forcing, even by Heidegger himself, could have introduced ontological difference anachronistically into *Sein und Zeit* if *Sein und Zeit* did not already move of itself within the horizon opened by ontological difference. The conclusion: "ontological difference" appears literally in *Sein und Zeit* because the 1927 breakthrough is accomplished within ontological difference itself.

### 4 - The Most Cardinal Unthought

Some ontological difference therefore runs through *Sein und Zeit* from one extreme to the other and through its centre. But establishing the fact does not demonstrate that *Sein und Zeit* already puts to work the later canonical concept. The whole question now becomes to identify the sense and measure the reach of what *Sein und Zeit* lets appear under that title, without understanding it in advance from what the same locution will indicate after 1927. Before any interpretation, then, one must find the guiding thread that could have led *Sein und Zeit* to introduce such a difference — better, to introduce itself into it. Marion's hypothesis: that thread takes its origin in Husserl. The proposal is the chapter's real wager, and it reverses the usual picture in which ontological difference is Heidegger's break with his teacher.

Begin with a coincidence: Husserl too uses the locution "ontological difference," and from 1913, in the second edition of the *Logical Investigations*. The Third Investigation, on the theory of wholes and parts, in a first chapter titled the difference between independent and dependent objects, qualifies that difference three times as ontological — the universal ontological difference between abstract and concrete contents; the essence of the ontological difference between abstract and concrete; shedding light on the essence of the ontological difference. These occurrences prove at least that in 1927 Heidegger had not far to look for a warrant for his own phrase. A second oddity: §39 of *Sein und Zeit* opposes entities, as independent of experience, knowledge and grasp, to being, which depends on the understanding of the entity that Dasein is — and that very passage is glossed by the late marginal note in terms of difference. The double encounter proves no theoretical filiation, but it does not make one unthinkable. And in another way still, Husserl appears as the indisputable interlocutor of one of the two occurrences in *Sein und Zeit*. When Heidegger sets up an ontological difference of existence and reality at §63, he is recovering a distinction fixed canonically in 1913 by §42 of *Ideas I* between consciousness and reality: being as consciousness and being as reality, the principial difference between modes of intuition; a fundamental and essential difference between being as consciousness and being as thing; whereby is announced the principial difference of manners of being, the most cardinal there is at all, that between consciousness and reality; a principial difference of modes of givenness. Heidegger keeps the term reality but substitutes Dasein for consciousness — which does not disqualify the Husserlian difference but, by repeating it with one correction, consecrates it in its full pertinence. That is why Heidegger sometimes takes over, as if in quotation and with the reserve of quotation marks, Husserl's own terms as equivalent to his: the difference of the being of existing Dasein from the being of the entity not of Dasein's measure, for example reality; and then, as though indifferently, the "difference" of "consciousness" and "thing." The Husserlian difference haunts the Heideggerian one to the last page of *Sein und Zeit*. That Husserl had indeed fixed such a principial and most cardinal difference other 1913 texts establish beyond dispute. *Ideas I* §43 locates between perception and symbolic representation an essential difference no bridge can span; §49 says that immanent or absolute being and transcendent being are both called "entity," "object," and each has its objective content of determination, but what is here called object and determination of object on both sides is alike only according to empty logical categories — between consciousness and reality a true abyss of sense yawns. Marion's rhetorical question is precise: an abyss of sense between two senses of "entity" — what is that if not a difference of manners of being, hence an already ontological difference? Finally §79, to establish the gap between the original region of consciousness as the original category of being in general and the other regions of being, says that the doctrine of categories must throughout proceed from this most radical of all differences of being — being as consciousness and being as announcing itself in consciousness, as transcendent. Here again, a difference of being, beyond a distinction among entities that as such are logically undistinguished, amounts to an ontological difference. Recapitulating: Husserl introduces between the manners of being of two types of entity, called consciousness and thing, an essential and quasi-ontological difference; he could therefore offer Heidegger from 1913 not only the locution, in the second edition of the *Investigations*, but above all the determination of the two entities and manners of being that make the locution phenomenologically operative, in *Ideas I*; and Heidegger himself avows that origin by using, sparingly but all the more significantly given that it is the finale of *Sein und Zeit*, the terms his teacher proposed. How then not presume that the starting point of ontological difference in 1927, and hence before, is given by Husserl in 1913?

One should go further. The theoretical rupture between Husserl and Heidegger before 1927 may have been played out precisely on the interpretation of the difference — named ontological or not — between consciousness (or existence) and thing (or reality). Heidegger does not separate himself from Husserl by introducing an ontological difference Husserl ignored, but by radically deepening as ontological a difference of essence and manner of being that Husserl left indeterminate as such. Two themes confirm that the debate precedes the writing of *Sein und Zeit*. (a) In the 1925 course, having cited the very passages of *Ideas I* §§49 and 76 in question and granted that Husserl did claim to establish a fundamental difference of being, Heidegger observes that he in no way succeeded: we now see the astonishing thing — the winning of the most radical difference of being is claimed here, and yet no question is properly posed about the being of the entity that figures in the difference; in winning that fundamental difference of being not once is a question posed about the manner of being of the differentiated terms themselves, nor about the manner of being of consciousness, nor fundamentally about what governs the whole differentiation of the difference of being — the sense of being; whence it becomes clear that the question of being is not just one possible question among others but the most pressing question in the very sense of phenomenology itself, pressing in a still more radical sense than has so far been clarified as regards the intentional. Husserl does name as an essential difference between manners of being the difference of intentional consciousness from the real thing; but he advances no further, never thinking what he names, never thinking what a difference of manners of being means, because he lacks access to that question's condition of possibility, the sense of being. Marion sums the charge as a double failure. First toward the difference he names, since he never takes seriously that this is a question about the differing *being* of differing entities — so that Husserl stops at the exact instant where the underlying difficulty begins, where it becomes a matter of really thinking the difference of manners of being as ontological. Second, not asking ontologically after the manner of being of the entity consciousness, he misses that of the intentional and so commits a fault in strict phenomenology: is phenomenological research in fact so non-phenomenological that it excludes its own domain from phenomenological questioning? What does Husserl lack in order to pass from a difference of manners of being to a properly ontological difference? He lacks staying a phenomenologist to the end. And what does he lack in order to stay a phenomenologist? Not to miss the sense of being — since only the sense of being permits, by preceding it, a question that can interrogate consciousness and the intentional in view of their being. What forbids Husserl to think the difference phenomenologically coincides with what holds him back from thinking it ontologically: the horizon of the sense of being. The rupture with Husserl concerns the crossing, on the difference of manners of being of consciousness and thing, of the demands of phenomenological method with the question of being. Difference, in those two ambiguities, is the crossroads.

The confrontation was also expressed directly between the two men. In the famous letter of 22 October 1927, Heidegger sets out disagreements that, beyond the projected *Encyclopaedia Britannica* article, concern the gap *Sein und Zeit* marks with Husserl's non-phenomenological phenomenology. It is remarkable that Heidegger introduces his ownmost breakthrough by mentioning difference twice. First about the world: the problem that arises directly is what the mode of being is of the entity in which the world is constituted; that is the central problem of *Sein und Zeit*, a fundamental ontology of Dasein; it must be shown that the mode of being of human Dasein is totally different from that of all others and that precisely by reason of that determinate mode of being it harbours in itself the possibility of transcendental constitution. For the couple entity-as-world and entity-constituting-the-world, what is at issue is directly the relation between the real thing and the irreal intentional consciousness, here also called human Dasein; their total difference opposes not only two entities but the two manners of being of those entities, and so must be recognised as an ontological difference. Establishing it is *Sein und Zeit*'s essential project — which means in fact that *Sein und Zeit* accomplishes what Husserl neither knew how nor wished to undertake. The same problematic returns about the understandability of entities: by regression toward what is that understanding won? What is meant by absolute ego in its difference from the pure psychical? What is the manner of being of that absolute ego, in what sense is it the same as the each-time factical I, in what sense not? By absolute ego Heidegger means Dasein itself, whose manner of being differs from that of any other entity, even the pure psychical conceived as itself intraworldly — Marion pauses to wonder whether that corresponds to the fallen I or to the "they." So this too is a difference between Dasein's manner of being and that of any entity not of Dasein's measure, comparable to what *Sein und Zeit* calls ontological. The confrontation with Husserl therefore bears doubly on difference: mediately through the 1925 course, immediately and face to face through the 1927 letter, which doubtless reflects conversations of the preceding days.

The rupture point is thus reached with the interpretation of the status of the difference between the manner of being of the entity "consciousness," become Dasein, and that of other entities, real things. Three conclusions follow. (a) Husserl from 1913 does furnish Heidegger the starting point toward a genuine ontological difference in *Sein und Zeit*, offering him the locution without the concept in the second edition of the *Investigations*, and the concept without its ontological determination in *Ideas I*. (b) Heidegger takes the decisive step against Husserl by interpreting the difference of manners of being of the entities "consciousness" and "thing" decidedly as ontological, from what the *Ideas* never stop missing — the sense of being, which alone apportions the understanding of being to Dasein. Husserl arrives at the cardinal difference without understanding it or thinking it as such; he stops exactly where the real difficulty opens and where phenomenological method should deploy itself, whence §7 of *Sein und Zeit*, which undertakes to treat the being of entities exactly as a phenomenon. Literally, Husserl does not understand what he said in naming the most cardinal of differences; Heidegger understands it by thinking it, for the first time, as ontological. (c) Difference not only works *Sein und Zeit* but precedes and makes it possible, since Husserl reveals, before anyone else, the ontological difference between two manners of being precisely by not thinking it as such. From 1925 at the latest, Heidegger finds himself confronted, thanks to and against Husserl, with ontological difference unthought as such. Only that Husserlian unthought could provoke the Heideggerian effort of thought. From the origin, what is at stake between Husserl and Heidegger is thinking the unthought of difference — whether or not it is qualified as ontological.

### 5 - The Irreducibility of the "Question of Being" to "Ontological Difference"

If *Sein und Zeit* does not so much discover difference as first undertake to interpret it as ontological, one must show concretely how its concept operates there, setting aside the occurrences of the locution. Does *Sein und Zeit* already clearly bring into play, in its concept, ontological difference as it canonically determines Heidegger's thought in and after the turn? At first sight *Sein und Zeit* does distinguish entities from being with an unambiguous force the sequel will not surpass: "being" is not something like an entity; the being of entities is not itself an entity; being cannot be explained from entities; being is never explicable through entities but is already for each entity the "transcendental." Does that formally indisputable distinction suffice to win ontological difference as such? Not at all — because so formulated it lets one suppose that being offers itself to be known as accessibly as entities, as another term that would make a number with entities, doubling them at the risk of concealing what is precisely to be thought: the difference not of entities but of manners of being. The being of entities does not differ from entities as another entity would differ from them, but as the manner of being differs from what is. As the manner in which the entity is — and not itself an entity — being remains inseparably united to entities, on whose surface it must be read. The difference of being from entities implies each in the other: being is each time the being of an entity; being means being of entities; because the phenomenon phenomenologically understood is always only what stipulates being, while being for its part is each time the being of an entity; entities are independently of the experience, knowledge and grasp through which they are disclosed, unveiled and determined, but being "is" only in the understanding of the entity to whose being something like the understanding of being belongs. Marion's inference is the section's pivot: the difference between being and entities is required as ontological in the strict sense precisely because ontically being is confused with entities and at first does not distinguish itself from them, which comes to the same as saying it cannot be reached as such. Ontic indifference between being and entities forces one to reach being only through a distinction neither real nor formal nor material nor of reason, but ontological. It is just because being, ontically null and void, becomes accessible only through, within and on the surface of entities that it must be ontologically differentiated from them. To accede to being — to bring it to light as a phenomenon — is possible only on the mode of the ontological difference that interprets entities in view of their being. In that sense ontological difference operates as the implementation of phenomenological method in the absolutely unique case where the phenomenon to be made manifest in its givenness is no entity, hence "is" not, since it "is" the being prior to entities. So *Sein und Zeit* does show a working of ontological difference between being and entities.

That finding raises two difficulties. (a) How is it that this working of the concept coincides with none of the occurrences of the locution? (b) Above all, how is it that the concept distinguishes being and entities, whereas all the occurrences of the locution in fact distinguish two entities from each other — Dasein and the thing — or the two manners of being of those entities, existence and reality? This is not merely a non-correspondence of phrase and concept but the irreducibility of one differentiated couple to the other: how reconcile a difference between two entities with a difference between being and an entity? Answering requires reconstructing the topography of ontological difference as Heidegger traces it in 1927 with far greater complexity than later. Three figures of difference are superimposed, at least partially. (α) The strictly ontic difference Husserl already marks between consciousness and reality. (β) The difference between the manners of being of those two entities — existence for one, reality or presence-at-hand for the other — which on the ground of an ontic difference claims to reach a difference concerning being; this and this alone is the difference *Sein und Zeit* calls ontological in the occurrences catalogued earlier. (γ) The difference between being on one side and entities on the other, as Heidegger will call it ontological after 1927, and as it explicitly inaugurates *Sein und Zeit* without that relation being given the qualifier there. Of the three, two are theoretically unambiguous: the first has nothing ontological about it, the third fully accomplishes its ontological function. The second is the problem. It occurs only in *Sein und Zeit* and combines, with complex ambiguity, the two parameters that were precisely to be distinguished, ontic and ontological. Everything happens as if Heidegger could break through the cardinal unthought Husserl left only by arming himself with the apparatus known as analogy of proportionality: the relation of an entity to its manner of being becomes intelligible only compared to the relation of another entity to its manner of being. First an analogy between two ontico-ontological relations — thing or *res* is to reality-presence-at-hand as Dasein or I is to existence. Then, by conversion, an analogy between the two entities and the two senses of being — thing or *res* is to Dasein or I as being-as-presence-at-hand is to being-as-existence. That complex play of two figures of one analogy is what *Sein und Zeit* calls ontological difference. Unlike the later ontological difference, the relation of entity to being is here doubled: it is always the being of one of the two types of entity and never entities in general facing being in general. And so the relation is complicated — is the being at issue with one or the other type of entity really being itself, or only an indication toward being as such, in general? Does the ontico-ontological analogy open entities onto being as such, or still only onto a difference between two manners of being of entities? The ontic mediation of the question of being massively characterises *Sein und Zeit*; and the ambiguity of its topography leads inevitably to asking whether it breaks through toward ontological difference or on the contrary erects an obstacle before it, one all the more insurmountable for claiming an irrefragable phenomenological necessity.

How, then, is the irruption of Dasein into the topography of ontological difference phenomenologically justified, at the risk of concealing it? Dasein intervenes within it because in 1927 it appears as the workman par excellence of the question of being as that question marks the gap of being from every entity. For the question of being can be posed, at the time of *Sein und Zeit*, only insofar as it is first posed in its formal structure: the question of the sense of being must be constructed. Such construction implies not two elements, as in a simple difference, but three. The question mobilises first the one it interrogates, the entity; but it interrogates the entity only to make it say what is in question about it, to answer the interrogation about what it must answer for as an entity — the being of that entity. Marion halts the enumeration to underline the essential point: if §2 advanced no further, we would already possess canonical ontological difference as it plays between the entity and the being of the entity — and the equivalence looks still more exact if one notes that the same §2 establishes precisely a difference between those two terms, since being as what is asked about demands a mode of showing that essentially differs from the uncovering of entities. Had the construction of the question of being stopped at that gap in 1927, it would coincide literally, in its first two terms, with ontological difference in the canonical sense, and the difficulty of harmonising them could not even have arisen. But — and this is the decisive point — §2 goes on: it introduces a third term, totally ignored by canonical ontological difference, which by that very fact makes it impossible to reconcile the two enterprises, one decidedly ternary, the other definitively binary. The question interrogates the entity about the being of the entity; neither exhausts the questioning; there remains what the entity itself does not know even when it answers unreservedly about its being, what only the questioner divines and seeks, hearing in the explicit answer the last word of the story — what he wants to find out, the sense of being. The sense of being marks the last aim of the question: to go back, from an entity and as though through it, not only to its being — a first gap, ontological difference — but, by way of the being of that entity, to the sense of being, hence to being in general, ultimately to the temporality of being from the temporality of Dasein. That redoubling of the first two terms by the sense of being can be read in two directions: toward canonical ontological difference, and then as an impasse; toward Dasein, and then as a phenomenological breakthrough. Marion takes the two directions in turn, in §§5 and 6.

Referred to ontological difference in its two terms, the question of being offers with its three terms two gaps: one between the entity and the being of the entity, one between the being of the entity and the sense of being. Which could accommodate canonical ontological difference? Apparently the first, since it already plays between being and entities. But what being is at stake there, if not the being of *this* entity? How to avoid its covering no other ontological leap than that from the thing to its *ousia*, or from the thing to its status as an entity? Evidently by stressing that the entity put in question in that gap ranks as Dasein and therefore puts in play, along with itself, not only its being but being itself. Correct as that answer seems, it holds only under the conditions *Sein und Zeit* fixes for the question of being — namely that a single entity can support a question about its being as a question about being itself. But does that restriction of the ontic field not violently betray either that entity-ness in general has not yet been reached, or that being is still being questioned within too narrow limits? The first gap therefore remains ontically too limited and ontologically too superficial to receive even an anticipation of canonical ontological difference. Must one then have recourse to the second gap, between the being of the entity and the sense of being? Apparently, since it looks like the analytic's last goal: the necessity of a fundamental ontology taking as theme the ontico-ontologically remarkable entity, Dasein — the first gap with its lacunae — so as to face the cardinal problem, the question of the sense of being in general. Heidegger himself seems to recognise canonical ontological difference in that second gap, glossing it: being of the entity; sense of the difference. Yet several arguments make the assimilation fragile. First, the sense of being in general was not reached by *Sein und Zeit*; if in 1927 it stood for the being of the difference, one would have to infer that ontological difference is itself equally missed — the opposite of what was to be proved. Second, can one identify the being of the difference with the sense of being, hence with temporality? Must being be understood within the horizon of time? Not only will *Sein und Zeit* end by doubting it, but the abandonment of the horizon of time will continue until, inversely, time itself is submitted to *Ereignis*. Third, the second assimilation — of the being of the entity to the *entity* of the difference — seems roughly unjustifiable. So neither of the two gaps opened by the three terms of the question of being can receive canonical ontological difference or even anticipate it. The topography of the question of being does not so much lead to that of ontological difference as dispense with it and substitute itself for it in advance.

The result is paradoxical, since it excludes from *Sein und Zeit* every ontological difference after the locution had been found there in the original question — the clean relation of being to entities. But the paradox is only apparent. *Sein und Zeit* does confront what will shortly be thematised under the single title of ontological difference; it simply does not confront it head on. It adds and prefers the construction of the question of being, and so submits to the obligatory mediation of Dasein. Ontological difference does not disappear as a stake; it appears protean and deformed by the two gaps of the question of being — under the grip of the question of being it disappears only by being multiplied into two differences, each equally inadequate to it: from Dasein to its being, from that being to temporality. A remark Heidegger made to Max Müller, who reports it, could confirm such a multiplication. In Division Three of Part One, titled *Zeit und Sein*, no fewer than three differences were to have intervened: a transcendental or ontological difference in the narrow sense, the difference of entities from their entity-ness; a difference according to transcendence, or ontological in the broad sense, the difference of entities and their entity-ness from being itself; and a transcendent or theological difference in the narrow sense, the difference of God from entities, entity-ness and being. Such an almost quantitative enumeration indicates hesitation rather than rigorous elaboration; it may even enumerate exactly the hesitations just identified, since the first difference, entity to entity-ness, looks like the first gap, and the second, entities and entity-ness to being itself, like the second. That both should count as ontological difference, narrow or broad, would suit the indeterminacy the question of being as constructed in 1927 imposes on an ontological difference reached but still undetermined. The ternary topography of the question of being, when it does not marginalise ontological difference, must double it to make room for it. So what *Sein und Zeit* lacks, in order to do justice to a difference already reached, is only a surplus of the lack itself.

### 6 - The Ontic Primacy of the Question of Being as Interrogation

The redoubling of the two first terms of the question of being by a third leads to an impasse from the standpoint of ontological difference, and to an advance from the standpoint of Dasein. For the sense of being is added to the other two terms only by specifying the identity of one of them: in the constructed question of being the entity put in question must be understood exclusively as Dasein, the remarkable entity. *Sein und Zeit* no more escapes the duty of making being differ from entities than it ignores the locution. But — and here lies its strange originality — it exercises ontological difference phenomenologically only from a topography other than the dual one of difference: from the three-termed topography of the question of being. By that distortion it falls to Dasein to accomplish the very difference it disturbs and nonetheless makes possible. For the question of being is constructed in 1927 thanks to the privilege and ontic priority it grants Dasein. So if, as Marion claims, the ontological difference effectively at work in 1927 is never exercised except within the inappropriate topography of the question of being, one must show by way of confirmation that *Sein und Zeit* submits the skewed accomplishment of ontological difference itself to the same privilege of Dasein. That may also explain why the occurrences of the locution do not coincide with the ontological difference actually put to work.

Dasein exercises ontological difference first as transcending. Husserl again supplies the negative starting point, having entrusted transcendence to objects and things external to consciousness, henceforth confined to immanence. Heidegger radically inverts that distribution, in favour not of consciousness but of what eliminates it by deploying it, Dasein: it is not objects that transcend — things can never transcend or be transcendent — but subjects, understood in the ontological sense of Dasein, that transcend, that is, transgress and surpass themselves. Dasein transcends itself and of itself. But in transcending itself Dasein on one hand transcends entities and on the other transcends into the dimension of being: being and the structure of being lie beyond every entity and every possible entitative determination of an entity; being is the *transcendens* pure and simple. Dasein's transcendence is established, against Husserl, only in the draught of the transcendence par excellence, that of being itself: Dasein enters transcendence only by virtue of being's disclosure by itself — transcendence of Dasein, but through being; transcendence of the being of Dasein. If Dasein must transcend the thematised entity, it owes that to the prior fact that being is never explicable from entities but is already for each entity the "transcendental." The formulas about the transcendence of the world or of world-time should not mislead: world and its temporality do not themselves belong to worldly entities but open in a dimension made possible only by being-in-the-world, hence by the Dasein that alone thereby transcends entities. The difference between entities and the being irreducibly inexplicable from them is phenomenologically attested by a gap, a gap to be traversed, hence a transcendence from one to the other — or better, a transcendence of the one that makes the other appear for the first time. Such transcendence can be accomplished only by starting from entities, hence by an entity, and going so far as to break with every entity, hence by an entity that escapes entity-ness. An entity that escapes entity-ness transgresses itself so as to perform in and outside itself the transcendence: this characterises Dasein, workman therefore of the ontological difference it does not name, since it does it.

But Dasein could not exercise ontological difference by self-transcendence if, more essentially, it were not itself that difference. How else understand its paradoxical determination — the remarkable ontic trait of Dasein lies in its being ontological? Dasein's qualification for transcending the entity it is toward and according to the being that no entity is or explains does not rest on any mixture or compromise but on the remarkable fact that it is the only entity for which being has a sense. Marion's analogy is exact and worth keeping: Dasein has the sense of being as a musician has the sense of melody, a painter of colours, an athlete of competition. For it and it alone being is to be understood; this entity includes in its simple ontic definition the understanding of being; and reciprocally being consists in nothing — in nothing of an entity — since it resides solely in the understanding the sense of being permits Dasein to take of it. Dasein is such that it takes itself into account as an entity only by understanding, through its sense of being, being itself. It is as a perpetual transition from the ontic to the ontological, or rather as the transition of the ontic within it by the ontological. How is that transition effectively accomplished? Because Dasein is the entity determined by possibility and project on so radical a mode — being-toward-death, care, anticipatory resoluteness — that in that project what is at issue is not merely an ontic determination of Dasein but its very being: it is ontically remarkable in that for this entity its being is at issue in its being. According to its project, or its refusal of any possible project, Dasein exists authentically or inauthentically, that is, determines its manner of being. And there is more: the being Dasein puts in play cannot be reduced to an entity-ness, first because being receives no determination from any entity whatever, and second because from the start the question of being aims at the sense of being in general and not merely at the being of entities. Hence the earlier formula recurs with being itself, as such, at issue: Dasein is on the mode of understanding, in being, something like being; it is of being that this entity has something at issue — to be read in the light of a later note: which being? To be the *there* and so to sustain being in general. Dasein therefore accomplishes within itself, offering itself as a non-spatial link, the transition from entities to being. It is on the mode of ontological difference because it is ontically the ontologically different.

Yet in 1927 Heidegger identifies Dasein not with ontological difference but with the question of being: the questioning is itself an entity, given in the accomplishment of the question about the being of an entity, whether or not that is explicitly envisaged. Two reasons may explain it. First, because here again ontological difference, with two terms, is reinterpreted by and included within the question of being, constructed with three. Second and above all, because the question of being adds to ontological difference — the duel of entity with being — no third term other than Dasein itself. The supplementary term is not the sense of being, since that comes down to being in general, but the Dasein which, over against the entity not of Dasein's measure and hence over against being, puts being itself in general in play. Dasein introduces less a third term than the only possible link, the *Da*-, between being in question and the entity. In any case Dasein's intervention as the ontically ontological entity renders dual ontological difference operative only by confusing it with, and inscribing it within, the three-termed question of being. So in *Sein und Zeit* ontological difference had to efface itself behind the question of being, letting itself be covered over by the "ontological difference" between the manner of being of Dasein alone and that of other entities — precisely because the question of being *is* Dasein itself.

### 7 - The Misconstrual of Ontological Difference by "Ontological Difference"

The double conclusion is paradoxical. Against the authorised interpretation, *Sein und Zeit* does know an "ontological difference." Following the received interpretation, *Sein und Zeit* does not yet think ontological difference merely by naming one. Marion assigns the paradox a motive: in *Sein und Zeit* the phrase obeys the ternary construction of the question of being and so forbids itself access to the strictly dual dimension of the future ontological difference. And the third term enters with Dasein, which between entity and being provokes the — perhaps opaque — mediation of a being of the entity. Whatever precautions one takes to stress that Dasein as pure ecstatic *da* opens an absolutely docile transition between entity and being rather than imposing a third instance, it remains that everything *Sein und Zeit* publicly accomplishes stays wholly and explicitly devoted to Dasein, directly in Division One and indirectly in Division Two, without the consideration of being in general, absolutely and in its sense, ever receiving more than strictly programmatic mentions. It therefore seems legitimate, even inevitable, to put a question in the form of a hypothesis: if it is Dasein itself that, precisely by making the ternary question of being constructible, forbids the passage to binary ontological difference and hence to the sense of being as such, has *Sein und Zeit* bound its incompletion — its inability to think being in general and absolutely — to the obstacle Dasein constitutes? Must Dasein be envisaged not only as the motor of the emergence of the question of being but as the obstacle to its access to ontological difference? Does Dasein make the question of being possible, through the named "ontological difference," only so as to forbid answering it, through ontological difference thought? If that could be found in Heidegger's own text it would confirm the whole analysis; and if *Sein und Zeit* itself literally attributed its incompletion to Dasein's very function, the confirmation would become an almost indisputable validation.

Marion then states the fact flatly, and the brevity is the point: the last two published pages of *Sein und Zeit*, those of §83, pose exactly this question, and better, answer it explicitly. The staging matters because it converts a reconstruction into a reading. What Marion has argued for six sections as an interpretive hypothesis, he now claims to find Heidegger conducting himself, in the work's closing lines.

(a) The question is posed when Heidegger recalls the results: Dasein's factual totality has been disengaged in its ontological and existential ground, according to the double possibility of authenticity and inauthenticity; that ground has itself been manifested from its being, care; and care in turn has recognised its sense of being in temporality. But however considerable, the result remains preparatory, like the analytic of Dasein — merely a path, a provisional goal, since the analysis of Dasein is not only incomplete but provisional. The goal, by contrast, is the elaboration of the question of being in general, the idea of being in general, the ecstatic project of being in general. The gap so designated opens between Dasein with its being, care, on one side, and the sense of being in general on the other; it is therefore the second gap of the ternary structure, expressly mentioned as remaining to be crossed as long as the first has not been surpassed. Philosophy is accomplished as universal phenomenological ontology only by *going out from* the analytic of Dasein. Going out does not mean disowning, criticising or forgetting it, since phenomenological procedure will never cease to come from it; but that provenance still implies a surpassing as decisive as itself, since from the original positing of the question of being it was never a matter of considering the first gap for its own sake. The urgency of the transition grows all the more in that, at the end, crossing the second gap looks problematic. What makes §83 remarkable is that instead of mentioning the second gap only as a not-yet-accomplished project within the programmatic frame of the work's plan, it confronts its non-accomplishment as an actual difficulty, not yet in fact surmounted and possibly insurmountable in principle. The difficulty redoubles: not only must one go out from the analytic of Dasein to reach being in general, but Dasein might not offer the means of passing from one gap to the other. A question is therefore posed: does Dasein permit access to being as such? (b) What answer does §83 give? First a capital point: Dasein — like the crossing of the first gap — is there determined twice by "ontological difference," or at least by the opposition characterising it throughout the book: in one case the difference of the being of existing Dasein from the being of the entity not of Dasein's measure, reality for example; in the other, the "difference" of "consciousness" and "thing." Dasein as such, in its own manner of being, is marked by a difference from every other manner of being — an "ontological difference." That identification, normal in *Sein und Zeit*, is nonetheless perfectly remarkable here, first because this is the work's last page, where Heidegger undertakes to stigmatise the motive of its incompletion and even to diagnose an aporia; but above all because the "ontological difference" so identified with Dasein appears only to be exposed to open criticism. It offers only a starting point for the ontological problematic, and nothing on which philosophy can rest; one must even ask whether it suffices at all for an originary unfolding of the ontological problematic. The path that Dasein, and hence the restricted "ontological difference" it puts in play, offers toward being in general — is that path a path, and is it even the sole and unique one?

Which path is at issue? The "ontological difference" in the restricted sense, hence the Dasein that alone can put it to work. The interrogation bears on a single point: can *Sein und Zeit*'s enterprise envisage traversing the second gap, from the being of the entity to the sense of being, by leaning on the same and only Dasein that traverses within itself the first gap, from entity to the being of the entity? Or: can access to being in general be founded on an entity, even a remarkable one, as access to the being of entities is? In short, does the question of being as such — ontological difference, being/entity — admit an ontic foundation, Dasein in its being, "ontological difference"? Heidegger declares it explicitly: the analytic of Dasein must not be taken for a dogma but as the formulation of a still enveloped fundamental problem — does ontology let itself be founded ontologically, or does it also need an ontic foundation for that, and in that case what entity must assume the function of the founding? That question, breaking out openly in what will become the last page, is not addressed to some indeterminate or future interlocutor: it aims back at one of *Sein und Zeit*'s most originary phenomenological decisions. As soon as the formal structure of the question of being is elaborated, Heidegger had already asked on what entity the sense of being is to be read off, from what entity the bringing-to-light of being should take its departure. And when the question is concretely constructed it claims and confirms its ontic primacy by devoting itself to and leaning on the remarkable entity, Dasein. Dasein secures the primacy of the question of being by virtue of its own threefold primacy: an ontic primacy, since this entity is determined in its being by existence; an ontological primacy, since on the ground of its determinacy of existence Dasein is in itself ontological; and, equiprimordially with the understanding of existence, an understanding of the being of every entity not of Dasein's measure — whence a third primacy as the ontico-ontological condition of possibility of all ontologies, so that Dasein shows itself as what is primarily to be interrogated before every other entity. The primacy of the question of being therefore rests on the primacy of Dasein; more, Dasein's primacy multiplies into ontic and ontological so as finally to exercise ontological difference. The ontic foundation impugned at §83 corresponds exactly to the Dasein established in its ontic but also ontological, hence ontico-ontological, primacy by §4, that is by the whole introduction. So if Dasein's role as ontic foundation becomes questionable to the point of opposing to it a directly ontological founding of ontology without ontic mediation; if the *gigantomachia* over being has not yet been able to break out because the eventual adversaries have not been able to arm themselves with Dasein's primacy alone; if one must still ask how a disclosive understanding of being is possible at all at Dasein's measure — then the conclusion must be admitted: *Sein und Zeit*'s last question puts Dasein's primacy in question, hence its claim to mediate the terms of the question of being, its capacity to traverse the gap between the being of the entity and being in general, and the legitimacy of its reduction of ontological difference to "ontological difference."

Several arguments confirm that this is self-criticism, and that §83 radically reopens the initial decisions. (a) The construction of the question of being begins by asking on what entity the sense of being is to be read off. In his own copy Heidegger comments and criticises: two different questions put together, misleading, above all as regards the role of Dasein. For the sense of being, the second gap, cannot be read directly off any entity whatever; an entity, even Dasein, only ever lets the being of the entity be read, the first gap. To attribute to Dasein the direct manifestation of the sense of being is therefore first to confuse the two moments and gaps of the question of being, and second to overvalue Dasein's reach arbitrarily — as the aporia of §83 confirms. (b) The same construction continues by asking about the primacy of the exemplary entity: from what entity should the bringing-to-light of being take its departure, is that departure arbitrary, or does a determinate entity hold a primacy, and in what sense? Heidegger notes: misleading. Dasein is exemplary because it echoes back the counter-play that being addresses and entrusts to it, as to a partner, in general, in its essence as Dasein watching over the truth of being. Marion glosses: Dasein has no intrinsic primacy in elaborating the question of being, as though it grounded the question in itself; it has only an exemplarity coming to it from the play of being itself put into play within it — the German *Beispiel* heard as *Bei-spiel*, a playing-alongside. Dasein opens the question of being not by virtue of an antecedent primacy but by letting itself be worked by the advent of being as such within it. In a word, primacy no longer qualifies Dasein as remarkable entity but being itself, in play ontologically and not ontically. (c) The ontic foundation is also criticised retrospectively *a contrario* in the situation of falling: falling being covers over ontically the authentic being of Dasein and so denies to an ontology directed at that entity its adequate basis. That is, negatively, the very situation instituted at §2 — reading being off the remarkable entity. But Heidegger will later note at that place: wrong! As if ontology could be read off from the authentic ontic. What is an authentic ontic, if it is not authentic from a pre-ontological project — already supposing that the whole should remain within that distinction. Marion picks out three things. First, the express rejection of any reading of being from an entity, even authentic Dasein, in literal contradiction of the initial construction of the question of being. Second, the affirmation that ontology in the most radical sense does not depend on the ontic but that the ontic depends on the primacy of the ontological, or even, before it, on a more originary sending. Third, that the ontico-ontological distinction becomes the — perhaps provisional — horizon of the question of being. These three self-criticisms confirm that Heidegger was afterwards perfectly conscious that the non-accomplishment of the 1927 breakthrough depended closely on the uninterrogated primacy granted to Dasein in constructing the question of being. And that primacy, by introducing a mediating term, forbids the question of being to disengage canonical ontological difference at once and substitutes for it the "ontological difference" between two entities and their manners of being. So it is licit to conclude that *Sein und Zeit* owes its incompletion to nothing but the concealment within it of ontological difference by "ontological difference" — that is, by Dasein itself, which therefore does indeed add, in itself, enigma upon enigma.

# Commentary on Henri Bergson, *Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience*

Source: the PUF Quadrige critical edition (2007), whose text keeps the pagination of the earlier PUF reference editions, reproduced in the margin of the Centenary *Œuvres* (1959). References in parentheses are to that pagination: roman numerals for the Foreword, arabic numerals for the main text. The editorial dossier is drawn on only where it fixes a target or a reference.

Several misreadings recur in the reception of this book, and the commentary guards against them throughout. Bergson does not deny that physical things can be measured or that science may legitimately count; he denies that the measure of causes transfers to conscious states, and he treats the transfer as useful before he treats it as illegitimate. Qualitative multiplicity is not the absence of multiplicity, nor an undifferentiated unity; it is a different mode of being many. Duration in the *Essai* belongs to consciousness alone: external things are said to have simultaneity without succession, a restriction that *Matière et mémoire* and *L'Évolution créatrice* will lift, and which should not be read back as already lifted. The "immediate data" are not empiricist sense-atoms; they are what consciousness gives before spatial symbolism reworks it, and they are reached only by an effort of reflection against habit. "Space" here means the homogeneous medium the intellect conceives, not felt extension, which Bergson keeps distinct from it. The superficial self is not a mere illusion; it is the socially and practically necessary refraction of the deep self. Freedom is neither indeterminism nor the liberty of indifference; Bergson dissolves the classical problem rather than taking the libertarian side of it. Finally, "intuition" is not yet a methodological term of art in this book, and the later method should not be projected onto it.

## Foreword

(vii) The book opens on a diagnosis of language. Words demand of our ideas the same sharp boundaries and the same discontinuity that hold between material objects, so that we think, for the most part, "in space." Bergson grants the arrangement its value: practical life needs it, and so do most sciences. His claim is narrower and more radical at once. Some philosophical problems may be insoluble because their very formulation juxtaposes in space what occupies no space; if so, contradictory solutions are the predictable result of a contradiction installed in the question. The operative figure is "translation": the unextended rendered as extended, quality rendered as quantity. The model will govern the whole book, down to the claim that freedom is untranslatable (166). The strategy is set here as well. Bergson does not promise a solution; he promises to strip away the crude images around which the dispute is fought, after which the dispute may simply end. The later doctrine that the true task of philosophy is to pose problems correctly (*La pensée et le mouvant*) is already the procedure of the first page. This is also the first appearance of space in Bergson's work, and its status is double from the start: the condition of practice, language and science, and the screen between us and a reality that is temporal.

(vii–viii) Bergson chooses freedom because it belongs to metaphysics and to psychology at once. The phrasing alludes to Lachelier, to whom the book is dedicated, and to his essay "Psychologie et métaphysique" (1885), where freedom crowns the ascent of consciousness. Bergson's thesis is that every debate between determinists and their opponents presupposes three confusions, which are one: duration with extension, succession with simultaneity, quality with quantity. Dissipate it, and the objections to freedom vanish, along with the definitions of freedom on offer and, "in a certain sense," the problem itself. The problem disappears as a problem of choosing between rival theories, not as a question about what a free act is; Chapter III will still describe free acts. The Foreword also fixes the architecture: the third chapter carries the demonstration, and the first two, on intensity and duration, serve as its introduction. The reader should therefore take Chapter I's long analyses of effort, emotion and psychophysics as preparing a single result about the series of conscious states, not as freestanding psychology.

## Chapter One: On the Intensity of Psychological States

(1) Bergson starts from a consensus. Common sense, philosophers and psychologists all hold that sensations, feelings, passions and efforts can grow and diminish; the psychophysicists add that one sensation can be two, three or four times as intense as another. The decisive observation concerns the opponents of psychophysics: they reject the numerical ratio but keep the "more" and "less." Bergson's eventual argument (52–54) is that this compromise cannot stand, and that whoever grants an intrinsic "more" has already granted Fechner his project. The position under attack is Kant's doctrine of intensive magnitude in the Anticipations of Perception, and behind it the school metaphysics of Wolff, for whom a degree of heat could be double or triple another as one line is double another. The paragraph ends by denying that the point is minor: a question nobody notices is precisely the kind that the Foreword said could be badly posed.

(1–2) When we say that one number or body is larger than another, we know what we mean, because both cases concern unequal spaces, the larger containing the smaller; the claim that number is spatial is promised for Chapter II. Containment is the criterion of magnitude. A more intense sensation does not contain a less intense one. One might answer that the greater intensity implies the lesser, since we reach it only by passing through the lower degrees. Bergson calls this circular. Numbers can be ranged in increasing order only because they already stand in relations of container and contained; the order presupposes the magnitude rather than founding it. The real question is how we form a series out of terms that cannot be superposed, and by what sign we know the series ascends rather than descends. Everything reduces to one question: why is an intensity assimilated to a magnitude?

(2–3) The standard remedy distinguishes extensive quantity, which is measurable, from intensive quantity, which admits only more and less. Bergson finds this evasive. Calling both "magnitude" asserts something in common, and he asks what could be common to the extended and the unextended. If an intensity can increase, we see the less within the more; it is then divisible, hence extended, and "inextensive quantity" contradicts itself. Yet the impression of "greater" is the same in both cases, and when we examine it consciousness offers the same image of container and contained: a greater effort is pictured as a longer coil of thread, a spring that will occupy more space when released. The word "intensity" itself carries the image of a present contraction and a future expansion, a virtual extension, a "compressed space." Bergson concludes that we translate the intensive into the extensive, and that comparing two intensities consists, or at least is expressed, in a confused intuition of a relation between two extensions. What remains obscure is the mechanism of the translation, and the rest of the chapter seeks it case by case.

(3–4) The obvious mechanism would define intensity by the number and size of its measurable causes: more identical sources at the same distance give a more intense light. Bergson rejects this as a definition. We usually judge the intensity of an effect without knowing its cause, and often infer the cause from the effect, even correcting the first verdict of the senses. Appeal to an earlier experience in which cause and effect were perceived together works in many cases but fails for the deep states that come from us and have no external cause. And we are most confident about intensity precisely where only the subjective side is given or the cause resists measurement: pulling a tooth hurts more than pulling a hair; the artist knows a master's canvas gives more pleasure than a shop sign; no theory of cohesion is needed to know that bending a steel blade costs less effort than bending an iron bar. Bergson will later reinstate "putting the cause into the effect" as the genetic mechanism of intensity for representative sensations (31). What he denies here is that our concept of intensity means the magnitude of the cause; he leaves open that an association with the cause may have shaped how we perceive it.

(4–5) A subtler version of the same hypothesis draws on mechanical and kinetic physics, which explains sensible qualities by movements of elementary parts and expects intensive differences to reduce to extensive ones. Perhaps we vaguely sense, beneath the louder sound, the wider vibration; or perhaps every conscious state corresponds to a disturbance of cerebral molecules whose amplitude the sensation's intensity measures. Bergson finds the cerebral version as plausible as the other and no more helpful. Consciousness gives the sensation, not the mechanical work, and we estimate the work from the sensation's intensity rather than the reverse. Intensity therefore remains, in appearance at least, a property of the sensation itself, and the question returns intact: why do we call a superior intensity "greater"? The objection does not contest the physiology; it contests the claim that physiology answers a question about what is given. And the refusal to let a brain-state's magnitude explain an experienced character anticipates the later critique of psychophysical parallelism in *Matière et mémoire* and "Le paralogisme psycho-physiologique" (1904).

(5–6) Bergson now changes tack. Perhaps the difficulty comes from calling by one name intensities of very different kinds, those of a feeling on the one hand and of a sensation or an effort on the other. Effort comes with muscular sensation; sensations depend on physical conditions that probably enter into our estimate; both lie at the surface of consciousness and are bound up with the perception of a movement or an external object. Some states, rightly or wrongly, seem self-sufficient: deep joys and sorrows, reflective passions, aesthetic emotions. Pure intensity should be easiest to define where no extensive element seems to intervene, and there it reduces to a quality or shade that colours a larger or smaller mass of psychic states, or, equivalently, to the number of simple states that pervade the fundamental emotion. The thesis thus has two components from the start: a qualitative tinge and an extent of diffusion. Bergson begins at the pole where the translation into space has the least foothold, and will then travel toward the pole where it seems irresistible. The verb "pervade" (*pénétrer*) introduces the vocabulary of interpenetration that Chapter II will make central, while the talk of a "number" of states shows that the distinction between two kinds of multiplicity is not yet drawn.

(6–7) The example of an obscure desire that becomes a deep passion makes the thesis concrete. The desire was weak because it seemed isolated, foreign to the rest of inner life. Gradually it pervaded more and more psychic elements and tinted them with its colour, until one's view of everything changed: the same objects no longer make the same impression, sensations and ideas seem refreshed, "like a new childhood." Certain dreams have the same character, ordinary in content yet traversed by an original note. The general law follows: the deeper one goes into consciousness, the less one may treat psychic facts as things set side by side. To say that an object takes up much room in the soul means only that its image has altered the shade of a thousand perceptions or memories and pervades them without showing itself. Reflective consciousness dislikes this dynamic picture because it prefers distinctions that words express easily and things with clear contours, like those in space. It therefore supposes that, everything else unchanged, one desire has passed through successive magnitudes, as if magnitude made sense where there is neither multiplicity nor space; here "multiplicity" still means numerical distinctness. The parallel with effort, to be developed at 15–20, is announced: reflection concentrates diffuse muscular contractions at one point and calls them a growing effort, and in the same way it isolates the progressive change of a whole mass of coexisting states as a single growing desire. The paragraph contains the first sketch of the opposition between the deep and the superficial self.

(7) Hope is intensely pleasant because the future, which we dispose of as we please, appears in a multitude of forms, all smiling and all possible. Even if the most desired is realized, the others must be sacrificed, and we lose a great deal. The idea of the future, pregnant with infinitely many possibilities, is therefore richer than the future itself, which is why hope charms more than possession, and the dream more than reality. The short paragraph applies the thesis of 5–6 to a single case: hope's intensity is the plurality of futures held together in one feeling, and fulfilment lowers it by reducing that plurality to one. The "possible" here is an affective richness of the present, not a logical precondition of the real. The later critique of the possible as a retrospective mirage ("Le possible et le réel," 1930) attacks a different notion and does not contradict this one.

(7–8) Bergson next isolates the growth of joy and sorrow in the rare cases free of physical symptoms. Inner joy is not a localized fact that spreads. At its lowest it resembles an orientation of conscious states toward the future; then, as if relieved of weight, ideas and sensations succeed one another faster and movements cost less; in extreme joy perceptions and memories take on an indefinable quality, like warmth or light, and we feel something like astonishment at existing. These are distinct forms, successive stages of qualitative change. The magnitude-illusion arises from a second factor: each form affects a larger or smaller number of states, and without counting explicitly we know whether the joy pervades all the day's impressions or leaves some untouched. We thus mark points of division between successive forms, and the gradual transition makes them appear as degrees of one feeling changing in size. Sorrow shows the same pattern in reverse: an orientation toward the past, an impoverishment in which each sensation and idea seems exhausted by the little it gives, a future closed off, and finally a crushing in which we long for nothingness and each new misfortune brings a bitter pleasure. Bergson describes the structure of the feeling's course, and the "intensity" turns out to be a reading of that course in spatial terms.

(9–10) Aesthetic feelings show still more clearly how new elements, visible within the fundamental emotion, seem to increase it while changing its nature. Grace begins as the perception of ease in outward movements. Easy movements are those that prepare one another, so we come to find a higher ease in movements we could foresee, in present attitudes in which future ones are indicated and as if preformed. Jerky movements lack grace because each is self-contained and announces nothing; the curve pleases more than the broken line because it changes direction at every moment, each new direction already indicated in the last. The perception of ease thus merges with the pleasure of arresting the march of time and holding the future in the present. Rhythm and music add a third element: by letting us foresee the dancer's movements, they make us feel that we command them, as if the regular beats were invisible threads by which we work an imaginary puppet; if it stops, our hand moves impatiently to push it on. Grace therefore includes a physical sympathy, which pleases by its affinity with moral sympathy. Against Spencer (*Essays on Progress*), grace cannot be mere economy of effort; its essence is a mobile sympathy, always on the point of giving itself, a virtual movement toward us. The growing intensities of the feeling resolve into a sequence of distinct feelings, each announced by the one before, then visible, then eclipsing it, and we read this qualitative progress as a change of magnitude because we like simple things and our language is ill-suited to fine analysis. Two later doctrines are seeded here. The temporal structure of grace, the future contained in the present and continuous change of direction, anticipates the description of pure duration in Chapter II. The notion of sympathy anticipates the "intellectual sympathy" by which *Introduction à la métaphysique* (1903) will define intuition.

(10–14) The feeling of the beautiful also admits degrees. Bergson thinks it hard to define because we take natural beauty to be prior to art, which reduces art to a means of expressing a beauty whose essence stays mysterious. He suggests the reverse: nature may be beautiful only by happily coinciding with certain procedures of our art, so that art precedes nature. Sound method studies beauty first where conscious effort produced it and descends from there to nature, "which is an artist in its own way." From this standpoint art aims to put to sleep the active, or rather resistant, powers of the personality and so bring us into perfect docility, in which we realize the suggested idea and sympathize with the expressed feeling. Its procedures are attenuated and spiritualized forms of the procedures that induce hypnosis. In music, rhythm and measure suspend the normal flow of sensations and ideas by making attention oscillate between fixed points, so that the faintest imitation of a moaning voice fills us with sadness; music moves us more than natural sounds because nature expresses feelings, whereas music suggests them. The poet is one in whom feelings unfold into images and images into rhythmic words; rhythm lulls the soul until it forgets itself and sees with the poet. The plastic arts work through the fixity they suddenly impose on life, which a physical contagion passes to the spectator's attention: the pale immobility of ancient statuary gives a light emotion something definitive and eternal. Architecture obtains effects analogous to rhythm through symmetry and the indefinite repetition of a motif, which make perception oscillate from same to same and break the habit of incessant change that ordinarily recalls us to our own personality. Art therefore impresses feelings rather than expressing them, and willingly abandons imitation when it finds more effective means. Nature also works by suggestion but lacks rhythm; it compensates by the long companionship that shared influences have created between it and us, so that we respond to its least indication as a practised subject obeys the hypnotist's gesture, especially when well-proportioned beings let attention divide evenly over the whole figure. The conclusion is that there is no special feeling of the beautiful: any feeling becomes aesthetic when it is suggested rather than caused. Aesthetic emotion therefore has degrees of intensity, which are really phases like those of hypnosis: the suggested feeling barely interrupts the close fabric of our history, then draws attention away from it, then replaces it and absorbs the whole soul. These are differences of state, not of degree. A work's merit, however, lies less in the force of the suggestion than in the richness of the feeling suggested, and so we distinguish, beside degrees of intensity, degrees of depth or elevation. Most emotions are pregnant with a thousand sensations, feelings and ideas that pervade them, so each is unique and indefinable, and one would have to relive another's life to grasp it. The artist makes us feel what he cannot make us understand: he fixes those outward signs of his feeling that our body will imitate slightly and mechanically, and so places us back in the indefinable state that produced them, abolishing the barrier that time and space set between his consciousness and ours. The more ideas, sensations and emotions the feeling contains, the deeper the beauty. The two scales map onto the chapter's two components: intensity tracks qualitative changes of state, depth the number of elementary facts confusedly discerned. This hypnotic and suggestive aesthetics, with its emphasis on bodily imitation, differs from the later account in *Le rire* (1900), where art lifts the veil that practical perception draws over things; the common element is the suspension of the active personality.

(14–15) Moral feelings submit to the same analysis, taking pity as the case. Pity begins by putting oneself in another's place and suffering with him. If that were all, Bergson argues against the theory that reduces it to imaginative transposition, it would make us flee the wretched, since suffering naturally repels us; horror may lie at its origin, but a new element soon joins it, the need to help. La Rochefoucauld's reduction of pity to calculation, a shrewd foresight of evils to come, captures a further stage, fear, which is still a lower form. True pity consists less in fearing suffering than in desiring it: a faint desire one would hardly want fulfilled, formed despite oneself, as if nature were committing a great injustice and one had to clear oneself of complicity. Its essence is a need for self-abasement, an aspiration to descend, whose charm is that it raises us in our own esteem and sets us above the sensible goods from which we momentarily detach ourselves. The growth of pity is therefore a qualitative progression from disgust to fear, from fear to sympathy, and from sympathy to humility. Each stage is a new feeling, and the earlier stages are not quantities contained in the later. The moral analysis has the same form as the aesthetic one, a sequence of states each announcing and absorbing its predecessor, which confirms that the model is general across the deep states and not an artefact of one example.

(15) Bergson closes the analysis of deep states and marks the turn. Those states seem detached from external causes and involve no perceived muscular contraction, but they are rare. Almost every passion, desire, joy or sorrow has physical symptoms, which probably serve in estimating intensity. Sensations proper are visibly bound to their external cause, and although their intensity cannot be defined by the size of that cause, some relation between the two must exist. In some manifestations consciousness even seems to spread outward, as if intensity developed into extension; muscular effort is the case. Bergson jumps at once to that extreme. The plan of the chapter becomes visible: a traverse of a single series from the pole where intensity is purely inner to the pole where it seems purely outer, with the intermediate cases (attention, violent emotions, affective sensations, representative sensations) to be filled in afterwards. The wager is that the same analysis will hold at both ends.

(15–16) If any phenomenon seems given immediately as a quantity, it is muscular effort. We picture psychic force shut up in the soul like the winds in Aeolus's cave, awaiting release; the will watches over it and opens an outlet in proportion to the effect desired. Bergson thinks this crude picture accounts for much of our belief in intensive magnitudes. Because muscular force, which unfolds in space in measurable effects, seems to have pre-existed them in a compressed state, we compress its volume further and further until we think we understand how a purely psychic state, occupying no space, can still have a size. The "compressed space" of 3 now has its source. Science reinforces the illusion: Bain holds that the feeling accompanying muscular movement coincides with the outward current of nervous force, so that consciousness would perceive the very emission of force; Wundt speaks of a sensation of central origin accompanying voluntary innervation, and cites the paralytic who clearly feels the force he spends trying to lift a leg that does not move. The doctrine of a "feeling of innervation" would have become accepted science, Bergson says, had William James not drawn attention to some neglected facts.

(16–18) When a paralytic tries to lift the inert limb, he does not make that movement, but he makes another, and without some movement somewhere there is no feeling of effort. Vulpian had observed that a hemiplegic asked to close his paralysed fist does so, unawares, with the healthy one. Ferrier described a stranger case: extend the arm and crook the index finger as if to fire a pistol, without moving the finger, and you still feel you are spending energy; on inspection the feeling coincides with fixing the chest, closing the glottis and contracting the respiratory muscles, and it vanishes when breathing resumes unless the finger actually moves. These facts already suggested that we are conscious not of an emission of force but of the resulting muscular movement. James's originality was to verify this on cases that seemed to resist it. When the external rectus of the right eye is paralysed, the patient tries in vain to turn the eye right, yet objects seem to move to the right; Helmholtz inferred that the effort of will itself had reached consciousness. James replied that the covered left eye moves during the experiment, and that the perception of its movement gives both the feeling of effort and the apparent motion of objects. Effort is therefore centripetal, not centrifugal: in James's words, a complex afferent sensation from contracted muscles, stretched ligaments, compressed joints, a fixed chest, a closed glottis, a knit brow, clenched jaws, in short from every peripheral point that the effort modifies. Bergson reports the debate with care because it delivers what he needs: whatever the effort is, its felt content lies at the periphery and is distributed over the body.

(18) Bergson then declines to take sides. His question is not whether the feeling of effort comes from the centre or the periphery but what our perception of its intensity consists in. Careful self-observation yields a conclusion James did not formulate but which fits his doctrine: the more an effort seems to grow, the more muscles contract in sympathy, and the apparent consciousness of greater effort at one point reduces to the perception of a larger area of the body taking part. Bergson's thesis concerns the phenomenal content of "more," so it would survive even a centrifugal account of effort. The thesis also does not abolish quantity; it relocates it. There really is a "more," but it belongs to the extent of the body involved, not to the effort at the place where we feel it.

(18–19) Clench your fist "harder and harder." The feeling seems to grow while staying in the hand, but the hand feels the same throughout; the sensation has spread to the arm and shoulder, then the other arm stiffens, the legs follow, breathing stops, and the whole body is at work. We notice these concomitant movements only when warned; until then we believe we have one state changing in size. The same holds for pressing the lips together: the lip sensation stays the same while muscles of the face, head and finally the rest of the body join in. We do feel this spreading, this increase of surface, which is a genuine change of quantity; but since we attend to the lips, we locate the increase there and turn the psychic force spent at that spot into a magnitude, though it had no extension. Someone lifting heavier and heavier weights shows the same pattern: contraction gradually invades the body, while the sensation in the working arm stays constant for a long time and changes only in quality, heaviness becoming fatigue and fatigue pain. The conclusion is exact: consciousness of growing muscular effort reduces to the double perception of a larger number of peripheral sensations and a qualitative change in some of them. The illusion consists in transferring the first, a real quantity of sites, onto the place where the second, a change of quality, occurs.

(19–20) The intensity of a superficial effort and that of a deep feeling thus receive the same definition: qualitative progress and growing complexity, confusedly perceived. Consciousness, used to thinking in space and to telling itself what it thinks, names the feeling with a single word and locates the effort where it produces a useful result. It then perceives an effort always the same, growing in its assigned place, and a feeling that keeps its name and swells without changing its nature. The two sources of the illusion, spatial thought and inner speech, are named together, and a third motive appears: utility fixes where the effort is localized. Bergson expects the same illusion in the states between the two poles, those accompanied by muscular contractions and peripheral sensations. These superficial elements are coordinated either by a purely speculative idea, giving intellectual effort or attention, or by a practical representation, giving the "violent" or "acute" emotions: anger, fear, certain kinds of joy, pain, passion and desire. The series is now complete in outline, and the next paragraphs fill the middle.

(20–21) Attention is not a purely physiological phenomenon, but movements accompany it, and, as Ribot showed, they are neither its cause nor its result: they are part of it and express it in extension. Fechner had already reduced the feeling of attentive effort in a sense organ to the muscular feeling produced by reflexively moving the muscles attached to it, and had noted the tension of the scalp and the pressure on the skull felt when straining to remember. Ribot describes the frontal muscle raising the brows and furrowing the forehead, the mouth opening in extreme cases, the lips pushed out in a pout in children and many adults. Bergson grants that voluntary attention always contains a purely psychic factor, if only the will's exclusion of ideas foreign to the one we wish to attend to. Once that exclusion is made, however, the feeling of a growing immaterial tension of the soul turns out, on analysis, to be the feeling of a muscular contraction that spreads or changes character, tension becoming pressure, fatigue and pain. The division of labour is precise: the psychic factor selects, the body supplies the intensity. Bergson's later treatments of attention in *Matière et mémoire* (attentive recognition) and "L'effort intellectuel" (1902), which describe intellectual effort as the passage from a dynamic schema to images, will give the psychic side far more structure than it receives here.

(21–22) Bergson sees no essential difference between attentive effort and what might be called the effort of tension of the soul: acute desire, unleashed anger, passionate love, violent hatred. Each reduces, he thinks, to a system of muscular contractions coordinated by an idea, a more or less reflective idea of knowing in attention and an unreflective idea of acting in emotion. The intensity of such emotions is nothing but the accompanying muscular tension, and Darwin's description of rage, with its racing heart, laboured breathing, trembling, clenched teeth and gestures of striking, shows what that tension contains. Bergson stops short of James's thesis ("What is an emotion?", 1884) that rage is the sum of these organic sensations. An irreducible psychic element remains, if only the idea of striking or fighting that gives so many different movements a common direction. The idea determines the direction of the emotion; its growing intensity is the deepening disturbance of the organism, which consciousness gauges by the number and extent of the surfaces involved. Bergson answers the objection from suppressed rage, which seems the more intense for being held in: when an emotion runs free, consciousness does not dwell on the details of the movements, whereas it fixes on them when it tries to hide them. Remove every trace of organic disturbance and every incipient contraction, and anger becomes an idea; if one still calls it an emotion, it has no assignable intensity. The split between direction, which belongs to the idea, and intensity, which belongs to the body, is Bergson's qualified version of the James–Lange theory.

(22–23) Spencer describes intense fear as expressed by cries, attempts to hide or flee, palpitations and trembling. Bergson goes further: these movements are part of the fear itself; through them fear becomes an emotion capable of degrees, and without them only an idea of fear remains, an intellectual representation of a danger to avoid. Joy, pain, desire, aversion and even shame have an acuteness explained by the automatic reactions the organism begins and consciousness perceives: love quickens the heart and breath and flushes the face, according to Darwin; aversion shows in small movements of disgust repeated whenever we think of the hated object; shame makes us blush and clench our fingers even when it is retrospective. The acuteness of these emotions is measured by the number and nature of their peripheral sensations. As an emotion loses violence and gains depth, internal elements replace peripheral ones, and ideas, memories and states in general, rather than outward movements, become oriented in greater or smaller number in one direction. Hence the general result: with respect to intensity, deep feelings and acute emotions differ in where the multiplicity lies, not in its structure. A feeling becomes "violent" by projecting itself outward and radiating to the surface, peripheral sensations taking the place of internal elements; superficial or deep, its intensity is always the multiplicity of simple states that consciousness confusedly discerns in it. The phrase "simple states" will itself be questioned in Chapter II, where conscious states lose the discreteness that makes them countable.

(23–24) So far the cases have been complex states whose intensity does not wholly depend on an external cause. Sensations appear simple, and their intensity seems to vary with the cause of which they are the conscious equivalent, so the question becomes sharper: how does quantity invade an effect that is unextended and, this time, indivisible? The appeal to a plurality of components seems blocked. Bergson first divides sensations into affective and representative. The two shade into each other, and most simple representations contain an affective element, but nothing prevents isolating that element and asking separately what the intensity of pleasure or pain consists in. The order of treatment is significant: the affective sensations come first because, being tied to action, they will reopen the route to a peripheral multiplicity that the simplicity of sensation seemed to close.

(24) The problem of affective intensity seems hard because the affective state is taken to be only the conscious expression of an organic disturbance, or the inner echo of an external cause. A greater nervous disturbance generally goes with a more intense sensation, but these disturbances are unconscious as movements, since consciousness takes them in as a sensation that does not resemble them; so it is unclear how they could pass on their magnitude. Superposable magnitudes like vibration amplitudes have nothing in common with sensations that occupy no space. If the stronger sensation seems to contain the weaker, it must keep something of the physical disturbance; but it keeps nothing if it is merely the conscious translation of a molecular movement, because the movement, precisely in being translated into pleasure or pain, remains unconscious as movement. On the translation model, what consciousness receives is by definition not given as what it was, so the quantity of the cause cannot cross over.

(25) Bergson proposes a change of perspective: pleasure and pain may signal not only what has happened or is happening in the organism but what is about to happen and tends to occur. It is unlikely that a profoundly utilitarian nature would assign consciousness the purely scientific task of reporting on the past or present, which no longer depend on us. Moreover, we rise by insensible degrees from automatic to free movements, and free movements differ chiefly in that an affective sensation intervenes between the external action that occasions them and the willed reaction. All our actions might have been automatic, as they are in countless organisms where stimulus produces response without consciousness. If pleasure and pain appear in some privileged beings, it is probably to allow them to resist the automatic reaction: either sensation has no reason to exist, or it is "a beginning of freedom." But resistance requires knowing the reaction in advance by some precise sign, and the sign can only be the sketch, the preformation, of the coming automatic movements within the sensation itself. The affective state therefore corresponds above all to the movements being prepared, those that would like to be. The turn from a retrospective to a prospective reading of affect is the most consequential move in the chapter. It ties consciousness and freedom together biologically, anticipates Chapter III, and seeds the account of affection and of the living body as a "centre of indetermination" in the first chapter of *Matière et mémoire*.

(25–26) The hypothesis seems at first only to invert the difficulty, since the question was what a physical phenomenon and a conscious state could share in respect of magnitude, and an index of future reaction looks no more shareable than a translation of past stimulus. Bergson insists the difference is considerable. The molecular disturbances were necessarily unconscious, because nothing of them as movements could survive in the sensation that translated them. The automatic movements that tend to follow the stimulus, and would be its natural continuation, are probably conscious as movements; otherwise the sensation, whose role is to invite a choice between that reaction and other possible movements, would serve no purpose. The intensity of affective sensations would then be our consciousness of the involuntary movements that begin, and are as it were drawn out, in these states, and that would have run their course had nature made us automata rather than conscious beings. The gain is exactly what 24 found missing: an extensive component that is present to consciousness. Quantity enters affect, as it entered effort, through a perceived bodily multiplicity, not through the cause.

(26–27) A pain of growing intensity should therefore be compared not to a note becoming louder but to a symphony in which more and more instruments play. Within the characteristic sensation that sets the tone, consciousness discerns many sensations from different points of the periphery, muscular contractions and organic movements of every kind, whose concert expresses the organism's new demands. We measure a pain's intensity by the interest that a larger or smaller part of the organism takes in it. Richet observed that a weak pain is referred to a precise point and a stronger one to the whole limb, and concluded that pain radiates more as it is more intense. Bergson reverses the proposition: intensity is defined by the number and extent of the parts of the body that sympathize and react under consciousness's eye. Richet's own description of disgust bears this out: weak excitation brings no nausea; stronger excitation spreads through almost the whole organic system, the face paling, the skin sweating, the heart pausing, a general perturbation that Richet calls the supreme expression of disgust. Bergson asks whether it is only the expression, and what the general sensation of disgust could be but the sum of these elementary sensations. Darwin's account of extreme pain, with its violent efforts to escape, contorted mouth and clenched teeth, points the same way: to call a pain extreme is to call it unbearable, an incitement to a thousand actions of escape. Bergson grants that a nerve might transmit pain independently of any reaction, and that stronger stimuli might affect it differently; but those differences would be read as quantities only by way of the reactions that usually accompany them. Without the reactions, a pain's intensity would be a quality, not a magnitude. A qualitative core of pain remains, and only its reading as a magnitude depends on the body.

(28) We have hardly any other way of comparing pleasures. A greater pleasure is a preferred pleasure, and preference is a disposition of our organs by which, when two pleasures present themselves together, the body leans toward one. Analyse the inclination and you find a thousand small movements beginning in the organs concerned and elsewhere, as if the organism went to meet the pleasure represented; to call inclination a movement is not a metaphor. We can arrest this spontaneous, almost reflex orientation, but the attraction of the pleasure is nothing other than the movement begun. The acuteness of a pleasure while we enjoy it is the inertia of an organism drowning in it and refusing every other sensation, an inertia we feel through our resistance to distraction; without it pleasure would still be a state but no longer a magnitude. The closing sentence draws a Newtonian lesson: in the moral as in the physical world, attraction serves to describe movement rather than to produce it. The intensity of a pleasure is not a force behind behaviour but our reading of the body's motion. This prepares Chapter III's attack on the picture of motives as forces of different weights, the "strongest motive" of the determinist.

(29–31) Many representative sensations have an affective side and provoke reactions that we take into account when judging their intensity. A great increase of light produces a sensation that is not yet pain but resembles dazzlement; as a sound's vibration grows, the head and then the body seem to vibrate or to receive a shock. Tastes, smells and temperatures are always pleasant or unpleasant: between more or less bitter tastes we would find only differences of quality, like shades of one colour, but these are at once read as differences of quantity because of the reactions of pleasure or disgust they suggest. Even when a sensation stays purely representative, its cause cannot pass a certain degree of strength or weakness without provoking movements that serve to measure it. To catch a distant sound, a faint smell or a weak light we strain all our activity, we "pay attention," and the sensation seems weak precisely because it needs our effort to reinforce it; conversely we recognize extreme intensity by irresistible automatic reactions or by the helplessness it inflicts, as when a cannon shot or a sudden blinding light briefly takes away the consciousness of our personality. In the middle range we often judge a sensation's importance by what it displaces or by its persistence: a watch ticks louder at night because it easily fills a consciousness nearly empty of sensations and ideas, and foreigners speaking a language we do not know seem to shout because their words, evoking no ideas, break into a kind of intellectual silence. Here "magnitude" is the share of the conscious field a sensation occupies. With these middle sensations, however, intensity needs a new meaning. The organism scarcely reacts in any visible way, yet we still treat pitch, brightness and saturation as magnitudes. Féré has shown that every sensation increases muscular force as measured by the dynamometer, but that increase hardly strikes consciousness, and the precision with which we distinguish sounds, colours, weights and temperatures demands another factor. The reaction model, which carried the analysis from effort to pleasure and pain, here reaches its limit.

(31–32) The new factor is easy to identify. As a sensation loses its affective character and becomes a representation, the reactions it provoked fade, but we perceive the external object that causes it, or have perceived it and think of it. The cause is extended and therefore measurable, and lifelong experience, beginning with the first glimmers of consciousness, has shown us a determinate shade of sensation answering to a determinate value of the stimulus. We associate with a certain quality of the effect the idea of a certain quantity of the cause, and finally, as in every acquired perception, we put the idea into the sensation, the quantity of the cause into the quality of the effect. At that moment intensity, which was a shade or quality, becomes a magnitude. A simple experiment shows the process: hold a pin in the right hand and prick the left hand more and more deeply. You feel a tickle, then a contact, then a prick, then a pain localized at a point, then a pain radiating around it. These are qualitatively distinct sensations, varieties of one species; yet you spoke of one prick growing more intense, because you located in the left hand's sensation the progressive effort of the right hand that caused it. The formula "putting the cause into the effect" is the chapter's second explanatory principle, alongside the bodily multiplicity of the earlier cases. It also recovers what 3–4 had rejected: the size of the cause does not define what intensity means, but a sedimented association with it explains why a representative sensation looks like a magnitude. The pin experiment is well chosen, because the measured cause is our own effort, which links the second principle back to the first.

(32) Sounds show well-marked degrees of intensity. Bergson has already allowed for their affective character, the shock to the whole organism, and for the competition among simultaneous sounds. Set those aside and an indefinable quality of the heard sound remains. It is read at once as a quantity because we have produced it countless times ourselves, striking an object with a determinate effort, and because we know how far we would have to raise our voice to produce a similar sound; the idea of that effort comes to mind whenever we make loudness a magnitude. Wundt has noted the special links in the brain between vocal and auditory nerve fibres, and it has been said that hearing is speaking to oneself; some neuropaths cannot follow a conversation without moving their lips, which only exaggerates what happens in everyone. The suggestive power of music depends on the same fact: we inwardly repeat the sounds we hear and so place ourselves back in the psychological state from which they came, a state that cannot be expressed but that the movements adopted by our whole body suggest. The cause put into the effect is here specified as our own productive effort, and the motor imitation that explains loudness is the same one that explained aesthetic suggestion at 10–14.

(33–34) For a sound of medium loudness, then, "magnitude" refers chiefly to the effort we would need to make to obtain the same sensation again. Pitch is a second property, and Bergson asks whether differences of pitch, as the ear perceives them, are quantitative. A higher sound evokes a higher position in space, but that does not show that the notes of the scale differ otherwise than in quality. Forget physics, he says, and examine your idea of a higher or lower note: you are thinking of the greater or lesser effort that the tensor muscle of your vocal cords would have to make to produce it. Because the effort by which the voice passes from one note to the next is discontinuous, we picture successive notes as points in space reached by abrupt leaps across empty intervals, and so we set intervals between the notes of the scale. Why the line is vertical rather than horizontal needs a further explanation. High notes seem to resonate in the head and low ones in the chest, which has helped us count intervals upward. Further, in the inexperienced singer, the greater the tension of the vocal cords in chest voice, the larger the area of the body involved, which is why he feels the effort as more intense; and since he breathes the air out from below upward, he gives the same direction to the sound the current of air produces. We call the note higher because the body makes an effort as if to reach a higher object. Once the physicist could define pitch by the number of vibrations per unit of time, we no longer hesitated to say that the ear perceives quantitative differences directly. The genealogy of the spatial metaphor is double, bodily and scientific, and the claim that the discrete scale is a projection of discontinuous effort prepares the contrast with the continuous melody of Chapter II.

(34–37) Blix, Goldscheider and Donaldson have shown that heat and cold are sensed at different points of the skin, so physiology already tends to treat them as different in kind. Attentive consciousness goes further and finds specific differences among sensations of heat, and among those of cold: a more intense heat is really another heat. We call it more intense because we have often felt the same change when approaching a source of heat or when more of the body was exposed to it, and because these sensations quickly become affective and provoke reactions that measure their cause; we then extend quantitative differences to the intermediate values. Bergson asks each reader to face the sensation itself, clearing away what experience has taught about its cause, and he predicts the result: the magnitude of a representative sensation comes from putting the cause into the effect, and the intensity of its affective element from introducing the reactive movements that continue the stimulus. Pressure and weight come next. When pressure on the hand grows stronger, contact has become pressure, then pain, which has radiated after several phases, and the growing counter-effort we oppose to it, which is a more extended effort, has been brought in. The psychophysicist who lifts a heavier weight says he feels an increase of sensation; Bergson asks whether it should rather be called a sensation of increase, and says that the whole question lies there, since in the first case the sensation would be a quantity like its cause, in the second a quality that has come to represent its cause's size. The distinction of heavy and light may seem as naive as that of hot and cold, but its naivety is what makes it a psychological reality: heavy and light are different genera for consciousness, and their degrees are species of those genera. The difference of quality is spontaneously translated into a difference of quantity because of the more or less extended effort the body makes; lift a basket you have been told is full of scrap iron when it is empty, and you almost lose your balance, as if muscles had joined the operation in advance and were suddenly disappointed. The illusion is reinforced by our habit of believing that we perceive a homogeneous movement in a homogeneous space. When I lift a light weight, I have a series of muscular sensations, each with its "local sign" and its own shade, which consciousness reads as a continuous movement. When I lift a heavier weight to the same height at the same speed, each sensation differs from its counterpart; but because I again read the series as a movement with the same direction, duration and speed, consciousness must locate the difference elsewhere, at the end of the moving arm, and persuades itself that the movement was the same while the weight differed in size. Movement and weight, however, are distinctions made by reflective consciousness. Immediate consciousness has the sensation of a heavy movement, which analysis resolves into a series of muscular sensations, each representing by its shade the place where it occurs and by its colouring the size of the weight. The formula "sensation of increase, not increase of sensation" condenses the chapter; the opposition between immediate and reflective consciousness names the book's title; and the "homogeneous movement in a homogeneous space" is the target of the analysis of motion and of Zeno in Chapter II.

(37–39) Is the intensity of light a quantity or a quality? In everyday life many different cues tell us about the light source: difficulty in making out outlines and details, the affective prelude to dazzlement, the way edges and shadows stand out as the number of sources changes. Bergson gives most weight to the changes of hue that surfaces, even pure spectral colours, undergo under weaker or stronger light. As the source approaches, violet turns bluish, green turns whitish yellow and red bright yellow; as it recedes, ultramarine turns violet and yellow green, and finally red, green and violet converge on whitish yellow. Physicists have noted these shifts, but most people do not see them unless attentive or warned. Having resolved to read changes of quality as changes of quantity, we first assume that each object has a fixed, proper colour; then, when its hue shifts toward yellow or blue, we say not that the colour has changed under a change of lighting but that the colour is the same and our sensation of luminous intensity has grown or diminished. The understanding's quantitative interpretation thus replaces the qualitative impression consciousness received. The postulate of an invariant "proper colour" is the hinge: it turns a variation of quality into the variation of a single quantity superimposed on a constant. Helmholtz points to a more complicated case of the same kind: a white composed of two spectral colours stays the same colour when both intensities change in the same ratio, though the ratio of sensations changes considerably, because daylight, our standard white, itself shifts in hue as its intensity varies. Bergson then marks the case still to be explained. When a single object, say a white surface, passes through degrees of brightness, the cue of relative hue is absent; physics measures such degrees with the photometer, and the psychophysicist claims that the eye itself evaluates them, as in the experiments of Delbœuf and of Lehmann and Neiglick. Bergson does not contest the results, only their interpretation.

(39–41) Look at a sheet of paper lit by four candles and have one, two, then three extinguished. You say the surface stays white and its brightness diminishes, because you know a candle has gone out or have often seen such a change accompany dimmer lighting. Set aside memory and habits of speech, and what you actually saw was a layer of shadow passing over the surface as the candle went out. The shadow is as real to consciousness as the light; if the original surface in full brightness is white, what you now see needs another name, a new shade of white. Bergson then pushes further. Experience and physics have taught us to treat black as an absence or minimum of luminous sensation, and greys as decreasing intensities of white light. In fact black is as real for consciousness as white, and the decreasing illuminations of a surface would be, for a consciousness without preconceptions, so many different shades, comparable to the colours of the spectrum. The proof is that the change in sensation is not continuous like the change in its cause: light can increase or decrease for a while with no apparent change in the surface, which seems to change only when the variation suffices to create a new quality. Apart from affective sensations, variations of brightness would reduce to qualitative changes if we had not acquired the habit of putting the cause into the effect. Saturation is treated the same way: if intensities of a colour are shades between it and black, degrees of saturation are shades between it and pure white; black is to intensity what white is to saturation. The discontinuity argument is the empirical lever of the whole critique. A sensation that changes by jumps while its cause varies continuously cannot be a magnitude that mirrors the cause. Fechner will build on exactly this discontinuity, and Bergson's reply (48–49) will turn on what is done with it. The positive status of black also agrees with Hering's opponent theory of colour vision, whose author the dossier lists among the real adversaries of psychophysics.

(41) The meaning of photometric experiments now becomes clear. A candle at a given distance lights a sheet of paper in a certain way; double the distance, and four candles are needed to give the same sensation; one concludes that at double distance the original candle would have produced a quarter of the effect. Bergson insists that this concerns the physical effect, not the psychological one. No two sensations were compared; a single sensation was used to compare two light sources, the second four times the first but twice as far away. The physicist never uses sensations that are double or triple one another, only identical sensations serving as intermediaries between two physical quantities that can then be equated. The sensation plays the part of an auxiliary unknown that a mathematician introduces into a calculation and that drops out of the result. The analysis isolates a criterion that governs the rest of the chapter: legitimate science uses sensation only as a detector of identity, never as a quantity with ratios of its own.

(41–42) The psychophysicist's object is different: the luminous sensation itself, which he claims to measure, either by integrating infinitely small differences, as Fechner does, or by comparing one sensation directly with another, as Plateau and Delbœuf do. Bergson says the second method differs less from the first than has been thought, but treats it first because it concerns light. Delbœuf shows an observer three concentric rings whose brightness can be varied from white to black. Two greys, A and B, are fixed; the brightness of the third ring, C, is varied until the observer judges grey B to lie equally far from the other two, so that contrast AB equals contrast BC. One could then construct a scale of luminous intensities proceeding by equal sensible contrasts, and sensations would measure one another. For Bergson the sole essential question is whether a contrast AB, made of A and B, is really equal to a contrast BC composed differently. If it were shown that two sensations can be equal without being identical, psychophysics would be founded. The whole dispute is reduced to that one condition, and Bergson thinks it is easy to explain how one sensation can be said to lie midway between two others without its being met.

(42–43) He offers a thought experiment. Suppose that from birth changes in the intensity of a light source had reached consciousness as the successive colours of the spectrum. Those colours would then appear to us like the notes of a scale, as higher or lower degrees, as magnitudes. Each could also be given its place in the series: the extended cause varies continuously, while the colour sensation changes discontinuously from one shade to another, so the shades between two colours A and B could be roughly counted, and compared with the number between B and a third colour C. If the numbers matched, B would be said to lie equally far from A and C. This would be a convenient interpretation and nothing more. Equal numbers of intermediate shades, passed by abrupt jumps, do not tell us that the jumps are magnitudes, still less equal ones; and it would have to be shown that the intermediaries used for measuring are somehow contained in the thing measured. Without that, the "equal distance" is a metaphor. The counterfactual makes visible what "distance" between sensations amounts to, a count of discrete qualitative steps, and names the two conditions such a count fails to meet: equality of the units and containment of the steps in the term, the criterion of magnitude laid down at 1–2.

(43–45) If what was said about luminous intensity is granted, Delbœuf's greys are, for consciousness, analogous to colours, and declaring one grey equidistant from two others means what it would mean to say that orange lies midway between green and red. The difference is that the series of greys has always appeared in our experience together with a gradual increase or decrease of lighting, so we do for brightness what we never think of doing for hue: we turn changes of quality into variations of magnitude. The measuring is easy because the greys produced by a continuous dimming are discontinuous, being qualities, and we can roughly count the principal intermediaries between two of them. Contrast AB is declared equal to contrast BC when imagination, aided by memory, interposes the same number of landmarks on each side. Such a judgment must be crude and should vary between persons; above all, hesitation and disagreement should grow with the difference in brightness between rings A and B, since estimating the number of intervening shades becomes harder. Delbœuf's own tables show exactly this: as the brightness difference grows, the spread between the values at which the same or different observers stop increases almost continuously, from 3 to 94, from 5 to 73, from 10 to 25, from 7 to 40. Bergson's counting hypothesis thus makes a prediction, and the data bear it out. Even if observers always agreed, two things would remain unproved: that successive elementary contrasts are equal quantities, when we know only that they are successive; and that a given grey contains the lower greys through which imagination passed. Delbœuf's psychophysics therefore rests on a theoretical postulate disguised as experiment, which Bergson states: when objective light increases continuously, the differences between successive greys, each corresponding to the smallest perceived increase of stimulus, are equal quantities, and any sensation can be equated with the sum of the differences separating the preceding sensations from zero. That, he says, is precisely the postulate of Fechner's psychophysics, so the two methods converge.

(45) Fechner started from a law discovered by Weber: given a stimulus producing a sensation, the amount of stimulus that must be added for consciousness to notice a change stands in a constant ratio to it, so that the ratio of the added stimulus ΔE to E is constant. Fechner's successors modified the formula, and Bergson leaves the choice between versions to experiment. He readily admits that a law of this kind probably exists, because it does not measure sensation; it fixes the moment at which an increase of stimulus makes the sensation change. If a determinate amount of stimulus produces a determinate shade of sensation, the minimum amount required to change that shade is also determinate, and since it is not constant it must be a function of the stimulus to which it is added. The concession is genuine: Weber's law concerns thresholds of qualitative change, which is exactly what Bergson's own account predicts. The illegitimate step is elsewhere: in passing from a relation between a stimulus and its minimal increment to an equation linking a "quantity of sensation" to the stimulus. All of psychophysics, Bergson says, lies in that passage.

(46) He identifies several artifices in it. One first agrees to treat our awareness of an increase of stimulus as an increase ΔS of the sensation S. One then lays down that all the ΔS corresponding to the smallest perceptible increase of stimulus are equal. One treats them as quantities, and since they are always equal while experiment has given a relation ΔE = f(E) between stimulus and minimal increment, one expresses the constancy of ΔS by writing ΔS = C·ΔE/f(E), C being a constant. One finally replaces the very small differences ΔS and ΔE by infinitely small ones, dS and dE, which yields a differential equation, dS = C·dE/f(E), and integrating both sides gives S = C∫dE/f(E). One thus passes from a verified law, which concerned only the appearance of a sensation, to an unverifiable one that claims to give its measure. Bergson's footnote adds that if Weber's law is taken without restriction, the integration gives S = C log(E/Q), Q being a constant: Fechner's "logarithmic law." The first artifice is the decisive one, since it converts the sensation of an increase into an increase of sensation, the very confusion diagnosed at 34–37; the others only exploit it.

(47) Without discussing the procedure in detail, Bergson undertakes to show three things in a few words: how Fechner grasped the real difficulty of the problem, how he tried to overcome it, and where his reasoning goes wrong. Fechner is not treated as a naive empiricist but as the one thinker who saw what measuring a psychic state would require, so that his failure, if shown, is the failure of the best possible attempt.

(47–48) Fechner understood that measurement cannot enter psychology unless equality and addition between two simple states, two sensations say, are first defined. Unless they are identical, it is unclear how two sensations could be equal. In the physical world equality is not identity, but only because every phenomenon there has two aspects, one qualitative and one extensive; nothing stops us from abstracting the first, which leaves terms that can be superposed directly or indirectly and thus identified. The qualitative element that is removed from external things to make measurement possible is exactly what psychophysics retains and claims to measure. It cannot measure a quality Q by some physical quantity Q′ underlying it, because one would first have to show that Q is a function of Q′, which could be done only by having measured Q against some fraction of itself. One may, of course, measure the sensation of heat by degrees of temperature, but that is a convention, and psychophysics consists precisely in refusing the convention and asking how the sensation varies as the temperature varies. Two different sensations, it seems, could be called equal only if some identical ground remained once their qualitative difference was removed; and since that difference is all we feel, nothing would remain. The dilemma is now fully formulated: for things, measurement abstracts from quality and leaves a remainder; for sensations, abstraction from quality leaves nothing.

(48) Fechner's originality was not to find the difficulty insurmountable. Taking advantage of the fact that sensation changes by jumps when stimulus increases continuously, he gave all these differences one name: they are minimal differences, since each corresponds to the smallest perceptible increase of the external stimulus. Abstract from the specific shade of each difference and a common ground remains by which they are identified: all of them are minimal. That is the required definition of equality, and the definition of addition follows: if the difference perceived between two successive sensations is treated as a quantity, the first called S and the second S + ΔS, then every sensation S must be regarded as a sum obtained by adding the minimal differences passed through on the way to it. The double definition then yields a relation between ΔS and ΔE and, through differentials, between the two variables. Mathematicians may object to passing from differences to differentials; psychologists may ask whether ΔS, instead of being constant, varies with S, as has lately been proposed; and the meaning of the law can be disputed. But simply by treating ΔS as a quantity and S as a sum, one has granted the postulate on which everything rests. The "common ground" Fechner finds is not a felt content but a relational property, minimal with respect to the stimulus, so his definition of equality already puts the cause into the effect. The familiar objections are internal to the programme; Bergson's targets its premise.

(48–49) That premise seems to him questionable and barely intelligible. Suppose I feel a sensation S and, as the stimulus increases continuously, I notice the increase after a while. I am alerted to an increase of the cause, but what relation is there between this alert and a difference? The alert consists in the fact that the initial state S has changed and become S′. For the passage from S to S′ to be comparable to an arithmetical difference, I would need to be conscious of an interval between S and S′, and my sensibility would have to rise from one to the other by adding something. By naming the passage ΔS one first makes it a reality and then a quantity. Not only can one not explain in what sense the passage is a quantity; on reflection it is not even a reality, since only the states S and S′ are real. If S and S′ were numbers, the difference S′ − S would be real even if only S and S′ were given, because that number, a sum of units, would represent the successive moments of the addition leading from S to S′. If S and S′ are simple states, there is no interval, and the passage is an act of the thinker who, to suit the case, assimilates a succession of two states to a difference between two magnitudes. Bergson's objection is that a transition between qualities is not a third term that could be measured. The claim needs care in view of what follows: here the terms are taken as the discrete sensations reflective consciousness reports, and Chapter II will dissolve that discreteness into a continuous duration. Bergson is not denying change; he is denying that an arithmetical gap between states is given.

(49–50) The alternative is therefore strict. Either we keep to what consciousness gives, and find between S and S′ a difference like that between the shades of the rainbow, not an interval of magnitude; or we use a conventional mode of representation, in which case we may introduce the symbol ΔS, but it is by convention that we speak of arithmetical difference and by convention that we treat a given sensation as a sum. Jules Tannery, the most penetrating critic of Fechner, made this point clear: to say that a sensation of fifty degrees is expressed by the number of differential sensations that would succeed one another from the absence of sensation up to it is, in his view, a definition, as legitimate as it is arbitrary. Bergson's verdict is thus not that psychophysics is false but that it is conventional, the same verdict he gave on measuring the sensation of heat by temperature (47–48). Tannery is the one opponent who attacks the premise rather than the details, which is why Bergson ranks him above the critics who concede intensive magnitude.

(50–52) Bergson denies that the method of mean gradations has set psychophysics on a new path. Delbœuf's originality was to choose a case where consciousness seemed to side with Fechner, where common sense itself was a psychophysicist: he asked whether some sensations do not appear immediately as equal though different, so that one could draw up a table of sensations two, three or four times one another. Fechner's error was to believe in an interval between successive sensations where there was only a passage. But if the two terms were given at once, there would be a contrast as well as a passage, and a contrast, though not yet an arithmetical difference, resembles one in a certain respect: the terms are present to each other, as in the subtraction of two numbers. If the sensations are of the same kind, and our past experience has repeatedly shown them filing past as the stimulus increased continuously, we will very probably put the cause into the effect and merge the idea of contrast with that of arithmetical difference; and having noticed that sensation changes by jumps while the stimulus grows continuously, we will estimate the distance between two sensations by a rough count of the jumps, or of the intermediate sensations that usually serve as markers. Contrast will appear as difference, stimulus as quantity, the jump as a unit of equality, and their combination will give the idea of equal quantitative differences. These conditions are nowhere better met than when surfaces of one colour, differently lit, are seen together: the sensations are alike, their cause has always seemed closely tied to its distance, and since distance varies continuously we have noted countless shades succeeding one another as the cause increased. So we can say that the contrast between a first and second grey seems roughly equal to that between the second and a third, and if equal sensations are defined as those a more or less confused reasoning interprets as equal, one does arrive at a law like Delbœuf's. But consciousness has passed through the same intermediaries as the psychophysicist, and its judgment is worth exactly what psychophysics is worth: a symbolic interpretation of quality as quantity, a crude estimate of the number of sensations that could be inserted between two given ones. Fechner arrives at a conventional measure; Delbœuf appeals to common sense in the special cases where it adopts a similar convention. All psychophysics therefore turns in a vicious circle: its theoretical postulate demands experimental verification, and it can be verified experimentally only if the postulate is first granted. The reason is that there is no point of contact between the unextended and the extended, between quality and quantity; one can interpret either by the other, but sooner or later the conventional character of the equivalence must be admitted. Delbœuf's arrangement turns a passage into a contrast by presenting the terms side by side, and it is that co-presence, a spatial condition, that makes subtraction look possible. Chapter II will make simultaneity and juxtaposition the conditions of all counting.

(52–54) Psychophysics only formulated precisely, and pushed to extremes, a conception familiar to common sense. Because we speak more than we think, and because external objects, which belong to everyone, matter more to us than the subjective states we pass through, we have every interest in objectifying those states by introducing into them as much as possible of the representation of their cause. The more our knowledge grows, the more we see the extensive behind the intensive and quantity behind quality, and the more we put the first into the second and treat our sensations as magnitudes. Physics, whose role is to subject the external causes of our states to calculation, cares as little as possible about the states and deliberately confuses them with their causes, thereby encouraging and even exaggerating common sense's illusion. The moment had to come when science, accustomed to confusing quality with quantity and sensation with stimulus, would try to measure the one as it measures the other; that was psychophysics. Fechner was encouraged in this by his adversaries themselves, the philosophers who speak of intensive magnitudes while declaring psychic states refractory to measurement. If one grants that a sensation can be stronger than another, and that the inequality lies in the sensations themselves, apart from any association of ideas or any consideration of number and space, it is natural to ask by how much one exceeds the other. Replying that all measurement implies superposition, and that intensities cannot be superposed, does not help; one must then explain why a sensation is called more intense, and how things admitting no relation of container and contained can be called greater or smaller. Distinguishing an intensive quantity, which admits only more and less, from an extensive quantity, which admits measure, comes close to conceding Fechner's case: once something can grow and diminish, it is natural to ask by how much, and the lack of a direct measure does not exclude an indirect one, by integrating infinitesimal elements or by some other detour. The dilemma follows: either sensation is pure quality, or, if it is a magnitude, one must try to measure it. The argument returns to the starting point of 1–3 and closes it. The Kantian compromise of intensive magnitude, held by the philosophical opponents of psychophysics, is unstable, and Fechner is its consistent heir. Only the denial that sensation is a magnitude at all escapes the circle.

(54–55) In summary, the notion of intensity has two aspects, depending on whether one studies conscious states that represent an external cause or states that are self-sufficient. In the first case, perceiving intensity means estimating the size of the cause by a certain quality of the effect; it is what the Scottish philosophers called an acquired perception. In the second, intensity is the more or less considerable multiplicity of simple psychic facts that we divine within the fundamental state; it is not an acquired perception but a confused one. The two senses usually interpenetrate, because the simpler facts that an emotion or effort contains are mostly representative, and most representative states, being also affective, themselves contain a multiplicity of elementary facts. The idea of intensity lies at the junction of two currents: one brings from outside the idea of extensive magnitude, the other has gone to fetch from the depths of consciousness the image of an internal multiplicity and bring it to the surface. Bergson then opens the next question: what that inner image consists in, and whether it coincides with number or differs radically from it. Chapter II will no longer consider conscious states in isolation but in their concrete multiplicity, as they unfold in pure duration, and will ask, as Chapter I asked about representative sensation without the idea of its cause, what the multiplicity of inner states becomes, and what form duration takes, once one abstracts from the space in which it unfolds. That question matters far more. Confined to single conscious facts, the confusion of quality with quantity creates obscurities rather than problems; but when it invades the series of our states and brings space into our conception of duration, it corrupts at the source our representations of external and inner change, of motion and of freedom. Hence the sophisms of the Eleatic school, and hence the problem of free will. Bergson will dwell on the second, and instead of solving it he will show the illusion of those who pose it. The distinction between "obscurities" and "problems" measures the step from Chapter I to Chapter II: the local error yields confusion about single states, the serial error yields antinomies. The final sentence restates the Foreword's programme of dissolving rather than solving.

## Chapter Two: On the Multiplicity of Conscious States. The Idea of Duration

(56) Number is usually defined as a collection of units, or more precisely as the synthesis of the one and the many. Every number is one, since we represent it by a simple act of mind and give it a name, but its unity is that of a sum and embraces parts that can be considered separately. Bergson sets the concepts of unity and multiplicity aside for the moment and asks whether the idea of number implies something further. His own footnote shows what is at stake. After finishing the chapter he read F. Pillon's refutation, in the *Critique philosophique* (1883–84), of G. Noël's article on the solidarity of number and space, and found nothing to change, because Pillon does not distinguish time as quality from time as quantity, the multiplicity of juxtaposition from that of mutual penetration. Without that distinction, which Bergson calls the chief object of the chapter, one could hold with Pillon that coexistence suffices to construct number. But coexisting terms either organize themselves together, and then number never emerges from them, or remain distinct, and then they are juxtaposed, which is space. Simultaneous impressions from different senses do not escape the dilemma: either they keep their specific differences and are not counted, or their differences are abstracted and they can be told apart only by position. The verb "to distinguish," Bergson announces, has a qualitative and a quantitative sense, and everyone who has discussed the relation of number to space has confused them. The chapter's thesis is stated in the footnote before the text proves it.

(57) A collection of units is not enough: the units must be identical, or at least treated as identical when counted. We count the fifty sheep of a flock though the shepherd tells them apart easily, because we agree to neglect their individual differences and keep only their common function. Attend to their particular features and you can enumerate them, but you can no longer sum them. Counting the soldiers of a battalion and calling the roll are two different standpoints. The idea of number therefore implies the simple intuition of a multiplicity of parts or units that are absolutely alike. The distinction between summing and enumerating already separates two attitudes toward a many: one that keeps each term's individuality and one that strips it away to obtain a sum.

(57–58) The units must nonetheless differ somehow, or they would collapse into one. Even identical sheep differ at least by their places in space; otherwise there would be no flock. Take only the idea of the fifty sheep. Either we include them all in one image, and so juxtapose them in an ideal space, or we repeat the image of one sheep fifty times in succession, and the series seems to fall in duration rather than space. Bergson denies the second appearance. If I picture each sheep in turn and alone, I only ever have one sheep. For the number to grow as I go on, I must retain the successive images and set each beside the new units I call up, and that juxtaposition happens in space, not in pure duration. Counting material objects plainly involves representing them simultaneously and so leaving them in space. The question becomes whether every idea of number, even abstract number, carries the same intuition of space. Retention is the pivot: succession alone yields one term at a time, and a sum needs the terms to be held together, which Bergson identifies with juxtaposition.

(58–59) Each of us can answer by recalling how the idea of number developed from childhood: first a row of balls, then points, then the image vanishes and what we call abstract number remains. At that stage, however, number has stopped being imagined or even thought; we keep only the sign needed for calculation, and can affirm that 12 is half of 24 without thinking either number, to the benefit of speed. To represent a number rather than figures or words, we must return to an extended image. The illusion of a purely temporal number comes from the habit of counting "in time": to imagine fifty we recite the numbers from one, and on reaching fifty believe we built the number in duration alone. We did count moments of duration rather than points of space; the question is whether we counted those moments with points of space. Time alone can give pure succession but not addition, a succession ending in a sum, because each term must remain when we pass to the next and wait to be added. How could it wait if it were only an instant of duration, and where, if we did not place it in space? We involuntarily fix each counted moment at a point in space, and only on that condition do abstract units form a sum. Bergson grants, and promises to show, that successive moments of time can be conceived independently of space; but when we add earlier instants to the present one, we operate not on those instants, which have vanished, but on the lasting trace they seem to have left in space. We usually dispense with the image after the first few numbers, knowing it would serve for the rest. Every clear idea of number nonetheless implies a vision in space. Bergson does not say succession is spatial; he says summation is.

(59–61) Number is a collection of units and also a unit itself, as the synthesis of its units. Bergson asks whether "unit" has the same sense in both cases. When we say a number is one, we mean we grasp it as a whole in a simple, indivisible act of mind; this unity contains a multiplicity, being that of a whole. When we speak of the units composing the number, we take them to be pure, irreducible unities that generate the number series by combining indefinitely. There seem to be two kinds of unity: a definitive one that forms a number by being added to itself, and a provisional one belonging to the number, which is multiple in itself and borrows its unity from the simple act by which intelligence apprehends it. The belief that we think indivisibles when we think the component units explains much of the idea that number could be conceived without space. On closer inspection every unity is that of a simple act of mind, and since the act is one of uniting, some multiplicity must be its material. While I think a unit by itself, I take it as indivisible, since I think only of it; once I leave it for the next, I objectify it and so make it a thing, which is to say a multiplicity. The units with which arithmetic forms numbers are provisional, divisible without end, each a sum of fractions as small and numerous as one likes. A definitive unity, that of a simple act, could not be divided; one can divide it while calling it one only by implicitly treating it as an extended object, one in intuition and many in space. Nothing analysis draws out of a constructed idea can exceed what was put in; if the unit were the unity of an act, analysis would yield only pure unity. When we equate 3 with 1 + 1 + 1, we may treat the units as indivisible because we make no use of the multiplicity each contains, and the number probably first appears in that simple form because we think of how we obtained it rather than how we might use it. But multiplication requires that any number can serve as a provisional unit added to itself, and conversely that units are real numbers treated as provisionally indecomposable. To admit that a unit can be divided into as many parts as we like is to treat it as extended. The analysis splits unity into its two sources: the indivisibility comes from the act of mind, the divisibility from the space the act operates on.

(61–63) The discontinuity of number must not mislead us. Forming a number does involve discontinuity: each unit used to form three seems indivisible while I work on it, and I pass from one to the next without transition, and the same holds if I use halves or quarters, since attention must fix on each in turn. The indivisibility of the act is translated into a mathematical point separated from the next by an empty interval of space. These points, however, tend to stretch into lines as attention leaves them, as if seeking to join, and when we consider the finished number the junction is complete: the points have become lines, the divisions have faded, and the whole has all the marks of continuity. That is why a number composed by one law can be decomposed by any law. We must distinguish the unit we think from the unit we make into a thing after thinking it, and the number in formation from the number formed: the unit is irreducible while thought, the number discontinuous while built, but once finished it is objectified and therefore appears divisible without end. Bergson then defines the terms. We call subjective what seems entirely and adequately known, objective what is known in such a way that an ever-growing multitude of new impressions could replace our present idea of it. A complex feeling contains simpler elements, but as long as they are not distinctly extracted they cannot be said to have been fully realized, and once consciousness perceives them distinctly the state resulting from their synthesis has changed. A body's total aspect, by contrast, does not change however thought divides it, because all its possible decompositions are already visible in the image though not carried out; this actual, not merely virtual, apperception of subdivisions in the undivided is what we call objectivity. The mind's own contribution to number is the indivisible process by which it fixes attention successively on parts of a given space; the parts isolated in this way are kept to be added to others and, once added, allow any decomposition, so they are parts of space. Space is the material from which the mind builds number and the medium in which it places it. The definitions of subjective and objective are among the most consequential in the book: the objective is what can be divided without changing, the subjective what changes in being divided. Chapter III's analysis of the deep self, and the later theory of the virtual in *Matière et mémoire*, depend on this asymmetry.

(63) Arithmetic is what teaches us to divide the units of number indefinitely; common sense tends to build number from indivisibles. This is natural, because the provisional simplicity of the units comes from the mind, which attends more to its acts than to the material it acts on. Science only turns our gaze to that material, and it could not make us transfer number into space if we did not already locate it there. So from the start we must have represented number as a juxtaposition in space, which confirms the first conclusion, reached from the fact that every addition implies parts perceived together. The paragraph closes the analysis of number by reconciling the two arguments: the retention argument (57–59) and the divisibility argument (59–63) reach the same result by different routes.

(63–65) If number is so understood, not everything is counted in the same way, and there are two very different kinds of multiplicity. Material objects, which we can see and touch, are counted without any symbolic invention: we think them first separately and then together in the medium where they present themselves. Purely affective states, or representations other than those of sight and touch, are not given in space and can be counted only through some symbolic figuration. That figuration seems natural when the cause of the sensation is clearly in space: hearing footsteps in the street, I vaguely see the walker and place each sound where a foot might fall, counting my sensations in the very space where their tangible causes are aligned. Some may count the strokes of a distant bell by picturing it swinging, which suffices for the first two units. Most minds instead align the successive sounds in an ideal space while believing they count them in pure duration. Bergson then states the dilemma that governs the chapter. The sounds reach me successively, but either I retain each and organize it with the others into a group that recalls a known tune or rhythm, in which case I do not count them but receive the qualitative impression their number makes on me; or I set out to count them, and must dissociate them in some homogeneous medium where, stripped of their qualities and as if emptied, they leave identical traces. A moment of time cannot be kept to be added to others, so if the sounds are dissociated, empty intervals lie between them, and if they are counted, the intervals persist, which they could not do if they were pure duration. The operation therefore takes place in space, and it grows harder the deeper we go into consciousness, where we meet a confused multiplicity of sensations and feelings that only analysis separates. Two kinds of multiplicity follow: that of material objects, which forms a number immediately, and that of conscious facts, which takes the form of number only through a symbolic representation involving space. "Organizing" and "counting" are now the two operations corresponding to the two senses of "distinguish" announced at 56.

(65–67) We all draw this distinction when we speak of the impenetrability of matter. Impenetrability is sometimes ranked as a basic property of bodies, known in the same way as weight or resistance. Yet so negative a property cannot be revealed by the senses, and some experiments with mixtures and chemical combinations would lead us to doubt it if our conviction were not already fixed. If one body penetrates another, we at once suppose voids in the second to receive the particles of the first, and further voids if those particles must penetrate one another, and thought continues the regress rather than represent two bodies in one place. If impenetrability were a sensed quality, it would be no harder to conceive two bodies merging than a surface without resistance or a weightless fluid. The necessity is not physical but logical: that two bodies cannot occupy the same place at once is a proposition whose denial implies contradiction. That amounts to recognizing that the idea of the number two, or of any number, contains juxtaposition in space; impenetrability is a property of number rather than of matter. The objection that we count feelings, sensations and ideas, which penetrate one another and each fill the whole soul, receives the expected answer: precisely because they interpenetrate, we count them only by representing them as homogeneous units occupying distinct places, units that no longer interpenetrate. Impenetrability appears together with number. Attributing it to matter to distinguish matter from everything else restates the distinction between extended things, translatable into number immediately, and conscious facts, which first require a symbolic representation in space. The argument converts a supposed physical datum into an analytic consequence of plurality in space, and so supplies an independent confirmation of the number–space thesis from the side of physics.

(67–68) If counting conscious facts requires representing them symbolically in space, that representation probably alters the normal conditions of inner perception. Bergson recalls Chapter I: a representative sensation taken in itself is pure quality, but seen through extension it becomes quantity in a certain sense and is called intensity. Projecting our states into space to form a distinct multiplicity must likewise affect the states themselves and give them, for reflective consciousness, a form that immediate apperception did not attribute to them. When we speak of time, we mostly think of a homogeneous medium in which conscious facts are aligned and juxtaposed as in space. Bergson asks whether time so understood might be to the multiplicity of our states what intensity is to some of them: a sign or symbol, wholly distinct from true duration. He proposes to ask consciousness to isolate itself from the external world and, by a vigorous effort of abstraction, to become itself again, and to put two questions to it: whether the multiplicity of our states resembles that of the units of a number, and whether true duration has any relation to space. The analysis of number already makes the analogy doubtful. If reflective time is a medium in which states succeed one another distinctly enough to be counted, and if number scatters in space whatever is directly counted, then time as a medium of distinguishing and counting is presumably space; the fact that every image we use for reflective time and even for succession is borrowed from space confirms this, so pure duration must be something else. These questions can be settled only by a direct study of the ideas of space and time in their relations. Homogeneous time is to the series what intensity was to the single state, the same translation at a higher level. Reaching the immediate is not passive reception; it requires abstracting from the habits that have spatialized consciousness.

(68–69) Bergson first sets aside the question of the absolute reality of space, which may be as idle as asking whether space is in space. Our senses perceive the qualities of bodies and space with them. The real difficulty has been to decide whether extension is an aspect of physical qualities, a quality of quality, or whether qualities are unextended by essence, with space added to them as something self-sufficient that subsists without them. On the first hypothesis space would reduce to an abstraction, or rather an extract, expressing what certain representative sensations have in common; on the second it would be a reality as solid as the sensations, though of a different order. Kant gave the second view its precise formulation: the Transcendental Aesthetic endows space with an existence independent of its content, declares separable in principle what each of us separates in fact, and denies that extension is an abstraction like others. Kant's conception thus differs less than is supposed from popular belief; far from undermining our faith in the reality of space, Kant fixed its meaning and justified it. Bergson's reading is deliberately deflationary. Kant appears not as the critic who made space subjective but as the philosopher who certified the common-sense separation of space from its contents, which is the separation Bergson himself will both accept (space as homogeneous medium) and limit (by denying it any role in duration).

(69–70) Since Kant, his solution has hardly been seriously contested, and it has imposed itself, sometimes unawares, on nativists and empiricists alike. Psychologists agree that Johannes Müller's nativism has a Kantian origin, but Lotze's local signs, Bain's theory and Wundt's broader synthesis seem at first independent of the Aesthetic. Their authors set aside the nature of space to ask by what process our sensations come to take their place in it beside one another; but in doing so they treat sensations as unextended and draw, in Kant's manner, a radical distinction between the matter of representation and its form. For Lotze, Bain and Wundt the sensations from which we form the notion of space are unextended and merely qualitative, and extension results from their synthesis as water from two gases. The genetic explanations take up the problem exactly where Kant left it: Kant detached space from its content, and they ask how that content, isolated by thought, returns to space. They then seem to neglect the activity of intelligence and to generate extension from an alliance of sensations, space arising from their coexistence. Bergson asks how such a genesis is possible without an active intervention of mind. Even if extension were only a relation among unextended terms, the relation must be established by a mind able to associate them. The chemical analogy fails: the new form and qualities of a compound arise because we grasp the multiplicity of atoms in a single apperception, and suppressing the synthesizing mind abolishes them. Unextended sensations stay unextended unless something is added; for space to arise from their coexistence, an act of mind must embrace them together and juxtapose them, an act *sui generis* resembling what Kant called an a priori form of sensibility. Bergson thus turns the empiricists back toward Kant, but only on one point: the conception of homogeneous space is an act of mind and not a product of sensation.

(70–73) Characterized more closely, the act consists in the intuition, or rather the conception, of an empty homogeneous medium. There is hardly another possible definition of space: it is what lets us distinguish several identical and simultaneous sensations from one another, a principle of differentiation other than qualitative differentiation, and therefore a reality without quality. The local-sign theorists object that simultaneous sensations are never identical, since no two points of a homogeneous surface affect sight or touch in the same way. Bergson grants this readily, since otherwise there would be no reason to place one point to the right rather than the left; but because we then interpret the difference of quality as a difference of position, we must have the clear idea of a homogeneous medium, a simultaneity of terms identical in quality yet distinct. The more one stresses the retinal differences, the larger the role one leaves to the mind, which perceives as extended homogeneity what is given as qualitative heterogeneity. Bergson adds that, if the homogeneous space is due to an effort of intelligence, the qualities that differentiate two sensations must contain a reason why each occupies its particular place. Perception of extension must therefore be distinguished from conception of space; they are implicated in each other, but the independent idea of homogeneous space emerges more clearly the higher one goes among intelligent beings. Animals may not perceive or represent externality as we do. Naturalists have noted how many vertebrates and some insects find their way, returning almost in a straight line over hundreds of kilometres of unknown ground, and have explained this by sight, smell or a magnetic sense. This means that space is less homogeneous for the animal than for us: each direction has its own shade, its own quality. We ourselves distinguish right from left by a natural feeling, as a difference of quality, which is why we cannot define them. Qualitative differences are everywhere in nature, and two concrete directions could be as distinct in immediate apperception as two colours. The conception of an empty homogeneous medium is far more extraordinary and seems to require a reaction against the heterogeneity that forms the ground of our experience. We should say not only that some animals have a special sense of direction but above all that we have a special faculty of perceiving or conceiving a space without quality. This faculty is not abstraction; since abstraction presupposes sharp distinctions and a kind of mutual externality of concepts or their symbols, abstraction already presupposes the intuition of a homogeneous medium. We know two realities of different orders, one heterogeneous, the sensible qualities, and one homogeneous, space, which, clearly conceived by human intelligence, enables us to make sharp distinctions, to count, to abstract and perhaps to speak. Extension is perceived and qualitative; space is conceived and homogeneous; and the latter is a human achievement against the former, not an abstraction from it. The derivation of abstraction and language from the conception of space links the chapter back to the Foreword's opening sentence.

(73–74) If space is defined as the homogeneous, then conversely every homogeneous, unlimited medium would seem to be space, since homogeneity here means the absence of all quality, and two forms of the homogeneous could not be told apart. Yet time is commonly taken to be an unlimited medium distinct from space but homogeneous like it, the homogeneous taking two forms according as coexistence or succession fills it. When time is made a homogeneous medium in which conscious states unfold, it is given all at once, which removes it from duration; that alone should warn us that we have unconsciously fallen back on space. Material things, external to one another and to us, owe this double externality to a homogeneous medium that sets intervals between them and fixes their outlines; conscious facts, even successive ones, interpenetrate, and the whole soul can be reflected in the simplest of them. Homogeneous time may therefore be a "bastard concept," produced by the intrusion of space into pure consciousness. In any case two forms of the homogeneous cannot be admitted without asking whether one reduces to the other. Externality belongs properly to things that occupy space, whereas conscious facts are not essentially external to one another and become so only by unfolding in time conceived as a homogeneous medium. If one derives from the other, the idea of space is the fundamental datum. Some philosophers, misled by the apparent simplicity of time, have tried the reverse reduction, constructing space from duration; refuting them will show that time as a homogeneous medium is only the ghost of space haunting reflective consciousness. The argument is a priori and economical: homogeneity admits no internal difference, so homogeneous time and space cannot be two things, and the direction of derivation is fixed by where externality is native.

(74–76) The English school tries to reduce relations of extension to more or less complex relations of succession. Running a hand over a surface with eyes closed gives a series of sensations differing only in quality and ordered in time; experience shows the series is reversible, that by a different effort, or one in the opposite direction, we could have the same sensations in reverse order; spatial position would then be defined as reversible succession. Bergson finds the definition circular, or at least based on a superficial idea of duration, and introduces the distinction the whole book rests on. There are two possible conceptions of duration, one pure of all admixture, the other secretly involving space. Pure duration is the form taken by the succession of our conscious states when the self lets itself live, when it refrains from separating the present state from earlier ones. It need not be wholly absorbed in the passing sensation or idea, for then it would cease to endure; nor must it forget earlier states. It is enough that in recalling them it does not set them beside the present state as one point beside another, but organizes them with it, as when we recall the notes of a melody fused together. Though the notes succeed one another, we perceive them in one another, and their whole is comparable to a living being whose parts, though distinct, interpenetrate through their solidarity. The proof is that if we break the time by dwelling too long on one note, what tells us of the mistake is not the excessive length as length but the qualitative change brought to the whole phrase. Succession can thus be conceived without distinction, as mutual penetration, solidarity, the intimate organization of elements each of which represents the whole and is distinguished from it only by abstracting thought. Such would be the representation of duration in a being both identical and changing that had no idea of space. Familiar with space and even obsessed by it, we introduce it unawares into our representation of pure succession, setting our states side by side so as to perceive them simultaneously, no longer one within the other but one beside the other; we project time into space, express duration as extension, and succession takes the form of a continuous line or chain whose parts touch without penetrating. This last image involves the simultaneous perception of before and after, and a succession that remained succession while fitting into one instant would be a contradiction. The English school's "order of succession" and its reversibility therefore concern succession unfolded in space, where several separate terms can be grasped together; order requires distinguishing terms and comparing their places, so the successive becomes simultaneous. Either my sensations succeed in duration alone, and then I cannot represent several of them at once as distinct, or I discern an order, and then I already have the idea of space. Reversible series and temporal order therefore presuppose space and cannot define it. The definition of pure duration guards against two misreadings at once: it is not a dissolution of the self into the passing moment, and it is not forgetfulness of the past. It is memory without juxtaposition. "Distinct parts that interpenetrate" is also the precise formula for qualitative multiplicity, which is a mode of plurality, not its absence.

(76–78) Bergson makes the argument more rigorous with a thought experiment. Imagine an infinite straight line and a material point A moving along it. If the point became conscious, it would feel itself change, since it moves, and so perceive a succession; but would the succession take the form of a line? Only if the point could rise above the line and perceive several of its points together, which would give it the idea of space, so that it would see its changes unfold in space rather than in pure duration. This exposes the error of those who treat pure duration as analogous to space but simpler: they line up psychological states in a chain and think they are not using the idea of space, since space has three dimensions. But to perceive a line as a line one must stand outside it, be aware of the surrounding void, and so think a three-dimensional space. If the point has no idea of space, the succession of its states cannot take the form of a line; its sensations will add themselves dynamically to one another and organize themselves like the notes of a melody that lulls us. Pure duration might then be nothing but a succession of qualitative changes that fuse and interpenetrate, without sharp outlines, without any tendency to externalize themselves with respect to one another, without kinship with number: pure heterogeneity. It suffices for now to have shown that attributing the least homogeneity to duration surreptitiously introduces space. The target is the one-dimensional "time line" that seems innocent because it is not yet three-dimensional space; Bergson argues that a line is only ever seen from a space that contains it. The phrase "pure heterogeneity" is the most extreme characterization in the book, and it should be read together with 74–76: heterogeneity here excludes homogeneous units, not organization.

(78–79) We do count successive moments of duration, and through its relation to number time first appears as a measurable magnitude like space. Bergson distinguishes cases. When I say a minute has passed, I mean a seconds pendulum has made sixty oscillations. If I represent the sixty all at once in a single apperception, I exclude succession by hypothesis and think of sixty points on a fixed line, each symbolizing an oscillation. If I represent them successively without changing how they occur in space, I must think each oscillation while excluding the memory of the last, since space has kept no trace of it, and I am condemned to remain in the present and give up thinking succession or duration. If I keep the memory of the previous oscillation joined to the present one, either I juxtapose the two images, which returns to the first case, or I perceive them in each other, interpenetrating and organizing themselves like the notes of a melody, forming what Bergson now names an indistinct or qualitative multiplicity, unlike number. That gives the image of pure duration, freed entirely from the idea of a homogeneous medium or a measurable quantity. Consciousness proceeds this way whenever it refrains from symbolizing duration. When the regular swing of a pendulum makes us drowsy, neither the last sound alone nor the memory of earlier sounds set beside it produces the effect; the sounds must have combined and acted through the quality their quantity presented, their rhythmic organization. The effect of a weak, continuous stimulus is explained in the same way: if the sensation stayed the same, it would stay indefinitely weak and bearable, but each increment organizes itself with the preceding ones, and the whole resembles a musical phrase always about to end and constantly altered as a whole by each new note. We call it the same sensation because we think of its objective cause in space, and so unfold the sensation in space too, seeing one sensation stretched out and juxtaposed to itself where there is really a developing organism of mutually penetrating changes. True duration should then be ranked among the so-called intensive magnitudes, if intensities could be called magnitudes; it is not a quantity, and any attempt to measure it unconsciously substitutes space for it. The threefold analysis of the pendulum is exhaustive: simultaneous representation, memoryless succession, or memory either juxtaposed or organized. Only the last yields duration. The link back to Chapter I is explicit: duration has the same status as intensity, a quality that measurement can only replace.

(79–80) It is extraordinarily hard to represent duration in its original purity, Bergson concedes, because we do not endure alone. External things seem to endure as we do, and time, seen from their side, looks like a homogeneous medium. Its moments seem external to one another like bodies in space, perceived motion is a palpable sign of homogeneous, measurable duration, and time enters the formulae of mechanics, astronomy and physics as a quantity; measuring the speed of a motion implies that time too is a magnitude. The analysis must be completed, for if duration proper is not measured, what do the oscillations of the pendulum measure? One might accept that inner duration, as consciousness perceives it, coincides with the nesting of conscious facts in one another and the gradual enrichment of the self, while insisting that the astronomer's time, divided into equal parts by our clocks, is something else, a measurable and therefore homogeneous magnitude. Bergson announces that careful examination will dissolve this last illusion. The objection is the strongest available, since it grants the inner thesis and relocates homogeneous time in the world. His reply will not be that the world endures differently but, more radically for this book, that the world does not endure at all.

(80–82) When I follow the clock's hand, which corresponds to the pendulum's swings, I do not measure duration; I count simultaneities, which is quite different. Outside me, in space, there is only ever one position of hand and pendulum, since nothing remains of past positions. Within me, a process of organization or mutual penetration of conscious facts goes on, and that is true duration. Because I endure in this way, I represent to myself what I call the past oscillations of the pendulum while perceiving the present one. Suppress the self that thinks the so-called successive oscillations and there will only ever be one oscillation, one position, hence no duration. Suppress the pendulum and only the heterogeneous duration of the self remains, without moments external to one another and without relation to number. In the self there is succession without mutual externality; outside the self, mutual externality without succession: externality, since the present oscillation is wholly distinct from the past one that no longer exists, but no succession, since succession exists only for a conscious spectator who recalls the past and juxtaposes the two oscillations or their symbols in an auxiliary space. Between this succession without externality and this externality without succession an exchange occurs, which Bergson compares to what physicists call endosmosis. Each phase of conscious life corresponds to a simultaneous oscillation, and since the oscillations are distinct, we acquire the habit of making the same distinction between moments of conscious life; the pendulum, as it were, cuts it into parts external to one another, hence the erroneous idea of a homogeneous inner duration whose identical moments follow one another without interpenetrating. Conversely, the oscillations, distinct only because one has vanished when the next appears, profit from their influence on consciousness: thanks to the memory consciousness has organized of their whole, they are preserved and then lined up, and we create for them a fourth dimension of space, which we call homogeneous time, allowing a motion that occurs in place to be juxtaposed to itself indefinitely. Sorting out the real from the imaginary in this complex process, we find a real space, without duration, in which phenomena appear and disappear simultaneously with our conscious states; and a real duration, whose heterogeneous moments interpenetrate but each of which can be brought into relation with a contemporaneous state of the external world and separated from the other moments by that very relation. From comparing these two realities arises a symbolic representation of duration drawn from space, in which duration takes the illusory form of a homogeneous medium, and the link between space and duration is simultaneity, which might be defined as the intersection of time with space. In this, the book's central metaphysical thesis, Bergson does not say the world endures in a different way; he says that outside consciousness there is only the present state, with no succession, so that homogeneous time is a hybrid produced by a two-way exchange. *Matière et mémoire* and *L'Évolution créatrice* will extend duration to matter and life, and *Durée et simultanéité* (1922) will return to simultaneity as the junction of the two orders.

(82–86) Motion, the living symbol of an apparently homogeneous duration, calls for the same dissociation. We say motion occurs in space and call it homogeneous and divisible, thinking of the space traversed as if it were the motion itself. On reflection, the successive positions of the moving body occupy space, but the operation by which it passes from one to another, which occupies duration and is real only for a conscious spectator, escapes space. We are dealing not with a thing but with a progress: motion, as passage from one point to another, is a mental synthesis, a psychic and therefore unextended process. In space there are only parts of space, and wherever we take the moving body we obtain only a position. If consciousness perceives more than positions, it is because it recalls the successive positions and synthesizes them. This synthesis cannot be a new unfolding of the positions in a homogeneous medium, since a further synthesis would then be needed to link them, and so on without end. It must be a qualitative synthesis, a gradual organization of successive sensations, a unity like that of a melodic phrase. That is our idea of motion when we think of it alone and extract mobility from it, as with a shooting star, where the dissociation occurs by itself between the space traversed, seen as a line of fire, and the absolutely indivisible sensation of motion, or as with a quick gesture made with closed eyes, which is felt as a purely qualitative sensation until we think of the space it covers. Motion therefore has two elements: the space traversed and the act of traversing it, the successive positions and their synthesis. The first is a homogeneous quantity; the second exists only in consciousness and is a quality or an intensity. Here too an endosmosis occurs between the intensive sensation of mobility and the extensive representation of space traversed. We give motion the divisibility of its space, forgetting that a thing can be divided but not an act; and we project the act into space, lay it along the line, solidify it, as though localizing a progress in space did not amount to claiming that, even outside consciousness, the past coexists with the present. From this confusion come the sophisms of the Eleatic school. The interval between two points is infinitely divisible, and if motion were composed of parts like the interval it would never be crossed. But each of Achilles' steps is a simple, indivisible act, and after a given number of such acts he passes the tortoise. Zeno identifies this series of indivisible acts with the homogeneous space that underlies them, and since space can be divided and recomposed by any law, he feels entitled to rebuild Achilles' motion from tortoise-steps; he substitutes for Achilles chasing the tortoise two tortoises regulated on each other, condemned to take the same kind of steps simultaneously and never meet. Achilles passes the tortoise because their steps are indivisible as motions and different magnitudes as space, so that the sum soon gives Achilles a distance greater than the tortoise's plus its lead. Bergson rejects, against "the fine and profound analysis of a contemporary thinker" (the dossier identifies Évellin, *Infini et quantité*, 1881), the need for a metaphysical hypothesis about a limit to the divisibility of concrete space, or a gap between real and imagined motion. Concrete space can stay infinitely divisible, provided one distinguishes the simultaneous positions of the two bodies, which are in space, from their motions, which occupy no space, being duration rather than extension, quality and not quantity. Measuring a velocity is only noting a simultaneity, and using velocity in calculations is a convenient way of predicting one. Mathematics stays within its role when it fixes the simultaneous positions of Achilles and the tortoise, or posits their meeting at a point X, itself a simultaneity; it exceeds that role when it claims to reconstitute what happens between two simultaneities, and even then it can only consider more simultaneities, whose indefinitely growing number should warn it that motion is not made from immobilities nor time from space. Just as the only homogeneous element in duration is what does not endure, namely space, where simultaneities line up, so the homogeneous element of motion is what belongs to it least, the space traversed, that is, immobility. The solution to Zeno is the thesis of 80–82 applied to motion: indivisible acts measured by divisible trajectories. In the *Essai* the act of motion is located in consciousness as a "mental synthesis," whereas *Matière et mémoire* (ch. IV) and *L'Évolution créatrice* (ch. IV) will treat real motion as indivisible in itself, not only for a spectator.

(86) For that very reason science handles time and motion only after first removing their essential, qualitative element: duration from time and mobility from motion. A look at the role of time, motion and velocity in astronomy and mechanics will confirm this. The next three paragraphs test the claim against the definitions science actually uses. The point is not that science errs but that its success depends on this elimination, which is why its formulae cannot be evidence about duration.

(86–87) Treatises on mechanics are careful to say they do not define duration itself, only the equality of two durations: two intervals of time are equal when two identical bodies, in identical circumstances at the start of each and subject to the same influences, have traversed the same space at the end. In other words, we note the instant the motion starts, a simultaneity between an external change and one of our states; we note the moment it ends, another simultaneity; and we measure the space traversed, the only thing that can be measured. Duration does not enter, only space and simultaneities. To announce that a phenomenon will occur after a time t is to say that consciousness will note, between now and then, t simultaneities of a certain kind; and "between now and then" must not mislead us, because the interval of duration exists only for us, through the interpenetration of our states. Outside us there are only space and simultaneities, of which one cannot even say they are objectively successive, since succession is thought by comparing present and past. That the interval itself does not count for science is shown by the fact that if all motions in the universe ran two or three times faster, nothing would change in our formulae or the numbers we put into them; consciousness would have an indefinable, qualitative impression of the change, but nothing would show outside it, since the same number of simultaneities would occur. Bergson anticipates the astronomer predicting an eclipse, who shrinks the intervals of duration indefinitely and so sees in a few seconds a succession of simultaneities that concrete consciousness, obliged to live the intervals, would take centuries to traverse. The thought experiment of the accelerated universe is the clearest statement of what physical time omits. *L'Évolution créatrice* (ch. I) will reuse it, with the sugar dissolving in water, to argue that duration is real precisely because the waiting cannot be compressed.

(87–89) The analysis of velocity yields the same conclusion. Mechanics first constructs uniform motion by representing a trajectory AB and a physical phenomenon repeated under identical conditions, say a stone always falling from the same height to the same place. If the points M, N, P, ... reached by the body each time the stone lands mark off equal intervals AM, MN, NP, ..., the motion is uniform, and any one interval is its velocity, the chosen phenomenon serving as the unit of duration. Uniform velocity is thus defined using only space and simultaneity. For varied motion, where the intervals are unequal, one imagines an indefinite number of bodies A₁, A₂, A₃, ... in uniform motion at all possible velocities v₁, v₂, v₃, .... Take two points M′ and M″ on A's trajectory on either side of M and very close to it; as A reaches M′, M and M″, the other bodies reach corresponding points on their own trajectories, and there must be two bodies whose displacements match A's on either side of M, so that A's velocity at M lies between their velocities. Bringing M′ and M″ closer replaces these bounds by tighter ones, and since the intervals can shrink toward zero, there is a velocity, the common limit, from which both series of bounds differ by less than any given quantity; that limit is called A's velocity at M. In this analysis, as in the uniform case, only spaces already traversed and simultaneous positions already reached appear. Hence the earlier claim: if mechanics keeps only simultaneity from time, it keeps only immobility from motion. Bergson reconstructs the limit definition faithfully because his argument depends on its being correct; he is not attacking the calculus but showing that even the instantaneous velocity is built from positions.

(89) The result could have been foreseen, since mechanics works with equations and an algebraic equation always expresses an accomplished fact. Duration and motion, as consciousness experiences them, are essentially in the course of formation. Algebra can translate the results reached at a given moment and the positions occupied by a body, but not duration and motion themselves. Multiplying the simultaneities and positions considered by assuming very small intervals, or even replacing difference by differential to mark that the number can grow without limit, does not help: mathematics always places itself at one end of the interval, however small, and the interval itself, the duration and the motion, stays outside the equation. Duration and motion are mental syntheses and not things; the body occupies the points of a line successively, but motion has nothing in common with the line; and although the body's positions vary with the moments of duration, and even create distinct moments by being different positions, duration proper has no identical or mutually external moments, being heterogeneous to itself, indistinct and unlike number. The opposition between the accomplished and the in-the-making (*le tout fait* and *le se faisant*, as the later works will say) here receives its first formulation. It also recalls the critique of Fechner's passage from difference to differential (46): in both cases the infinitesimal multiplies end-points without capturing the transition.

(89–90) The analysis yields first that space alone is homogeneous, that things in space form a distinct multiplicity, and that every distinct multiplicity is obtained by unfolding in space. It also yields that in space there is neither duration nor even succession, in the sense consciousness gives these words: each so-called successive state of the external world exists alone, and their multiplicity is real only for a consciousness that first preserves them and then juxtaposes them, externalizing them with respect to one another. It preserves them because these states give rise to conscious facts that interpenetrate, organize themselves imperceptibly, and bind the past to the present through that very solidarity. It externalizes them because, thinking of their radical distinctness (one has ceased when the other appears), it perceives them as a distinct multiplicity, which amounts to lining them up in the space where each existed separately. The space used for this purpose is what we call homogeneous time. The summary makes clear the double role of consciousness: memory makes the series possible, and the thought of mutual exclusion, projected into space, makes it homogeneous. Neither space nor duration alone produces homogeneous time.

(90–92) A further conclusion follows: the multiplicity of conscious states, taken in its original purity, bears no resemblance to the distinct multiplicity that forms a number. It is a qualitative multiplicity. We must admit two kinds of multiplicity, two senses of the word "distinguish," two conceptions, one qualitative and one quantitative, of the difference between the same and the other. Sometimes this multiplicity, distinctness and heterogeneity contains number only potentially, as Aristotle would say: consciousness makes a qualitative discrimination with no thought of counting the qualities or even making them several, and there is multiplicity without quantity. Sometimes the terms are counted or countable, and we then think of the possibility of externalizing them and unfold them in space. We are so used to clarifying each sense by the other, even seeing one in the other, that we find it extraordinarily hard to distinguish them, or at least to say the distinction. Bergson catches his own language in the act: to say that "several" conscious states organize themselves and interpenetrate, he has already isolated, externalized and juxtaposed them, and so betrayed the deep habit of unfolding time in space. The terms for a soul that has not made the projection are borrowed from the image of the projection made, and so carry an original flaw; the representation of a multiplicity unrelated to number or space, though clear to thought that turns inward and abstracts, cannot be translated into the language of common sense. Yet we cannot even form the idea of distinct multiplicity without considering a qualitative multiplicity alongside it. When we count units aligned in space, a dynamic organization of those units goes on in the depths of the soul, like the purely qualitative representation a sentient anvil might have of a growing number of hammer blows. In this sense the numbers of daily use each have an emotional equivalent, as merchants know when they price an article just below a round number and make up the difference in centimes. Counting therefore has two aspects: we suppose the units identical, which is possible only if they line up in a homogeneous medium, but the third unit, added to the other two, changes the nature, aspect and rhythm of the whole, and without this interpenetration and qualitative progress no addition would be possible. It is thanks to the quality of quantity that we form the idea of a quantity without quality. The final formula reverses the expected priority: number does not stand beside qualitative multiplicity as an independent achievement but depends on it, since the synthesis that makes a sum is itself a duration. Bergson's admission that his own vocabulary is contaminated is also a methodological principle, which governs how the qualitative multiplicity can be described at all: by images and corrections rather than by definitions.

(92–96) Outside symbolic representation, then, time never takes the form of a homogeneous medium for consciousness. We arrive at that representation naturally because, in a series of identical terms, each term has a double aspect for us: one always the same, since we think of the identity of the external object, and one specific, since adding the term reorganizes the whole. Hence the possibility of unfolding in space, as numerical multiplicity, what was a qualitative multiplicity, and of treating the one as equivalent to the other. Nowhere does this double process occur as easily as in the perception of the external phenomenon, unknowable in itself, that takes for us the form of motion: the terms are identical, since it is the same body, yet the synthesis consciousness performs between the present position and what memory calls the earlier ones makes the images interpenetrate and continue one another. Duration takes the form of a homogeneous medium chiefly through motion, though any repetition of a well-defined external phenomenon would have suggested it: the sounds of a series of hammer blows form, as pure sensations, an indivisible melody and a dynamic progress, but knowing that one objective cause is acting, we cut the progress into phases we treat as identical, and this multiplicity of identical terms can only be conceived by unfolding in space. Our self touches the external world at its surface; our successive sensations, though fusing, keep something of the mutual externality of their causes, so our superficial psychological life unfolds in a homogeneous medium without much effort. The symbolic character of the representation becomes more striking the deeper we go. The inner self, the one that feels and is passionate, that deliberates and decides, is a force whose states and modifications interpenetrate intimately and are profoundly altered once separated and unfolded in space. Since this deeper self and the superficial self form one person, they seem to endure in the same way; and since the constant representation of an identical, repeated objective phenomenon cuts our superficial life into mutually external parts, the moments so defined mark off distinct segments in the dynamic, undivided progress of our more personal states. The externality that juxtaposition in homogeneous space guarantees to material objects thus spreads into the depths of consciousness: little by little our sensations detach from one another like their external causes, and feelings and ideas like the sensations contemporary with them. The proof that our ordinary conception of duration results from a gradual invasion of pure consciousness by space is that removing the superficial layer of psychic facts the self uses as regulators suffices to deprive it of homogeneous time. Dreaming does this: by slowing organic functions, sleep alters chiefly the surface of communication between the self and external things. We no longer measure duration but feel it; it returns from quantity to quality, and mathematical estimation gives way to a confused instinct, capable like every instinct of gross errors and sometimes of extraordinary accuracy. Waking experience should teach the same distinction between duration as quality, which consciousness reaches immediately and animals probably perceive, and time materialized, become quantity by unfolding in space. As Bergson writes, a nearby clock strikes; his distracted ear notices only after several strokes, so he did not count them, yet a retrospective effort of attention lets him add the four already struck to those he now hears. On careful reflection he finds that the first four sounds had reached his ear and moved his consciousness, but their sensations had fused into a whole with its own character, a kind of musical phrase. To estimate the number, he reconstituted the phrase in thought: imagination struck one blow, then two, then three, and until it reached four, sensibility, consulted, answered that the total effect differed in quality. It had registered the succession of four strokes in its own way, quite otherwise than by addition and without any image of juxtaposed terms. The number of strokes was perceived as a quality, not a quantity. Bergson then concludes the core of the chapter: beneath homogeneous duration, the extended symbol of true duration, attentive psychology discerns a duration whose heterogeneous moments interpenetrate; beneath the numerical multiplicity of conscious states, a qualitative multiplicity; beneath the self with well-defined states, a self in which succession implies fusion and organization. We usually settle for the first, the shadow of the self projected into homogeneous space. Consciousness, tormented by an insatiable desire to distinguish, puts the symbol in place of reality or sees reality only through the symbol, and since the refracted, subdivided self suits the demands of social life and of language far better, it prefers that self and gradually loses sight of the fundamental one. The clock example gives qualitative multiplicity an operational test: a determinate number is registered without being counted, and recovered by matching qualities rather than adding units. It also blocks the reading of duration as an undifferentiated blur, since four and three are told apart. The two selves are introduced here as two levels of one person, distinguished by depth, not as two subjects.

(96) Recovering the fundamental self as an unaltered consciousness would perceive it requires a vigorous effort of analysis, which separates the inner, living psychic facts from their image, first refracted and then solidified in homogeneous space. Our perceptions, sensations, emotions and ideas each have a double aspect: one sharp and precise but impersonal; the other confused, infinitely mobile and inexpressible, because language cannot grasp it without fixing its mobility, nor fit it to its commonplace form without bringing it into the common domain. If there are two forms of multiplicity and of duration, each conscious fact taken singly must look different depending on whether it is considered within a distinct or a confused multiplicity, in the time-quality where it occurs or the time-quantity where it is projected. Bergson now applies the distinction, established for the series, to its single members, perception, sensation, feeling and idea in turn. The images of refraction and solidification name two successive stages of the falsification, the first by space, the second by language.

(96–97) Walking for the first time through a town where I will stay, the things around me make on me both an impression meant to last and one that will keep changing. Every day I see the same houses, and since I know they are the same objects I always call them by the same name and imagine they always appear to me the same way. Yet if after a long time I go back to the impression of the first years, I am surprised by a singular, inexplicable and above all inexpressible change in it. It seems that these objects, constantly perceived and constantly pictured in my mind, have borrowed something of my conscious existence: like me they have lived and aged. Bergson insists this is not pure illusion, for if today's impression were identical to yesterday's, there would be no difference between perceiving and recognizing, between learning and remembering. Most people miss the difference unless warned and led to examine themselves. The reason is that our outer, social life has more practical importance than our inner, individual existence, and we instinctively solidify our impressions to express them in language. We then confuse the feeling, which is in perpetual becoming, with its permanent external object, and above all with the word that names the object. Just as the fleeting duration of the self is fixed by its projection into homogeneous space, so our changing impressions, wrapping themselves around the external object that causes them, take on its precise outlines and its immobility. The very possibility of recognizing presupposes that perception changes by accumulating its past, which anticipates the analysis of recognition in *Matière et mémoire* (ch. II).

(97–98) Simple sensations, taken in their natural state, would have even less consistency. A taste or a scent I liked as a child now repels me, yet I still give the experience the same name and speak as if the scent and taste had stayed the same and only my tastes had changed. I solidify the sensation again, and when its mobility becomes too evident to ignore, I extract that mobility, give it a separate name and solidify it in turn as a "taste." In reality there are neither identical sensations nor multiple tastes, for both appear as things once I isolate and name them, and there is hardly anything in the human soul but progressions. Every sensation changes by being repeated, and it seems unchanged from day to day only because I perceive it through its cause and through the word that translates it. The influence of language on sensation goes deeper than is usually thought: it not only makes us believe our sensations are invariable but can mislead us about the character of the sensation felt. When I eat a dish reputed exquisite, its name, heavy with general approval, comes between my sensation and my consciousness, and I may believe I like the taste when a slight effort of attention would show the contrary. The sharply bounded word, the brute word, which stores what is stable, common and therefore impersonal in humanity's impressions, crushes or at least covers the delicate and fleeting impressions of individual consciousness. To fight on equal terms these would need precise words of their own, but such words, once formed, would turn against the sensation that gave rise to them and, invented to attest its instability, would impose their own stability on it. The splitting of change into a constant sensation and a changing "taste" is a precise instance of the general mechanism: wherever mobility is noticed, it is isolated as a new fixed term instead of being restored to the term it belongs to. The closing aporia rules out a reformed vocabulary as a solution, which is why the next paragraph turns to the novelist.

(98–100) Nowhere is this crushing of immediate consciousness so striking as in feeling. A violent love or a deep melancholy invades the soul as a thousand different elements that fuse and interpenetrate, without sharp outlines and without any tendency to externalize themselves; their originality depends on it. They are already deformed when we pick out a numerical multiplicity in their confused mass, and more so when we unfold them, isolated, in a homogeneous medium that may now be called time or space as one likes. Each element took an indefinable colouring from its setting; isolated, it is discoloured and ready to be named. A feeling is a living thing that develops and so changes constantly; otherwise it could not lead us gradually toward a resolution, which would be taken at once. It lives because the duration in which it develops has interpenetrating moments; separating them and unrolling time in space strips it of its life and colour. We are then faced with our own shadow: we believe we have analysed the feeling, but we have replaced it with a juxtaposition of inert states translatable into words, each the common and therefore impersonal residue of what society as a whole feels in such a case. That is why we reason about these states and apply our simple logic to them; by isolating them we have turned them into genera ready for deduction. If a bold novelist tears the cleverly woven canvas of our conventional self and shows beneath its apparent logic a fundamental absurdity, beneath its juxtaposed simple states an infinite interpenetration of impressions that have already ceased when named, we praise him for knowing us better than we knew ourselves. Yet he too unrolls our feeling in homogeneous time and expresses its elements in words, so he offers only a shadow; but he has arranged the shadow so as to make us suspect the extraordinary, illogical nature of what casts it, and has invited reflection by putting into the outward expression something of the contradiction and mutual penetration that constitute the elements expressed. Encouraged by him, we lift for a moment the veil we interpose between our consciousness and ourselves, and he brings us back into our own presence. The feeling that "leads us to a resolution" anticipates Chapter III's account of deliberation, where the act matures in the feeling instead of being decided by weighed motives. The novelist's role is carefully limited: art cannot give the immediate, only a symbol so arranged as to point beyond itself, and the image of the veil returns in *Le rire* as the function of art in general.

(100–102) A similar surprise awaits us if we break the frames of language and try to grasp our ideas themselves in their natural state, as a consciousness freed from the obsession with space would perceive them. Dissociating the elements of an idea, which leads to abstraction, is too convenient to give up in ordinary life or even philosophical discussion. But when we imagine that the dissociated elements are the very ones that formed the concrete idea, substituting for the interpenetration of real terms the juxtaposition of their symbols and claiming to rebuild duration out of space, we fall into the errors of associationism, which Chapter III will examine. The unreflective ardour with which we take sides on some questions shows that our intelligence has its instincts, and these can only be represented as a common impulse running through all our ideas, that is, as their mutual penetration. The opinions we hold most dearly are those we can least easily justify, and the reasons we give for them are rarely those that made us adopt them. In a sense we adopted them without reason, because what makes them precious is that their shade matches the common colouring of all our other ideas, that we saw in them from the start something of ourselves. In our minds they do not have the commonplace form they will take once expressed in words, and although they bear the same name in other minds, they are not at all the same thing. Each lives like a cell in an organism, modified by whatever modifies the general state of the self; but whereas a cell occupies a definite point of the organism, an idea that is truly ours fills our whole self. Not all ideas are incorporated in this way. Many float on the surface like dead leaves on a pond: the mind always finds them motionless when it thinks them, as if they were external to it. Such are ideas received ready-made that never become part of us, and ideas we have neglected and that have withered. As we move away from the deep layers of the self, conscious states increasingly take the form of numerical multiplicity and unfold in homogeneous space, precisely because they become more inert and impersonal. It is no surprise, then, that only the ideas least our own are adequately expressible in words, and that only to them does the associationist theory apply: external to one another, they stand in classifiable relations in which their inner nature plays no part, and are said to be associated by contiguity or by some logical relation. But below the surface where the self touches things, in the depths of organized and living intelligence, we witness the superposition or rather the intimate fusion of many ideas that, once dissociated, seem to exclude each other as logical contradictories. The strangest dreams, in which two images overlap and present two different persons who are yet one, give a faint idea of how our concepts interpenetrate in waking life; the dreamer's imagination, cut off from the external world, parodies on mere images the work that goes on constantly on ideas in the deeper regions of intellectual life. Bergson's verdict on associationism is not that it is false but that it is true of the surface: it correctly describes the ideas that are least ours. The same restriction of a theory to its proper layer will govern his treatment of determinism in Chapter III. The fusion of logically contradictory ideas in the depths is also the strongest form of the thesis that qualitative multiplicity escapes the principle that governs distinct terms.

(102–104) The principle stated at the outset is thus confirmed and will be clarified by closer study of inner facts: conscious life has a double aspect, depending on whether it is perceived directly or by refraction through space. Taken in themselves, deep conscious states have no relation to quantity; they are pure quality, mingling in such a way that one cannot say whether they are one or several, nor even examine them from that standpoint without distorting them. The duration they create has moments that do not form a numerical multiplicity, and even to say that the moments overlap would still distinguish them. Bergson asks whether, if each of us lived a purely individual life without society or language, consciousness would grasp the series of inner states in this indistinct form. Not entirely, he answers, since we would keep the idea of a homogeneous space where objects are sharply distinct, and it is too convenient to line up in that medium the nebulous states that first meet consciousness in order to resolve them into simpler terms. But the intuition of homogeneous space is already a step toward social life. Animals probably do not represent, beyond their sensations, an external world clearly distinct from them and common to all conscious beings, and the tendency by which we picture the externality of things and the homogeneity of their medium is the same one that leads us to live together and speak. As the conditions of social life are more fully realized, the current carrying our states from inside to outside grows stronger; little by little those states become objects or things, detached not only from one another but from us, and we perceive them only in the homogeneous medium where we fixed their image and through the word that lends them its commonplace colour. A second self forms that covers the first, a self whose existence has distinct moments and whose states separate and are easily expressed in words. Bergson anticipates the charge that he has split the person and reintroduced the numerical multiplicity he excluded: it is the same self that perceives distinct states and that, attending more closely, sees them melt together like snow crystals under the prolonged touch of a hand. For the convenience of language this self has every interest in not restoring confusion where order reigns, and in not disturbing the ingenious arrangement of quasi-impersonal states by which it has ceased to be "an empire within an empire," Spinoza's phrase for the illusion of the mind's separateness from nature. An inner life of clearly distinct moments and sharply characterized states suits the demands of social life, and a superficial psychology can describe it without error, provided it confines itself to facts already produced and neglects how they came about. But if, passing from statics to dynamics, it claims to reason about facts in the making as it did about facts accomplished, and presents the concrete, living self as an association of distinct terms juxtaposed in a homogeneous medium, it will meet insurmountable difficulties, which multiply the harder it tries to solve them, because every effort only exposes the absurdity of the basic hypothesis that unrolled time in space and placed succession within simultaneity. The contradictions of causality, freedom and, in a word, personality have no other origin, and to set them aside it is enough to substitute the real, concrete self for its symbolic representation. The two selves are not two entities; the difference is one of attention and depth within one person. The superficial self is legitimate within its domain, the statics of the accomplished, and becomes an error only when it claims the dynamics of the self in the making. And the social genesis of homogeneous space, here tied to language and common life, will become in *Les deux sources* the social obligation that shapes the surface of the self. The final sentence announces Chapter III: freedom will be approached not as a property to be proved but as what appears when the symbol is removed.

## Chapter Three: On the Organization of Conscious States. Freedom

(105–107) The question of freedom sets two opposed systems of nature against each other, mechanism and dynamism. Dynamism starts from the idea of voluntary activity, which consciousness supplies, and reaches the idea of inertia by gradually emptying it; it therefore has no trouble conceiving a free force on one side and law-governed matter on the other. Mechanism goes the other way: it supposes the materials it combines to be governed by necessary laws, and however rich, unpredictable and apparently contingent its combinations become, it never leaves the circle of necessity it began in. The two conceptions imply different views of the relation between law and fact. The dynamist, looking higher, finds facts that escape laws more and more, so makes the fact the absolute reality and the law its more or less symbolic expression; the mechanist finds within the particular fact a set of laws of which it is the intersection, so makes the law the fundamental reality. The deeper reason is that they mean different things by "simple." For mechanism, a principle is simple if its effects can be foreseen and calculated, so inertia is simpler than freedom, the homogeneous than the heterogeneous, the abstract than the concrete. Dynamism seeks the real genealogy of notions, and often finds that the notion the mechanist takes as primitive was obtained by fusing several richer notions that neutralized one another, as darkness arises from the interference of two lights. On this view spontaneity is simpler than inertia, since inertia can be understood and defined only through spontaneity, which suffices by itself: each of us has an immediate feeling, real or illusory, of free spontaneity in which inertia plays no part, whereas inertia is defined as the inability of matter to set itself in motion or stop itself, which refers back to activity. A priori, then, two opposed conceptions of human action follow from how one understands concrete and abstract, simple and complex, fact and law. Bergson sides with the dynamist's genealogy without adopting dynamism as a theory: the order of definition, in which inertia presupposes activity, is the analogue in natural philosophy of Chapter I's order, in which quantity is read into quality. The image of interference, two lights producing darkness, gives a precise sense to the claim that the abstract is not the simple but the product of a cancellation.

(107) A posteriori, however, specific facts are cited against freedom, some physical and some psychological: our actions are said to be necessitated by our feelings, ideas and the whole prior series of our conscious states, or freedom is declared incompatible with the basic properties of matter, especially the conservation of energy. There are thus two kinds of determinism, two seemingly different empirical proofs of universal necessity. Bergson announces his plan: he will show that physical determinism reduces to psychological determinism and rests on a psychological hypothesis; then that psychological determinism, and the usual refutations of it, rest on a mistaken conception of the multiplicity of conscious states and above all of duration. In the light of Chapter II, a self will appear whose activity cannot be compared to that of any other force. The structure of the chapter follows this plan. The decisive word in the announcement is "refutations": the libertarians will be criticized on the same grounds as the determinists.

(107–108) Physical determinism in its latest form is tied to mechanical, or rather kinetic, theories of matter. The universe is pictured as a mass of matter that imagination resolves into molecules and atoms in ceaseless vibration and translation, and the physical, chemical and sensible properties of things, heat, sound, electricity and perhaps attraction, reduce objectively to these elementary motions. Organized matter obeys the same laws, so the nervous system contains only molecules and atoms that move, attract and repel one another. If all bodies act and react in their elementary parts, the molecular state of the brain at any moment will be modified by the impacts the nervous system receives from its surroundings, and our successive sensations, feelings and ideas can be defined as mechanical resultants of those impacts combined with the prior motions of nervous atoms. Conversely, molecular motions in the nervous system, combining with one another, will often produce a reaction of the organism on the world: reflex movements, and also the actions called free and voluntary. Since the conservation of energy is assumed inflexible, no atom in the nervous system or the universe has a position not fixed by the sum of mechanical actions the other atoms exert on it. A mathematician who knew the positions of the molecules of a human organism at a given moment, and those of all atoms in the universe that could affect it, would calculate with infallible precision the past, present and future actions of the person, as one predicts an astronomical event. Bergson states the position at its strongest, in essentially Laplacean form, before examining it.

(108–110) Bergson grants that this conception of physiological and nervous phenomena follows naturally from the conservation of energy. The atomic theory remains a hypothesis, and purely kinetic explanations lose more than they gain by tying themselves to it: Hirn's experiments on the flow of gases suggest that heat is more than molecular motion; hypotheses about the luminiferous ether, which Comte already treated with disdain, fit badly with the regularity of planetary motion and the dispersion of light; the elasticity of atoms raises insurmountable difficulties even after William Thomson's brilliant hypotheses; and nothing is more problematic than the atom itself, which, judging by the growing list of properties it has needed, Bergson is inclined to regard not as a real thing but as the materialized residue of mechanical explanations. The necessary determination of physiological facts by their antecedents, however, follows independently of any hypothesis about the ultimate elements, simply by extending the conservation theorem to all living bodies. To admit its universality is to suppose that the material points composing the universe are subject only to attractive and repulsive forces emanating from those points and depending only on distances, so that their relative positions at any moment are strictly determined by those at the preceding moment. Bergson adopts this hypothesis provisionally in order to show, first, that it does not entail the absolute determination of conscious states by one another, and second, that the universality of conservation can be admitted only on the strength of some psychological hypothesis. The critique of the atom as a "materialized residue" of explanation applies the Foreword's diagnosis to physics; but Bergson does not rest his case on it, since the determinist argument needs only the conservation principle.

(110–111) Even if every atom of the brain had its position, direction and speed determined at every moment, it would not follow that psychological life is subject to the same necessity. One would first have to prove that a given brain state corresponds to a strictly determined psychological state, and that proof has not been given. It is rarely demanded because a determinate vibration of the eardrum and of the auditory nerve gives a determinate note, and the parallelism of the physical and psychological series has been established in many cases. But nobody has claimed that we are free, in given conditions, to hear whatever note or see whatever colour we like. Such sensations, like many psychic states, are clearly tied to determining conditions, which is why a system of motions governed by abstract mechanics could be imagined or found beneath them. Wherever a mechanical explanation succeeds, there is a nearly strict parallelism, and that is unsurprising, since such explanations will be found only where the two series have parallel elements. To extend the parallelism to the series as wholes is to settle the problem of freedom a priori. That is permissible, and the greatest thinkers did it, but not for physical reasons: Leibniz ascribed the correspondence to pre-established harmony without allowing that motion could ever produce perception as a cause its effect; Spinoza held that modes of thought and of extension correspond without influencing each other, expressing the same eternal truth in two languages. Contemporary physical determinism lacks their clarity and geometric rigour. It pictures molecular motions in the brain from which consciousness somehow emerges and lights up their trace like a phosphorescence, or an invisible musician playing backstage while the actor strikes a silent keyboard. Whatever the image, it is not and never will be shown that the psychological fact is necessarily determined by molecular motion, because a motion can explain another motion but not a conscious state; only experience can show that the one accompanies the other, and that constant connection has been verified in very few cases, all concerning facts nearly independent of the will. Bergson has located the illegitimate step precisely: from parallelism where it is observed to parallelism of the whole, which is a metaphysical thesis and not a result of physics. The phosphorescence image anticipates the epiphenomenalism attacked at 113–116, and the argument as a whole is the germ of "Le paralogisme psycho-physiologique" (1904) and of *Matière et mémoire*.

(111–112) Why, then, does physical determinism extend the connection to all cases? Consciousness tells us that most of our actions are explained by motives. Determination here does not seem to mean necessity, since common sense believes in free will; but the determinist, misled by a conception of duration and causality to be criticized later, takes the determination of conscious facts by one another as absolute. Hence associationist determinism, which appeals to the testimony of consciousness but cannot yet claim scientific rigour. It is natural that this approximate determinism, a determinism of quality, should seek support in the mechanism that underlies natural phenomena, which would lend it geometric precision while itself becoming universal. A happy circumstance favours the union: the simplest psychological facts rest of themselves on well-defined physical phenomena, most sensations being linked to certain molecular motions. This beginning of experimental proof suffices for someone who, for psychological reasons, has already accepted that our states are necessarily determined by their circumstances; he then treats the play on the stage of consciousness as a literal and servile translation of scenes performed by the molecules of organized matter. Physical determinism is thus psychological determinism seeking to verify itself and fix its outlines by appeal to the natural sciences. The genealogy reverses the apparent order of justification: the physics is invoked to confirm a conviction about motives that was formed first.

(112–113) Bergson concedes that a strict application of the conservation of energy leaves little room for freedom. Even if the law does not necessarily govern the course of our ideas, it will govern our movements; inner life would still depend on us to some extent, but to an outside observer nothing would distinguish our activity from complete automatism. So it must be asked whether extending the principle to all bodies itself implies a psychological theory, and whether a scientist with no prior bias against freedom would think of making it a universal law. The concession is important: a freedom confined to inner life with no effect on movement would be worthless, so Bergson cannot leave the physical argument at the level of parallelism. He must also contest the scope of the conservation principle.

(113–116) The conservation of energy, Bergson argues, should not be given an exaggerated place in the history of science. In its present form it marks a phase in the development of certain sciences; it did not preside over that development, and it is wrong to make it an indispensable postulate of all research. Any mathematical operation on a given quantity implies that the quantity persists through the operation however it is decomposed: what is given is given, and summing the same terms in any order gives the same result. Science will always be subject to this law, which is only the law of non-contradiction; but it implies no hypothesis about what is given or what remains constant. It tells us that something cannot come from nothing, but only experience can tell which aspects of reality must count for something scientifically and which for nothing. To predict the state of a system, something must be conserved in constant quantity through its combinations; experience must decide what that is, and whether it is found in every possible system, that is, whether every system lends itself to calculation. Not all physicists before Leibniz believed with Descartes in the conservation of a constant quantity of motion, and their discoveries were no worse for it; Leibniz's conservation of *vis viva* admitted an obvious exception in the central impact of inelastic bodies; science long did without a universal conservation principle. Since the mechanical theory of heat, the principle seems to apply to all physico-chemical phenomena, but nothing guarantees that physiological, and especially nervous, phenomena will not reveal, beside kinetic and potential energy, a new kind of energy that resists calculation. Natural science would lose none of its precision; it would only be understood that conservative systems are not the only possible ones, or that they play in concrete reality the role the chemist's atom plays in bodies. The most radical mechanism makes consciousness an epiphenomenon added under certain conditions to molecular motions; but if molecular motion can create sensation out of a nothingness of consciousness, why could consciousness not create motion out of a nothingness of kinetic and potential energy, or use that energy in its own way? Moreover, every intelligible application of conservation concerns a system whose points can return to their initial positions, at least in principle, with nothing changed in the system or its parts: time has no hold on it. Perhaps humanity's vague instinctive belief in the conservation of matter and force comes from the fact that inert matter does not seem to endure, or keeps no trace of elapsed time. Life is different: there duration seems to act as a cause, and putting things back after a certain time is absurd, since no living being has ever gone backward. Even if that absurdity is only apparent, due to the infinite complexity of the physico-chemical processes in living bodies, a return backward is unintelligible for conscious facts: a sensation changes merely by lasting, until it becomes unbearable; the same does not stay the same but grows with its whole past. The material point of mechanics stays in an eternal present, but the past is real for living beings perhaps, and certainly for conscious ones; elapsed time is neither gain nor loss for a conservative system, but a gain for the living and the conscious being. Bergson asks whether this does not create a presumption in favour of a conscious force or free will which, subject to time and storing up duration, would escape the conservation law. Three arguments are interwoven. The conservation principle as a law of thought is only identity and says nothing about what is conserved. Its physical content is empirical and has a history. And it presupposes reversibility, which duration excludes. The last argument is the one that matters, and it is the first time the *Essai* speaks of duration in living beings, "perhaps," as well as in conscious ones; *L'Évolution créatrice* will make the irreversibility of the living its starting point.

(116–117) It is not the need to found science but a psychological error that has made this abstract principle of mechanics a universal law. Because we do not observe ourselves directly but through forms borrowed from the outer world, we come to believe that real duration, lived by consciousness, is the same as the duration that slides over inert atoms without changing them. Hence we see no absurdity in putting things back after time has passed, supposing the same motives acting again on the same persons, and concluding that the same causes would produce the same effect, a hypothesis Bergson promises to show unintelligible. Once on this path, one inevitably makes conservation universal, having abstracted from the fundamental difference between the outer and the inner world and identified true with apparent duration; it then becomes absurd to treat time, even our own, as a cause of gain or loss, a concrete reality, a kind of force. Without any hypothesis about freedom one would say only that conservation governs physical phenomena until psychological facts confirm it; under the influence of a metaphysical prejudice one says instead that it governs all phenomena until psychological facts refute it. Science has nothing to do with this shift of the burden of proof; it is an arbitrary assimilation of two conceptions of duration. Physical determinism reduces to psychological determinism, which is what must now be examined. The reversal of the burden of proof is the paragraph's main point, and it shows the psychological hypothesis concealed in physical determinism to be a hypothesis about time.

(117–118) Psychological determinism in its most precise and recent form implies an associationist conception of mind. The present state is represented as necessitated by earlier ones, yet the necessity is felt not to be geometric, like that linking a resultant to its components, because successive states differ in quality and one can never deduce a state a priori from those before it. One turns to experience and asks it to show that the passage from one state to the next is always explained by some simple reason, the second answering the call of the first. Experience does show this, and Bergson readily grants that there is a relation between the present state and any new state consciousness passes to. But does the relation that explains the passage cause it? The distinction between a relation that makes a transition intelligible and one that produces it is the hinge of the following examples.

(118) Bergson reports a personal observation. Resuming a conversation interrupted for a few moments, he has noticed that he and the other speaker were thinking at the same time of a new subject. The obvious explanation is that each had pursued the natural development of the last idea and the same chain of associations had formed on both sides. He accepts this for many cases, but careful inquiry produced an unexpected result: both speakers connect the new subject to the old and can name the intermediate ideas, but they do not always attach the shared idea to the same point of the earlier conversation, and the two chains of intermediate associations can differ completely. The conclusion is that the common idea comes from an unknown cause, perhaps some physical influence, and that, to justify its appearance, it called up a series of antecedents that explain it and seem to be its cause but are in fact its effect. The observation does not prove freedom; it detaches the associative chain from causal efficacy by showing that the chain can be constructed after the fact.

(118–119) A hypnotized subject who carries out a suggestion at the appointed time believes the act was brought about by the preceding series of his conscious states. In fact those states are effects, not causes: the act had to be performed and the subject had to explain it to himself, so the future act determined, by a kind of attraction, the continuous series of states from which it then seemed to issue naturally. Determinists will seize on the case as proof that we sometimes undergo irresistibly the influence of another's will. Bergson turns it the other way: it shows equally how our own will can will for the sake of willing and then let the accomplished act explain itself by antecedents of which it was the cause. The example is chosen because it gives empirical form to "effects that precede their causes"; the post-hypnotic case is the laboratory version of the rationalization noted in conversation.

(119) Careful self-examination shows that we sometimes weigh motives and deliberate when our resolution is already made. An inner voice, barely audible, murmurs that we know the outcome and what we will do; yet we seem anxious to respect the principle of mechanism and to put ourselves in order with the laws of association. The will's sudden intervention is like a coup d'état that our intelligence foresees and legitimizes in advance by a regular deliberation. Bergson leaves aside for now whether the will, even when it wills for willing's sake, obeys some decisive reason, and whether willing for willing's sake would be willing freely; he will return to it. It suffices that, even from the associationist standpoint, the absolute determination of the act by its motives and of conscious states by one another is hard to maintain, since a closer psychology reveals effects preceding their causes and phenomena of psychic attraction that escape the known laws of association. The question now is whether the associationist standpoint itself implies a faulty conception of the self and of the multiplicity of conscious states. Bergson's hesitation is significant. "Willing for willing's sake" is the liberty of indifference, and he explicitly declines to identify it with freedom. The examples were a negative exercise, weakening associationism on its own ground before the attack on its premise.

(119–120) Associationist determinism represents the self as an assemblage of psychic states of which the strongest prevails and carries the rest along. It thus sharply separates coexisting psychic facts. Mill writes that he could have refrained from killing if his aversion to the crime and fear of its consequences had been weaker than the temptation, and that a good man's desire to do good and aversion to evil are strong enough to overcome any contrary desire; desire, aversion, fear and temptation are presented as distinct things that can be named separately. Even when he relates them to the self that undergoes them, he keeps sharp divisions: the conflict is between "me who desire a pleasure and me who dread remorse." Bain devotes a chapter to the "conflict of motives," weighing pleasures and pains as terms with at least an abstract existence of their own. The opponents of determinism readily follow it onto this ground and speak of associations of ideas and conflicts of motives too; Fouillée, one of the most profound, even makes the idea of freedom itself a motive able to counterbalance others. Bergson warns of a grave confusion arising from the fact that language cannot express all the nuances of inner states. The inclusion of Fouillée's *idées-forces* is the first concrete instance of the chapter's thesis that libertarians share the determinist premise: to make freedom a motive among motives is to accept the picture of the self as a balance of distinct forces.

(120–121) I get up to open the window and, once standing, forget what I meant to do, and stand still. The associationist says I had associated two ideas, a goal and a movement, and the first has vanished leaving only the second. But I do not sit down again; I feel confusedly that something remains to be done. My immobility is not any immobility: the act to be performed is as if preformed in my posture, so I need only hold the position and study it, or rather feel it inwardly, to recover the vanished idea. The idea must therefore have given the inner image of the sketched movement and the position taken a special colouring, which would have been different had the goal been different. Language would still have described the movement and posture identically, and the associationist would distinguish the cases by saying that a new goal was associated with the same movement, as if the novelty of the goal did not alter the shade of the represented movement even when it is identical in space. One should say not that the representation of a position can be linked to the images of different goals, but that geometrically identical positions appear to consciousness in different forms according to the goal represented. Associationism first strips the act of its qualitative element, keeping only what is geometric and impersonal, and then has to associate some specific difference to the decoloured idea to distinguish it from others; that association is the work of the associationist philosopher who studies my mind rather than of my mind. The example applies Chapter I's analysis of nascent movement (25–26) to the will: the posture carries its goal as a quality, not as an attached idea. The final sentence states the general charge, that associationism projects the analyst's reconstruction into the thing analysed.

(121–122) I smell a rose and confused memories of childhood return. These memories were not evoked by the scent; I breathe them in the scent itself, which is all that for me. Others will smell it differently. The associationist replies that it is the same scent associated with different ideas. Bergson accepts the phrasing on condition that one remembers what it presupposes: one has first eliminated from the various impressions the rose makes on each of us what is personal, keeping only the objective aspect, what belongs to the common domain and so to space. Only on that condition could the rose and its scent be named, and then specific characters had to be added to the general idea of rose-scent to distinguish our personal impressions; the "association" exists only for the explainer, as a method of explanation. In the same way one can imitate a sound peculiar to one language by juxtaposing letters of an alphabet common to many languages, but none of the letters went into making the sound. The alphabet analogy fixes the relation between the associationist analysis and the experience: the analysis is a transcription, accurate enough for communication, from which the experience was not composed. The rose became the most cited example of the whole book, and its point is the identity of perception and memory in a single quality, not the triggering of one by the other.

(122–123) We are brought back to the distinction between the multiplicity of juxtaposition and the multiplicity of fusion or mutual penetration. A feeling or an idea contains an indefinite plurality of conscious facts, but the plurality appears only by being unrolled in the homogeneous medium some call duration and which is really space. We then perceive terms external to one another, and these are not the conscious facts themselves but their symbols, or more precisely the words that express them. As Chapter II showed, the capacity to conceive a homogeneous medium like space is intimately correlated with the capacity to think in general ideas. Once we try to account for a conscious state and analyse it, the highly personal state resolves into impersonal elements, external to one another, each evoking a genus and expressed by a word. But because reason, armed with the idea of space and the power to create symbols, extracts these elements from the whole, it does not follow that they were contained in it; within the whole they occupied no space and did not seek symbolic expression; they interpenetrated and fused. Associationism constantly substitutes the philosophical reconstruction for the concrete phenomenon, confusing the explanation of the fact with the fact. This will become clearer as deeper and more comprehensive states are considered. The principle that what analysis extracts was not therefore contained recalls Chapter II's definition of the subjective as what changes in being divided (61–63), and it disqualifies the associationist inference from analysability to composition.

(123–124) The self touches the outer world at its surface, and since that surface keeps the imprint of things, it associates by contiguity terms it has perceived side by side; the associationist theory fits such connections of very simple and almost impersonal sensations. But as one digs beneath this surface, as the self becomes itself again, its states cease to be juxtaposed and interpenetrate, fuse and take on the colouring of all the others. Each of us has his own way of loving and hating, and that love or hatred reflects his whole personality, yet language designates these states by the same words in everyone and can fix only their objective, impersonal aspect. We judge a novelist's talent by the power with which he draws feelings and ideas out of the public domain into which language had lowered them and tries to restore, through a multiplicity of juxtaposed details, their original living individuality. But just as one can insert points indefinitely between two positions of a moving body without ever filling the space traversed, so, simply because we speak, because we associate ideas and these are juxtaposed instead of interpenetrating, we fail to translate entirely what our soul feels: thought remains incommensurable with language. The comparison with Zeno is exact: the novelist's details stand to the feeling as the points of the trajectory stand to the motion, an infinite approximation that never reaches the act. The paragraph thus extends the solution of Chapter II's paradox to the theory of expression.

(124–126) A crude psychology, duped by language, shows the soul determined by a sympathy, an aversion or a hatred as by so many forces weighing on it. Such feelings, once deep enough, each represent the whole soul, in the sense that its entire content is reflected in each of them; to say that the soul is determined by any of them is to say that it determines itself. The associationist reduces the self to an aggregate of sensations, feelings and ideas, but if he sees in them only what their names express, keeping their impersonal aspect, he can juxtapose them indefinitely and obtain only a phantom self, the shadow of the self projected into space. If instead he takes them with the colouring they have in a particular person, which comes to each from the reflection of all the others, there is no need to associate several facts to reconstitute the person: the person is wholly in one of them, provided it is well chosen. The outward manifestation of such an inner state is what is called a free act, since the self alone is its author and it expresses the whole self. Freedom so understood lacks the absolute character spiritualism sometimes gives it; it admits of degrees. Not all conscious states mix with their kind like raindrops in a pond. Because the self perceives a homogeneous space, it has a surface on which independent growths can form and float. A post-hypnotic suggestion is not incorporated into the mass of conscious facts but, endowed with its own vitality, takes the place of the person when its hour comes; a violent anger raised by some accident, or an inherited vice suddenly emerging from the organism, acts much like a suggestion. There are also more complex series whose elements interpenetrate but which never fuse completely with the compact mass of the self, such as the feelings and ideas left by a misconceived education addressed to memory rather than judgment; a parasitic self forms within the fundamental one and constantly encroaches on it. Many live this way and die without knowing true freedom. But a suggestion would become persuasion if the whole self assimilated it; a passion, even sudden, would lose its fatal character if the whole history of the person were reflected in it, as in Alceste's indignation; and the most authoritarian education would take nothing from our freedom if it communicated only ideas and feelings able to impregnate the whole soul. The free decision comes from the whole soul, and an act is the freer the more the dynamic series to which it belongs tends to coincide with the fundamental self. Two features define Bergson's concept of freedom here and guard against standard misreadings. Freedom is a relation of expression between the act and the whole self, not an absence of causes; and it is graded, so that the alternative "free or determined" is replaced by degrees of integration. The criterion of freedom is the same as the criterion of depth in Chapter I (6–7): how much of the self a state pervades.

(126–127) Free acts so understood are rare, even among those most given to self-observation and reasoning about their conduct. We usually perceive ourselves by refraction through space; our states solidify into words; our concrete, living self is covered by an outer crust of sharply drawn, separate and therefore fixed psychological facts; and for the sake of language and social relations we have every interest in not breaking the crust and in assuming it gives the exact shape of what it covers. Our daily actions are inspired far less by our infinitely mobile feelings than by the unchanging images to which these feelings adhere. In the morning, when the hour strikes at which I usually rise, I could receive the impression "with the whole soul," as Plato says, and let it melt into the confused mass of impressions that occupy me, and then it might not make me act. Usually, instead of stirring my whole consciousness like a stone falling into a pool, it only moves an idea solidified on the surface, the idea of getting up and going about my business; impression and idea have become linked, and the act follows without my personality taking part. Here I am a conscious automaton, and I am so because it is to my advantage. Most daily actions are performed this way, and thanks to the solidification of certain sensations, feelings and ideas in memory, external impressions provoke movements that, though conscious and even intelligent, resemble reflexes in many respects. To these very numerous but mostly insignificant actions the associationist theory applies. Together they form the substratum of our free activity and play toward it the role our organic functions play toward conscious life as a whole. Bergson grants the determinist further that we often abdicate freedom in graver circumstances and, from inertia or weakness, let the same local process run when our whole personality should vibrate: when our most trusted friends urge an important act, their insistently expressed feelings settle on the surface of the self and solidify like the ideas just described, forming a thick crust over our own feelings; we believe we act freely and recognize the mistake only later. But it is not rare, as the act is about to be performed, for a revolt to occur. Bergson's concession to determinism is large, and it is a theory, not a retreat: automatism is the normal and useful regime of the surface self, as associationism is the correct theory of surface ideas (100–102). Freedom is not everywhere; it is located.

(127–128) It is the self below that rises to the surface; the outer crust bursts under an irresistible pressure. Beneath the reasonably juxtaposed arguments, a seething and so a growing tension of feelings and ideas was going on, not unconscious but unattended to. Gathering our memories carefully, we find that we formed these ideas and lived these feelings ourselves, but from an inexplicable reluctance to will, we pushed them back into the obscure depths each time they surfaced. That is why we try in vain to explain our sudden change of resolution by its apparent antecedents. We want to know the reason for our decision and find that we decided without reason, perhaps against all reason. But in certain cases that is precisely the best of reasons, for the act then expresses not some superficial idea, almost external to us and easy to state, but the whole of our feelings, thoughts and deepest aspirations, the particular conception of life that is the equivalent of all our past experience, in short our personal idea of happiness and honour. It was therefore a mistake to look in the ordinary and indifferent circumstances of life for proof that man can choose without a motive; such insignificant actions are easily shown to be tied to some determining motive. It is in solemn circumstances, when what is at stake is the opinion we will give others and above all ourselves of who we are, that we choose in spite of what is conventionally called a motive, and the absence of any tangible reason is the more striking the more deeply we are free. The paragraph completes the reversal of the classical case for free will. Libertarians cited trivial choices, Buridan's indifference, as the purest freedom; Bergson finds there the most determined acts, and places freedom where the act is overdetermined by the whole person and therefore cannot be traced to any one motive. "Without reason" means without a separable reason, not without ground.

(128–129) Even when the determinist refrains from making deep emotions into forces, he still distinguishes them from one another and so arrives at a mechanistic conception of the self. He shows the self hesitating between two contrary feelings, going from one to the other and finally choosing one, so that the self and its feelings are treated as well-defined things that remain identical throughout. But if the same self deliberates and the two feelings do not change either, then by the very principle of causality the determinist invokes, how would the self ever decide? In truth the self has already changed somewhat by having felt the first feeling when the second arrives; at every moment of deliberation the self changes and so changes the two feelings. A dynamic series of interpenetrating, mutually reinforcing states forms and ends in a free act by a natural evolution. The determinist, obeying a vague need for symbolic representation, designates the opposed feelings and the self by words; by crystallizing them in well-defined words he removes in advance all living activity from the person and its feelings, and then sees an invariant self on one side and invariant contrary feelings on the other, contending for it, with victory necessarily going to the stronger. This mechanism, to which one has condemned oneself in advance, has only the value of a symbolic representation and cannot stand against the testimony of attentive consciousness, which presents inner dynamism as a fact. The objection turns the determinist's principle against him: if the terms were invariant, nothing would cause the decision. Deliberation is possible only because the deliberating self changes, which makes the decision a development rather than a verdict.

(129–131) We are free when our acts spring from our whole personality, express it, and have with it the indefinable resemblance sometimes found between a work and its artist. It is useless to object that we then yield to the omnipotent influence of our character: our character is still ourselves, and because we have split the person into two, considering by abstraction the self that feels or thinks and the self that acts, it would be childish to conclude that one weighs on the other. The same applies to those who ask whether we are free to change our character. Our character changes imperceptibly every day, and our freedom would suffer if these acquisitions were grafted onto the self rather than fused with it; once fusion has occurred, the change is ours, appropriated by us. If one agrees to call free every act that comes from the self and the self alone, the act that bears the mark of our person is truly free, since our self alone will claim its paternity. The thesis of freedom would be verified if freedom were sought only in a certain character of the decision taken, in the free act. But the determinist, feeling this position escape him, takes refuge in the past or the future: either he goes back in thought to an earlier moment and affirms that the future act was then necessarily determined, or he supposes the act already performed and claims it could not have happened otherwise. The opponents of determinism follow him onto this ground and include in their definition of the free act, perhaps dangerously, the foresight of what one could do and the memory of some other course one could have taken. Bergson will therefore examine what pure consciousness, apart from external influences and the prejudices of language, teaches about the future or past act, and so grasp from another side, as bearing explicitly on a conception of duration, determinism's basic error and its opponents' illusion. The artist analogy fixes the positive concept: freedom is a resemblance between act and agent, which is a relation of expression, not of production by a separable faculty. The objection from character is dissolved by the same refusal to split the person, and the argument then shifts from the act in the making to retrospection and prevision, where both parties have spatialized time.

(131–132) Mill writes that to be conscious of free will means to be conscious, before choosing, of having been able to choose otherwise. Defenders of freedom understand it this way and claim that when we act freely some other act would have been equally possible, appealing to consciousness, which gives us besides the act the power to choose the contrary. Determinism holds that, given certain antecedents, only one resulting act was possible; when we suppose we might have acted otherwise, Mill says, we always suppose a difference in the antecedents, pretending to have known what we did not know, or the reverse; and true to his principle he assigns consciousness the role of informing us about what is, not what might be. Bergson reserves the psychological question of how the self perceives itself as a determining cause, and isolates a more metaphysical question that both sides settle a priori in opposite ways: whether given antecedents allow only one act, or several equally possible ones. He will examine first this question of the equal possibility of two contrary acts, hoping to learn something about the operation by which the will chooses. The formulation "able to choose otherwise" is the target of the next four paragraphs, and Bergson's strategy is to show that both answers to it rest on the same picture.

(132) I hesitate between two possible actions X and Y and go from one to the other. This means I pass through a series of states that can be divided into two groups according as I lean more toward X or toward its contrary. Only these opposed inclinations really exist, and X and Y are symbols by which I represent, at their end points as it were, two different tendencies of my person at successive moments of duration. If X and Y designate the tendencies themselves, is the notation more faithful? The self grows, enriches itself and changes as it passes through the two states, otherwise it would never decide; so there are not exactly two contrary states but a multitude of successive and different states within which, by an effort of imagination, I discern two opposed directions. It is closer still to reality to let the invariant signs X and Y stand not for the tendencies or states, which constantly change, but for the two directions our imagination assigns them for the convenience of language. It remains understood that these are symbolic representations, that in reality there are neither two tendencies nor even two directions but a self that lives and develops through its very hesitations, until the free act drops from it like an over-ripe fruit. Bergson corrects the notation in three steps, from actions to tendencies to directions, each step conceding less reality to the alternatives, and ends by withdrawing even the directions. The ripe fruit image replaces the picture of a choice between paths by that of a maturation.

(132–134) Common sense, essentially mechanistic, is not satisfied with this, because it likes sharp distinctions expressed by well-defined words or different positions in space. It pictures a self that, having traversed a series MO of conscious facts, finds itself at point O facing two equally open directions OX and OY. These become things, real paths at the end of the great road of consciousness, which the self could take indifferently. The continuous, living activity of the self, in which only abstraction distinguished two opposed directions, is replaced by the directions themselves, made into inert, indifferent things awaiting our choice. The activity must then be put somewhere, and it is put at O: arriving at O before two courses, the self hesitates, deliberates and finally opts. Since it was hard to represent the double direction of conscious activity through all the phases of its continuous development, the two tendencies were crystallized apart and so was the self's activity, giving an indifferently active self hesitating between two inert, solidified courses. If it chooses OX, line OY still subsists, and if OY, path OX remains open, waiting for the self to retrace its steps; that is the sense in which, of a free act, the contrary act is said to have been equally possible. Even without drawing the figure, we think of it involuntarily as soon as we distinguish successive phases in the free act, the representation of opposed motives, hesitation and choice, hiding the geometric symbolism under a verbal crystallization. This truly mechanistic conception of freedom leads by natural logic to the most inflexible determinism. Bergson's thesis is that the "liberty of indifference" is not the opposite of mechanism but a mechanistic image of choice, the spatialization of duration applied to deliberation.

(134) The living activity of the self, in which two opposed tendencies were distinguished by abstraction, will end in either X or Y. Since the double activity of the self has been localized at O, there is no reason to detach it from the act in which it ends and with which it forms one body. If experience shows that one decided for X, one must place at O not an indifferent activity but one directed in advance toward OX, despite apparent hesitations; if Y, the activity at O favoured that direction despite some oscillation toward the other. To say that the self at O chooses indifferently between X and Y is to stop halfway along the path of geometric symbolism, crystallizing at O only part of the continuous activity, in which two directions were distinguished but which also ended at X or Y. Why not give this last fact its place in the figure too? But if the self at O is already determined in one direction, the other path may stay open, but it cannot take it. The same crude symbolism on which the contingency of the act was to be founded establishes, by a natural extension, its absolute necessity. The argument is internal: once the process is represented as a figure, consistency requires putting the outcome into the figure, and a figure that contains its outcome is a determinist figure. Libertarian indifference is an unstable halfway house.

(134–135) Defenders and opponents of freedom thus agree in preceding the act by a kind of mechanical oscillation between X and Y. If I choose X, the former say: you hesitated and deliberated, so Y was possible. The latter reply: you chose X, so you had some reason, and declaring Y equally possible forgets that reason and leaves out one of the conditions of the problem. Beneath these opposed solutions lies a common postulate: both place themselves after the act X is accomplished and represent my voluntary activity by a road MO that forks at O, the lines OX and OY symbolizing the two directions abstraction distinguishes in the continuous activity of which X is the end. The determinists take into account everything they know and note that the path MOX was traversed; their opponents affect to ignore one of the data with which they built the figure, and after drawing OX and OY, which together should represent the progress of the self's activity, send the self back to O to oscillate until further notice. The shared postulate is retrospection: both sides reason about the act after it is done, as a path traversed, which is the confusion of motion with its trajectory (82–86) transposed to the will. The libertarian is the more inconsistent of the two, since he must pretend not to know the end he used to draw the figure.

(135–136) The figure, a real doubling of psychic activity in space, is purely symbolic and can be constructed only on the hypothesis of a completed deliberation and a resolution taken. Even if you draw it in advance, you have imagined yourself at the end, watching the final act. It shows not the action being done but the action done. So Bergson refuses the question whether the self, having traversed MO and chosen X, could or could not have chosen Y: the question is meaningless, because there is no line MO, no point O, no path OX, no direction OY. To ask it is to assume that time can be adequately represented by space and succession by simultaneity, to give the figure the value of an image rather than a symbol, and to believe that one could follow the process of psychic activity on it like an army's march on a map. One has watched the self's deliberation through every phase to the act; then, recapitulating the series, one sees succession as simultaneity, projects time into space and reasons on the figure. But the figure represents a thing, not a progress; in its inertia it corresponds to the frozen memory of the whole deliberation and final decision, and cannot tell us anything about the concrete movement by which deliberation ends in the act. Yet once it is built, one goes back in imagination and insists that our psychic activity followed exactly the path it traces, which repeats the illusion already noted: one explains a fact mechanically and then substitutes the explanation for the fact. Hence the inextricable difficulties: if both courses were equally possible, how did one choose? if only one was possible, why did one think oneself free? Both questions come down to one: is time space? A symbol may be used for calculation; taken as an image of the process, it generates the antinomy of freedom.

(136–137) If I look over a road traced on a map, nothing stops me from going back and checking whether it forks somewhere. But time is not a line one can retrace. Once it has elapsed, we may represent its successive moments as external to one another and think of a line through space, but that line symbolizes time elapsed, not time elapsing. Both sides forget this, one in affirming and the other in denying that one could have acted otherwise. The first says: the road is not yet traced, so it can take any direction; the answer is that one can speak of a road only once the act is done, and then it will have been traced. The second says: the road was traced this way, so its possible direction was not any direction but this one; the answer is that before the road was traced there was no direction, possible or impossible, for there was not yet any road. Remove the crude symbolism that obsesses you unawares, and the determinist argument takes the childish form "the act, once done, is done," while the reply is "the act, before being done, was not yet done." The question of freedom emerges untouched, as is natural, since freedom must be sought in a certain shade or quality of the act itself, not in a relation between the act and what it is not or might have been. All the obscurity comes from representing deliberation as oscillation in space, when it is a dynamic progress in which the self and its motives are in continual becoming, like living beings. The self, infallible in its immediate findings, feels itself free and says so; but when it tries to explain its freedom, it perceives itself only by refraction through space, which yields a mechanistic symbolism equally unfit to prove free will, to make it understood, or to refute it. The reduction of both positions to tautologies is the dissolution in its sharpest form. Its consequence is positive: freedom is located in a quality of the act, not in alternative possibilities, which is why the classical concept of "could have done otherwise" is set aside rather than defended.

(137–139) The determinist will not admit defeat and will pose the question anew: set aside accomplished acts and consider future ones; could a superior intelligence that knew today all the future antecedents predict with absolute certainty the decision that will follow? Bergson accepts the formulation, which lets him state his view more rigorously, but first distinguishes those who think knowledge of antecedents would allow a probable conclusion from those who speak of infallible prediction. To say that a friend in certain circumstances would very probably act in a certain way is less to predict his future conduct than to judge his present character, which means his past. Our feelings, ideas and character change constantly, but sudden change is rare, and it is rarer still to be unable to say of someone we know that certain actions fit his nature and others are wholly foreign to it. All philosophers can agree on this, since establishing a relation of fittingness between a given conduct and a person's present character does not bind the future to the present. The determinist goes much further: the contingency of our forecasts comes from never knowing all the conditions, their probability would grow with more conditions, and complete knowledge of all antecedents would make the prediction infallible. That hypothesis is what must be examined. Bergson's concession of probable prediction is significant. It shows that his conception of freedom does not make conduct capricious; the free act resembles the person, and resemblance is what grounds expectation. What he contests is only the passage to the limit.

(139) To fix ideas, imagine a person called Pierre who must make an apparently free decision in serious circumstances. The question is whether a philosopher Paul, living at the same time as Pierre or, if one prefers, several centuries earlier, could have predicted with certainty the choice Pierre made, knowing all the conditions in which Pierre acts. The thought experiment is set up so that Paul's position is indifferent to time; the possibility of his living centuries earlier presupposes that knowing conditions is independent of living through them, which is exactly what the next paragraph will deny.

(139–142) There are several ways of representing a person's state at a given moment. We try when reading a novel, but however carefully the author depicts his hero's feelings and reconstructs his history, the ending, foreseen or not, adds something to our idea of the character, so we knew him only imperfectly. The deep states that issue in free acts express and sum up our whole past history, so if Paul knows all the conditions of Pierre's act, he presumably misses no detail of Pierre's life and reconstructs and even relives it in imagination. Here a capital distinction must be made. When I myself pass through a psychological state, I know its intensity and its importance relative to others precisely, not by measuring or comparing but because the intensity of a deep feeling is nothing but the feeling itself. If I try to convey that state to you, I can make you understand its intensity only by a precise, mathematical sign: I must measure its importance, compare it with what precedes and follows, and fix its share in the final act, declaring it more or less intense according as the act is explained with or without it. For my consciousness, which perceived the state, no such comparison was needed; intensity appeared as an inexpressible quality of the state itself. Intensity is not given as a special sign attached to a state and determining its power like an algebraic exponent; as Chapter I showed, it expresses the state's shade, and the intensity of a feeling consists in its being felt. There are therefore two ways of assimilating another's states: dynamically, by experiencing them, or statically, by replacing the consciousness of them with their image, or rather their intellectual symbol, their idea. In the second case one must add to the image an indication of intensity, since the states no longer act on the person who represents them; and that indication will be quantitative, saying that one feeling had more force, counted for more, played a larger role. How could that be known without knowing in advance the person's later history and the acts in which these states ended? For Paul to represent Pierre's state adequately at any moment, either he must, like a novelist who knows where he is taking his characters, already know Pierre's final act, or he must pass through the states himself, not in imagination but in reality. The first option is excluded, since the question is whether Paul can foresee the act from the antecedents alone. Paul must therefore be not a spectator whose gaze plunges into the future but an actor playing Pierre's part in advance, and none of the part can be spared: the most trivial events matter in a history, and could be judged insignificant only relative to the final act, which is not given. Nor may any of Paul's states be shortened by a second, since the effects of a feeling accumulate at every moment of duration, and their sum could be felt all at once only if one knew the feeling's importance relative to the act, which remains in the dark. But if Pierre and Paul have had the same feelings in the same order, and their souls have the same history, how are they to be distinguished? Not by their bodies, for then they would differ at every moment, never representing the same body; not by their place in duration, for then they would not witness the same events, whereas by hypothesis they share past and present. Pierre and Paul are one and the same person, called Pierre when he acts and Paul when one recapitulates his history. As the sum of conditions needed for prediction was completed, one approached Pierre's existence more closely and tended to relive it in every detail, arriving at the precise moment when, the action taking place, there could no longer be any question of predicting it, only of acting. Every attempt to reconstruct an act of the will leads to the bare acknowledgment of the accomplished fact. The thesis of Chapter I, that intensity is quality and not magnitude, becomes the premise of the refutation of predictive determinism: a static representation must supply quantities of influence, and those quantities can be read only from the outcome. The identity of Paul and Pierre is the reduction to absurdity of a knower who could know a duration without living it, and its premise is the principle of identity of indiscernibles applied to histories.

(142) It is therefore a meaningless question whether the act could or could not have been foreseen given the complete set of antecedents. There are two ways of assimilating them. Dynamically, one comes by insensible transitions to coincide with the person, pass through the same states and return to the very moment the act is performed, so that there is no longer any question of foreseeing it. Statically, one presupposes the final act simply by placing beside the indication of the states a quantitative estimate of their importance. Once again one side is led to observe that the act is not yet done when it is about to be done, the other that once done it is done for good, and the question of freedom emerges intact from this discussion as from the previous one. The dilemma is the temporal counterpart of the one at 136–137: the dynamic route abolishes prevision, the static route assumes the outcome.

(142–143) Digging deeper, one finds at the root of this double argument the two fundamental illusions of reflective consciousness. The first takes intensity to be a mathematical property of psychological states rather than, as the beginning of the essay said, their special quality and proper shade. The second replaces the concrete reality, the dynamic progress consciousness perceives, by the material symbol of that progress at its end, the accomplished fact together with the sum of its antecedents. Once the final act is done, I can assign each antecedent its value and represent their combined play as a conflict or composition of forces. But to ask whether, the antecedents and their values being known, the final act could have been predicted is a vicious circle, since the value of the antecedents already contains the act to be predicted; it wrongly supposes that the symbolic image by which one represents the completed operation was drawn by the operation itself as it progressed, as on a recording device. The recording-device image identifies the fallacy exactly: it treats the after-the-fact diagram as a trace left during the process, like the line of the pendulum's pen, and so endows the diagram with causal history. The two illusions are those of Chapters I and II respectively, so this paragraph shows that the argument against freedom needs both.

(143–144) These two illusions imply a third, and the question of prevision always comes down to the question whether time is space. You began by juxtaposing in an ideal space the states that succeeded one another in Pierre's soul, seeing his life as a trajectory MOXY traced by a moving point M. You then erase in thought the part OXY and ask whether, knowing MO, you could have determined in advance the curve OX the point describes from O. That was your question when you brought in Paul, Pierre's predecessor, charged with imagining the conditions of Pierre's act: you materialized the conditions and made future time a road already traced across the plain, visible from the mountaintop without having been or ever being travelled. You soon saw that knowing MO would not suffice unless the positions of its points were given relative not only to one another but to the points of the whole line MOXY, which would mean giving in advance what was to be determined. You then changed your hypothesis: you understood that time asks not to be seen but to be lived, and that your knowledge of MO was insufficient because you looked at it from outside instead of merging with the point M that describes the whole curve and adopting its movement. So you made Paul coincide with Pierre, and naturally Paul traced MOXY, since Pierre traces it; but that no longer proves that Paul foresaw Pierre's act, only that Pierre acted as he did, since Paul has become Pierre. Then, without noticing, you return to your first hypothesis, constantly confusing the line MOXY being traced with the line MOXY traced, time with space: having identified Paul with Pierre for the sake of the argument, you send Paul back to his old observation post, and he sees the complete line, which is unsurprising, since he has just completed it. "Time asks not to be seen but lived" is the methodological maxim the whole chapter enforces. The final move exposes the equivocation on which the determinist relies: Paul is a participant when knowledge is needed and a spectator when prediction is claimed.

(144) The confusion seems natural and even inevitable because science appears to offer undisputed examples of foreknowledge: conjunctions of stars, eclipses of sun and moon, most astronomical phenomena are determined in advance, and human intelligence seems to embrace in the present moment as large a stretch of future duration as one likes. Bergson grants this, but says that such prediction has no resemblance to that of a voluntary act. The reasons that make astronomical prediction possible are the very ones that prevent us from determining a free act in advance, because the future of the material universe, though contemporaneous with that of a conscious being, has no analogy with it. The paragraph announces a demonstration that turns the determinist's best example into evidence against him, and its premise is the Chapter II thesis that the material world has no duration (80–82, 86–87).

(145) To make the capital difference palpable, suppose an evil genius more powerful than Descartes' ordered all the motions of the universe to go twice as fast. Nothing would change in astronomical phenomena, or at least in the equations that predict them, because in those equations t denotes not a duration but a ratio between durations, a number of units of time, in the last analysis a number of simultaneities. The coincidences would occur in the same number; only the intervals between them would shrink, and the intervals play no part in the calculations. But those intervals are lived duration, the duration consciousness perceives, and consciousness would soon tell us of a shortening of the day if we had endured less between sunrise and sunset. It would not measure the shortening, and might not at first perceive it as a change of quantity, but it would register in some form a drop in the being's usual enrichment, a change in the progress it ordinarily achieves between sunrise and sunset. The Cartesian frame is deliberate. Descartes' evil genius threatened the reality of the external world; Bergson's leaves physics intact and reveals that physics never contained duration. Consciousness detects the change as a qualitative impoverishment, which presupposes that the "quantity" of lived time is itself a quality, as Chapter II concluded (78–79).

(146–147) When the astronomer predicts a lunar eclipse, he exercises in his own way the power attributed to the evil genius. He orders time to go ten, a hundred, a thousand times faster, and is entitled to, since he changes only the nature of the conscious intervals, which do not enter the calculations. He can therefore fit several years, even centuries, of astronomical time into a psychological duration of a few seconds; that is what he does when he draws a heavenly body's trajectory in advance or represents it by an equation. He establishes a series of positional relations between the body and others, a series of simultaneities, coincidences and numerical relations; duration proper stays outside the calculation and would be perceived only by a consciousness able not only to witness the successive simultaneities but to live their intervals. One can even conceive a consciousness living so slow and lazy a life that it took in the whole trajectory in a single apperception, as we see the successive positions of a shooting star drawn as a line of fire. That consciousness would really be in the conditions where the astronomer places himself in imagination: it would see in the present what the astronomer sees in the future. The astronomer foresees a future phenomenon only by making it, to some degree, a present one, or by reducing enormously the interval separating us from it. Astronomical time is a number whose units cannot be specified in the calculations, so they can be made as small as one likes, provided the same assumption covers the whole series and preserves the successive positional relations. One then witnesses in imagination the phenomenon to be predicted, knows where and after how many units it occurs, and need only restore to the units their psychic nature to push the event back into the future and say one foresaw it, when in fact one saw it. "Prevision is vision" follows from Chapter II's thesis that the material world contains only simultaneities. The slow consciousness that sees a planet's orbit at a glance anticipates the theory of different rhythms or "tensions" of duration in *Matière et mémoire* (ch. IV).

(147–148) The units of time that make up lived duration, which the astronomer can dispose of freely because science has no hold on them, are precisely what concerns the psychologist, for psychology deals with the intervals themselves, not their end points. Pure consciousness does not perceive time as a sum of units of duration and, left to itself, has no means or reason to measure it; but a feeling that lasted half as many days would no longer be the same feeling, lacking a multitude of impressions that enriched it and modified its nature. When we give the feeling a name and treat it as a thing, we think we could halve its duration, and that of the rest of our history, and have the same existence at reduced scale. We forget that conscious states are progressions, not things; that we designate each by one word for the convenience of language; that they live and, living, change constantly; and that no moment can be removed without impoverishing them and changing their quality. One can see a planet's orbit at once or in a very short time because only its successive positions, the results of its motion, matter, not the duration of the equal intervals between them. A feeling has no precise result except having been felt, and to appreciate that result adequately one would have to pass through all its phases and occupy the same duration. Even if the feeling ended in some determinate act, comparable to a planet's position, knowing that act would not tell me the feeling's influence on the whole history, which is what we need to know. All prevision is really vision, achieved when a future interval can be reduced more and more while preserving the relations of its parts, as in astronomy. But reducing an interval of time means emptying or impoverishing the states that succeed one another in it. The very possibility of seeing an astronomical period in abridged form thus implies the impossibility of modifying a psychological series in the same way, since only by taking the psychological series as an invariable base can one vary at will the unit of duration of an astronomical period. The astronomer's freedom to rescale presupposes a fixed standard, lived duration, against which the rescaling is defined; that standard cannot itself be rescaled. *L'Évolution créatrice* will put the same point as the impossibility of hurrying the sugar's dissolving.

(148–149) To ask whether a future action could be foreseen is unconsciously to identify the time of the exact sciences, which reduces to a number, with real duration, whose apparent quantity is really a quality and which cannot be shortened by an instant without changing the nature of the facts that fill it. What makes the identification easy is that in many cases we may treat real duration like astronomical time. When we recall the past, a series of accomplished facts, we always abridge it without altering the event that interests us, because we already know it; having reached the end of the progress that constitutes its existence, the psychological fact has become a thing that can be represented all at once. We are then in the astronomer's position when he grasps in one apperception an orbit a planet will take years to travel. Astronomical prediction should be compared to the memory of a past conscious fact, not to foreknowledge of a future one. But to determine a future conscious fact, however little deep, the antecedents must be considered not statically, as things, but dynamically, as progressions, since only their influence is in question, and their duration is that influence. There can be no question of abridging future duration to represent its fragments in advance; one can only live it as it unfolds. In the region of deep psychological facts there is no appreciable difference between foreseeing, seeing and acting. The asymmetry of past and future replaces the symmetry of the time line: the past can be abridged because it is finished, the future cannot because its duration is still efficacious. "Their duration is that influence" is the formula that unites the chapter's attack on prediction with its account of freedom.

(149–150) Only one course remains for the determinist. He will no longer claim that a future act or state can be foreseen today, but will maintain that every act is determined by its psychic antecedents, that conscious facts obey laws like natural phenomena. This argument avoids the detail of concrete psychological facts, from an instinctive fear of meeting phenomena that defy all symbolic representation and so all prediction. It leaves their nature in the dark but asserts that, as phenomena, they remain subject to the law of causality, which requires that every phenomenon be determined by its conditions, that the same causes produce the same effects. Either the act is indissolubly bound to its psychic antecedents, or the principle of causality suffers an incomprehensible exception. The determinist retreats from a claim about knowledge, prediction, to a claim about being, lawfulness, and the dilemma he now poses is the one Bergson must dissolve without accepting an "exception" to causality.

(150–151) This last form of the argument differs less than one might think from the others. To say that the same inner causes produce the same effects is to suppose that the same cause can appear more than once on the stage of consciousness. Bergson's conception of duration asserts the radical heterogeneity of deep psychological facts and the impossibility of two of them being wholly alike, since they are two moments of a history. The external object bears no mark of elapsed time, so the physicist can meet identical elementary conditions despite the diversity of moments; but duration is real for the consciousness that keeps its trace, and one cannot speak of identical conditions there, because the same moment does not occur twice. It is useless to reply that, if no two deep states are alike, analysis would find stable elements within them that could be compared. Even the simplest psychological elements, if deep, have their own personality and life; they are always becoming, and the same feeling, merely by being repeated, is a new feeling. We have no reason to keep its old name, except that it corresponds to the same external cause or shows itself by similar signs; to infer from the supposed similarity of the two states that the same cause produces the same effect would be a petitio principii. If causal relation exists in the inner world, it cannot resemble what we call causality in nature. For the physicist the same cause always produces the same effect; for a psychologist not misled by apparent analogies, a deep inner cause gives its effect once and will never produce it again. If one then says that this effect was indissolubly bound to its cause, that means either that the act could have been foreseen from the antecedents or that, once done, any other act appears impossible in the given conditions, and both claims have been shown empty. The principle of causality is not violated but deprived of application, because its antecedent, "the same cause," is never satisfied in duration. That is how Bergson avoids the "incomprehensible exception": freedom does not break the law; it lies where the conditions of the law are never repeated.

(151–152) Bergson nonetheless pauses on this last form of the argument, if only to clarify the two words determination and causality from his own standpoint. He has argued that a future act cannot be foreseen like an astronomical event, that it cannot be said after the act that any other was impossible in the given conditions, and that "the same causes produce the same effects" loses all meaning in the inner world. The determinist may concede each point, admit that "determination" can have none of these three senses in psychology, and fail to find a fourth, yet still repeat that the act is indissolubly bound to its antecedents. An illusion so deep and a prejudice so tenacious can be overcome only by attacking their principle, the principle of causality. Analysing the concept of cause will reveal its ambiguity and, without defining freedom, may take us beyond the purely negative idea of it so far reached. The announcement is significant on two counts. The persistence of the determinist conviction after its arguments fail is itself a datum calling for explanation, which the analysis of causality will supply. And Bergson explicitly withholds a definition of freedom, consistently with the Conclusion's claim (166) that freedom cannot be defined.

(152–153) We perceive physical phenomena, and they obey laws. This means that phenomena a, b, c, d perceived before can recur in the same form, and that a phenomenon P that appeared after conditions a, b, c, d, and only after them, will recur whenever the same conditions are given. If the principle of causality said no more, as the empiricists claim, one could grant that it comes from experience, but it would prove nothing against freedom. It would remain true that determinate antecedents give rise to a determinate consequent wherever experience shows the regularity, and the whole question of freedom is whether that regularity is found in consciousness. If the principle only summarizes uniform and unconditional successions observed in the past, by what right is it applied to deep conscious facts in which no regular successions have yet been found, since they cannot be predicted? And how can it ground the determinism of inner facts, when on the empiricist view the determinism of observed facts is its only ground? When empiricists use causality against freedom, they take "cause" in a new sense, which is that of common sense. Bergson turns Mill's own theory of causation against Mill's determinism: an inductive principle cannot be extended to a domain where the inductions are lacking, so the determinist must be relying on a stronger notion of cause than his epistemology allows.

(153) To note a regular succession of two phenomena is to recognize that, the first being given, one already perceives the second. Common sense finds this purely subjective connection between two representations insufficient: if the idea of the second is already implied in the idea of the first, the second phenomenon itself must exist objectively, in some form, within the first. Common sense was bound to conclude this, because a precise distinction between objective connection and subjective association presupposes a fairly advanced philosophical culture. One passes insensibly from the first sense to the second and represents causality as a kind of preformation of the future phenomenon in its present conditions. This preformation can be understood in two very different senses, and the ambiguity begins there. The genealogy of the strong concept of cause is psychological: expectation, which is an anticipation in the mind, is projected into things as the containment of the effect in the cause.

(153–156) Mathematics provides one image of such preformation. The motion that traces a circle in a plane generates all the properties of the figure, so that an indefinite number of theorems preexist in the definition, though they unfold in time for the mathematician who deduces them. This is the domain of pure quantity, where geometric properties take the form of equalities and a first equation can be transformed into an indefinite number of others virtually contained in it. Physical phenomena, which succeed one another and are perceived by our senses, differ in quality as well as quantity and are hard to declare equivalent at first. But because the senses perceive them, nothing prevents attributing their qualitative differences to their effect on us and supposing a homogeneous physical universe behind the heterogeneity of our sensations. Strip matter of its concrete qualities, colour, heat, resistance, even weight, and one is left with homogeneous extension, space without bodies; one can then only cut figures in space, move them by mathematically formulated laws, and explain the apparent qualities of matter by the form, position and motion of these figures. Position is given by a system of fixed magnitudes and motion by a law, a constant relation between variable magnitudes; but form is an image, and however thin and transparent one supposes it, it remains, as a visual perception of imagination, a concrete and so irreducible quality of matter. It too must be removed and replaced by the abstract formula of the motion that generates the figure. Picture algebraic relations interlocking, becoming objective through their very interlocking, and producing by their complexity alone concrete, visible and tangible reality: this is only drawing the consequences of causality understood as the actual preformation of the future in the present. Contemporary scientists have hardly pushed abstraction so far, except perhaps Sir William Thomson, who supposes space filled with a homogeneous, incompressible fluid in which vortices move and generate the properties of matter; the atom becomes a motion, and physical phenomena reduce to regular motions within an incompressible fluid. But since this fluid is perfectly homogeneous, with no void between its parts and no difference to tell them apart, any motion in it amounts in fact to absolute immobility, since before, during and after the motion nothing changes in the whole. The motion in question is not a motion that occurs but a motion that is thought, a relation between relations. One admits, perhaps unawares, that motion is a fact of consciousness, that space contains only simultaneities, and one is given the means of calculating these simultaneities for any moment of our duration. Mechanism has nowhere gone further, since even the form of the ultimate elements of matter is reduced to motion. But Cartesian physics already admits a similar interpretation: if matter reduces to homogeneous extension, as Descartes holds, the motions of its parts can be conceived through the abstract law that governs them or an algebraic equation between variables, but not represented as concrete images. The more mechanical explanation develops this conception of causality and lightens the atom of its sensible properties, the more the concrete existence of natural phenomena tends to vanish in algebraic smoke. The first sense of preformation is logical implication, and its consistent physical form is the reduction of things to equations. Bergson's verdict on Thomson's vortex atom applies Chapter II's analysis exactly: motion in a perfectly homogeneous medium is indistinguishable from rest, so mechanism, carried to its end, confirms that motion belongs to consciousness. "Algebraic smoke" names the price: a causality of pure implication explains phenomena by abolishing them.

(156–157) So understood, the causal relation is necessary in the sense that it approaches the relation of identity indefinitely, as a curve its asymptote. The principle of identity is the absolute law of consciousness: it affirms that what is thought is thought at the moment it is thought. Its absolute necessity comes from the fact that it binds not the future to the present but only the present to the present; it expresses the unshakeable confidence consciousness has in itself as long as it confines itself to noting the apparent present state of the soul. The principle of causality, insofar as it would bind the future to the present, could never take the form of a necessary principle, for the successive moments of real time are not bound to one another, and no logical effort will prove that what has been will be or continue to be, that the same antecedents will always call forth identical consequents. Descartes understood this so well that he attributed the regularity of the physical world and the continuation of the same effects to a constantly renewed grace of Providence; he built a kind of instantaneous physics, fit for a universe whose duration fits entirely in the present moment. Spinoza held that the series of phenomena, which takes for us the form of succession in time, is equivalent in the absolute to the divine unity, so that apparent causal relations between phenomena reduce to identity in the absolute and the indefinite duration of things is contained in a single moment, eternity. Whether one examines Cartesian physics, Spinozist metaphysics or contemporary science, one finds the same concern to establish a relation of logical necessity between cause and effect, expressed as a tendency to turn relations of succession into relations of inherence, to cancel the action of duration and replace apparent causality by fundamental identity. The asymptote image states the thesis exactly: necessary causation is identity minus time, and the more necessary it becomes the less temporal. Descartes' continuous creation and Spinoza's eternity are read as the two consistent ways of paying for necessity by abolishing duration, a reading of the history of metaphysics that *L'Évolution créatrice* (ch. IV) will develop at length.

(157–158) If developing causality as necessary connection leads to the Cartesian or Spinozist conception of nature, conversely every relation of necessary determination between successive phenomena must come from perceiving confusedly, behind heterogeneous phenomena, a mathematical mechanism. Common sense need not intuit kinetic theories or a Spinozist mechanism, but the more necessarily the effect seems bound to the cause, the more we tend to put it into the cause as a mathematical consequence in its principle, and so to cancel the action of duration. That I do not behave today as yesterday under the same external conditions is unsurprising, because I change, because I endure; but things, considered apart from our perception, do not seem to endure, and the more we explore this idea, the more absurd it seems that the same cause should not produce today the effect it produced yesterday. We feel that if things do not endure as we do, there must still be some incomprehensible reason why phenomena appear to succeed one another rather than unfold all at once, which is why causality, though approaching identity indefinitely, never seems to coincide with it, unless we clearly see a mathematical mechanism or a subtle metaphysics removes some quite legitimate scruples. Still, our belief in the necessary determination of phenomena grows firmer as we take duration to be a more subjective form of consciousness: the more we make causality a relation of necessary determination, the more we affirm that things do not endure as we do, and so the more we accentuate the difference between a psychological series and a physical one. However paradoxical it seems, the supposition of a relation of mathematical inherence among external phenomena should lead, as a natural or at least plausible consequence, to belief in human freedom. Bergson leaves that consequence aside for now; he has only sought to fix the first sense of causality and to show that the mathematical preformation of the future in the present is easy to conceive thanks to a conception of duration more familiar to common sense than it seems. Strict physical determinism, far from extending to consciousness, presupposes the very difference between durationless things and enduring consciousness that grounds freedom. The "incomprehensible reason" why things succeed at all is the scruple that *L'Évolution créatrice* will resolve by granting duration to the universe.

(158–159) There is another kind of preformation, still more familiar to the mind, because immediate consciousness provides its image. We pass through successive conscious states, and although the next was not contained in the previous one, we had a more or less confused idea of it beforehand. Its realization did not appear certain, only possible. Between the idea and the action came barely perceptible intermediaries whose whole takes for us the *sui generis* form called the feeling of effort, and the progress from idea to effort and from effort to act was so continuous that we cannot say where idea and effort end and act begins. In a certain sense the future was again preformed in the present, but very imperfectly, since the future action whose idea is present is conceived as realizable, not realized, and even when we sketch the effort needed to perform it we feel there is still time to stop. If causality is conceived in this second way, one can affirm a priori that cause and effect will no longer stand in a relation of necessary determination, since the effect is no longer given in the cause but resides there only as a pure possibility, a confused representation that may not be followed by the corresponding act. Common sense is satisfied with this approximation, as the ease with which children and primitive peoples accept an inconstant nature, where caprice plays as large a role as necessity, shows; and this representation of causality is more accessible, since it requires no abstraction, only an analogy between the outer and the inner world. The second preformation is the one Chapter I found in affective sensation, the sketch of the act within the state (25–26), here generalized into a model of causation. The continuity of idea, effort and act, with no assignable boundary, is the positive phenomenology of the free act that the chapter has otherwise approached only negatively.

(159–160) This second conception is more natural than the first because it answers at once to the need for a representation. If we look for B within the phenomenon A that regularly precedes it, it is because the habit of associating the two images has given us the idea of the second as enveloped in the idea of the first. It is natural to push this objectification to the end and make A itself a psychic state in which B is contained as a confused representation, supposing that the objective connection resembles the subjective association that suggested it. The qualities of things then become states analogous to those of the self, and the material universe is given a vague personality diffused through space, which, without a conscious will, passes from one state to another by an inner push, an effort. Such was ancient hylozoism, a timid and even contradictory hypothesis that kept extension for matter while giving it real states of consciousness, unrolling the qualities of matter along extension while treating them as inner, and so simple, states. Leibniz removed the contradiction by showing that, if the succession of external qualities is understood like the succession of our ideas, qualities must be simple states or perceptions and the matter supporting them an unextended monad like our soul. The successive states of matter can then no more be perceived from outside than our own states, and pre-established harmony is needed to explain how these inner states represent one another. The second conception of causality leads to Leibniz as the first led to Spinoza, and in both cases one only pushes to the extreme two timid and confused ideas of common sense. Spinoza is the consistent development of causation as implication, Leibniz of causation as effort, and common sense oscillates between them without noticing.

(160–161) Causality understood in this second way plainly does not entail the determination of the effect by the cause. History confirms it. Ancient hylozoism explained the regular succession of causes and effects by a real *deus ex machina*, now a necessity external to things and hovering over them, now an inner Reason guided by rules like those of our conduct. The perceptions of Leibniz's monad do not necessitate one another either; God must have ordered them in advance. Leibniz's determinism comes not from his conception of the monad but from his building the universe out of monads alone: having denied any mechanical influence of substances on one another, he had to explain how their states correspond, hence a determinism rooted in pre-established harmony and not at all in the dynamic conception of causality. Setting history aside, consciousness testifies that the abstract idea of force is that of an undetermined effort, one not yet issued in the act, in which the act exists only as an idea. The dynamic conception of causality thus attributes to things a duration wholly analogous to ours, whatever its nature, and supposes that the future is no more bound to the present in the external world than it is for our consciousness. The separation of Leibniz's dynamism from his determinism is a careful piece of history of philosophy: the necessity comes from the architecture of harmony, not from the monad's inner life. The final clause states, as a hypothesis, the generalized duration of things that Bergson's later work will affirm.

(161–164) The double analysis shows that the principle of causality contains two contradictory conceptions of duration and two incompatible images of the preformation of the future in the present. Either all phenomena, physical or psychological, are represented as enduring in the same way, our way, so that the future exists in the present only as an idea and the passage to it takes the form of an effort that does not always realize the idea; or duration is made the proper form of conscious states, things no longer endure as we do, and a mathematical preexistence of the future in the present is admitted for things. Each hypothesis taken alone safeguards freedom: the first by putting contingency into natural phenomena themselves, the second by attributing the necessary determination of physical phenomena to the fact that things do not endure as we do, which invites us to make the enduring self a free force. Every clear and self-consistent conception of causality therefore leads to human freedom as a natural consequence. Unfortunately we have acquired the habit of taking the principle in both senses at once, because one flatters imagination and the other favours mathematical reasoning: sometimes we think of the regular succession of physical phenomena and the inner effort by which one becomes the other, sometimes of their absolute regularity, from which we pass by insensible degrees to mathematical necessity, which excludes duration in the first sense, and we temper each image by the other according to the interests of science. To apply causality in this equivocal form to conscious facts is to create gratuitously inextricable difficulties. The idea of force, which really excludes necessary determination, has become amalgamated with necessity through the use made of causality in nature. We know force only through consciousness, and consciousness neither affirms nor even understands the absolute determination of future acts; experience alone would say that we feel free and perceive force, rightly or wrongly, as free spontaneity. But the idea of force, carried into nature and having travelled beside the idea of necessity, comes back corrupted by the journey, impregnated with necessity, and in the light of the role we made it play outside we see force as determining its effects necessarily. Here again consciousness errs because it considers the self not directly but by refraction through the forms it lent to external perception, which perception returns only after they have taken on its colour. A compromise has formed: the mechanical determination of two external phenomena now looks like the dynamic relation of our force to the act it produces, while that relation in turn looks like a mathematical derivation, the human act issuing mechanically and so necessarily from its force. The fusion suits common sense, since it lets us represent and name alike the relation between two moments of our own existence and that between successive moments of the external world. Just as we break our deepest states into mutually external parts and give concrete duration moments as distinct as bodies scattered in space, it is no wonder that we set up between the moments of our objectified existence a relation like objective causality, and that an exchange, again comparable to endosmosis, occurs between the dynamic idea of free effort and the mathematical concept of necessary determination. In the natural sciences the two ideas have already been dissociated. The physicist may speak of forces and even picture their action by analogy with inner effort, but never uses this in a scientific explanation; even those who, with Faraday, replace extended atoms by dynamic points treat centres and lines of force mathematically, without regard to force as activity or effort. External causality is purely mathematical and bears no resemblance to the relation of psychic force to the act that proceeds from it. The argument repeats the form of the Chapter II analysis of homogeneous time: two real terms, a durationless physical necessity and an enduring inner effort, produce by endosmosis a hybrid, here "necessitating force," which belongs to neither. The determinist's tenacity, noted at 151–152, is explained: he lives inside the hybrid. Physics has already dissolved it on its side, and Bergson's task is to dissolve it on the side of consciousness.

(164–165) It is time to add the converse: inner causality is purely dynamic and has no analogy with the relation of two external phenomena that condition each other. External phenomena can recur in homogeneous space and so enter into a law, whereas deep psychic facts appear to consciousness once and never again. Attentive analysis of the psychological phenomenon first led to this conclusion; the study of causality and duration in themselves has only confirmed it. The paragraph closes the demonstration by matching its two routes, introspective and conceptual. Inner causation is not suspended; it is a different relation, unrepeatable and therefore not lawlike.

(165) Bergson can now state his conception of freedom. The single sentence marks the transition from critique to thesis, and the thesis that follows will be stated in a form that resists the definitions the chapter has dismantled.

(165) Freedom is the relation of the concrete self to the act it performs. This relation is indefinable precisely because we are free. One analyses a thing, not a progress; one decomposes extension, not duration. If one insists on analysing anyway, one unconsciously turns the progress into a thing and duration into extension: merely by claiming to decompose concrete time, one unrolls its moments in homogeneous space and puts the accomplished fact in place of the fact being accomplished, and having first frozen the self's activity, one sees spontaneity resolve into inertia and freedom into necessity. That is why every definition of freedom will prove determinism right. The indefinability is not a confession of ignorance but a consequence of the thesis: to define is to analyse, and analysis spatializes. Freedom is accordingly a relation, not a property; and it holds between the concrete self and the act, which recalls the artist and the work (129–131).

(165) Will the free act be defined by saying that, once done, it could have not been done? That assertion, like its contrary, implies an absolute equivalence between concrete duration and its spatial symbol, and once that equivalence is admitted, the formula developed leads to the most inflexible determinism. This recapitulates 132–137: the counterfactual definition presupposes the forking road, and the forking road, completed, is determinist.

(165) Will the free act be defined as "one that could not be foreseen even when all its conditions are known in advance"? To conceive all conditions as given is, in concrete duration, to place oneself at the very moment the act is performed. Otherwise one admits that the stuff of psychic duration can be represented symbolically in advance, which is to treat time as a homogeneous medium and to admit, in a new form, the absolute equivalence of duration and its symbol. Pressed, this second definition also leads to determinism. This recapitulates 137–149: unpredictability defined against complete knowledge either collapses into coincidence with the agent, Paul becoming Pierre, or presupposes the spatialized time it was meant to exclude.

(166) Will the free act finally be defined as one not necessarily determined by its cause? Either the words lose all meaning, or they mean that the same inner causes will not always produce the same effects. One then admits that the psychic antecedents of a free act can recur, that freedom unfolds in a duration whose moments resemble one another, and that time is a homogeneous medium like space; one is brought back to the equivalence of duration and its spatial symbol, and pressing the definition will once again bring out determinism. This recapitulates 149–151: indeterminism presupposes repeatable causes, and repeatable causes are what determinism needs. The three definitions correspond to the three determinist arguments, retrospective, predictive and nomological, and each libertarian definition shares the premise of the argument it opposes.

(166) In sum, every request for clarification about freedom comes down, unawares, to the question whether time can be adequately represented by space. Bergson answers yes for time elapsed, no for time elapsing. The free act takes place in time elapsing, not in time elapsed. Freedom is therefore a fact, and among the facts we observe none is clearer. All the difficulties of the problem, and the problem itself, come from wanting to find in duration the same attributes as in extension, to interpret a succession by a simultaneity, and to express the idea of freedom in a language into which it is plainly untranslatable. The chapter ends where the Foreword began, with the language of translation (vii), and fulfils its promise: the problem, not freedom, disappears. The distinction between elapsed and elapsing time grants the spatial representation its legitimate field, the past, and denies it only the present of action. "Freedom is a fact" should be read with that restriction: it is a fact for the consciousness that acts, given immediately and lost to any representation that comes after.

## Conclusion

(167) To summarize, Bergson sets aside Kant's terminology and doctrine, to which he will return, and adopts the standpoint of common sense. Contemporary psychology has seemed to him chiefly concerned to show that we perceive things through forms borrowed from our own constitution. The tendency has grown since Kant: where Kant sharply separated time from space, the extensive from the intensive and, as one would now say, consciousness from external perception, the empiricist school, pushing analysis further, tries to rebuild the extensive from the intensive, space from duration and externality from inner states. Physics completes the work of psychology on this point by showing that, to predict phenomena, one must disregard the impression they make on consciousness and treat sensations as signs of reality rather than reality itself. The Conclusion opens by placing the book within the post-Kantian programme of studying the forms through which we know things. The empiricist reconstruction of space from inner states, already criticized at 69–70, is presented as the extreme of that programme.

(167–168) Bergson poses the inverse problem: whether the most evident states of the self, which we think we grasp directly, are not mostly perceived through forms borrowed from the external world, which would thus return to us what we lent it. This seems likely a priori. Even if the forms to which we fit matter come wholly from the mind, it is hard to apply them constantly to objects without the objects soon colouring them, so that in using them to know ourselves we risk taking for the colour of the self a reflection of the frame we place it in, the external world. One can go further: forms applicable to things cannot be entirely our own work; they must result from a compromise between matter and mind, so that if we give matter much we doubtless receive something from it, and when we try to recover ourselves after an excursion into the external world, our hands are no longer free. The "inverse problem" is the book's most compact self-description, a critique of inner sense where post-Kantian psychology had offered a critique of outer sense. The final claim, that forms are a compromise between matter and mind, already qualifies Kant's apriorism and anticipates the genesis of intellect and matter in *L'Évolution créatrice* (ch. III).

(168–169) Just as we abstract from whatever in our ways of perceiving and thinking clearly does not fit physical phenomena in order to find their real relations, so psychology should eliminate or correct the forms that bear the visible mark of the external world if it is to contemplate the self in its original purity. Bergson names them. Psychological states, isolated and taken as distinct units, seem more or less intense; taken in their multiplicity, they unfold in time and constitute duration; in their relations to one another, and insofar as some unity persists through their multiplicity, they seem to determine one another. Intensity, duration and voluntary determination are the three ideas to be purified of what they owe to the intrusion of the sensible world, in short to the obsession with space. The triad matches the three chapters, and the parallel with physics is methodological: the psychologist's "abstraction" from spatial forms is the counterpart of the physicist's abstraction from sensible qualities. The phrase "original purity" names the goal, not a starting point; the immediate is recovered by correction.

(169) On the first idea, Bergson found that psychic facts are in themselves pure quality or qualitative multiplicity, while their cause in space is quantity. Insofar as the quality becomes the sign of the quantity, and we suspect the latter behind the former, we call it intensity. The intensity of a simple state is not quantity but its qualitative sign, and it originates in a compromise between pure quality, the fact of consciousness, and pure quantity, which is necessarily space. We give up this compromise without scruple when studying external things, setting aside forces themselves, if they exist, to consider only their measurable and extended effects; why keep the bastard concept when analysing consciousness? If magnitude outside us is never intensive, intensity within us is never magnitude. Failing to see this, philosophers had to distinguish extensive and intensive quantity without explaining what they share or how the same words "increase" and "diminish" apply to things so unlike; and so they bear responsibility for the excesses of psychophysics, since once sensation is credited, other than metaphorically, with the power to grow, we are invited to ask by how much. That consciousness does not measure intensive quantity does not mean science cannot do so indirectly, if it is a magnitude. Either a psychophysical formula is possible, or the intensity of a simple psychic state is pure quality. The summary sharpens Chapter I by stating the symmetry: physics discards intensive magnitude outside, psychology should discard it inside. The phrase "bastard concept," used in Chapter II for homogeneous time (73–74), is now applied to intensity, which shows that Bergson sees the two as the same kind of hybrid.

(169–170) On the concept of multiplicity, the construction of a number was found to require first the intuition of a homogeneous medium, space, where distinct terms can be aligned, and second a process of penetration and organization by which the units add up dynamically and form what was called a qualitative multiplicity. The units add up thanks to this organic development, but they stay distinct because of their presence in space; number, or distinct multiplicity, is also the result of a compromise. When we consider material objects in themselves, we give up the compromise, taking them to be impenetrable and divisible, indefinitely distinct from one another; we must give it up too when we study ourselves. Not having done so, the associationists fell into sometimes crude errors, trying to rebuild a psychic state by adding distinct conscious facts and putting the symbol of the self in place of the self. The summary makes explicit what Chapter II stated only at its end (90–92): number itself is a compromise, depending on qualitative multiplicity for its synthesis and on space for its distinctness. The parallel with intensity is now exact: both are mixtures, and in each case the task is to separate the components rather than to deny either.

(170) These preliminary considerations made it possible to approach the main object of the work, the analysis of the ideas of duration and voluntary determination. The sentence confirms the Foreword's plan (vii–viii) and corrects a possible impression left by the length of Chapter I: intensity and number are preliminaries, and the book's core is duration and freedom.

(170) What is duration within us? A qualitative multiplicity, with no likeness to number; an organic development that is nevertheless not a growing quantity; a pure heterogeneity within which there are no distinct qualities. The moments of inner duration are not external to one another. The three formulas are the book's most condensed definition of duration, each pairing a positive term with a correction. "No distinct qualities" is the strictest of them: heterogeneity here does not mean a set of different qualities, which would already be a distinct multiplicity, but difference without separation.

(170–171) What exists of duration outside us? Only the present, or, if one prefers, simultaneity. External things change, but their moments succeed one another only for a consciousness that remembers them. Outside us, at a given moment, we observe a set of simultaneous positions; of earlier simultaneities nothing remains. To put duration in space is, by a real contradiction, to place succession within simultaneity. One should therefore not say that external things endure, but rather that there is in them some inexpressible reason why we cannot consider them at successive moments of our duration without finding that they have changed. This change does not imply succession, unless the word is taken in a new sense; on this point science and common sense agree. The "inexpressible reason" is the same scruple noted at 157–158, and it is the point at which the *Essai*'s restriction of duration to consciousness is most visibly provisional. Bergson acknowledges that things change without granting them succession, a position *Matière et mémoire* will abandon.

(171) In consciousness, then, we find states that succeed one another without being distinguished; in space, simultaneities that, without succeeding one another, are distinguished, in the sense that one has ceased when the other appears. Outside us, mutual externality without succession; within, succession without mutual externality. The chiasmus restates 80–82 as the formula of the whole book. "Distinguished" is again used in its quantitative sense, which is why inner states can succeed without being distinguished.

(171–172) Here too a compromise intervenes. We grant the simultaneities that constitute the external world, which are distinct but succeed one another only for us, a succession of their own; hence the idea of making things endure as we do and putting time into space. Conversely, if consciousness introduces succession into external things, those things externalize the successive moments of our inner duration with respect to one another. The simultaneities of physical phenomena, absolutely distinct in that one has ceased when the other occurs, cut into distinct, mutually external parcels an inner life in which succession would imply mutual penetration, as the pendulum of a clock breaks into separate fragments and spreads out lengthwise the dynamic, undivided tension of the spring. So, by a real endosmosis, the mixed idea of measurable time is formed, which is space as homogeneity and duration as succession, in short the contradictory idea of succession within simultaneity. The image of the spring and the pendulum is new and exact: the spring's tension is indivisible, and the escapement turns it into countable beats. It complements the Chapter II analysis by showing which side contributes which feature of measurable time, homogeneity from space and succession from consciousness.

(172) Science dissociates these two elements, extension and duration, when it undertakes the thorough study of external things. Bergson believes he has shown that it keeps from duration only simultaneity, and from motion only the position of the moving body, that is, immobility. The dissociation is very clear here, and it favours space. The summary of 86–89 is reduced to its principle: science has already performed half of the required separation.

(172) The dissociation must therefore be performed again, but in favour of duration, when inner phenomena are studied: not inner phenomena in their completed state, after discursive intelligence has separated and unrolled them in a homogeneous medium, but inner phenomena in the course of formation, insofar as, by their mutual penetration, they constitute the continuous development of a free person. Duration so restored to its original purity will appear as a wholly qualitative multiplicity, an absolute heterogeneity of elements that fuse into one another. The symmetry with science gives the book's method its most precise statement. Psychology is to do for duration what physics did for space, and the qualification "in the course of formation" restricts the field to the dynamics of the self, the domain Chapter II (102–104) denied to superficial psychology.

(172–173) Failing to perform this necessary dissociation, some have been led to deny freedom, and others to define it and thereby, involuntarily, deny it again. One asks whether the act could or could not have been foreseen given all its conditions, and whether one says yes or no, one assumes the conditions could be conceived as given in advance, which amounts to treating duration as homogeneous and intensities as magnitudes. Or one says the act is determined by its conditions, without seeing the play on the double sense of causality, which gives duration two mutually exclusive forms at once. Or one invokes the conservation of energy without asking whether it applies equally to the moments of the external world, which are equivalent, and to those of a being both living and conscious, which swell one another. However freedom is approached, it is denied only by identifying time with space, defined only by asking space for an adequate representation of time, and argued about, either way, only after confusing succession and simultaneity. Every determinism will therefore be refuted by experience, but every definition of freedom will prove determinism right. The concluding formula is the book's most quoted sentence, and its two halves must be read together: freedom is given in experience, and it is lost in any definition, because definition spatializes. The list of determinist arguments runs in reverse order to Chapter III, ending with conservation, which there came first.

(173) Bergson asks why the dissociation of duration and extension, which science performs so naturally in the external world, demands such effort and arouses such reluctance for inner states. Science aims chiefly to predict and to measure; physical phenomena can be predicted only on the assumption that they do not endure as we do, and only space can be measured, so the break between quality and quantity, true duration and pure extension, happened there of itself. With our conscious states, however, we have every interest in keeping the illusion that makes them share the mutual externality of external things, because this distinction and solidification let us give them stable names despite their instability and distinct names despite their interpenetration; they let us objectify them and bring them into the current of social life. The asymmetry is explained by interest: the same practical motive that favours the dissociation in physics opposes it in psychology. Freedom is hard to see not because it is hidden but because seeing it is useless for social life.

(173–174) There would thus be two different selves, one of which is like the external projection of the other, its spatial and, so to speak, social representation. We reach the first by deep reflection, which makes us grasp our inner states as living beings constantly in formation, refractory to measure, interpenetrating, and whose succession in duration has nothing in common with juxtaposition in homogeneous space. The moments in which we recover ourselves in this way are rare, which is why we are rarely free. Most of the time we live outside ourselves; we perceive only the discoloured ghost of the self, the shadow pure duration casts in homogeneous space. Our existence unfolds in space rather than in time; we live for the external world rather than for ourselves; we speak rather than think; we "are acted" rather than act. To act freely is to take possession of oneself again, to place oneself back in pure duration. The "two selves" of the summary must be read with Chapter II's warning (102–104) that they are one person at two depths; "projection" says exactly that. The final sentence gives freedom its only quasi-definition, which is a description of an act of recovery rather than of a property.

(174–175) Kant's error was to take time for a homogeneous medium. He seems not to have noticed that real duration is made of moments internal to one another, and that when it takes the form of a homogeneous whole it is because it is expressed in space. His very distinction between space and time thus amounts at bottom to confusing time with space, and the symbolic representation of the self with the self. He judged consciousness unable to perceive psychological facts except by juxtaposition, forgetting that a medium in which facts are juxtaposed and distinguished is necessarily space and no longer duration. He was thus led to believe that the same states can recur in the depths of consciousness as the same physical phenomena recur in space, as he implicitly admitted in giving causality the same meaning and role in the inner as in the outer world. Freedom then became an incomprehensible fact. Yet, through an unlimited though unconscious confidence in the inner apperception whose scope he tried to restrict, he believed in freedom unshakeably. He raised it to the level of noumena, and since he had confused duration with space, he made this real and free self, which is indeed foreign to space, equally foreign to duration and so inaccessible to our faculty of knowledge. The truth is that we perceive this self whenever, by a vigorous effort of reflection, we turn our eyes from the shadow that follows us and return into ourselves. Though we usually live and act outside our own person, in space rather than duration, and so give a hold to the law of causality that binds the same effects to the same causes, we can always place ourselves back in pure duration, whose moments are internal and heterogeneous to one another, and where a cause cannot reproduce its effect, since it will never itself recur. Kant's solution, freedom as noumenal and timeless, is diagnosed as the consequence of having spatialized time: once time is homogeneous, freedom can only be placed outside it. Bergson's alternative locates the free self in time, not beyond it, and declares it knowable by reflection. The phrase "moments internal to one another," replacing Chapter II's "interpenetrating," is the most compact definition of duration's structure.

(175–176) In this confusion of true duration with its symbol lie both the strength and the weakness of Kantianism. Kant imagines things in themselves on one side and, on the other, a homogeneous Time and Space through which they are refracted, giving rise to the phenomenal self that consciousness perceives and to external objects. Time and space would then be no more within us than outside us; the very distinction between outside and inside would be their work. The doctrine has the advantage of giving empirical thought a solid foundation and of assuring us that phenomena, as phenomena, can be adequately known. We could even make them absolute and do without incomprehensible things in themselves, if practical reason, revealing duty, did not intervene like Platonic reminiscence to tell us that the thing in itself exists, invisible and present. What dominates the theory is the sharp distinction between the matter and the form of knowledge, between the homogeneous and the heterogeneous, and this capital distinction would doubtless never have been made had time too not been considered a medium indifferent to what fills it. Bergson's assessment is balanced. He credits Kant with securing the adequacy of phenomenal knowledge, and locates the source of the form–matter distinction in the treatment of time as a container, not in the analysis of space, which Bergson largely accepts (68–73). The comparison of practical reason to Platonic reminiscence is pointed: freedom reaches Kant only as a reminder from beyond experience, whereas for Bergson it is the clearest datum of experience.

(176) If time, as immediate consciousness perceives it, were a homogeneous medium like space, science would have a hold on it as on space. But Bergson has tried to show that duration as duration and motion as motion escape mathematical knowledge, which keeps only simultaneity from time and only immobility from motion. Neither Kantians nor their opponents seem to have noticed this: in the so-called phenomenal world made by science, every relation that cannot be translated into simultaneity, that is, into space, is scientifically unknowable. Second, in a duration supposed homogeneous, the same states could recur, causality would imply necessary determination, and all freedom would become incomprehensible, which is where the *Critique of Pure Reason* ends up. Instead of concluding that real duration is heterogeneous, which would have cleared up the second difficulty and drawn his attention to the first, Kant preferred to place freedom outside time and erect an impassable barrier between the world of phenomena, which he hands over entirely to the understanding, and that of things in themselves, which he forbids us to enter. Bergson identifies two consequences of homogeneous time that Kant missed or misread: the phenomenal world of science is narrower than Kant thought, since it contains no duration, and freedom need not be expelled from time if time is heterogeneous. The two errors have a single root, and correcting it opens a path between Kant's two worlds.

(176–177) Perhaps the distinction is too sharp, and the barrier easier to cross than supposed. If the moments of real duration, perceived by attentive consciousness, interpenetrated instead of being juxtaposed, and formed a heterogeneity in which the idea of necessary determination lost all meaning, then the self grasped by consciousness would be a free cause, we would know ourselves absolutely, and, precisely because this absolute constantly mingles with phenomena and, taking on their colour, penetrates them, those phenomena would not be as accessible to mathematical reasoning as is claimed. The conditional form is deliberate, but the content is a programme. Against Kant, self-knowledge is absolute, not merely phenomenal; and because the free self is mixed with phenomena, the phenomenal world is not wholly mathematizable. The two halves of Bergson's later metaphysics, intuition as absolute knowledge (*Introduction à la métaphysique*) and the limits of mechanism in nature (*L'Évolution créatrice*), are both announced here.

(177) Bergson recalls his assumptions. He supposed a homogeneous Space and, with Kant, distinguished it from the matter that fills it. With Kant he admitted that homogeneous space is a form of our sensibility, meaning only that other intelligences, those of animals for instance, perceive objects without distinguishing them as sharply from one another or from themselves. This intuition of a homogeneous medium, proper to man, lets us externalize our concepts with respect to one another and reveals the objectivity of things; by this double operation, favouring language on one side and presenting a world external to us in whose perception all intelligences commune on the other, it announces and prepares social life. The agreement with Kant is real but reinterpreted: "form of sensibility" now means a specifically human capacity, graded across species, rather than a universal condition of possible experience. The double function of space, linguistic and intersubjective, repeats the last paragraph of Chapter II (102–104) and makes social life the end that the form of space serves.

(177–180) Facing this homogeneous space, Bergson placed the self as attentive consciousness perceives it, a living self whose indistinct and unstable states cannot be dissociated without changing nature, nor fixed or expressed without falling into the common domain. The temptation was great for this self, which distinguishes external objects so sharply and represents them so easily by symbols, to introduce the same discrimination into its own existence, replacing the intimate penetration of its states and their wholly qualitative multiplicity by a numerical plurality of distinct, juxtaposed terms expressed in words. Instead of a heterogeneous duration with interpenetrating moments, we then have a homogeneous time whose moments line up in space; instead of an inner life whose successive phases, each unique, are incommensurable with language, an artificially recomposable self and simple psychic states that aggregate and disaggregate like letters forming words. This is not merely a mode of symbolic representation, because immediate intuition and discursive thought are one in concrete reality, and the mechanism by which we first explained our conduct ends by governing it. Our states, detaching from one another, solidify; stable associations form between our crystallized ideas and our outward movements; and gradually, consciousness imitating the process by which nervous matter acquires reflex actions, automatism covers freedom. Bergson's footnote adds that Renouvier had already spoken of voluntary acts comparable to reflexes and restricted freedom to moments of crisis, but did not notice that the process of our free activity continues unawares at every moment of duration in the obscure depths of consciousness, that the very feeling of duration comes from there, and that without this heterogeneous, indistinct duration in which the self evolves there would be no moral crisis; the study of any single free act, however thorough, will not settle the problem, which requires considering the whole series of heterogeneous states, so that the key should have been sought in an attentive analysis of the idea of duration. At this point the associationists and determinists on one side and the Kantians on the other arrive. Seeing only the most common aspect of conscious life, they perceive well-defined states, able to recur in time like physical phenomena and subject to causal determination in the same sense as natural phenomena; and since the medium in which these states are juxtaposed has mutually external parts where the same facts seem able to recur, they make time a homogeneous medium and treat it as space. All difference between duration and extension, succession and simultaneity, is abolished, and it remains only to proscribe freedom or, out of moral scruple, to escort it respectfully into the timeless domain of things in themselves, whose mysterious threshold consciousness cannot pass. A third course is possible: to return in thought to those moments of our existence when we made some grave decision, moments unique in their kind that will no more recur than the vanished phases of a people's history. We would see that if these past states cannot be adequately expressed in words or artificially rebuilt from simpler states, it is because they represent, in their dynamic unity and wholly qualitative multiplicity, phases of our real and concrete, heterogeneous, living duration. We would see that if our action seemed free, it is because its relation to the state from which it came cannot be expressed by a law, that state being unique and never to recur. We would see that the idea of necessary determination loses all meaning here, that there can be no question of foreseeing the act before it occurs or of reasoning about the possibility of the contrary act once it is done, since to give oneself all the conditions is, in concrete duration, to place oneself at the very moment of the act rather than to foresee it. And we would understand the illusion that leads some to deny freedom and others to define it: one passes by insensible degrees from concrete duration, whose elements interpenetrate, to symbolic duration, whose moments are juxtaposed, and so from free activity to conscious automatism; we are free whenever we choose to return into ourselves, but we rarely so choose; and even when an act is freely performed, we cannot reason about it without unfolding its conditions outside one another in space rather than in pure duration. The problem of freedom was thus born of a misunderstanding: it has been for the moderns what the sophisms of the Eleatic school were for the ancients, and like them it originates in the illusion that confuses succession with simultaneity, duration with extension, and quality with quantity. Spatialization is not only a way of representing the self but a way of becoming automatic: "immediate intuition and discursive thought are one in concrete reality," so the symbol, once adopted, governs conduct, which is why freedom is a task and not only a fact. The Renouvier footnote corrects any impression that freedom is confined to crises: crisis presupposes the continuous free process of the deep self, and the feeling of duration itself comes from that process, which makes duration and freedom two names for one reality. The closing comparison with Zeno returns to the Foreword's diagnosis and to the end of Chapter I (54–55): the problem of free will is a modern antinomy of the same structure as the Eleatic paradoxes, dissolved by the same distinction between the act and its trajectory. The final triad, succession and simultaneity, duration and extension, quality and quantity, repeats, in a different order, the three confusions named at the outset (vii–viii), closing the book on its opening.

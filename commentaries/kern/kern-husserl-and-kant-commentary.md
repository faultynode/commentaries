# Iso Kern, *Husserl and Kant: An Investigation of Husserl's Relationship to Kant and to Neo-Kantianism* (1964)

## **Foreword**

Kern opens by placing on the page a quotation from Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* that Husserl himself inscribed on the title page of his own copy of Kant's major work. The quotation states that it is nothing unusual, through comparison of an author's thoughts, to understand him better than he understood himself, since the author may not have sufficiently determined his own concept and thereby sometimes spoke or even thought contrary to his own intention. This epigraph is programmatic for the entire study: Kern's project is to understand Husserl's relationship to Kant more clearly than Husserl himself did, to uncover both conscious and unconscious Kantian motives in Husserl's phenomenology.

The next paragraph surveys the existing literature on the relationship between Husserl's phenomenology and Kantian/neo-Kantian philosophy. Kern observes that a vast range of interpretations has been offered, running the full gamut from seeing Husserl as the polar opposite of Kant and all neo-Kantianism, to seeing Husserl as essentially a neo-Kantian himself, with many nuanced positions in between. This diversity of interpretations shows that the relationship is genuinely complex, but also that real internal connections — whether positive or negative — exist between the two traditions.

Kern then reports that Husserl himself felt driven to clarify his relationship to Kant's philosophy. For his last work, the *Crisis*, Husserl had planned a systematic confrontation with Kant, but death prevented him from executing this plan. Husserl also commissioned his student Fritz Kaufmann to carry out a confrontation with the two most important neo-Kantian schools (the Marburg and Southwest German schools), but this commission was only partially fulfilled.

The following paragraph states the task of the present study: to investigate Husserl's relationship to Kant and to neo-Kantianism both historically and systematically. The purpose is to provide as complete a picture as possible of Husserl's engagement with Kantian and neo-Kantian philosophy — his interpretation, his critique, and his conscious and unconscious absorption of Kantian and neo-Kantian motifs into his own thinking. By investigating this internal relationship, the study hopes to contribute to clarifying the meaning of Husserl's philosophy itself. It is thus a study about Husserl, not a systematic comparison between phenomenology and criticism. Comparisons are drawn only insofar as they serve this goal.

Kern next justifies why, in the systematic presentation of Husserl's relationship to neo-Kantianism, only Paul Natorp and Heinrich Rickert are treated. This limitation is grounded in the fact that these two philosophers were the only neo-Kantians who had greater significance for Husserl. The historical part of the study will justify this claim while also briefly addressing Husserl's relationships to other neo-Kantians. Moreover, Natorp and Rickert are not arbitrary choices: they are the most prominent representatives of the two most important neo-Kantian schools (Marburg and Southwest German), and can therefore serve as representatives for neo-Kantianism as a whole.

A paragraph on the sources follows. Kern's investigations rest not only on Husserl's published works but also on the entirety of his unpublished *Nachlass* (over 40,000 pages in Gabelsberger shorthand, roughly half transcribed by the time of writing). Further, Kern was able to examine Husserl's preserved private library in Leuven, which provides many clues about Husserl's study of Kantian and neo-Kantian philosophy. This extremely broad foundation gives the study the possibility of drawing a more definitive picture than the existing literature, which was largely based only on Husserl's published works.

The next paragraph explains the two-part structure: a historical part and a systematic part. The weight falls entirely on the systematic, since this perspective allows a deeper and clearer insight into the problem. Yet a historical presentation could not be foregone, since it makes certain connections visible that remain hidden in a systematic treatment. To avoid redundancy, the historical part limits itself to mere indications regarding the substantive relations between Husserl's philosophy and that of Kant and neo-Kantianism. The resulting somewhat abstract character is compensated by the concreteness of the systematic part. The systematic part, for its part, does not abstract from the fact that Husserl's relationship to Kant underwent a very significant development, and provides the necessary precision in this regard throughout.

The next paragraph addresses the internal organization of the systematic part. While the historical part treats Husserl's position toward Kant and neo-Kantianism in a single sweep, the systematic part gives a separate treatment of each. This separation has disadvantages, since the two relationships are closely intertwined at many points. But a separate treatment was advantageous for the clarity of the exposition. It always remained possible to indicate the interconnections.

Kern closes the foreword with words of deeply felt gratitude: first to H. L. Van Breda in Leuven, who directed and richly supported the work and kept the Husserl *Nachlass* open to him at all times; then to Eugen Fink in Freiburg, with whom Kern was able to discuss the plan of the work in detail; and finally the warmest thanks to Rudolf Boehm in Leuven, who took Kern's dissertation on as if it were his own. The foreword is dated Leuven, July 1962\.

## **Part 1: Historical Overview of Husserl's Relationship to Kant and to Neo-Kantianism**

### **Chapter 1: From the Student Years to the *Logical Investigations* (1900/01)**

#### **§ 1\. The Student Years**

The first paragraph establishes that already during his mathematical studies in Berlin (1878–1884), Husserl's interest in philosophical problems was awakened. This happened primarily through the mathematical foundational questions posed with full sharpness by his teachers Weierstrass and Kronecker — the latter discussing their philosophical significance — but also through the Berlin professor of philosophy and pedagogy Friedrich Paulsen, whose lectures Husserl attended. Paulsen represented an idealistic monism influenced by Spinoza and Schopenhauer. Epistemologically he relied on Kant, but reshaped Kant in a physiological-psychological direction, supplementing him with teachings from Fechner and Wundt. In ethics, however, Paulsen opposed Kant's "formalistic apriorism."

The next paragraph contextualizes this physiological-psychological interpretation of the Kantian critique of reason as very widespread during Husserl's student years. It was inaugurated by one of the first neo-Kantians, Helmholtz, and spread widely by Friedrich Albert Lange's *History of Materialism* (first published 1866), one of the most influential books of the second half of the nineteenth century. During Husserl's time in Berlin (the 1870s and 1880s), Helmholtz was teaching there. Another early champion of the "Back to Kant" movement, Eduard Zeller, also held a professorship in Berlin. Even if Husserl did not attend the lectures of these two famous neo-Kantians, he lived in a spiritual milieu increasingly shaped by Kant — though at first by a Kant reinterpreted in a natural-scientific and positivistic direction. While interest in and esteem for Kant was rapidly growing, the works of the other great idealists still languished in total disregard and contempt. This was somewhat less the case for Fichte, whom Helmholtz still greatly esteemed. But the entirely experimental-natural-scientific spirit of the age was completely hostile to the works of Schelling and Hegel, whose "unscientific" philosophy of nature was for it an insurmountable stumbling block.

The third paragraph turns to the figure of Franz Brentano. According to Husserl's own reports, his earliest relationship to Kant and to German idealism was primarily shaped by Brentano. Brentano's position toward Kant may at first glance appear very different from that of the Berlin milieu, but in essence only the signs are different. Brentano's Kant-interpretation was determined to a great extent by Lange and Helmholtz. Kern proposes to look at Brentano's relationship to Kant, which was of great importance for Husserl's early period.

The next paragraph provides biographical context: Brentano, whose lectures Husserl attended from 1884 to 1886 in Vienna, and who not only communicated essential philosophical impulses to Husserl but also persuaded him to make philosophy his life's task, was around the turn of the century probably the most significant and at the same time the most embittered opponent of Kant. Brentano's overall judgment of Kant is revealed when we consider the place he assigned him in the history of philosophy. In the three periods of this history — Antiquity, Middle Ages, and Modernity — Brentano distinguishes four phases each: a phase of ascent and flourishing, a phase of shallowing, a phase of skepticism, and finally a phase of radical decline — the stage of excessive anti-skeptical reaction that sets up arbitrary theories and appeals to extravagant cognitive faculties. To this last stage of "mystical excess" or "unleashed constructive drive" belong in antiquity the Neo-Pythagoreans and Neo-Platonists, in the Middle Ages German mysticism and Cusanus, and in modernity the German idealism inaugurated by Kant. Kant is thus seen not as the overcomer of dogmatism and founder of a new scientific philosophy, but as the starting point of ultimate decadence. The "mystical" element in Kant's philosophy Brentano sees in the appeal to "synthetic judgments a priori."

Kern then details Brentano's critique. Brentano directs his criticism primarily against these judgments and the function Kant assigns them in science. He interprets them as judgments lacking all evidence — blind prejudices — and accuses Kant of breaking with the demand of science to admit no proposition that is not immediately evident without proof. Brentano argues that justifying the use of those prejudices by pointing to the subjective conditioning of phenomena does not work: even if phenomena have something subjective about them, the claim that they harmonize with our prejudices is arbitrary, and even if they did, we could not know this since the relevant judgments lack all evidence. Kern quotes Brentano's summary from *Die vier Phasen der Philosophie*. It appears that Brentano never troubled himself with Kant's transcendental deduction and proofs, in which Kant attempts to show that synthetic judgments a priori are not "arbitrary blind prejudices" but conditions of the possibility of objectivity and subjectivity.

The following paragraph continues Brentano's critique: according to Brentano, there are no synthetic a priori cognitions. Those judgments Kant takes to be synthetic a priori are either blind prejudices (hence no cognitions at all — a "blind cognition" is a *contradictio in adjecto*) or they are empirical or analytic cognitions. For Brentano, "analytic" means judgments that are evident from their concepts (*ex terminis*), including not only judgments with the principle of contradiction but also those expressing "positive opposition" (e.g., something blue is not yellow). Pure geometry and arithmetic are for Brentano, against Kant, analytic sciences.

Kern then reports Brentano's rejection of a priori intuitions and concepts. Kant's arguments do not prove the apriority of spatial and temporal intuition, and the intuitions as Kant describes them (especially infinite intuitions) do not actually occur in humans. Many of Kant's supposed "categories" are not concepts at all but "co-signifying expressions" that have a meaning-function only in the context of speech. Their origin lies in the inner experience of modes of judgment. The genuine concepts among the Kantian categories can be traced back to experience.

A further paragraph covers Brentano's rejection of Kant's restriction of knowledge to phenomena. Brentano interprets Kant's concept of phenomena in a psychological sense, as psychic images, and raises objections accordingly: it is unintelligible how one phenomenon could cause another since the causes of a phenomenon are the "thing in itself" and subjectivity, and the natural scientist cannot possibly stick to phenomena since phenomena "do not fall and do not enter into combinations." The restriction to phenomena is also impossible because "phenomenon" is a correlative concept that only has meaning in reference to something that has the phenomenon and is not itself a phenomenon — inner perception provides evidence of a thing as it is in itself. Moreover, Kant himself does not keep to his own restrictions: he infers to "things in themselves" by the law of causality, asserts the existence of subjectivity (which is no phenomenon), and assumes a plurality of conscious beings.

The next paragraph describes Brentano's charge that Kant is not only an agnostic regarding things in themselves but a total skeptic regarding phenomena, since the Transcendental Dialectic shows that both the assumption that the world has spatial and temporal limits and the contrary assumption lead to contradictions — which amounts to saying that the principle of contradiction has no absolute validity for the domain of phenomena. Kern also mentions a specific critique important for Husserl: Brentano charges that Kant misunderstands judgment by seeing its essence in a special kind of representing (a representing of representations to achieve their unity), whereas for Brentano, although representations underlie judgment, judging itself is a fundamentally different act of accepting or rejecting.

Kern then provides the assessment that this very negative relationship to Kant, resting on deep misunderstandings, strongly determined Husserl at the beginning of his philosophical career and above all aroused in him an emotional anti-Kantian affect. Husserl did not hear lectures on Kant from Brentano, but through other lectures and transcripts he obtained, he became thoroughly familiar with Brentano's picture of Kant.

#### **§ 2\. The Pre-Phenomenological Period**

The opening paragraph characterizes this first period of Husserl's philosophizing — which he would later brand as psychologistic and which is marked by interest in the psychological origins of mathematical and logical fundamental concepts — as reflecting clearly the influences of his student years regarding his relationship to Kant. He judges Kant, faithfully following his teacher Brentano, entirely negatively and studies him only very little. He engages more thoroughly with Lange and Helmholtz. His Kant-interpretation, like that of these neo-Kantians and Brentano, is physiological-psychological. This picture emerges from surveying the writings and surviving manuscripts from that period.

The next paragraph examines the *Philosophy of Arithmetic*, Husserl's main work of this period. It refers to Kant several times, always in a rejecting sense. It opposes the Kantian thesis of the "intuitive form of time" as the basis of the concept of number, without going deeper into the sense in which Kant understood the fundamental character of time with respect to arithmetic. Husserl believes he can refute Kant by pointing out that time does not enter into the content of the number concept, and thus is not the subject matter of arithmetic. Husserl is also negative toward Kant's doctrine of schematism.

In the following paragraph Kern reports that in another context Husserl addresses the Kantian doctrine of synthesis — later so important for him — interpreting it as a process that creates objective wholes and relations as its product. He rejects it on the ground that inner psychological experience, which alone matters here, knows nothing of such creative processes.

A further paragraph notes that at a third point Husserl criticizes Kant's interpretation of the addition sign and thereby implicitly rejects Kant's view of the synthetic character of arithmetic. Throughout his life, Husserl maintained against Kant the analytic character of arithmetic.

Kern then discusses Husserl's extensive engagement with Lange and Helmholtz in the *Philosophy of Arithmetic*, where he subjects Lange's theory of space as the intuitive foundation of the number concept and Helmholtz's nominalistic conception of numbers to detailed criticism.

The next paragraph covers a small diary from 1893 containing preliminary studies for the planned *Philosophy of Euclidean Geometry* announced in the preface to the *Philosophy of Arithmetic*. In this preliminary study Husserl engages — again entirely negatively — with Kant's doctrine of the origin of geometrical representations. Husserl dropped this plan, likely due to the deep-reaching change of his views and the new problem-formulations that came with it.

Kern then notes that both the discussions of Kant in the *Philosophy of Arithmetic* and the preliminary study for the geometry book refer to specific passages in the *Critique of Pure Reason* and presuppose a certain direct reading of Kant — though only individual passages concerning the method of mathematics or the concept of schema.

A paragraph on the minor essays and reviews published before 1894 reports that only the *Psychological Studies on Elementary Logic* contains a remark on Kant, and for the first time a positive one: Husserl believes his division of representations into representations and intuitions is confirmed by the Kantian opposition of immediate representations (intuitions) and mediate representations (conceptual and pictorial representations). This is a general parallelization that allows no inference to an essential change in Husserl's relationship to Kant.

Kern then examines the lectures of this period. In contrast to the period immediately before and after the *Logical Investigations*, Husserl held neither seminars nor special lectures on Kant. Only within other lectures did he speak about Kant, as in a lecture on philosophical problems of mathematics from his first year of teaching (1887). These discussions clearly show the extremely negative stance toward Kant, based on very deficient knowledge, and they also reveal the spirit of Brentano. Kant's interpretation of mathematics as synthetic judgments a priori is called the actual foundation of his philosophy, such that the entire edifice of critical philosophy would collapse if these pillars were removed. Husserl interprets Kantian subjectivity in a purely physiological-psychological sense and presents Kant's philosophy as complete skepticism. The same psychologistic Kant-interpretation is found in a lecture from 1895\.

The next paragraph identifies the authors Husserl mainly engaged with in this first period: on one side psychologists (Brentano, Stumpf, Wundt, James), on the other the English empiricists (Locke, Berkeley, Hume, the Mills), to whom Brentano had directed him. Also to be mentioned are Sigwart, and the representatives of objective logic and the Platonic doctrine of essences — Leibniz, Lotze, and Herbart — who became truly significant for Husserl only in the following years. The great German idealists, including Kant, seem to have existed for Husserl's philosophical consciousness only as a bad reputation. And yet, Husserl reveals in a 1915 letter to Rickert that already in his naturalistic beginnings his soul was filled with a secret longing for the old romantic land of German idealism, awakened by reading Windelband's historical works. In that first, as Husserl put it, naturalistic period, his philosophical positions had not yet penetrated into those spiritual spheres that corresponded to his "idealistic" genius; but he already felt secretly attracted to them.

#### **§ 3\. The Period of the *Logical Investigations***

The opening paragraph marks the years 1894 and 1895 as a decisive turning point in Husserl's philosophical development. He turned away from his former positions, which he henceforth condemned as "psychologistic," and developed the thoughts he presented to the public in the *Logical Investigations* (whose first volume was essentially completed by 1896). Kern qualifies this by noting that what holds for all such turnings in the lives of philosophers holds here too: they are neither an absolute end nor an absolute beginning, but lead old thoughts into new dimensions where they appear in a new light. Close connections exist between the second volume of the *Logical Investigations* and the *Philosophy of Arithmetic*; certain investigations of that work go back essentially to the "naturalistic" epoch, though they had a different sense there.

The next paragraph establishes that in this same decisive period, Husserl's relationship to Kant and partly also to neo-Kantianism began to change. Since at latest 1894, Husserl was in correspondence with Paul Natorp. Natorp's fundamental article "On Objective and Subjective Grounding of Knowledge" (1887) was of the greatest importance for Husserl's turn away from his earlier psychologistic standpoint, and equally for the direction of his new thinking — probably much more important than Frege's critique of the *Philosophy of Arithmetic*, which the existing literature considered the decisive motive for Husserl's turn. Natorp's main argument against logical psychologism — that it constitutes a *metabasis eis allo genos* from the objective-logical sphere to the subjective-psychological sphere — is taken over by Husserl into the *Prolegomena* and plays the decisive role there. On the other hand, Natorp's determination of the right and function of subjectively directed research becomes of ever-growing importance for Husserl's conception of phenomenology.

Kern then notes that Natorp was the only neo-Kantian to whom Husserl had a genuinely positive relationship in this period. However, other Kantians entered Husserl's intellectual field of vision. He became acquainted with the methodological basic ideas of Rickert, whose 1894 essay he sharply criticized, though he developed a lively interest in the head of the Southwest German School only after 1907\.

A detailed paragraph describes the neo-Kantian milieu at Halle. Hans Vaihinger (author of the great Kant commentary and founder of critical fictionalism) held a chair there from 1884 to 1906\. Benno Erdmann, the well-known Kant scholar whose positions were strongly influenced by Helmholtz, taught simultaneously with Husserl (1890–1899); Husserl devotes a whole paragraph of the *Prolegomena* to criticizing Erdmann's anthropologism. Like Helmholtz, Lange, and Vaihinger, Erdmann saw Kant primarily as an anti-metaphysician — an interpretation that did not make Kant's philosophy attractive to Husserl, whose innermost intentions always aimed at metaphysics. A third famous Kant interpreter and neo-Kantian was Husserl's colleague at Halle for three years (1898–1901): Alois Riehl. Husserl was in close personal and philosophical contact with him and received from him the strongest moral support for publishing the *Logical Investigations*. Riehl represented a critical realism and maintained against the great neo-Kantian schools a realistic interpretation of the Kantian "thing in itself" — an interpretation that always remained Husserl's own. On the other hand, Husserl's polemic against the "picture" and "sign" theories was aimed primarily against Riehl's realism. Kern concludes that Husserl lived in Halle in a milieu strongly shaped by neo-Kantianism, which by the end of the nineteenth century had advanced far beyond its earliest physiological-psychological forms and represented the dominant philosophical current in Germany.

The next paragraphs trace the far more positive relationship to Kant evident in the *Logical Investigations* themselves. In the introduction to the *Prolegomena*, Husserl names the idea of pure logic he represents with appeal to "Kant and the other representatives of a 'formal' or 'pure' logic." Within the work, he links his idea of pure logic to "great thinkers of the past and first of all to Kant." His determination of the object of the most general idea of pure logic as the "conditions of the possibility of science in general" is characterized as a thoroughly necessary generalization of the Kantian question about the "conditions of the possibility of experience." In a letter to Albrecht (1901), Husserl acknowledges that Natorp correctly observed that the goals Husserl sets for pure logic essentially coincide with those of Kantian critique of knowledge. In the second volume, Husserl says he "feels very close" to Kant. But the *Logical Investigations* also contain negative remarks: Kant's logic intends the genuine idea of logic but does not correctly grasp and determine it in content and scope; Kant's idealism belongs under a genus of psychologism, though his epistemology has sides that strive beyond and actually reach beyond this psychologism of faculties; Kant's "mythical" concepts of understanding and reason are criticized; Kant is faulted for not undertaking a clarifying analysis of the acts of logical thought.

A long passage then details Husserl's lecture activity on Kant during this period (1897–1900): two exercises on the *Prolegomena*, two on the *Critique of Pure Reason*, and several lectures on Kant and post-Kantian philosophy — a dramatic increase from the pre-1896 period. However, Husserl never studied texts purely historically in his seminars; as Plessner reports, "There was no interpretation; one moved at right angles to the text and took something as a pure occasion for independent reflection." Only fragments of these Kant lectures survive.

The closing paragraphs of §3 provide a detailed account of Husserl's reading of Kant based on his private library. Husserl owned two complete editions (Hartenstein and Cassirer) plus separate editions of individual works. He gave the most attention to the *Critique of Pure Reason*, especially the Transcendental Aesthetic, the transcendental deduction of the categories in the first edition (the three syntheses\!), the proofs of the Principles (especially the Analogies of Experience), and parts of the Transcendental Dialectic and Transcendental Doctrine of Method. Kern also inventories secondary literature Husserl likely read in this period: Ueberweg's *Grundriss*, Mellin's encyclopedia, Vaihinger's commentary, Cohen's *Kants Theorie der Erfahrung*, Stadler's *Die Grundsätze der reinen Erkenntnistheorie*, and Steckelmacher's work on formal logic in Kant. These secondary works were probably quite significant for Husserl and contributed to his turn away from a merely psychological Kant-interpretation and his connection to Kant's idea of pure logic.

### **Chapter 2: The Breakthrough to Transcendental Phenomenology (From the *Logical Investigations* to the *Ideas*)**

#### **§ 4\. The Period Immediately Before the Discovery of the Phenomenological Reduction**

The opening paragraph establishes that in this period Husserl achieves his breakthrough to transcendental phenomenology via the transcendental-phenomenological reduction. The concept of pure consciousness and the universal problem of the constitution of objects presenting themselves to this consciousness move to the center of his thought. The breakthrough has been dated to between 1905 and 1907\. Some interpreters greeted or lamented this turn as an ascent or fall into Kantian idealism. Certain formulations of Husserl from after the breakthrough do recall criticist philosophy, making it important to examine the nature of his relationship to Kant and neo-Kantianism in those decisive years.

The next paragraph provides context about Husserl's move from Halle to Göttingen in 1901\. He already possessed a far more positive relationship to Kant and neo-Kantianism than at the beginning of his Halle period. He knew most of Kant's writings from personal reading, was in lively exchange with Natorp, and had come into contact with several other Kantians. At Göttingen, the influence of neo-Kantianism was less than at Halle. Husserl's relationship to Kant remains essentially the same as at the end of the Halle years; relations with Natorp seem to strengthen. He continues holding lectures and seminars on Kant, now including practical philosophy. Three surviving lecture fragments from the earlier Göttingen period show a Kant-picture little different from that of the *Logical Investigations*: Kant's basic problem is presented as that of the synthesis of synthetic judgments a priori, and his solution is sharply criticized in a strongly psychological-anthropological interpretation.

A crucial paragraph argues that the introduction of the phenomenological reduction in 1907 does not appear to go back directly to a deepened engagement with Kant or Natorp. This is confirmed by the shape of the reduction in those lectures: it is primarily shaped not by a Kantian but by a Cartesian thought — the idea of beginning philosophy in something absolutely given and the epoché regarding everything that does not meet this requirement. Kant is expressly said to lack the concept of the phenomenological reduction. And yet Kant's and Natorp's philosophy is unmistakably present in these five fundamental lectures. The question of the right of cognitive pretensions and the possibility of knowledge is the starting question; the demand for a critique of reason to make metaphysics possible is the fundamental demand. Natorp's thoughts are also at work without being named: the radical opposition of two dimensions of science and the warning against a *metabasis* from one to the other.

The concluding paragraphs of §4 offer Kern's thesis about the relationship between Kant/Natorp and Husserl's 1907 turn: through a decade of moderate engagement with Kant and a more intensive engagement with Natorp, Husserl gained a certain familiarity with their thoughts; their problematic penetrated his own philosophical struggle and worked itself out — partly incognito to Husserl himself. But Husserl had great reservations about their philosophy, mainly methodological. Their philosophy did not seem to correspond to his intuitionism and his ideal of absolute science. It was under the aegis of Descartes, not Kant, that Husserl grasped the "pure consciousness." But the Kantian thoughts were at work in the background and determined the ultimate sense of the phenomenological reduction more deeply and more essentially — though also more hiddenly — than Descartes. Husserl himself always pointed to Descartes' influence for the discovery of the reduction, never to Kant's or Natorp's.

#### **§ 5\. The Years of Intensive Engagement with Kant After the Turn to Pure Phenomenology**

The opening paragraphs present Husserl's own judgment: only after he had worked out the phenomenological reduction in 1907 did he realize that his own philosophical intentions were in deep accord with Kant's. Kern cites three important letters: to Rickert (1915), Natorp (1918), and Cassirer (1925), in which Husserl describes how his own development, originally hostile to Kant, led him by its own inner logic into "idealist terrain" where Kant became accessible and the deeper meaning of German idealism became comprehensible. Husserl insists that the idealist philosophy offered him no teacher, and that his own path through the medium of a (perhaps modified) Platonism led to heights from which Kant suddenly became accessible.

The next paragraphs document the abundant evidence that immediately after the decisive 1907 lectures, Husserl engaged far more intensively with Kant than before. A diary entry from March 1908 notes that he learned through deepened engagement with Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason*. A large number of research manuscripts from 1907–1909 deal with Kant's question about the possibility of science, the relationship of his transcendental-logical method to the phenomenological, the sense of the a priori, the distinction between analytic and synthetic, the division of transcendental aesthetic and analytic, Kant's concept of synthesis, the proofs of the principles, and the general task of searching for the phenomenological in Kant's critique of reason. Husserl also read secondary literature on Kant in these years, notably Wernicke's work on the thing in itself (which strongly shaped Husserl's own Kant-interpretation in a realistic direction) and Ewald's work on Kant's methodology. In 1908, Husserl adopted the Kantian term "transcendental" to designate his phenomenology.

Further paragraphs document the intensification of Husserl's relations to neo-Kantianism: his twice-unsuccessful attempt to have Natorp (or Cassirer) appointed to Göttingen, his growing engagement with Natorp's psychology and method (including the 1909 study of Natorp's *Introduction to Psychology*), and the beginning of more regular correspondence with Rickert from 1910\. Kern also notes Husserl's study of Rickert's fundamental works and Jonas Cohn's epistemology. The *Ideas I* (1913) reflects this engagement: in §62, Husserl praises phenomenology as "the secret longing of the whole of modern philosophy," assigning Kant a distinguished role among its forerunners — "Kant's spiritual gaze rested on this field" — while immediately noting Kant's methodological shortcomings.

### **Chapter 3: The Period of Genetic Phenomenology**

#### **§ 6\. The World War Period as a Time of Husserl's Worldview Alliance with German Idealism**

The first paragraph establishes the external circumstances: Husserl's move to Freiburg (1916), where he succeeded Rickert, who himself had supported Husserl's appointment. In Freiburg, Husserl found a philosophical milieu strongly influenced by Rickert, including Jonas Cohn, Richard Kroner, Georg Mehlis, and the newly habilitated Martin Heidegger.

The war presented Husserl with a new philosophical task. He felt the need for a philosophical worldview that could serve as a source of spiritual resistance and a means of spiritual overcoming for the German people. His own philosophy was poorly suited for this practical purpose — still in its foundations and unable to directly inspire a wider public. The war also made Husserl feel the necessity of spiritual unity with his philosophical fellow citizens and the philosophical tradition of Germany. In this situation he turned increasingly to the bearers of Germany's great idealistic tradition, since his own development had already brought him close and awakened in him a lively feeling of inner kinship with Kant and certain neo-Kantian currents.

Kern then documents this worldview-based alliance with German idealism through Husserl's correspondence with neo-Kantians. The 1915 letter to Rickert declares that Husserl feels closely bound with the leaders of the German idealist schools as allies against naturalism. The 1918 letter to Natorp goes even further, expressing deep agreement with Natorp's worldview of the development of spirit and God's unfolding in subjectivity.

The three Fichte lectures (November 1917/January 1918\) receive detailed treatment. Fichte is presented as the "philosopher of the Wars of Liberation," a practical reformer and educator of humanity. Husserl endorses Fichte's fundamental ideas — the primacy of the active ego over the world of objects, the theological and ethical thoughts — but rejects Fichte's concrete theoretical foundations as "constructions of hardly tolerable violence." Husserl's acquaintance with Fichte's work was quite substantial for the theological and ethical writings, but very limited for the *Wissenschaftslehre* itself.

Kern then assesses Husserl's knowledge of other post-Kantian idealists. Of Hegel, Husserl knew little more than the prefaces to the *Encyclopedia* and the *Phenomenology of Spirit* and some chapters of the *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*. Schelling was completely ignored — Husserl told Jaspers in 1913 that it was embarrassing to be compared with Schelling, who was "not a seriously to be taken philosopher at all."

The section closes with observations about Husserl's continuing intensive engagement with Kant himself during this period: the 1917 Kant studies, the last special Kant lecture (1917), the long surviving text from the 1916 introductory lecture, and the broader significance of the war years for Husserl's sense of standing in a philosophical tradition — a dramatic contrast with the ahistorical spirit of the 1911 *Logos* article.

#### **§ 7\. The Last Decade of Husserl's Academic Teaching**

The opening paragraph establishes that immediately after the war, years of most intensive productivity set in. Husserl systematically drafted the idea of genetic phenomenology. During this time his relationship to Kant and neo-Kantianism underwent yet another change, again in a positive direction. In a 1925 letter to Cassirer, Husserl writes that "only truly in the most recent years" can he "receive rich instruction from Kant and the Kantians."

The next paragraph identifies the influence from the Kantian side as of enormous significance for Husserl's development from static to genetic phenomenology. In August and September 1918, Husserl had systematically studied Natorp's essay "Philosophy and Psychology" and his *General Psychology*. In this work he found the distinction between static phenomenology and genetic psychology (in Natorp's sense), which became extraordinarily important for him. Husserl also continued studying the *Critique of Pure Reason* in these years, coming to the view that Kant's doctrine of synthesis should be interpreted in a genetic-constitutive sense. Kant's concept of "transcendental apperception" now gained a deeper significance for Husserl.

Kern then surveys the lectures of this period. No more special Kant lectures, but extended discussions of Kant in other lectures, especially the very important *Nature and Spirit* (1927), in which Husserl subjects Rickert's methodology to detailed critique, constantly appealing to Kant. In no other context does such a positive Kant-relationship come to expression as in this lecture, where the life-world problematic is first systematically developed. Kant's transcendental discussions of the possibility of experience are here valued as genuine — if still imperfect — transcendental-phenomenological researches that even contain the "bracketing" with respect to natural-objective experience.

The essay "Kant and the Idea of Transcendental Philosophy" (1924) receives careful analysis. Husserl originally intended it for the *Kantstudien* but pulled back, possibly to avoid classification as a "neo-Kantian." The essay attempts to phenomenologically re-enact the Copernican turn to transcendental idealism, praises Kant's doctrine of synthesis as a discovery of intentional connections, and proclaims transcendental phenomenology as an attempt to make the deepest sense of Kantian philosophizing true. Yet it also criticizes Kant's lacking radicalism, his "metaphysical" doctrines, and his lack of a developed method of consciousness-analysis.

#### **§ 8\. Husserl's Late Works**

The opening paragraphs sketch Husserl's post-retirement (1928) work on major publications — none brought to satisfactory completion. *Formal and Transcendental Logic* (1929) grew out of the introduction to a planned systematic work. The *Cartesian Meditations* (1931) arose from the Paris lectures of 1929\. Husserl worked on an expanded German version in collaboration with Eugen Fink until 1932, then abandoned it due to inner dissatisfaction with the Cartesian approach. Since 1935, prompted by lectures in Vienna and Prague, he worked on the *Crisis*.

Kern notes that during these last years Husserl was entirely absorbed with his own thoughts, barely reading other authors and living an ever more solitary life — solitude deepened by his successor Heidegger's dominance of the Freiburg scene, the anti-Jewish regime, and the emigration of his Kantian correspondents.

The discussion of *Formal and Transcendental Logic* shows it containing various references to Kant: Husserl expressly connects his own concept of the analytic with Kant's, appeals to Kant's concept of transcendental aesthetic, and acknowledges constitutive analyses of scientific nature in Kant while critically noting the absence of a constitutive problematic for pre-scientific nature and formal logic.

The *Cartesian Meditations*, shaped by a Cartesian approach with no relationship to Kantian thinking, mention Kant very little.

The *Crisis*, by contrast, stands in a particularly close relationship to Kant. Husserl's departure from Descartes here is connected with an express connection to Kant. This is no accident: Descartes and Kant are the two constant, diametrically opposed poles in whose field of tension Husserl struggles to grasp the sense of his phenomenological reduction. Kern provides a detailed analysis of the *Crisis*'s structure: the historical parts based on the Vienna and Prague lectures, the life-world section (Section A of Part III), and Husserl's planned but unexecuted systematic Kant-critique. The *Crisis* presents Kant as discoverer of the new transcendental dimension — the subjectivity constituting the sensible world — while showing that this great discovery was only a pre-discovery, not a genuine taking-possession, because of Kant's lack of an intuitively demonstrating method and his presupposition of the life-world. Kern concludes that it would not be arbitrary to call the *Crisis*, in contrast to the *Cartesian Meditations*, "Kantian Meditations."

The introductory remarks to Part 2, Division 1 explain the division of the systematic presentation into a chapter on Husserl's Kant-critique and five chapters on the positive aspects of Husserl's Kant-relationship. This separation is partly artificial since the two moments are closely bound in Husserl's thinking, but it is also motivated by the structure of Husserl's Kant-relationship itself: Husserl found in Kant questions and truths of the highest value but saw them veiled or even falsified by false prejudices, insufficiently clarified presuppositions, and methodological deficiencies. The systematic presentation follows the inner movement of Husserl's philosophy: positive logic and ontology (Chapter 2), radical grounding and critique of objective a priori (Chapter 3), the transcendental-phenomenological reduction (Chapter 4), constitution (Chapter 5), metaphysical consequences (Chapter 6), and Kant's place in the teleological history of philosophy (Chapter 7).

## **Part 2: Systematic Presentation of Husserl's Relationship to Kant and to Neo-Kantianism**

### **Division 1: Husserl's Relationship to Kant**

### **Chapter 1: Husserl's Kant-Critique**

#### **§ 9\. Critique of Some False Prejudices of Kant**

The introductory paragraph announces that this first section of the systematic presentation of Husserl's Kant-critique will address critical discussions that concern certain false prejudices of Kantian philosophizing — false conceptions that Kant, as a "child of his time," brought into his questioning, his methodological procedure, and his problem-solution without having radically examined them first.

**(a) Kant Lacks the Genuine Concept of the A Priori (Kant's Anti-Platonic and Formal-Rationalistic Prejudice)**

The first paragraph establishes that Husserl's concept of the a priori is grounded in his doctrine of the eidos. Kern quotes *Formal and Transcendental Logic*: the concept eidos "defines the only one among the concepts of the ambiguous expression 'a priori' that we philosophically acknowledge." Under "eidos" Husserl understands the universal essence grasped through ideation (universal intuition). Every individual stands under a hierarchy of essences of various levels of generality (through generalization); on the other hand, taken merely formally (as a something-in-general), it falls under analytically-formal essences that relate to the material essences not through generalization but through formalization. As a priori Husserl designates the necessary relationships holding between universal essences, i.e., essence-laws, and a priori cognition is cognition of such laws. This cognition can take two forms: as judgment about the universal essence, and as judgment about possible individuals as instances of the universal essence.

The next paragraph reports that Husserl highlighted in nearly every Kant-critique that Kant missed this "Platonic" concept of the a priori as essence-law grasped in universal intuition. Husserl saw Kant as under the influence of the anti-Platonism widespread in his era.

Kern then asks how Husserl assessed Kant's concept of the a priori. Insofar as the Kantian concept merely negatively signifies logical priority or independence from empirical experience (as experience of individual existence), it is taken up by Husserl into his own concept — hence the identity of the terms — even though this independence does not have entirely the same sense in Kant and Husserl. For Husserl, essence-cognition is independent of experience insofar as it is equally possible to start from experiencing intuitions or from "non-experiencing, non-existence-grasping" (merely imagining) intuitions, so that the grasping of essences "does not imply the slightest positing of any individual existence." However, the sense of the apriority of the Kantian a priori vis-à-vis experience is not exhausted by this existential independence. For Kant, apriority also and above all means logical independence from all particular "material" or content that experience provides — independence from all content whatsoever, since for Kant only experience can originally provide content. Therefore for Kant a material a priori is a *contradictio in adjecto*.

This is by no means the case for Husserl, Kern explains. For Husserl there is a material a priori: the laws that determine the relations between material essences and are grounded in their material content itself. From the phenomenological-static standpoint, the material essence is not bound to empirical experience; the individual exemplar underlying all essence-grasping can be presented through phantasy. But from the phenomenological-genetic standpoint, individual perception as the most originary cognition constitutes the origin of essence-cognition.

Kern then traces Husserl's critique of Kant's determination of the synthetic a priori. Husserl calls the material a priori, taking up a Kantian term, the "synthetic." He does not charge Kant with failing to recognize the material a priori altogether — for the Kantian synthetic judgments a priori do represent such a thing — but only with failing to correctly determine its scope and essence. Husserl accuses Kant of the formal-rationalistic prejudice: coming from Wolff, Kant regarded only analytic cognition as genuinely rational. Husserl acknowledges Kant's insight that synthetic judgments a priori possess evidence, even going so far as to present his own concept of ideation as "the phenomenologically clarified concept of Kant's pure intuition." But Husserl could have seen that his concept of ideation as a universal-consciousness is fundamentally different from Kant's "pure intuition," which does not relate to universal ideal objects but is exclusively the a priori basis of intuition of "real" time and space.

Kant's characterization of synthetic judgments a priori through necessity and unconditional universality is acceptable to Husserl, since these characters mark essence-laws. But Husserl sees a contradiction when Kant relativizes these universal and unconditionally necessary judgments to the human being. Kern quotes from *First Philosophy*: the character of universality and necessity points to absolute evidence, but then we see that Kant does not mean this — that the a priori lawfulness through which transcendental subjectivity shapes objectivity has only the significance of a general anthropological fact. Because synthetic judgments a priori mean necessary and universally valid essence-laws, they are for Husserl not relativizable to a factual subject but unconditionally valid, even for God. Husserl charges Kant with treating universality and necessity as "symptoms" of origin in the pure human subject, failing to see that universality and necessity, as grounded in the objective content of these judgments, make any relativization to a particular subject absurd.

Kern reports Husserl's view that Kant's disastrous interpretation of Hume's "relations of ideas" — falsely identifying them with his concept of analytic cognition — contributed to this error, causing Kant to miss what is significant between Hume's lines: the genuine a priori, i.e., the general formal (analytic) and material (synthetic) relations grounded in the universal essences of formal and material concepts and capable of being grasped in evidence.

The section closes with Husserl's critique of Kant's concrete execution of the analytic/synthetic distinction. Husserl considered mathematics (arithmetic, set theory, formal theory of manifolds) as belonging to the analytic domain, while geometry, chronology, phoronomy etc. belong to the synthetic. Kant's classification of arithmetic as synthetic introduced "a great confusion into the task, which was in itself discerned and tremendous, of a transcendental logic."

**(b) Kant Separates Sensibility and Understanding Too Sharply**

Kern opens by reporting Husserl's repeated charge that Kant opposes sensibility and understanding "in a harsh manner." For Husserl, sensibility and understanding/reason are not strictly separated but flow into one another; indeed, they are not two different domains but two aspects of a deeper unity.

This did not yet hold for the *Logical Investigations*, where the sphere of "sensibility" (simple or monothetic intuition, including all simple thing-experience) stands strictly delimited against the sphere of "understanding" (founded, synthetic, or categorial acts). But from about 1909 Husserl uses the terms "reason" and "understanding" also for simple thing-experience, which he always already thought of as sense-apprehension rather than as empiricist succession of sensations. He now speaks of "experiencing reason" in which the objects of all regions constitute themselves.

Kern explains that for Husserl the distinction between activity/spontaneity and passivity/receptivity is only relative. Spontaneity is always interwoven with receptivity; receptivity is itself the lowest level of spontaneity. The real things of simple experience constitute themselves in receptivity, in sensibility; but in this receptivity the spontaneity of sensuous apprehension already functions. Conversely, higher acts of reason produce a possession toward which the subject then behaves passively — Husserl speaks of "reason that has sunk down into sensibility" or "secondary sensibility." "Absolute" sensibility is for him only primal sensibility, the primal hyle, ultimately a mere limiting concept. The distinction of activity and passivity is not rigid but must be drawn anew for each concrete analytical situation.

Kern acknowledges that Husserl was aware that Kant's concept of the imagination — both sensible and intellectual, whose synthesis intuitively forms the phenomenal world — overcomes the sharp division. But Husserl's objection retains its target: the radical division of sensibility and understanding persists in Kant as the correlate of the metaphysical opposition of *mundus sensibilis* and *mundus intelligibilis*. Husserl knows no such reference to an intelligible world separated from the sensible world. He does recognize an ideal world (*mundus intelligibilis*), but in a completely different sense than Kant: the world of the ideal, clearly to be distinguished from the real world of perception, but always referred back to it and founded in it. Husserl thus does not share Kant's concept of pure reason as totally separated from sensibility. When Husserl speaks of "pure reason," he means essence-cognition that encompasses even the domain of sensible contents. He can thus formulate the paradoxical (for Kantian ears) sentence: "For every sensibility there are a priori laws; every sort of sensibility is a sphere of genuine rationality, of pure reason in the proper sense."

**(c) Kant Confuses Noesis and Noema and Neglects a Differentiated Study of the Noetic Side of Consciousness**

Kern reports that in the eighth chapter of the *Prolegomena* Husserl identifies the fundamental error of psychologism as the confusion of the objective and the experiences directed toward it. He raises this charge against Kant as well, both where he combats Kant's psychologism and more broadly where he discusses Kantian transcendental arguments. For Husserl, the distinction between intentional experience and objective sense constitutes the fundamental structure of phenomenological research.

In a study on Kant's space-arguments, Husserl observes that under the title "pure intuition" Kant does not distinguish pure intuiting (the giving consciousness of ideation) from the idea of space itself. After declaring that space is the essence-form of the thing itself, apart from the subjective conditions of intuiting, Husserl notes the constant confusion between the experience of appearing and the appearing-as-such, where on the latter side various objectivities come into question.

The same critique is raised regarding the Transcendental Analytic. After discussing the proof-style of the "Analogies of Experience," Husserl notes the lack of a clear distinction between intuitions and what is intuited, between forms of appearances as modes of consciousness and forms of the appearing objectivity — and that this is why the transcendental deduction becomes so confused and the transcendental apperception harbors so many mysteries.

Kern explains that Husserl saw in Kant's failure to distinguish clearly between noetic and noematic and in the consequent one-sided orientation toward the noematic the influence of the rationalist Wolff. Kant remained ontologically interested even in the transcendental attitude, focusing almost exclusively on meaning-structures and truth-forms. He held a systematic, concretely intuitive study of the accomplishing subjectivity and its consciousness-functions to be dispensable for his problematic. Though Kant made first deep glances into the a priori of sense-giving conscious life — especially under the title "subjective deduction" — he did not recognize that transcendental philosophy cannot be narrowed as he believed, and that its radically scientific execution requires the full, concrete consciousness-life to be subjected to study. A transcendental logic is possible only within a transcendental noetics.

#### **§ 10\. Critique of Kant's Fundamental Problematic**

**(a) Kant Does Not Advance to the Radical Problem of Knowledge; His Critique of Reason Contains "Dogmatic" Presuppositions Deriving from the "Natural" View of the World**

The opening paragraph presents what is perhaps Kern's most important developmental thesis about Husserl's Kant-interpretation: Husserl's interpretation of Kant's fundamental problematic changed very strongly over the years. This change consisted in Husserl's growing clarity that the Kantian basic problem is not a fixed problem given prior to the *Critique of Reason* to which the critique simply provides the answer, but rather that it is in flux — undergoing a radical transformation of sense through the critique itself. Husserl increasingly adopted the hermeneutic stance that the fundamental problem of a historical philosophy must not be read off "the first page" of the relevant works, but is the one that emerges in the course of the investigations, since the fundamental task of philosophizing consists precisely in correctly posing one's questions.

Kern then presents Husserl's first, early interpretation of Kant's basic problem: How can the human being make a priori synthetic judgments that hold for objects that exist in themselves outside his representations? This interpretation Husserl soon regarded as "quite inadequate" — not that he declared it directly false, but he saw that Kant penetrated into problem-dimensions exceeding such a question. Yet even in 1917 Husserl could still write that by reading the letter to Marcus Herz and comparing it with the second-edition development and all the elaborations of the work, one becomes convinced that Kant "remained permanently distant from the radical problem of knowledge" and "constantly operates with" natural-worldly presuppositions.

The subsequent paragraphs develop at great length Husserl's critique of the presuppositions embedded in this first interpretation: the critique of the presupposition of a "natural" (psychological) concept of the subject and the critique of a "natural" and "dogmatic" concept of the object. Husserl argues that Kant's Vernunftkritik, as interpreted through the early problematic, presupposes a worldly, psychologically conceived subject trying to reach objects "outside" it — presuppositions that belong to the "natural attitude" and should have been suspended.

Kern then traces Husserl's second, more mature interpretation of the Kantian fundamental problem: What are the a priori, objective and subjective conditions of the possibility of the constitution of natural-scientific objectivity in subjectivity? Here Kant's problem of knowledge becomes an "immanent" one, no longer concerned with bridging a gap between subject and object but with understanding the constitutional achievements of subjectivity. Husserl even credits Kant with an implicit "bracketing" of natural science.

**(b) Kant's Problematic of Knowledge Is Too High-Level; Deeper-Lying Problems Must Be Developed Prior to It**

Kern explains that Husserl charged Kant's problem of the constitution of scientific nature with presupposing a constitutive problematic of the life-world that Kant never thematized. Further, Kant's problem presupposes a transcendental elucidation of formal logic that he also failed to undertake. And the constitution of inner time-consciousness represents the deepest problematic of all, one that Kant never reached.

**(c) Kant's Problematic Is Too Narrow**

Husserl faults Kant for lacking a critique of knowledge of the spiritual world, for narrowing the concept of world-experience, and (in a third interpretation of the Kantian basic problem) for failing to pose the problem of universal ontological knowledge of the world.

**(d) Kant Fails to Subject His Transcendental Philosophy to a Transcendental Self-Critique**

Husserl argues that Kant never turned his critical method reflexively upon his own transcendental philosophy, never asked about the conditions of possibility of the critique of reason itself.

#### **§ 11\. Husserl's Kant-Critique from the Standpoint of Transcendental-Philosophical Method**

Kern emphasizes at the outset that the question of method is the point at which Husserl perceived the strongest divergences between Kantian and his own philosophy. These divergences he regarded as principled, and when he explained why his phenomenological philosophy could not be neo-Kantian, he pointed to methodological divergences.

**(a) Kant Lacks the Concept of the Phenomenological Reduction**

This critique appears already in the first text where Husserl systematically develops his idea of the reduction: the 1907 Five Lectures. Husserl never dropped it, even where he acknowledges that Kant's investigations operate on the transcendental-phenomenological level. For the transcendental level of Kant's critique is only a "factual" one — never methodically achieved, never secured against alien intrusions from the natural-objective worldview. Kern quotes the key passage from the essay on Kant: Kant's thinking *de facto* moves within the framework of phenomenological attitude, and the force of his genuinely transcendental theories rests on pure intuitions drawn from original sources. But it makes an essential difference whether one naively theorizes in phenomenological attitude or achieves principled clarity about its essence.

**(b) Critique of Kant's Regressive-Constructive Method; Kant Lacks an Intuitively Demonstrating Method**

This is the most extensive sub-section of §11. Kern first notes a positive use of the terms "regression" and "construction" by Husserl, then turns to the critique of regressive construction as an unintuitive devising of explanatory "principles." Husserl's "principle of all principles" demands that everything that presents itself in intuition be accepted as it gives itself. Husserl's "positivism" means accepting only what is originally given and refusing to go behind or beyond what intuition delivers.

Kern traces Husserl's complex relationship to Kant's own demand for a "deduction" of evident synthetic a priori propositions, his appropriation of the Kantian formula "concepts without intuition are empty," and the mathematical sources of Husserl's intuitionism (the Weierstrass ideal of rigor applied to philosophy). Husserl connected intuitionism with intellectual honesty: philosophy must not build on constructions but on what can be shown.

The critique of Kant's "constructive method" is extensive: Kant's transcendental philosophy constructs hypothetical explanatory principles rather than demonstrating them in intuitive evidence. Kant's lack of intentional analysis, the rationalist and theological origins of his constructive method, and the blame of empiricist psychology for Kant's "constructions" are all explored. Ultimately, the lack of the phenomenological reduction is identified as the deepest reason for Kant's overlooking the possibility of a transcendental experience.

An excursus compares Kant's and Husserl's concepts of reason.

**(c) Kant's Transcendental Philosophy Lacks the Eidetic Method**

Kern briefly presents Husserl's charge that Kant failed to develop the method of eidetic variation as a systematic procedure for arriving at essence-laws, relying instead on the merely regressive-formal procedure of seeking conditions of possibility.

#### **§ 12\. Critique of Kant's "Solution" to His Problem of Reason**

**(a) Critique of Kantian Psychologism**

This critique is bound to Husserl's early Kant-interpretation and to texts before 1910\. It demonstrates the absurdity of tracing a priori necessary laws back to an empirical-psychological mechanism. Husserl argues against Kant's confusion of objective necessity and subjective compulsion (*Nötigung*): the objective a priori necessity of essence-laws cannot be derived from any psychological mechanism. Ironically, Husserl uses against Kant an argumentation that Kant himself used against empirical-psychological grounding of the a priori.

**(b) Critique of Kantian Anthropologism**

This sub-section — the longest in §12 — addresses the connection between psychologism and anthropologism in Kant. Kern shows that Husserl overlooked the unity of Kant's "thing in itself" and "intellectus archetypus." Husserl consistently interpreted Kant's thing in itself realistically (as an entity existing behind appearances), criticizing it on the ground of the necessary correlation of being and consciousness, as the indicated but veiled substrate of perceptual appearances, and as the unknown cause of appearances. Kern argues that Husserl's critique rests on a misinterpretation: Husserl fails to see that for Kant the thing in itself is not a second entity behind appearances but the same object considered apart from its relation to our sensibility.

Husserl's critique of the *intellectus archetypus* — the idea of a non-sensible, creative intuition — is presented and shown also to rest on a misinterpretation. Husserl denies the possibility of an essentially different kind of cognition and truth, and consequently does not pose the problem of analogy. Kern traces this to Husserl's rationalism: his conviction that all being is in principle knowable to finite, discursive reason. The section concludes with a comparison of the concepts and conditions of the possible in Kant and Husserl.

### **Chapter 2: Husserl's Relationship to Kant's Idea of Logic**

#### **§ 13\. Husserl's Adoption of the Kantian Distinction Between Analytic and Synthetic A Priori**

Despite rejecting Kant's concrete execution of the analytic/synthetic distinction, Husserl saw in this principled distinction a fundamental insight for his own conception of logic and ontology. Kern quotes Husserl: "Kant was the first to discern the demarcation here, but could not bring it to ultimate principled transparency." In Kant's contrast between a formal logic determined by the analytic a priori and a transcendental logic determined by the synthetic a priori, Husserl saw his own distinction between merely formal ontology and material logic or material ontology anticipated. He therefore used the Kantian terms: "Analytik" for formal disciplines, "Synthetik" or "transcendental logic" for the a priori material disciplines.

Kern traces Husserl's lifelong effort to determine and delimit the concept of analyticity, culminating in *Formal and Transcendental Logic*, where the analytic is defined through the lawfulness of the domain of "mere senses" grasped in the evidence of distinctness. The 1927 Kant-exercises, devoted to investigating the concept of the analytic, already reached the basic determination later set forth in that work.

The section then explores in detail how Husserl's concept of the analytic both appropriates and transforms the Kantian one, and how the second, "Kantian" concept of the a priori (independence from all content) plays a fundamental role for Husserl's critique of reason and phenomenology of constitution — a role distinct from but complementary to the first, "Platonic" concept.

#### **§ 14\. Husserl's Adoption of Kant's Idea of the *Natura Formaliter Spectata***

Kern shows how Husserl took over from Kant the idea of a formal ontology of nature that provides the principles for empirical natural science. The Kantian principles and constitutive forms — space, time, substantiality, causality — are each examined in their appropriation and transformation by Husserl. Space: Husserl accepts Kant's thesis that space is an a priori form of appearing things but rejects the subjectivization; space is essence-form of the thing itself. Time: similarly accepted as a priori form but conceived as the deepest dimension of constitution. Substantiality: Husserl takes over the idea that substance-identity is constitutive of objectivity. Causality: the causal principle is accepted as a priori condition of nature-experience.

#### **§ 15\. Husserl and Kant's Thought of the Transcendental Deduction of the A Priori**

This long and important section addresses what one might least expect: Husserl's intensive engagement with Kant's transcendental deduction. The Kantian problem of deduction seems initially foreign to Husserl's eidetic and intentional-analytic thinking. Yet Husserl frequently refers to it in research manuscripts and lectures.

Kern grounds the exposition primarily in the 1927 lecture *Nature and Spirit*, which asks about the method of gaining and grounding the various a priori sciences and which criticizes Rickert's transcendental deduction as a "formalistic pseudo-deduction." Against Rickert, Husserl sets his own method of "transcendental deduction" and relates it to Kant's.

Kern distinguishes two ways or "paths" of the transcendental deduction: the way "from above" (starting from the idea of objectivity and asking what subjective conditions make it possible — the Kantian regressive path) and the way "from below" (starting from the analysis of concrete experience and working up to the constitutive conditions). Husserl holds that the way "from above" presupposes a phenomenological foundation, that the method of eidetic variation offers a better and more direct path, but that the regressive question after conditions of possibility retains its value — particularly for the systematic articulation of the a priori.

The section traces Husserl's engagement with Kant's transcendental-logical method over the years (1908–1927), the guiding viewpoints of identification and objective time-determination as transcendence, and the Kantian determination of objectivity as lawfulness. It concludes by distinguishing Husserl's and Kant's conceptions of what the transcendental deduction accomplishes: for Husserl, a method for systematically ascertaining the principles of experience; for Kant, a legitimation and limitation of the right of a priori concepts.

### **Chapter 3**

#### **§ 16\. Husserl and Kant's Demand for a Critique of Pure Reason as the Condition of the Possibility of a Scientific Metaphysics**

Husserl regarded Kant as the last of the great philosophers for whom the ideal of philosophy as a rigorous science was guiding. In the renewal of this ideal Husserl saw a reaching back to Kant. Kern quotes Husserl: Kant "wanted philosophy as rigorous science," he "believed himself to be the representative of that quite other philosophy which grounds all its theoretical and, further, its axiological and practical positions in absolute truth." And: "We see ourselves in any case in complete agreement with Kant in that we strive to realize transcendental philosophy not in the spirit of a worldview adapting to temporal needs, but in that of a rigorous science striving toward the idea of finality."

Husserl used the Kantian title "critique of reason" to designate his own philosophical main task throughout his transcendental-phenomenological period (from the years shortly after the *Logical Investigations* to the *Crisis*). Kern documents this from 1903 through 1910 and beyond, showing how Husserl's conception of a critique of all pre-critical (dogmatic) knowledge constantly echoes Kantian formulations.

Yet in contrast to Kant, Husserl never speaks of a "critique of pure reason" — because for him there is no "pure reason" in Kant's sense (reason separated from sensibility). The ultimate goal of Husserl's critique of reason is the new grounding of a scientific metaphysics.

An excursus treats Husserl's relationship to the question of being (*Seinsfrage*).

### **Chapter 4: Husserl and Kant's Return to Transcendental Subjectivity**

#### **§ 17\. Husserl's View of Kant's Path to Transcendental Subjectivity**

Kern establishes that Husserl repeatedly affirmed that Kant's research *de facto* played out on the ground of the absolute transcendental subjectivity. Not only the transcendental deduction of the first edition but all of Kant's investigations were credited with this character. Kant's path to transcendental subjectivity consisted, in Husserl's view, in the critical regression from the objective (ontological) to the subjective (phenomenological) a priori. All ontological a priori "points back" to the subjectivity and to an a priori of subjectivity as such. The great problem behind Kant's efforts regarding the theory of the synthetic a priori is to clarify the essential connections between all ontological a priori truths and the a priori truths about reason-consciousness. All ontological a priori must be made "understandable" through the phenomenological a priori of constitution.

Kern emphasizes that this Husserlian view of Kant must be seen in connection with Natorp's Kant-interpretation, which conceived philosophical psychology as constituted by a turning away from the objective direction toward subjectivity purely as such, taking its departure from "transcendental philosophy" as the most universal science of the a priori of objectivity.

#### **§ 18\. The Significance of Kant's Return to Subjectivity for Husserl's Idea of the Transcendental-Phenomenological Reduction**

This is the longest and most philosophically important section in the book. Kern identifies three "path-types" of Husserl's transcendental-phenomenological reduction and examines each in detail:

**(a) The Cartesian Way**: Starting from the idea of an absolutely given starting point and the epoché of everything that does not meet this demand. Kern traces this path through the *Logical Investigations*, a 1904 text, the Five Lectures (1907), *Ideas I*, *First Philosophy* (1923/24), and the *Cartesian Meditations*. Three fundamental deficiencies are identified: (1) the reduction appears as a *loss* (of the world) and a retreat to a *residuum* (consciousness); (2) neither intersubjectivity nor full individual subjectivity is won; (3) the path never reaches its goal of an absolute beginning of philosophy. Kern discusses Husserl's terms "immanence" and "transcendence."

**(b) The Way via Intentional Psychology**: Starting from natural psychology and attempting to purify it into transcendental phenomenology. Traced through *First Philosophy* and the *Crisis*. Its deficiency: pure consciousness appears as an "abstraction," and the way via psychology does not as such lead beyond psychology.

**(c) The Way via Ontology**: Starting from the positive sciences or ontology, recognizing the necessary reference of all objective a priori to constituting subjectivity, and thereby achieving the transcendental turn. This is the most extensively analyzed path. Kern traces it from the *Logical Investigations* through texts of 1904, 1906/07, the Five Lectures, crucial lectures of Winter 1910/11, *Ideas I*, the 1919/20 introductory lectures, the essay on Kant, *Formal and Transcendental Logic*, and the *Crisis*. He argues that this path has decisive advantages over the others: the sense of the transcendental-phenomenological reduction as it emerges on the way via ontology is the one ultimately intended by Husserl. Only from this path can one understand why Husserl can address Kant's critique of reason as a transcendental research into consciousness. Kern establishes the connection between this path and Kant's/Natorp's return to subjectivity.

#### **§ 19\. Kant's Concept of the Transcendental**

This section supplements the previous one. Kern presents Fink's contrast between the criticist and phenomenological concepts of the transcendental, then Husserl's distinction between two Kantian concepts of the transcendental: (1) "transcendental" as referring to the investigation of the possibility of a priori cognition in general — adopted by Husserl; (2) "transcendental" as referring to the dependence of knowledge on the forms of a finite, human subjectivity — rejected by Husserl as anthropologistic.

### **Chapter 5: Husserl and Kant's Doctrine of the "Nature-Forming Subjectivity"**

#### **§ 20\. Kant's Grasp of the Essential Structures of Subjectivity**

Husserl credited Kant with genuine insights into the essential structures of subjectivity: Kant's conception of consciousness as a flow, his determination of the fundamental structure of consciousness as temporality, and above all his doctrine of synthesis, which grasps the peculiar character of intentional connections. Kern shows that Husserl saw in Kant's synthesis the link between subjective accomplishment and objective constitution.

#### **§ 21\. Kant's Distinction Between Transcendental Aesthetic and Transcendental Analytic**

Husserl interpreted Kant's distinction between transcendental aesthetic and transcendental analytic as a distinction between the phantom-level and the reality-level in thing-constitution. The transcendental aesthetic lacks synthesis — which Husserl finds questionable. Three meanings of the Kantian term "transcendental aesthetic" in Husserl are presented: (1) as constitutive problematic of the phantom; (2) as constitutive problematic of the sensibly intuitive world (life-world); (3) as problematic of primordial constitution.

#### **§ 22\. Kant's "Synthesis" as Genetic Constitution of Nature**

Husserl interpreted Kant's synthesis as productive constitution of the experience-thing. From the standpoint of static phenomenology, this productivity was rejected — the constituting subjectivity merely "makes possible" but does not "produce" the object. From the standpoint of genetic phenomenology, however, this productivity was affirmed — the object is genuinely constituted in a genesis of sense-formation.

#### **§ 23\. "Synthesis" and Understanding**

Husserl interpreted Kant's synthesis as a performance of the understanding — Kant's doctrine of the doubly functioning understanding: the conceptual-scientific understanding and the understanding that, functioning hiddenness, constitutes the sensibly intuitive thing. Husserl overlooked the non-intuitiveness of the Kantian understanding. He underlined the role of intuition in Kant's conception of cognition. Kant's doctrine of the doubly functioning understanding was for Husserl a mere pre-discovery that needed to be transformed into a genuine discovery. The significance of this Kantian doctrine for the genealogy of conceptual cognition is explored.

#### **§ 24\. "Synthesis" and Sensation**

Husserl took over the Kantian dualism of "matter" and subjective "forming" into his problematic of the constitution of transcendence. Kern examines the relative and absolute difference between consciousness-forming and matter, the contingency of matter, and references Fink's explanation of these relationships.

### **Chapter 6: Husserl and Kant's Transcendental Idealism**

#### **§ 25\. Kant's Copernican Turn**

Husserl's transcendental idealism is presented as his appropriation of Kant's idealistic explanation and interpretation of the sense of being of the world. The grounds of Husserl's idealism are examined: (1) the being of the world is only being for subjectivity; (2) intentionality is genetic (the object is produced in a genesis). The first ground: being of the world is being through appearances, necessarily having a presumptive character. Spatiality is identified as the root of the ideality of the world. Transcendental idealism is the deliverance from the absolutization of the world. The relative absoluteness of transcendental subjectivity is affirmed.

#### **§ 26\. Constitution of Nature and "Transcendental Apperception"**

Husserl's doctrine of the ego is traced from the negation of the Kantian-Natorpian ego in the *Logical Investigations*, through the revision in the *Ideas*, to the ego-pole and the ego of habitualities. The Kantian ego of transcendental apperception is interpreted as the ego that has a world. The correlation of ego-unity and the constitution of a consistently maintainable world is presented. But from the ego of transcendental apperception no determinate world-ontology can be deduced — the influence of Kant and possibly Fichte is noted.

#### **§ 27\. The Facticity of World-Constitution in Husserl and the Possibility of Metaphysics**

The mere facticity of the ego of transcendental apperception is presented: the "possibility" of the dissolution of the world into a chaos, the contingency of the existence and essence of the world, the sense of the "a priori" of world-existence. The opposing standpoint of German idealism is noted: for Fichte and Hegel, the world is necessarily deducible from the absolute ego. Husserl's position: transcendental subjectivity is not the sufficient ground for the being of the world. The facticity of world-constitution serves as the point of departure for metaphysics. God as the ultimate ground of world-constitution in transcendental subjectivity. Husserl's metaphysical method. Kant's doctrine of the postulates.

### **Chapter 7**

#### **§ 28\. The Position of Kant in the Teleological Development of the History of Philosophy**

Husserl conceived the European history of philosophy as a teleological development toward transcendental phenomenology. Kern presents Husserl's conception of the history of philosophy and his interpretive method, the relationship between history of philosophy and philosophy, and a sketch of Husserl's view of the entire history: the origin of the European idea of philosophy in Plato; the origin of the European idea of culture in Socrates; the insufficiency of Plato and the falling-away of Aristotle; medieval philosophy; Descartes as father of the main currents of modern philosophical history (transcendentalism, psychologism, rationalistic and physicalistic objectivism); the psychologistic and skeptical aberrations of the transcendental motive in English empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume); the objectivistic and transcendental motif in rationalism (Spinoza, Leibniz); Kant's critique of reason as the first systematic transcendental philosophy; German idealism as a preliminary stage of phenomenological idealism; the victory of objectivistic positivism and the crisis of European humanity; the methodically newly grounded transcendental philosophy as the hope for the preservation of European humanity.

### **Division 2: Husserl's Relationship to Neo-Kantianism**

### **Section 1: Husserl's Relationship to Paul Natorp**

### **Chapter 1**

#### **§ 29\. Husserl and Natorp's Argumentation Against Psychologism**

Kern begins by noting that in the first part of Husserl's *Logical Investigations* — the *Prolegomena to Pure Logic* — the eighth chapter presents the definitive refutation of the psychologistic prejudice. The parallelism between Husserl's refutation of the fundamental psychologistic prejudice and Natorp's argumentation in the 1887 article "On Objective and Subjective Grounding of Knowledge" is examined in detail. Natorp's core argument — that psychologism constitutes a *metabasis eis allo genos* from the objective-logical to the subjective-psychological sphere — is shown to be taken over by Husserl and to play the decisive role in the *Prolegomena*.

Kern then documents the influence of Natorp on Husserl's turn away from psychologism in the years 1894/95, arguing that this influence was far more important than the usually cited Frege review. The subjective grounding of logic in Natorp and Husserl is presented as transitional to the next chapter: both philosophers recognized that the refutation of psychologism does not eliminate the legitimate question about the subjective conditions and sources of logical knowledge, but rather demands a properly conducted subjective investigation — which for Natorp is philosophical psychology and for Husserl is phenomenology.

### **Chapter 2: Husserl's Position Regarding Natorp's Psychology**

The introductory remark establishes Husserl's own view of the relationship between his phenomenology and Natorp's psychology: Husserl saw both as directed toward the same domain of investigation (the subjective accomplishments correlative to objective knowledge) but as fundamentally differing in method.

#### **§ 30\. Natural and Phenomenological Attitude and Natorp's Contrast of Objective and Subjective Directions of Knowledge**

Kern presents Natorp's determination of the formal object of psychology through the subjective direction or dimension of knowledge, and Husserl's conception of this direction or dimension as transcendental. The lack of the phenomenological reduction in Natorp is highlighted. Natorp's determination of psychology as "Last Philosophy" is contrasted with Husserl's determination of phenomenology as "First Philosophy" — but Kern shows that analysis of these opposed determinations reveals fundamental agreements.

The relationship between ontology and phenomenology in Husserl is examined. Three remaining oppositions between Natorp and Husserl are identified: (1) Natorp holds that psychology must begin with the highest objectifications, while Husserl demands prior analysis of deeper constitutive levels; (2) Natorp maintains the independence of objective science from subjective science, while Husserl increasingly emphasizes the dependence of positive ontology on phenomenology; (3) unlike Natorp, Husserl holds fast to a mundane psychology alongside transcendental phenomenology. The "symmetric" correlativity of objectivity and subjectivity in Natorp is contrasted with the absoluteness of subjectivity over objectivity in Husserl.

A supplement to §18 addresses Husserl's relationship to the Kantian-Natorpian return to subjectivity.

#### **§ 31\. The Genetic Problematic of Natorp's Psychology**

This long and crucial section examines the significance of Natorp's psychology for Husserl's conception of genetic phenomenology. Kern presents Natorp's distinction between static phenomenology and dynamic psychology of the sequence of experiential unities — a distinction that proved extraordinarily important for Husserl.

Husserl's "genetic" problematic before 1918 is examined: already present in the analysis of inner time-consciousness, the supra-temporality of time-constituting consciousness, and the relationship between consciousness and time. Husserl's distinction between static and genetic phenomenology, or static and genetic constitution, emerges around 1920\. Kern documents far-reaching parallels between Husserl's and Natorp's distinction and determination of static and dynamic (or genetic) phenomenology/psychology. The influence of Natorp on Husserl's conception of genetic phenomenology is directly argued.

Kern shows that Natorp's *General Psychology* (which Husserl studied intensively in August-September 1918\) provided the conceptual framework for the static/genetic distinction. Natorp conceived the dynamic aspect as a "reconstruction" proceeding in the reverse direction from the objective, constituted to the subjective, constituting — from the highest to the lowest levels. Husserl adopted this schema while transforming it phenomenologically: for him, genetic constitution traces the actual history of sense-formation in passive and active synthesis.

#### **§ 32\. Critique of Natorp's Concept of Subjectivity**

Natorp rejected the distinction between content of consciousness and act of consciousness. He conceived the sole graspable expression of consciousness as the connection of consciousness-contents. Husserl found lacking in Natorp a distinction between noema, noesis, and sensation. Natorp's failure to recognize a special noetics (in the narrow sense) is presented.

However, Kern makes a striking observation: Husserl's actually practiced consciousness-research corresponds to a large extent to Natorp's position — the constructive character of Husserl's own noetics is acknowledged. Natorp's influence on Husserl's inclusion of the noema within phenomenology is noted, as is Natorp's influence on Husserl's doctrine of the ego-pole. Husserl's critique of Natorp's thesis of the intuitive ungraspability of the ego and the ego-reference is presented alongside Husserl's own acknowledgment of the intuitive ungraspability of the most originary ego.

#### **§ 33\. Critique of Natorp's Psychological Method**

Divergences between Natorp's and Husserl's determination of the method of psychology/phenomenology are presented. Natorp's reconstructive-inferential method is described: since consciousness as such is never directly given but always only objectified consciousness, the path to consciousness must be one of reconstruction or inference proceeding backward from objectification to its subjective source. Husserl's critique of Natorp's method is presented: phenomenological consciousness-research must be intuitive, not constructive or inferential.

But Kern then introduces an important qualification: the constructive elements in Husserl's own static phenomenology, the constructive character of Husserl's genetic phenomenology, and Husserl's use of Natorp's concept of "reconstruction" for the research into the primal constitution of the ego. The upshot is that the methodological opposition between Husserl and Natorp is less absolute than it first appears: Husserl's own practice, particularly in genetic phenomenology and the theory of primal constitution, involves constructive and reconstructive elements that bring him closer to Natorp's position than his official methodology would suggest.

### **Section 2: Husserl's Relationship to Heinrich Rickert**

Introductory remarks establish that Husserl's relationship to Rickert was less intensive and less productive than his relationship to Natorp. Rickert was the leading representative of the Southwest German (or Baden) school of neo-Kantianism, whose central concern was the philosophy of values and the methodology of the sciences. Husserl engaged critically with Rickert primarily in the area of epistemology and methodology of the sciences.

### **Chapter 1: Husserl's Critique of Rickert's Epistemology**

Introductory remarks note that Husserl's critique of Rickert's epistemology focuses on three main points: the presuppositions of the problem, the conception of the problem as a form-problem, and the determination of the transcendent object.

#### **§ 34\. Critique of Rickert's Presuppositions in Posing the Problem of the Object of Knowledge**

Rickert's determination of the task of epistemology is presented: epistemology must clarify how knowledge can reach a transcendent object. Husserl's critique addresses the presuppositions Rickert demands for epistemology: (1) epistemology should presuppose not an actual piece of knowledge but only the *idea* of knowledge; (2) it should presuppose this idea only implicitly and not uncritically adopt a given idea of knowledge or truth. Kern documents Husserl's misinterpretation of Rickert's presuppositions, then presents Rickert's rejection of the pure immanence-standpoint and Husserl's demand for a purely immanent epistemology. Husserl's rejection of the "real" immanence-standpoint (as opposed to "intentional" immanence) is noted, and certain unrecognized agreements between Husserl and Rickert are identified.

#### **§ 35\. Critique of Rickert's Conception of the Problem of Knowledge as a Form-Problem**

Rickert's formal epistemological problematic is presented. Husserl misunderstands Rickert's problem as a formal-analytic one. But an expanded concept of form allows Husserl to acknowledge the Rickertian problem-formulation. This short section shows how what initially appears as a deep disagreement between Husserl and Rickert partly dissolves when the concept of "form" is appropriately broadened.

#### **§ 36\. Critique of Rickert's Determination of the Transcendent Object**

Rickert's determination of knowing as an affirming or denying that is guided by a transcendent ought (*Sollen*) on the basis of a feeling of evidence is presented. Rickert's transcendental-logical determination of the transcendent object as a transcendent value is examined. Husserl's critique of Rickert's conception of judgment-sense as a value is presented: judgment-sense is not a value but ideal being. An approach of Husserl toward Rickert through the strict distinction between meaning and species within the domain of ideal being is noted. The relationship of being and value is addressed.

Husserl's critique of Rickert's determination of the actual cognitive act as an affirming or denying is presented: affirming and denying are mere modifications of a primal *doxa* that constitutes the fundamental character of every judgment. In contrast to Rickert, for Husserl the actual being-constituting act is not a theoretical one. Husserl's critique of Rickert's conception that knowledge is guided by a transcendent ought is examined: the ought belongs not to the theoretical but to the practical sphere. Evidence as the source of the right of knowledge is presented, along with critique of Rickert's concept of evidence and Rickert's emotionalism.

### **Chapter 2**

#### **§ 37\. Critique of Rickert's Methodology**

This final and very extensive section examines Husserl's critique of Rickert's philosophy of science — specifically the methodology presented in *The Limits of Natural-Scientific Concept-Formation*. Kern provides a substantial preliminary exposition of Rickert's methodology: his conscious operation on the ground of empirical realism, his opposition of the objective reality and the knowing subject as two "transcendent" instances, and his formal deduction of the methodological ideas of the natural and human sciences.

Husserl's appreciation of Rickert's methodology is noted first: Husserl valued Rickert's rejection of naturalistic methodological monism and recognized that for both Rickert and Husserl, the concept of value plays an essential role in distinguishing natural and human sciences. But for Husserl it is not the formal method of the human sciences that is determined by the value-reference but their content. The difference in the conception of the relationship between nature and spirit serves as Husserl's most fundamental criterion for distinguishing natural and human sciences.

Husserl's agreements and disagreements with Rickert on the determination of the difference through a difference of *Auffassung* (conception) are noted. Husserl's critique of Rickert's formal deduction of the natural-scientific and historical-scientific methodological ideas is then presented at length. The critique of the result of this deduction, the critique of its starting point (charges of pragmatism and realism), and Husserl's appeal to Kant against Rickert are all examined.

Husserl's implicit rejection of Rickert's distinction between the formational level of the epistemological ego and the formational level of the real ego is noted. A crucial parallel between Rickert's distinction between constitutive forms of reality and methodological forms of knowledge, on one hand, and Husserl's distinction between life-world forms and scientifically idealized forms, on the other, is identified — though this parallel is far from complete, since there is discontinuity between Rickert's two sorts of forms but continuity between Husserl's.

Husserl's middle position between Kant and Rickert is presented: like Kant and unlike Rickert, Husserl grounds the a priori of the sciences in the constituting achievements of transcendental subjectivity; but like Rickert and unlike Kant, he acknowledges the fundamental distinctness of natural-scientific and human-scientific methodology.

The section and the book conclude with Husserl's charge that scientific methodological ideas can be gained philosophically only through the return to full, concrete experience — phenomenology as "life-philosophy" — and that the structures of the life-world form the basis for the cognitive goals of the positive sciences. Rickert's "pseudo-deductions" are criticized, his naturalism exposed, and a final appeal to Kant's transcendental deduction is made: Rickert's procedure is called "a formalistic externalization of the Kantian thoughts of a transcendental deduction."

# **Lacan, *Seminar II: The Ego in Freud's Theory and in the Technique of Psychoanalysis* (1954–1955)**

## **Chapter-by-Chapter Analytical Commentary**

This commentary works through the twenty-four sessions of Lacan's second seminar, delivered at the Sainte-Anne hospital in 1954–1955 and edited by Jacques-Alain Miller (Seuil, 1978). Each session is treated as a unit of argument: I reconstruct what Lacan is doing, identify the philosophical and psychoanalytic interlocutors he summons, track the conceptual moves that prepare later developments (the symbolic order, the big Other, the schéma L), and indicate where the session is positioned within the larger architecture of the seminar. The seminar's overall trajectory runs from a polemic against ego psychology, through an extensive rereading of Freud's metapsychological turn of 1920, into a series of close readings (the *Entwurf*, the *Traumdeutung*, the Irma dream, the Purloined Letter), and culminates in the explicit thematization of the big Other and the symbolic order's structural priority over the imaginary. The pedagogical scene is a "seminar of texts," and the polemical horizon is the American ego psychology of Hartmann, Kris, and Loewenstein.

# **Introduction**

## **I. Psychology and Metapsychology**

*Truth and knowledge. The cogito of the dentists. The* je *is not the* moi\*, the subject is not the individual. The crisis of 1920.\*

The opening session is the most programmatic of the seminar. Lacan uses it both to lay out the year's thesis — that the ego (*moi*) in Freud is radically distinct from the ego of philosophy and common sense, and that the post-Freudian movement has catastrophically collapsed the two — and to install the polemical opposition that will structure everything to follow: between a Freudianism that is "Copernican" (decentering) and a Freudianism that has been recaptured by the very pre-analytic conception of selfhood it was meant to subvert. The greeting to his audience is informal, but the opening declaration is already a paradox: to define the nature of the ego, he says, leads very far away — and it is from this "very far away" that the seminar will set out, in order to return to the center, which will only carry us very far away again. This is more than a rhetorical flourish. It announces a method in which the apparent center (the *moi*) will turn out to be eccentric, displaced by something that operates from elsewhere. The structure of the year's argument is already prefigured in this opening gesture.

Lacan immediately distinguishes the analytic concept of the ego from what he calls the "pre-analytic" conception — a notion of the self elaborated over centuries by philosophers and by common consciousness alike. The danger he identifies is a kind of gravitational pull: the pre-analytic *moi* exerts an attraction on Freud's radically new functional concept, drawing it back toward familiar ground. He uses three escalating terms — *attraction*, *subduction*, *subversion* — to describe this pull, and the word "subversion" here is significant because it inverts the value he usually attaches to it (Freudian subversion is normally Lacan's positive term). What is being subverted, in this case, is Freud's own subversiveness. The pre-analytic conception is colonizing the analytic one.

This pull is what justifies treating the Freudian discovery as a "Copernican revolution." Lacan picks up the trope from his previous year's seminar on the technical writings, where he had used it to characterize the decentering of the subject. The point of the analogy is precise: just as Copernicus moved the earth from the center of the cosmos, Freud has moved the ego from the center of psychic life. That displacement is what is being reversed when the post-Freudian movement reinstalls the ego as the principal agent. The seminar of textual reading he is about to undertake, then, has a defensive purpose: to recover the displacement against the tendency to undo it.

A short methodological remark follows: the seminar will be a "seminar of texts" (*séminaire de textes*). Lacan insists that the previous year's results will be integrated into the new phase, and that the reading of Freud is the *fil conducteur* — the guiding thread. The emphasis on textuality is not innocent. The argument against ego psychology is going to be made by demonstrating that Hartmann and his school have *misread* Freud's post-1920 texts, and that the only way to recover what Freud was doing is to read those texts with attention to their actual sequence and structure.

### **Section 1**

Lacan opens the first numbered section by stating his historical thesis in its sharpest form. Freud's discoveries had the vocation of abolishing earlier conceptions of the ego, but instead, "by a thousand byways," a notion of the ego has reappeared that belongs to a pre-analytic frame and that tends toward what is openly called the "reabsorption" of analytic knowledge into general psychology. Theory and practice cannot be separated, so this theoretical regression has bent the direction of the analytic relation itself. The current state of analytic technique testifies to this.

The stakes of this regression, Lacan insists, are not merely academic. It would not be worth being moved by if it were only a conflict between schools, between the Ptolemaic and the Copernican. The deeper issue is the establishment of what he calls a "concrete complicity" between psychoanalysis — which ought to be a liberating, demystifying handling of human relations — and what he calls the "fundamental illusion" of modern man's lived experience. Here Lacan is introducing one of the seminar's most important polemical claims: that contemporary man, the man of the mid-twentieth century, lives within a certain culturally diffused, half-naive, half-elaborated idea of himself. He imagines that this self-image arises from natural inclination, but in fact it is taught from every side by the contemporary civilization. Freud's technique, in its origin, transcends this illusion. The question is whether psychoanalysis will gently let itself abandon what was briefly opened, or whether it will instead reaffirm and renew the relief of that opening.

A brief turn toward the Tuesday lectures — extra-ordinary in the literal sense — allows Lacan to fold in a reference to Alexandre Koyré's lecture on the Platonic dialogue, particularly the *Meno*. The point is the function of truth in its nascent state. Knowledge, once it is tied to truth, acquires its own inertia, and in acquiring inertia it begins to lose precisely the virtue from which it began to deposit itself. Knowledge tends to misrecognize its own sense. Nowhere is this degradation more evident, Lacan says, than in psychoanalysis itself, and this very fact reveals the elective place psychoanalysis occupies in the progress of human subjectivity.

The detour through Socrates serves a double purpose. First, it lets Lacan articulate the strange topology of *aretē*: the moment Socrates inaugurates the new "being-in-the-world" of subjectivity tied to coherent knowledge, he discovers that the most precious thing — the excellence of the human being — is precisely not what science can transmit. A decentering is already operative in the Socratic moment itself. The field opened by virtue extends to knowledge, but virtue itself, in its transmission and formation, remains outside that field. Second, the Socratic detour lets Lacan pose his properly historicizing question: since Socrates, the *notion of the ego has come into the world*. The ego, Lacan claims, has a date.

This claim opens onto a meditation on emergence. When something new comes into the structure, it creates its own retrospective perspective, and we say: this was always there, it has existed eternally. Lacan uses the example of language: we suppose that at some moment on earth people began to speak, but once language is grasped in its proper structure, we cannot speculate about what preceded it except through symbols already drawn from within language. The new always seems to extend indefinitely backward, beneath itself. We cannot abolish a new order by thought. This is true, he says, of everything, including the origin of the world. And it applies to the *moi*: we can no longer think without the register of the ego that we have acquired in history, even when we are dealing with the traces of human self-speculation from epochs in which that register was not yet operative.

The consequence is that when we read Socrates and his interlocutors, we project: we suppose they must have had, like us, an implicit notion of that central function. From all this psychology, it is hard for us to think that it is not eternal. But the question is at least worth posing — and posing it leads us to look more closely at whether there is in fact a certain moment when this notion of the ego can be grasped at its nascent state. The documents are not so far off. Lacan introduces a small but characteristic example: when someone arrives in the morning at Socrates' house in the *Protagoras*, the scene takes place in what Plato casually mentions is "black darkness" — a detail no one has remarked on because it can only be of interest to people who, for less than seventy-five years, have been accustomed to turning the electric switch. The remark is droll but it is also methodological: the obvious is always historical.

The "dentists" make their entrance here. Lacan uses the term as a conventional designation for the kind of confident bourgeois rationalist who is reassured by the order of the world because he believes that Descartes laid down the laws and processes of clear reason in the *Discourse on Method*. For these dentists, the *cogito* is straightforward. But Lacan insists that the *je pense, donc je suis* is not so simple. Consciousness may be transparent to itself, but the *je* is not transparent to consciousness. It is not given to consciousness any differently than an object is. The apprehension of an object does not deliver up its properties; the same holds for the *je*. The cogito provides the *je* as a kind of immediate datum in the act of reflection, but nothing indicates that the totality of its reality is thereby exhausted. The reading of Descartes Lacan is proposing is one in which the *je* of the cogito is not the *moi* of the dentists. This is the first explicit articulation of the seminar's central distinction.

The philosophers' tradition, Lacan continues, has led to an increasingly purely formal notion of the *moi* — to a critique of it as a function. The idea that the ego is a substance has been turned away from, at least provisionally, as a myth to be submitted to scientific critique. Locke, Kant, and the psychophysicists carried this further, treating the *moi* with suspicion insofar as it perpetuates the substantialism of the religious notion of the soul. The shock, then, is that by an extraordinary sleight of hand of history, post-Freudian psychoanalysis has come back to the near side of even this philosophical critique. Having abandoned Freud's subversive contribution, the analytic movement has retreated behind ground that had already been won by philosophy itself.

Lacan returns to the Copernican analogy and qualifies it carefully. What is not Copernican is not univocal. Pre-Copernican cosmologies were diverse — flat-earth, hat-shaped, and so on — but they shared the idea that there was a center, that something was at the bottom or in the middle, with the rest of the world built upward. So too with the *moi*: we cannot know exactly what a contemporary of Socrates thought of his ego, but something had to be at the center. The *moi* in the form we recognize begins to take shape only in the mid-sixteenth, early-seventeenth century. The Freudian discovery has the same decentering sense, in relation to this conception, that the Copernican discovery had. And Lacan caps the section with Rimbaud's formula: *Je est un autre*. The poets, he says, who don't know what they say, always say things before the others.

### **Section 2**

The second numbered section opens with a warning against taking *Je est un autre* as a slogan. The phrase by itself means nothing if one does not first know what an *other* is. The *other* is not a term to be gargled. Lacan recalls a colleague who, having brushed up against the existentialist journal *Les Temps Modernes*, had brought back the claim that for someone to be analyzable, he must be capable of apprehending the other as such. Lacan dismisses this with sarcasm: what other? — *son semblable*, *son prochain*, *son idéal du moi*, *une cuvette*? All of these are others. The distinction between several registers of otherness — which will become the formal differentiation between *autre* and *Autre*, lowercase and uppercase — is being prepared here, even if not yet codified.

The decisive point is then made. The unconscious entirely escapes the circle of certainties in which man recognizes himself as *moi*. Outside this field there exists something that has every right to express itself as *je*, and that demonstrates this right by coming to light as *je*. This is precisely what is most misrecognized by the field of the *moi*, which in analysis comes to formulate itself as properly being the *je*. The register opened by Freud's discovery of the unconscious is thus a register in which the *je* and the *moi* are not the same. Freud's own terminology made this difficult, since in the period when he began to express it he was operating under the philosophical equivalence *moi \= conscience*. But the more Freud advanced, the less he was able to situate consciousness, and the more he had to confess that it was finally unsituatable. Everything organizes itself increasingly into a dialectic in which the *je* is distinct from the *moi*.

Lacan then makes what may be the most decisive epistemic claim of the chapter. The distinction between subject and individual, which he has presented on the subjective plane, is *also* graspable on the objective plane — and this, he says, may be the most decisive step from a scientific point of view. If one considers behavioristically what the human animal presents objectively, one finds properties, displacements, maneuvers, and from the organization of these conducts one infers the amplitude of the detours of which the individual is capable in attaining what one posits by definition as his ends. One thereby gets an idea of the level of his relations with the external world, the degree of his intelligence, the *aretē* of his species. But what Freud brings is this: the elaborations of the subject at issue in psychoanalysis are in no way situable on an axis along which, the higher they rise, the more they would coincide with the intelligence, the excellence, the perfection of the individual. The subject is not the individual's intelligence. It is not on the same axis. It is *excentric*. The subject as such, functioning as subject, is something other than an organism that adapts. Lacan settles on the topical metaphor: *the subject is decentered with respect to the individual*. This is what *Je est un autre* means.

The section ends with a long detour through La Rochefoucauld, which is more important than it first appears. Lacan reads the *Maximes* as a contemporary of Descartes' decentering, but on a parallel track — the tradition not of philosophers but of moralists, which culminates in Nietzsche's *Genealogy of Morals*. The moralists introduce a perspective of truth into the observation of moral comportment. What is striking in La Rochefoucauld is not the claim that *amour-propre* is at the base of all human conduct — that was already in Thomistic theories of love, where the subject in love seeks his own good. What is new and scandalous in La Rochefoucauld is that *amour-propre* is *deceptive*, *inauthentic*. There is a hedonism proper to the ego, and it is precisely what dupes us — frustrating us at once of our immediate pleasure and of the satisfactions we might draw from our superiority over that pleasure. Here for the first time a separation of plane and a relief are introduced, a kind of *diplopie* that opens onto what will appear as a real separation of plane. The point is to prepare the reader for the way Freudian truth will be poured into this hollow: you are duped, certainly, but the truth is elsewhere — and Freud tells us where. What then bursts in with a clap of thunder is the sexual instinct, the libido. But Lacan immediately pulls back: do we really know what we mean by the sexual instinct, the libido, the primary process? We will have to look at this closely, and that is the work of the year.

### **Section 3**

The third section opens with a diagnosis of the present: theoretical cacophony and a striking inversion of position. Why? Because, Lacan says, Freud's metapsychological work after 1920 was read badly, interpreted in a delusional way, by the first and second generations after Freud — those "insufficient people." Why did Freud believe he had to introduce these new metapsychological notions, the so-called topical notions of *moi*, *surmoi*, and *ça*? Because in the experience that unfolded from his discovery, there was a turn, a concrete crisis. To say it plainly: the new *je*, with which one had to dialogue, refused after a certain time to respond.

This is the crisis of 1920 named in the title-list of the session. Lacan locates it precisely in the historical record: in the testimony of those who lived through the years between 1910 and 1920\. In the first analytic revelations, subjects were cured more or less miraculously — and this, Lacan says, is still palpable when one reads Freud's case histories, with their dazzling interpretations and endless explanations. But it is a fact that it worked less and less well, that it was damped down over time. This is what gives reality, Lacan argues, to what he is teaching about the existence of subjectivity as such, its modifications over time according to its own causality and its own dialectic — a dialectic that runs from subjectivity to subjectivity and that perhaps escapes every kind of individual conditioning. What happens, what closes itself, what resists, in those conventional units we call subjectivities?

It was precisely in 1920, just after the technical crisis, that Freud believed he had to introduce his new metapsychological notions. Reading Freud after 1920 carefully, one sees a tight link between this technical crisis to be overcome and the fabrication of the new notions. But to see this one has to read the writings — and read them in order. That *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* was written before *Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego*, and before *The Ego and the Id*, ought to pose some questions, Lacan says — and no one has ever asked them. This is the program: the year will read *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* as the pivot-text, the first text of the new metapsychological period.

The misunderstanding that followed was a "general rush, a true liberation of schoolchildren" — Ah, here he is, the good little *moi*\! We can find ourselves again\! We are returning to the paths of general psychology\! And this was joyfully welcomed, Lacan insists, because general psychology is not merely a school matter or a mental convenience: it is the psychology of *everyone*. People were content to be able to believe once more that the ego is central. And we see the latest manifestations of this, he adds, in the brilliant elucubrations that come at the moment from beyond the water — that is, from American ego psychology. Heinz Hartmann, the "cherub of psychoanalysis," announces the great news that will let us sleep peacefully: the existence of the *autonomous ego*. An ego that, since the very beginning of Freud's discovery, has always been considered conflictual, that has never ceased to be held as something that, like reality itself, is won only in a drama — this ego is suddenly restored to us as a central datum.

To what internal necessity does it correspond, Lacan asks, to say that there must somewhere be an *autonomous ego*? This conviction surpasses the individual naïveté of the subject who believes in himself, who believes he is himself — a fairly common madness, and not entirely a madness, since it is part of the order of beliefs. We all tend to believe we are ourselves; but we are not so sure of it as that, when we look closely. In many very precise circumstances we doubt it, without thereby undergoing any depersonalization. So it is not merely to this naive belief that they want to bring us back. The phenomenon is properly sociological. It concerns analysis as a technique, or if you will as a ceremonial, a priestly function determined within a certain social context.

Why reintroduce the transcendent reality of the *autonomous ego*? Looked at closely, what is at stake is autonomous egos *more or less equal* depending on the individuals. We return to an *entification* according to which not only do individuals exist as such, but some exist more than others. This is what contaminates, more or less implicitly, the notions of the *strong ego* and the *weak ego* — so many ways of evading the problems posed by understanding neuroses and by handling technique.

The session closes with the program. The year will pursue the examination and critique of the *moi* in Freud's theory, specifying its sense in light of Freud's discovery and the technique of psychoanalysis, while studying in parallel certain of its current incidences, tied to a certain way of conceiving in analysis the relation between individual and individual. Freudian metapsychology does not begin in 1920; it is present from the very beginning — see the early writings, the letters to Fliess, the metapsychological writings of that period, and the end of the *Traumdeutung*. It is present enough between 1910 and 1920 that the previous year's seminar made it visible. From 1920 onward we enter what one can call the last metapsychological period. For this period, *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* is the first text, the pivot-work. It is the most difficult. The seminar will not resolve all its enigmas at once. But that is how it happened: Freud brought this text first, before elaborating his topography. If one waits to approach it until one has supposedly deepened the works of the period that follows, one can only make the greatest errors. This is why most analysts, regarding the famous death instinct, simply give up. Lacan ends by asking someone of goodwill — Lefèbvre-Pontalis, for example — to make a first reading of *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*. The date: 17 November 1954\.

## **II. Knowledge, Truth, Opinion**

*Psychoanalysis and its concepts. A true that cannot be grasped by linked knowledge. Form and symbol. Pericles as psychoanalyst. The year's program.*

The second session continues the introductory work but shifts the register. Where the first session laid out the polemical opposition (Freudian decentering against ego-psychological recentering), this session develops the epistemological undergirding of that opposition. Lacan needs to clarify what kind of truth analysis deals in, what relation it bears to *episteme* (linked, scientific knowledge), and what the status of analytic *concepts* is. The session unfolds through a sustained engagement with Plato's *Meno*, prepared by the Tuesday lecture by Alexandre Koyré, with substantive interventions by Octave Mannoni, Jean Hyppolite, and the mathematician Riguet, and closes with Jean-Bertrand Pontalis's first report on *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*. The session thereby fuses the philosophical reflection on knowledge with the practical agenda: this is the year that will read Freud's post-1920 metapsychology, and the question of what kind of truth that metapsychology articulates has to be settled before the reading begins.

The opening recapitulates the previous session's distinction. The Freudian *moi* extends the classical notion but adds something that changes its functional value entirely. Lacan reiterates that the *moi* has a date — that in the time of Socrates the word in our sense is absent from the texts, and that the function it now occupies was not yet operative. What has changed since is the perspective on the good. The unitary notion of the good as *aretē* — the perfection that polarizes and orients the individual's completion — has been struck with a suspicion of inauthenticity at some moment. La Rochefoucauld remains the exemplary witness here, but now Lacan adds a question that points toward the deeper issue: was this a concrete shift in man's relation to himself, or only a coming-to-consciousness of something that had not been seen before? The question is left open, but the framing matters: Lacan refuses to decide in advance between historical emergence and theoretical recognition. Both possibilities are kept in play.

Psychoanalysis, Lacan repeats, has the value of a Copernican revolution with respect to this whole problematic. The entire relation of man to himself changes perspective with Freud's discovery, and *this is what is at stake in the practice we carry out every day*. The sentence is important because it ties the epistemological claim back to clinical work. The polemic against general psychology is not a matter of theoretical purity; it concerns what one is actually doing when one analyzes a patient.

He recalls the previous Sunday's discussion, where he had rejected categorically the attempt to refuse psychoanalysis into general psychology. The idea of a unilinear, pre-established individual development with typified stages succeeding one another is, he says, an outright denial — even a repression — of what is essential in what analysis has brought. The vocabulary here is deliberate: he is accusing the syncretist tendency of doing to Freud what neurosis does to a wish, denying and repressing the disturbing core.

The polemic against this syncretism takes on a particular sharpness. Lacan reports that the most articulate partisan of the syncretist tendency had been led to declare that analytic concepts have no value, that they do not correspond to reality. But, Lacan asks, how does one grasp this reality if not by means of one's vocabulary? If one continues nonetheless to use analytic vocabulary while treating it as mere floating labels in the unnamed of daily experience, this would mean that one needs to invent another vocabulary — that is, to do something other than psychoanalysis. *If psychoanalysis is not the concepts in which it is formulated and transmitted, it is not psychoanalysis, it is something else, but then one has to say so.* This is one of Lacan's most categorical methodological assertions: the science is its concepts, and there is no behind-the-concepts reality to which appeal can be made.

The sleight of hand he diagnoses is that the syncretists continue to use the concepts — they cannot do otherwise, or the experience would dissolve entirely — but they use them in a way that is neither integrated nor articulated, neither transmissible nor defensible. When they dialogue with psychiatrists, they retreat from the vocabulary altogether, claiming that what matters in analytic experience are "exchanges of forces" — that is, something into which the outsider cannot put his nose. The argumentative tactic Lacan describes is one of strategic obscurantism: the concepts are used in private and disavowed in public.

The figure of Meno, Lacan now declares, has not been a vain preamble. Its value is exemplary. He has to correct a confusion that has apparently arisen in some minds: Meno is not the analysand, the wretched patient ridiculed. Meno is the analyst — most analysts. The reversal is sharp. Meno is the one who has accumulated opinions about virtue, who repeats what he has heard from the sophists, and who cannot give an account of what he says. Lacan is identifying the analyst who repeats received doctrine — about stages, about the strong ego, about adaptation — with the Platonic figure of bad pedagogical reception.

### **Section 1**

The first numbered section opens with an extended exchange. Octave Mannoni intervenes to develop what had struck him in Koyré's lecture: a too-direct assimilation of the Platonic dialogue and Socratic maieutics to analysis. For Plato, there is a forgotten truth, and maieutics consists in bringing it to light; the dialogue is a mixture of truth and error, and dialectic is a sieve of truth. In analysis, by contrast, what is involved is a different kind of truth — a historical truth, whereas the Platonic truth appears, from one angle, as a natural-scientific truth. Mannoni then raises an important paradox: Fromm calls the unconscious the forgotten language, while Schreber calls it the fundamental tongue — that is, sometimes wisdom and sometimes madness. So what comes back to light in analytic maieutics is truth in error and error in truth, which is quite different from the Platonic register. Mannoni also remarks that Koyré pulls *orthodoxa* (right opinion) toward what primitives call the customs that make one live — the customs by which Meno, and especially Anytus, feel threatened by epistemic inquiry. There may be an analogous conflict in analysis when those who are secure feel disturbed by being put into question.

Lacan agrees that there has been some abusive solicitation in comparing the Meno dialogue to analytic experience, but he wants to refine the question. The aim of the *Meno*, he insists, is not what is usually claimed. The standard reading takes the dialogue to show that the slave already possesses eternal forms and that reminiscence explains the passage from ignorance to knowledge. But this reading misses the paradox at the heart of the dialogue. The actual aim is to show that *epistēmē* — knowledge bound by formal coherence — does not cover the whole field of human experience, and in particular that there is no *epistēmē* of what realizes the perfection, the *aretē*, of that experience. Meno does not know what he is saying, but more specifically he does not know what he is saying *about virtue*. He is a bad student of the sophists, because he did not understand that what the sophists had to teach was not a doctrine that explains everything but the use of discourse, which is quite different. The system is always located in the other, Lacan remarks acidly: it is always Gorgias who would explain it.

What Socrates establishes, then, is that there is no *epistēmē* of virtue — and very precisely of what is the essential virtue, both for us and for the ancients: *political virtue*, by which citizens are bound together in a body. The excellent practitioners — Themistocles, Pericles, not the demagogues — act at this highest level of action that is political government, in accordance with an *orthodoxa* that is defined only by this: that there is a true here that is not graspable in linked knowledge. *Orthodoxa* has been translated as "true opinion," and that is indeed the sense. The introduction of this term is decisive for the whole epistemology of the seminar. There is a domain of truth that operates without being founded in scientific knowledge — and analysis works in this domain.

Hyppolite interjects to remind Lacan that he is evading Mannoni's question. Lacan replies that he is not evading it, only turning aside from it for the moment, and asks whether Hyppolite agrees with what he has just advanced. Hyppolite is reserved: he is waiting for what follows. He thinks Mannoni has formulated a fundamental difference between the Platonic dialogue and the analytic one, and he wonders whether Lacan wants to attempt to circumvent the radicality of this difference. Lacan promises that what follows will clarify.

What follows is the celebrated demonstration of the slave's geometrical lesson. Lacan retells it carefully. The slave, supposedly carrying all the sciences in himself through anamnesis, begins by *making a mistake*. He does so by deploying the equivalence A/B \= C/D — the relation that, Lacan notes, is the foundation of standard intelligence tests, the way the intelligence proceeds most constantly. This procedure leads him mathematically to the error of believing that doubling the side will double the surface. Socrates corrects him through a figure drawn on the sand: by removing the four corners of the large square, the surface is halved, and the inner square (rotated 45°) is the answer.

Lacan then poses the decisive question. Is there not a *fault line* between the intuitive element and the symbolic element? The result is obtained using a notion drawn from the order of number — that 8 is half of 16\. What one obtains is not eight unit-squares: at the center there are four units of surface, and an *irrational element*, √2, which is not given on the intuitive plane. There is therefore a passage from a plane of intuitive linkage to a plane of symbolic linkage. The demonstration is an example of the passage from the imaginary to the symbolic, and it is the *master* who accomplishes this passage. It is Socrates who brings the operation that 8 is half of 16\. The slave, with all his reminiscence and intelligent intuition, sees the right form only once it has been pointed out to him. What we touch here, Lacan says, is the cleavage between the plane of the imaginary or the intuitive — where reminiscence operates, the eternal form, what may also be called the *a priori* intuitions — and the symbolic function, which is in no way homogeneous with it and whose introduction into reality constitutes a *forcing*. Lacan turns to Riguet, the mathematician present, to confirm that he is not saying anything controversial. Riguet agrees.

This is one of the most important conceptual moves of the early seminar. The distinction between imaginary and symbolic, which is going to organize the rest of the year, is being illustrated through a specific historical-mathematical example. The √2 — the irrational that emerges in the diagonal — is not given at the intuitive level. It belongs to a different register, the register of the symbolic, whose introduction "forces" reality. There is a moment in the history of geometry when √2 appears; before, geometers turned around it. Retrospectively, the Egyptian and Hindu geometers can be said to have glimpsed it, but its autonomy is not manifest in the dialogue. Once it appears, it generates a whole development of mathematics in which the slave has no further part. The symbolic, once unleashed, develops by its own laws.

Hyppolite picks this up brilliantly. He observes that Lacan is showing in Plato a structure in which every invention, once made, turns out to engender its own past, to appear as an eternal discovery. We are perverted, Hyppolite says, by Christianity, which makes us locate eternal truths as *anterior*. Whereas Platonism, following more closely what could be called historicity, shows that the invention of the symbol, once invented, turns out to be an eternal past. The notion of eternal truth perhaps does not have in Platonism the sense the Middle Ages gave it, and on which Mannoni's interpretation is clearly founded. This is why, Hyppolite says, he thought there could be a paradoxical link between the Platonic dialogue and analysis, and that this is what Lacan was seeking through the relation between symbolism and truth.

Lacan replies that this is not yet quite it. There are, he insists, two sorts of relations to time. From the moment a part of the symbolic world emerges, it creates its own past — but not in the same way as a form does at the intuitive level. Error consists precisely in the confusion of these two planes: believing that what science constitutes by the intervention of the symbolic function was there from time immemorial, was given. This error exists in every form of knowledge insofar as it is a crystallization of symbolic activity that, once constituted, forgets that activity. There is in every constituted knowledge a dimension of error, which is the forgetting of the creative function of truth in its nascent form. In experimental science this forgetting may pass, since experimental science is tied to purely *operating* activities — Lacan plays on "operative" against "operational" — but analysts cannot afford to forget it, because they work in the dimension of truth in its nascent state.

What we discover in analysis is at the level of *orthodoxa*. Everything that operates in the field of analytic action is anterior to the constitution of knowledge. This does not prevent us, while operating in this field, from having constituted a knowledge that has even proved exceptionally effective — as is natural, since every science arises from a handling of language that precedes its constitution, and it is in this handling of language that analytic action unfolds.

For this very reason, however, the more one knows, the greater the risks. Everything taught in the so-called psychoanalytic institutes in a pre-digested form — sadistic, anal stages, and so on — is no doubt useful, especially for people who are not analysts. It would be foolish for an analyst to ignore it systematically, but he has to know that this is not the dimension in which he operates. He must train himself in another domain than the one where the knowledge of his experience is sedimented and deposited.

Mannoni concurs. Lacan then ties the explanation back to the enigma Mannoni had posed about the symmetrical and inverse distribution of truth and error. The "founding speeches" that envelop the subject — those of his parents, his neighbors, the whole structure of the community — constitute him not only as symbol but in his very being. There are laws of nomenclature that determine and channel the alliances from which human beings copulate and end up creating not only other symbols but real beings who, coming into the world, immediately have the little label that is their name, the essential symbol of their lot. The *orthodoxa* that Socrates leaves behind, but in which he feels himself wholly enveloped — since he too sets out from it, since he is in the process of constituting that *orthodoxa* he leaves behind — *we* now put back at the center. Analysis is this.

The argument culminates in a striking formulation. For Socrates (not necessarily for Plato), if Themistocles and Pericles were great men, it is because they were good psychoanalysts. They found, in their own register, what right opinion means. They are at the heart of the concrete of history where dialogue is engaged, even though no kind of truth is identifiable there in the form of a generalizable knowledge always true. To respond as one must to an event insofar as it is significant, insofar as it is a function of the symbolic exchange between human beings — this might be the order given to the fleet to set out from Piraeus — is to make the right interpretation. And to make the right interpretation at the right moment is to be a good psychoanalyst. Lacan does not say that the politician is the psychoanalyst — Plato precisely, in the *Statesman*, begins to give a science of politics, and God knows where that has led. But for Socrates, the good politician is the psychoanalyst.

Mannoni demurs. Pericles and Themistocles were good statesmen, he suggests, for another reason: they had *orthodoxa* because they were what one would call today *gentlemen*. They were so integrated into their social milieu, there were so few problems, so little need of science, that it is almost the opposite. Lacan agrees but reframes: this is precisely what he is saying. It is not because they were psychoanalysts by birth, without having been psychoanalyzed, that they were not good psychoanalysts. At that moment only the masters made history; the slave to whom Socrates wanted to give a little turn around the ring had nothing to say. He would take some further time to become Spartacus. And Socrates, by an excess of *epistēmē*, missed the *orthodoxa* and would pay for it foolishly. But, as Merleau-Ponty had remarked, Socrates put some good will into the affair — he could very nearly have possessed them, the gentlemen. Perhaps he did not have all his means at that moment. There was no doubt some reason for engaging in another form of demonstration. After all, it was not so ineffective — it had a symbolic sense.

### **Section 2**

The second section is the programmatic section. Lacan turns to Pontalis and asks whether he has something to tell the seminar. He explains his methodological principle: one must always begin by taking up questions at their most difficult — afterward, one only has to descend. This is why he wanted to begin with *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*. He has not wanted to burden Pontalis with giving an exhaustive analysis at the outset; the text can only be understood after going around the entirety of what Freud says about the ego from the beginning of his work to the end.

Lacan now gives the year's reading list, which is one of the few moments in the published seminar where the syllabus is made explicit. First, *Aus den Anfängen der Psychoanalyse* — the letters to Fliess and the *Entwurf*, which is a first psychological theory, already complete. The great post-war discovery, as he puts it, is these early papers of Freud. The *Entwurf*, which Lacan translates as *Esquisse* (Sketch), is already a metapsychology with a theory of the ego, available in English as *Origins of Psycho-analysis*. Second, the *Traumdeutung*, particularly the chapter on the *Psychology of the Dream Processes*, in German if possible. Third, the texts of what is called Freud's second metapsychology, grouped in the French translation as *Essais de psychanalyse*: *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*, *Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego*, and *The Ego and the Id* — the three fundamental articles for understanding the ego. Fourth, supplementary texts: *Neurosis and Psychosis*, *The Function of the Reality Principle in Neurosis and Psychosis*, and *Analysis Terminable and Interminable*. Fifth, one must know Freud's last unfinished work, the *Abriss der Psychoanalyse* (Outline), which alone gives indications of how Freud made the first topographical division — unconscious, preconscious, conscious — overlap with the new topography of ego, superego, and id.

Mannoni asks whether he can also mention the last paper, "Splitting of the Ego." Lacan replies that this is precisely where all the confusions started. He then turns to Pontalis with the request: ten minutes to give the questions inspired by a first reading of *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*.

Pontalis's exposition (delivered, by his own description, in the role of the naive mouthpiece) is reproduced in the published seminar at substantial length, and it functions as the first technical introduction of the year's themes. He summarizes the structure of Freud's text: the predominance Freud had first established for the pleasure principle, linked to the principle of constancy (according to which the organism must be able to reduce tensions to a constant level), turns out not to be exclusive as initially affirmed. Freud is pushed by a number of facts to go *beyond* his initial affirmation, but he is embarrassed in the text. The facts are: first, the dreams of traumatic neurotics, which curiously always reprise the dream of the traumatizing situation, so that the idea of the dream as hallucinatory wish-fulfillment collapses. Second, the games children repeat indefinitely — the famous example of the eighteen-month-old who throws an object away and retrieves it, the *fort-da* of redisappearance and reappearance, in which the child tries to assume an active role. Third, and most important, what happens in the transference situation: the analysand starts over with the same dreams, and more generally is led to repeat instead of simply remembering. Everything proceeds as if resistance does not come, as Freud had first believed, solely from the repressed, but solely from the ego. Freud's first conception of transference is modified: it is no longer defined only as the product of a disposition to transference, but of a compulsion to repetition.

These facts, Pontalis continues, lead Freud to the affirmation that there is something other than the pleasure principle — an irresistible tendency to repetition that transcends the pleasure principle and the reality principle, and that, while in a certain way opposed to the pleasure principle, would complement it within the principle of constancy. Beside the repetition of needs, there would be a *need of repetition* — which Freud notes rather than introduces.

Pontalis declines to follow Freud into the biological infrastructure he attempts to give, and instead poses two questions. The first is that the tendency to repetition appears defined in a contradictory way. Defined by its aim — as in the child's game, to master what threatens an equilibrium, to assume an active role, to triumph over unresolved conflicts — it appears as generative of tension, a factor of progress, while the instinct in Freud's strict sense is only a principle of stagnation. The central idea is then that the tendency to repetition modifies the pre-established harmony between pleasure and reality principles, leading to increasingly broad integrations. This justifies the title: the compulsion to repetition would be *beyond* the pleasure principle, the condition of human progress. But defined by its mechanism, repetition appears as pure automatism, as regression. Freud illustrates this with biological examples; the tension aspect is illustrated by human progress, the regression aspect by phenomena of nutritional hygiene. Hence Pontalis's first question: there is a tension between repetition as factor of progress and repetition as mechanism.

The second question concerns the ego. The inertia at stake could be figured by the ego, which Freud defines very expressly as the nucleus of resistances in the transference. This is a step in the evolution of his doctrine: the ego in analysis — that is, in a situation that puts the precarious equilibrium back into question — presents security, stagnation, pleasure. The binding function (*Bindung*) would thus not define the entire subject. The ego, whose principal task is to transform everything into secondary, bound energy, would not define the entire subject — hence the appearance of the tendency to repetition. The question of the nature of the ego could be linked to the function of narcissism. Here too, Pontalis notes, there are contradictions in Freud, who sometimes seems to identify the ego with the conservation instinct and sometimes speaks of it as a kind of seeking of death.

Lacan praises the formulation. Despite its brevity, Pontalis has aimed straight at the heart of the ambiguities with which the seminar will have to deal. Lacan picks up two points. First, Pontalis spoke of the pleasure principle as equivalent to the adaptive tendency, but this is precisely what he then put in question. There is a profound difference between the pleasure principle and something else that is differentiated from it — analogous to the English distinction between *need* and *drive*. Lacan emphasizes that the notion of the repetition-tendency as *drive* is very explicitly opposed to the idea that there is anything in life that tends toward progress, contrary to the perspective of traditional optimism and evolutionism. This leaves the problematic of adaptation — and even, Lacan adds, of reality — entirely open.

Second, the distinction Pontalis drew between the biological and human registers can only be of interest if one perceives that it is precisely from the confusion of these two registers that the question of the text arises. There is no text that puts the very meaning of life into question to a higher degree. It results in a confusion — almost a radical confusion — of the human dialectic with something that is in nature. And there is one term Pontalis did not pronounce that was essential: the *death instinct*. He has shown rightly that this is not simply Freudian metaphysics; the question of the ego is fully implicated in it. But Pontalis only sketched it — otherwise he would have done what Lacan plans to do this year.

The session closes with a brief signal of what comes next: the question of the ego and the pleasure principle, taking up at once what was at the end of Pontalis's question and what he had encountered at the very beginning. The date: 24 November 1954\. The transition is then explicit: the next section of the seminar is titled *Beyond the Pleasure Principle, Repetition* — which will run through chapters III–VII.

# **Beyond the Pleasure Principle, Repetition**

## **III. The Symbolic Universe**

*Dialogues on Lévi-Strauss. Life and the machine. God, nature, and the symbol. The natural imaginary. Freudian dualism.*

The third session marks a shift in register. The Tuesday before the seminar had hosted Claude Lévi-Strauss for a discussion on the *Elementary Structures of Kinship*, and Lacan opens by acknowledging the noticeable progress in the seminar's dialogical functioning — the back-and-forth has begun to take hold. He devotes some opening minutes to the pedagogical scene itself: these open sessions are not scientific communications, no one is on display, and the only reproach he can make to his audience is that they want to appear too intelligent. The remark is more than throwaway humor. The pedagogical method of the seminar — its insistence that questions be posed at the deepest point where they can reach the participant, even hesitantly, even baroquely — is part of the *epistemic* argument being developed. Truth in its nascent state, the thematic of Chapter II, requires a corresponding mode of speech. The seminar's form mirrors its content.

The substance of the session is the elaboration of what Lacan will now call the *symbolic universe*, in dialogue with Lévi-Strauss's structural anthropology. The conceptual stakes are very high. Lacan needs to show that the symbolic order is not reducible to nature, not reducible to culture in the old anthropological sense, and not reducible to a collective psychology of the Jungian type. He needs to install the symbolic as a distinct register with its own laws, in such a way that the practice of analysis can be understood as operating within that register. The session also makes a first major intervention on the imaginary and on the machine, and it ends with a remarkable formulation about Freud's dualism that programs the rest of the early seminar.

### **Section 1**

The session takes its first concrete shape through an exchange with Didier Anzieu, whose question Miller does not reproduce. Lacan answers it by clarifying the status of the Oedipus complex relative to Lévi-Strauss's analysis. Durandin had apparently suggested that the violence of the incest prohibition was measurable by patent social acts. Lacan denies this: to discover the Oedipus complex, one had first to examine neurotics, and only then pass to a much wider circle of individuals. The Oedipus complex, with the phantasmatic intensity that has been discovered for it, the importance and presence it has on the imaginary plane for the subject of analytic experience, must therefore be conceived — relative to what Lévi-Strauss is talking about — as a *recent, terminal* phenomenon and not an originary one. This is an important methodological point: the Oedipus complex as a clinical structure is not the same thing as the prohibition of incest as a foundational anthropological function. The two operate at different registers.

Lacan then objects to Anzieu's reading of certain demographic remarks in Lévi-Strauss. The term *compensation*, used by Lévi-Strauss when discussing Tibetan or Nepalese tribes that kill girl infants and end up with more men than women, has merely statistical value and no relation to the analytic term. To make the point about the explanatory power of statistical-spatial considerations, Lacan invokes Buffon: Buffon asked why bees make pretty hexagons, and observed that there is no other polyhedron with which one can fill a surface so practically and so neatly. It is the pressure of spatial occupation that produces hexagons; one need not ask whether bees know geometry. There are entire pseudo-finalistic problems that dissolve once spatial-numerical considerations are taken into account. Anzieu's deeper error, however, lies elsewhere — in thinking that Lévi-Strauss gives a soul to society when he speaks of circulation from one family to another. The whole question of finality and its relation to causality would deserve a careful discussion, but the operative point is: in causalist thought, finality does not exist, although the very insistence required to maintain this denial proves how hard the notion is to handle.

What, then, is the originality of Lévi-Strauss's contribution? Lacan answers in one of the session's most important passages. Lévi-Strauss insists that nothing in the long-collected phenomena of kinship and the family can be understood if one tries to deduce them from any natural or naturalizing dynamic. *Incest as such arouses no natural feeling of horror* — this is Lévi-Strauss's claim, not Lacan's, but Lacan endorses it. There is no biological and in particular no genetic reason that motivates exogamy. Lévi-Strauss shows this after an extremely precise discussion of scientific data. In a society — and one can envisage non-human societies — a permanent and constant practice of endogamy would not only have no disadvantages but would, in time, eliminate the supposed inherited defects. There is no possible deduction, starting from the natural plane, of the elementary structure called the preferential order.

Lacan extracts the conceptual yield: in the human order, we are dealing with the *total emergence* — encompassing the whole human order in its totality — of a new function. The symbolic function is not new as a function; it has its rudiments elsewhere than in the human order, but they are only rudiments. The human order is characterized by the fact that the symbolic function intervenes at every moment and every degree of its existence. Everything hangs together. To conceive what happens in the domain proper to the human order, one must begin from the idea that this order constitutes a *totality*. The totality in the symbolic order is called a *universe*. The symbolic order is given first of all in its universal character. It is not constituted little by little. As soon as the symbol arrives, there is a universe of symbols. The question of how many symbols, numerically, are needed remains open. But however few symbols one might conceive at the emergence of the symbolic function as such, they imply the totality of everything that is human. Everything is ordered with respect to the symbols that have arisen.

Why does Lévi-Strauss call his structures *elementary* and not *primitive*? Because elementary is the opposite of complex — and curiously he has not yet written the *Complex Structures of Kinship*; that is us, and complex structures are characterized by being much more amorphous. In elementary structures the rules of alliance are taken up in an extraordinarily rich and luxurious network of preferences, prohibitions, indications, commandments, blazed paths, and they cover a much vaster field than in complex forms. The closer one approaches not to the origin but to the element, the more imposing become the structuration, the amplitude, and the intricacy of the properly symbolic system of nomenclature. From this Lacan formulates the hypothesis that this symbolic order, since it always posits itself as a whole, as forming a universe by itself — even constituting the universe as such, in its distinction from the world — must also be structured as a whole, that is, must form a dialectical structure that holds together, that is complete. Some kinship systems are more or less viable; some reach properly *arithmetical* impasses, requiring periodic crises with their ruptures and re-departures. It is from these arithmetical studies — understanding "arithmetic" not just as the manipulation of collections of objects but as the comprehension of the scope of combinatorial operations, which goes beyond anything one could deduce experimentally from the vital relation of subject to world — that Lévi-Strauss demonstrates a correct classification of what the elementary structures of kinship present. This supposes that symbolic instances function in society from its origin, from the moment it appears as human. And this is also what the unconscious supposes, as we discover and manipulate it in analysis.

There had been some hesitation in Lévi-Strauss's response to Lacan's question the previous evening — a frequent movement among those who introduce new ideas, a hesitation to maintain the full sharpness of what they propose. Lévi-Strauss had almost reverted to a psychological plane and had used the term *collective unconscious*. Lacan rejects this firmly. The question he posed in no way implied a collective unconscious. What solution could the word "collective" provide here, when the collective and the individual are strictly the same thing? It is not a matter of supposing a common soul somewhere where all these calculations would take place; it is not a matter of any psychological entification; it is a matter of the symbolic function. The symbolic function has nothing to do with a para-animal formation, a totality that would make all of humanity into a kind of great animal — for that is what the collective unconscious ultimately is. If the symbolic function functions, we are inside it. And more: we are so far inside that we cannot get out. In a great part of the problems posed for us when we try to scientize — that is, to put order into a number of phenomena, foremost among them the phenomenon of life — it is always finally the paths of the symbolic function that lead us, far more than any direct apprehension.

Thus, Lacan notes, it is always in terms of mechanism that we try, in spite of everything, to explain the living. The question that poses itself for us analysts — and perhaps here we can step out of the controversy between vitalism and mechanism — is: why are we led to think life in terms of mechanism? In what respect are we effectively, as men, *related to the machine*? Hyppolite intervenes to suggest: insofar as we are mathematicians, insofar as we have the passion of mathematics. Lacan agrees and goes further. The philosophical critiques made of mechanist research suppose that the machine is deprived of freedom. It would be easy to show, he insists, that *the machine is much more free than the animal*. The animal is a *blocked* machine: a machine some of whose parameters can no longer vary. Why? Because it is the external environment that determines the animal and makes it into a fixed type. It is insofar as we, relative to the animal, are *machines* — that is, something decomposed — that we manifest a greater freedom, in the sense in which freedom means multiplicity of possible choices. This is a perspective that is never made explicit. The provocation here is real: Lacan is inverting the standard humanist objection against mechanism by showing that "machine" in the modern, cybernetic sense designates a system of decomposable, recombinable elements, which has greater flexibility than the fixed program of an animal.

Hyppolite picks this up: hasn't the word "machine" changed in meaning, sociologically and profoundly, from its origins to cybernetics? Lacan agrees and elaborates. He is trying, for the first time, to inculcate in his audience that the machine is not what the common run of people think. The sense of the machine is in the process of changing completely, whether or not one has opened a cybernetics book. The eighteenth-century mechanism — La Mettrie and the *Homme-Machine*, the *Encyclopédie*, the mechanism that today it is fashionable to execrate as the mechanism of little machines far from life — those people were still much entangled in earlier categories, scholastic categories that truly dominated their minds. One has to read the thirty-five volumes of the *Encyclopédie* end to end to see the extent to which scholastic notions still dominated what they were trying, not without effort, to introduce. Their attempts at mechanical reduction were far in advance of the mental sequences they maintained when they took up any specific theme. Open the *Encyclopédie* at the entry "love" or "self-love" and one sees how far their human sentiments were from what they were trying to construct concerning the knowledge of man.

Mechanism, then, has only taken on its full, purified, exclusive sense — the sense excluding every other interpretive system — much later, in our minds or those of our fathers. This remark lets Lacan define what it is to be a precursor. It is not (which is impossible) to anticipate categories that come later and are not yet created — human beings are always plunged in the same cultural network as their contemporaries and cannot have notions other than their own. To be a precursor is to see what one's contemporaries are constituting as thoughts, as consciousness, as action, as techniques, as political forms, *to see them as one will see them a century later*. That, yes, can exist. And there is a mutation underway in the function of the machine that leaves behind all those who are still at the level of critiquing old mechanism.

### **Section 2**

The second section opens with Mannoni's intervention on nature and culture. Lévi-Strauss had taken up the problem of nature and culture by observing that for some time the opposition between them had become unclear. The interventions of the previous evening continued to look for nature on the side of affectivity, impulses, the natural ground of being. But what had led Lévi-Strauss to pose the question was the observation that a certain form of incest, for example, was both *universal and contingent* — and this kind of contradiction led him to a sort of conventionalism that bewildered many listeners. Mannoni had remarked that the problem of contingent and universal is found in disturbing form outside the institutional world: right-handedness is universal yet contingent (one could be left-handed); the spiral of shell-mollusks (helices) is also universal yet contingent. So the question Lévi-Strauss poses overflows by a great deal the classical opposition of the natural and the institutional. We are in the presence of something that dissolves both the old idea of nature and the idea of institution.

Hyppolite proposes calling this "universal contingency." Mannoni is uncertain. Lacan steps in and distinguishes. The contingent for Lévi-Strauss was opposed to the necessary, and the deep question concealed under this opposition is what we might call *the necessity of mathematics*. This requires a special definition, and Lacan ties it to what he called the symbolic universe. His proposal: *the Oedipus complex is at once universal and contingent because it is purely and uniquely symbolic*. Hyppolite demurs ("I don't think so") but Lacan presses on. The value of the nature/culture distinction Lévi-Strauss introduces in the *Elementary Structures of Kinship* is that it allows us to distinguish the universal from the generic. The symbolic universal does not need to spread over the surface of the whole earth to be universal. There is nothing, so far as Lacan knows, that achieves the worldwide unity of human beings. There is nothing concretely realized as universal. And yet, as soon as any symbolic system whatsoever takes form, it is already, by right, universal as such. That men have, with rare exceptions, two arms, two legs, and a pair of eyes — that they are featherless bipeds — is *generic*, not universal. The helices that wind one way or the other are introduced under that same category: they raise the question of the natural type, not of the universal.

The point matters: Lacan is splitting off the symbolic-universal from the generic-statistical. The symbolic universal is the universality of *form*, the form of a structured order; the generic is the universality of *type*. The two registers must not be conflated. The question of why men, naturally given to certain regularities, end up with the particular institutions they have — the question of contingent choice within a frame of necessary symbolic structuration — cannot be answered by appeal to the natural type alone.

Mannoni, dissatisfied, raises a deeper question. Up to the present, men have opposed to nature a pseudo-nature: human institutions are encountered like oaks and birches. They then agreed that these pseudo-natures were a fact of human freedom or of contingent choice — and gave the greatest importance to the new category of *culture* opposed to *nature*. Studying these questions, Lévi-Strauss comes no longer to know where nature ends and culture begins, because problems of choice are met not just in the universe of nomenclatures but in the universe of forms. From the symbolism of nomenclature to the symbolism of all form, nature speaks. It speaks by winding right or left, by being right-handed or left-handed: nature's manner of making contingent choices, like families or arabesques. Mannoni finds himself at a watershed and no longer sees how the waters divide. He brings not a solution but a difficulty.

Hyppolite reformulates: Lacan has very justly opposed the universal to the generic, saying that universality is tied to symbolism itself, to the modality of the symbolic universe created by man. But this is a pure form. Lacan's word *universality* means deeply that a human universe necessarily takes the form of universality; it tends toward a totality that universalizes itself. Lacan: this is the function of the symbol. Hyppolite: but does this answer the question? It only shows the formal character that a human universe takes on.

Lacan distinguishes two senses of *formal*. In mathematical formalization, *formal* means a set of conventions from which a whole series of consequences, of linked theorems, can be developed, establishing relations of structure within an ensemble, properly a *law*. In the gestaltist sense, by contrast, form means a totality, but realized and isolated. Hyppolite: is your sense the first or the second? Lacan: the first, unquestionably. Hyppolite: but you nonetheless spoke of totality, so this symbolic universe is purely conventional, taking on the form of "universal form" without being generic or even general. He wonders whether Lacan is giving a *formal* solution to Mannoni's problem.

Lacan answers that Mannoni's question has two faces. First, the problem of *signatura rerum*: do things themselves naturally present a certain asymmetry? There is a real, a given; this given is structured in a certain way; there are natural asymmetries. Should we, in our line of cognitive progress, set out to plumb their mysterious sense? An entire human tradition, called philosophy of nature, has worked at this sort of reading; we know where it leads — to ineffable things that stop fairly quickly, unless one wants to continue and enter the plane of what is commonly called delirium. This is certainly not Mannoni's case, whose mind is far too acute and dialectical to pose such a question except in a problematic form.

Second, the question is whether this was what Lévi-Strauss was aiming at when he said, the previous evening, that he was there at the edge of nature, seized by vertigo, wondering whether it was not in nature that he had to find the roots of his symbolic tree. Lacan, having had personal dialogues with Lévi-Strauss, can shed light on this. *Lévi-Strauss is retreating from the very sharp bipartition he had made between nature and the symbol*, even though he senses its creative value, since it is a method that allows one to distinguish between registers, and at the same time between orders of facts. He oscillates, and for a reason that may seem surprising but is openly avowed: he fears that under the form of the autonomy of the symbolic register a *transcendence* might reappear, masked — a transcendence for which, in his affinities, in his personal sensibility, he feels only fear and aversion. He fears, in other words, that after we have shown God out by one door, he may come in by another. He does not want the symbol, even in the extraordinarily purified form he himself presents, to be merely a reappearance of God under a mask. This is at the origin of the oscillation he manifested when he put into question the methodical separation of the symbolic from the natural plane.

The diagnosis is striking. Lacan is locating, in the founder of structural anthropology, a *theological anxiety* about the autonomy of the symbolic. The fear of theology pushes back toward nature; but the symbolic, properly grasped, is neither theological nor natural. It is the order of the law in its formal autonomy.

Hyppolite's last push: appealing to the symbolic universe does not resolve the question of the choices made by man. Institutions, which imply contingent choices, no doubt enter into a symbolic universe, but this does not give us an explanation of those choices. Lacan: it is not a matter of explanation. Hyppolite: nonetheless we are facing a problem. Lacan: this is exactly *the problem of origins*. Hyppolite presses: in what way does the term *symbolic* serve us? What does it bring? Lacan answers that it serves him in the exposition of analytic experience. As had been shown the previous year, it is impossible to order correctly the various aspects of the transference if one does not begin from a definition of *speech*, of the creative, founding function of *full speech* (*la parole pleine*). In experience, transference is grasped in different aspects — psychological, personal, interpersonal — but always imperfectly, refracted, multiplied. Without a radical position-taking on the function of speech, the transference is purely and simply inconceivable. Inconceivable in the proper sense: there is no concept of the transference, only a multiplicity of facts linked by a vague and inconsistent bond.

### **Section 3**

The third section announces the next session and the operative framework. Lacan will introduce the question of the ego the next time in the form: relations between the function of the ego and the pleasure principle. To conceive the function Freud designates by the name *moi*, as for reading the whole Freudian metapsychology, it is indispensable to make use of the distinction of planes and relations expressed by the terms *symbolic*, *imaginary*, and *real*. This is the first explicit announcement of the famous triad as the operative framework. The purpose, Lacan says, is to keep its sense to a particularly pure symbolic experience — that of analysis. He gives an example by initiating what he will be led to say about the ego.

The ego, in its most essential aspect, is an *imaginary* function. This is a discovery of experience, not a category that Lacan would call almost *a priori* like the symbolic. Through this point — and almost through this point alone — we find in human experience an open door onto an element of typicity. This element appears on the surface of nature, but in an always disappointing form. The fundamental, central structure of our experience is properly of the imaginary order. We can even grasp how distinct this function is in man from what it is throughout nature. The imaginary function is found in nature in a thousand forms — all the gestaltic captations linked to mating display, so essential to the maintenance of sexual attraction within the species. But the ego-function in man presents distinct characteristics. *This is the great discovery of analysis*: at the level of the generic relation, tied to the life of the species, man already functions differently. There is already in him a crack, a profound disturbance of vital regulation. This is the importance of Freud's notion of the *death instinct*. Not that the death instinct is in itself such a luminous notion. What must be grasped is that he was forced to introduce it to bring us back to an acute datum of his experience, at a moment when this datum was beginning to be lost.

When an apprehension regarding structure is in advance of itself, there is always a moment of weakening when one tends to abandon it. This is what happened in the Freudian circle when the sense of the discovery of the unconscious passed to the background. There was a return to a confused, unitary, naturalistic position regarding man, the ego, and consequently the instincts. *It was precisely to recover the sense of his experience that Freud wrote* Beyond the Pleasure Principle. Lacan will show by what necessity Freud was led to write those last paragraphs — paragraphs whose fate at the hands of the analytic community at large is well known. People say one understands nothing in them. And even when, after Freud, one repeats *death instinct*, one understands no more than the Jacobins, so prettily riddled by Pascal in the *Provinciales*, understood of sufficient grace. He asks everyone to read this extraordinary, incredibly ambiguous, even confused text of Freud, to read it several times — otherwise the literal critique he will make of it will not be grasped. The last paragraphs have remained literally a closed letter with closed lips. They have never yet been elucidated. They can only be understood if one sees what Freud's experience wanted to bring. *He wanted to save a dualism at all costs*, at the moment when this dualism was melting in his hands, and where the ego, the libido, and so on were all becoming a kind of vast whole that reintroduced us to a philosophy of nature.

This dualism is nothing other than what Lacan speaks of when he puts forward the autonomy of the symbolic. Freud never formulated it this way. To make it understood will require a critique and an exegesis of Freud's text. Lacan cannot consider as already established what is precisely to be proved this year. But he believes he will be able to demonstrate that the category of *symbolic action* is founded.

The session closes with Hyppolite's elegant question and Lacan's response. Hyppolite reads the symbolic function as *transcendence* in the precise sense that we can neither remain in it nor leave it — what is it for? We cannot do without it, yet we cannot install ourselves in it either. Lacan: of course; it is the presence in absence and the absence in presence. Hyppolite then insists that to remain purely phenomenological is not enough. Lacan agrees. The use he is making of the symbolic register must not lead merely to locating somewhere Hyppolite's transcendence, which after all must well exist; that is not what is at stake. The allusions to a quite different use of the notion of machine are perhaps there to indicate this. Hyppolite reports he was only asking questions; he was wondering what allowed Lacan not to answer Mannoni's question. Lacan: he did not think this was the sense in which one could say Lévi-Strauss returns to nature. He has indicated that we must take into account the formal side of nature, in the sense of what he qualified as "pseudo-significant asymmetry," because this is what man seizes upon to make his fundamental symbols. What is important is what gives to natural forms a symbolic value and function, what makes them function in relation to one another. *It is man who introduces the notion of asymmetry*. Asymmetry in nature is neither symmetric nor asymmetric; it is what it is.

The closing formulation is the session's distilled thesis. The *moi*, as function and as symbol, is the locus of an ambiguity. The ego is an imaginary function, but it intervenes in psychic life only *as symbol*. One uses the ego as the Bororo use the parrot — the Bororo say "I am a parrot," we say "I am me." None of this has any importance in itself; the important thing is the function this has. The reference to the Bororo (from Karl von den Steinen, much discussed by Lévi-Strauss, Cassirer, and others) places the predication "I am me" on the same plane as a totemic identification: an imaginary identification that operates as a symbolic position in a structure of exchange.

Mannoni's closing remark gives the session its diagnostic edge. After Lévi-Strauss, one has the impression that one can no longer use the notions of culture and nature; he destroys them. Similarly for the idea of adaptation. To be adapted means only to be alive. Lacan agrees: there is something right in this. It is of the same order as what he had evoked in saying that at a given moment Freud wanted at all costs to defend a certain dualism. Through the rapid evolution of analytic theory and technique, Freud had found himself faced with a drop in tension analogous to the one Mannoni detects in Lévi-Strauss's mind. But — Lacan notes carefully — this perhaps is not Freud's last word. The date: 1 December 1954\.

## **IV. A Materialist Definition of the Phenomenon of Consciousness**

*Lived experience and destiny. "The kernel of our being." The ego is an object. Fascination, rivalry, recognition.*

The fourth session is one of the great conceptual sessions of the seminar. Lacan executes three large tasks in it. First, he establishes, through a clinical example of dream interpretation, the principle that analytic interpretation must operate at the level of the symbolic — that of the subject's history understood as a question about destiny — rather than at the level of inter-ego dialogue or affective dependency. Second, he gives what he himself calls a "materialist definition of the phenomenon of consciousness," using the device of the lake-and-camera thought experiment to detach consciousness from the privileged metaphysical position of the subject. Third, he develops the doctrine that the ego is an object — a particular imaginary object that functions in the subject's experience — and unfolds the dialectic of fascination, rivalry, and recognition that this thesis requires. The session ends with a remarkable proto-cybernetic model of the two little machines and the necessity of a third term, which prefigures the seminar's eventual articulation of the big Other.

The session opens with an epigraph from the seventeenth-century Silesian baroque poet Daniel von Czepko: a couplet on what the Most High creates in creating all, and what He creates before all — answers "Himself" and "Me." Lacan promises to return to it. The poem will function as a kind of conceptual frame: the question of what is created before the world bears on the priority of the symbolic over what we take to be the subject's centeredness.

He then makes a methodological remark of importance. The laws of this teaching contain in themselves a reflection of its sense. Lacan claims no more than to lead his audience to the reading of Freud's works; he does not pretend to replace it. The form he is trying to give to Freudian teaching will only take on its sense and scope for them if they go back to the texts and confront the insights he offers with the difficulties those texts present. These texts are sometimes difficult, woven through with contradictions — organized contradictions, certainly, but contradictions, not simple antinomies. Freud, following his own path, sometimes arrives at positions that appear contradictory even to himself, and returns to some of them — which does not mean he thinks they were not justified at their date. This movement of Freud's thought, which is not finished, which has never formulated itself in a definitive, dogmatic edition, is what they must learn to apprehend for themselves. The seminar is meant to facilitate this apprehension by communicating what Lacan himself has been able to draw from his reflection on Freud's texts, illuminated by an experience that, at least in principle, was guided by them.

What Lacan teaches, in summary form: Freud discovered in man the weight and axis of a subjectivity that goes beyond individual organization as a sum of individual experiences, and even beyond individual development as a line. Lacan offers a possible definition of subjectivity: an *organized system of symbols* that claims to cover the totality of an experience, to animate it, to give it sense. What is the seminar itself attempting, if not to constitute a subjectivity? The directions and openings offered are made to inspire prolongation into concrete action.

### **Section 1**

In this teaching, as in an analysis, one is dealing with resistances. Resistances always have their seat in the ego — this is what analysis teaches. What corresponds to the ego is what Lacan sometimes calls the sum of prejudices that every knowledge entails, what each of us individually drags along. It includes what we know or believe we know, since to know is always, in some respect, to *believe* one knows. When a perspective is brought decentered from one's experience, a movement always takes place by which one tries to recover the balance, the habitual center of one's point of view — sign of what Lacan is explaining, and which is called resistance. One should rather open oneself to notions arising from another experience.

He gives an example. Lévi-Strauss the other day had brought a perspective implying the radical relativization of the family reality, which should have been an occasion for analysts to revise what may be too fascinating, too absorbing in the reality they handle daily. How did one of their companions react? Rather than worrying about the conventionalism of the family system, he said, let us remember that in the family there are not only parents but children; from the child's point of view, the reality of the family is restored. This brings analysis back to solid ground. But this intervention itself testified to the inclination of the mind to recenter the analytic experience on individual, psychological experience — and this, Lacan says, is precisely what must not be done.

He illustrates with a case presented the very next day in a supervision group. The subject dreamed of a child, a nursling in its primitive stage of helplessness, lying on its back like a little overturned turtle, agitating its four limbs. An isolated image. For certain reasons, Lacan was led to tell the analyst that this child is the subject; there is no doubt. A second dream confirms this. The dreamer bathes in a sea with very particular characteristics: it is at once the analyst's couch, the cushions of the analyst's car, and of course the mother. On this sea are inscribed numbers manifestly referring to the date of the subject's birth and to his age.

What is the background? The subject is preoccupied with a child about to be born for whom he feels responsible, and around whom he fantasizes an imaginary paternity. The situation is so ambiguous that the subject must, in order to fantasize this, have deep motives, since the reality of the matter is itself blurred. In a kind of sub-delusional anxiety about his responsibilities as a progenitor, the subject reproduces a question that is essential for him: is he himself, yes or no, a *legitimate* child? If he produces this dream, it is because the analyst has already formulated to him: this is about you. And beneath the dream lies the question: am I not, after all, your child, you the analyst?

The decisive observation follows. What is in relief here is not, as one is always tempted to believe, the concrete affective dependence of the child on adults more or less parental. If the subject asks himself the question of what he is as a child, it is not insofar as he is more or less dependent, but insofar as *recognized* or not — having or not having the right to bear his name as the child of so-and-so. It is insofar as the relations in which he is caught are themselves taken up to the degree of symbolism that the subject interrogates himself. The problem poses itself for him at the second power, on the plane of the symbolic assumption of his destiny, in the register of his autobiography.

This grounds the central claim. Lacan will not say that everything in the analytic dialogue always proceeds at this level, but they must understand that this is the essentially analytic level. Many children fantasize having another family, being the child of others than those who care for them — a typical, normal phase of childhood development. Now: what is the analysis of resistances? Not — as it is too often practiced — intervening so that the subject becomes conscious of how his attachments, his prejudices, the balance of his ego, prevent him from seeing. Not persuasion, which soon issues into suggestion. Not reinforcing the subject's ego, or making an ally of his healthy part. Not convincing. *It is, at every moment of the analytic relation, knowing at what level the response must be brought.* That response may sometimes need to be brought at the level of the ego. But in the case described, this is not so. The subject's question in no way refers to what might result from such-and-such weaning, abandonment, or lack of love or affection; it concerns *his history insofar as he misrecognizes it*, and this is what he expresses, despite himself, through his whole conduct, insofar as he obscurely seeks to recognize it. His life is oriented by a problematic that is not that of his lived experience but that of his destiny — namely: what does his history signify?

Speech, Lacan now says, is the matrix of the misrecognized part of the subject, and this is the proper level of the analytic symptom — a level decentered with respect to individual experience, since it is that of the historical text that integrates it. The symptom will yield only to an intervention brought at this decentered level. Every intervention will fail that is inspired by a prefabricated reconstitution forged from one's idea of normal individual development, aiming at normalization (here is what he lacked, here is what he must learn to undergo as frustration). It is a question of whether the symptom resolves itself on one register or another; there is no middle ground.

The matter is nonetheless problematic, since the inter-egoic dialogue does have certain reverberations and can perhaps even be psychotherapeutic. Psychotherapy has always been practiced without anyone knowing very well what they were doing, but certainly with the function of speech intervening. The question for analysis is whether this function of speech exercises its action by the substitution of the analyst's authority for the subject's ego, or whether it is *subjective*. The order instituted by Freud proves that the axial reality of the subject is not in his ego. Intervening by substituting oneself for the subject's ego — as is done in a certain practice of resistance analysis — is suggestion, not analysis.

The argument culminates in the introduction of a key Freudian formula. The unconscious, Lacan reminds his audience, is that subject unknown to and misrecognized by the ego — *der Kern unseres Wesen*, Freud writes in the chapter of the *Traumdeutung* on the dream process. When Freud treats the primary process, he means something with an ontological sense and calls it the *kernel of our being*. The kernel of our being does not coincide with the ego. This is the sense of the analytic experience, and it is around this that the experience has organized itself, depositing the strata of knowledge now taught. But is it enough to stop there and say: the *je* of the unconscious subject is not the *moi*? It is not enough, since nothing — for one thinking spontaneously — implies the converse. One slips normally into thinking that this *je* is the *true moi*. One imagines that the *moi* is only an incomplete, erroneous form of this *je*. So the essential decentering of Freud's discovery has been made — but immediately reduced. It is the same diplopia that oculists know: place two very close images, and through a certain squint they merge into one. So one slides the *moi* back into the *je* discovered by Freud and restores unity.

This is what happened in analysis. When, for reasons retrospectively to be elucidated, the first fertility of the analytic discovery began to exhaust itself in practice, one returned to what is called ego analysis, claiming to find in it the exact reverse of what had to be demonstrated to the subject. By analyzing the ego, one believed one was getting at the underside of what had to be made understood. One thus performed a reduction of the same order as the diplopia: two different images into one. The true *je* is not the *moi* — but it is not enough to say so, since one can always slip into believing the *moi* is only an error of the *je*, a partial point of view that a simple coming-to-consciousness would suffice to expand. The important thing is the converse, which must always be kept in mind: *the moi is not the je*, *is not an error* in the classical sense of a partial truth. *It is something else — a particular object inside the subject's experience. Literally, the ego is an object — an object that fulfills a certain function we here call imaginary*.

This thesis, Lacan insists, is absolutely essential to technique. He challenges his audience not to draw this conception from reading the metapsychological writings after 1920\. Freud's investigations around the second topography were carried out to put back in its place an ego that was beginning to slide back to its old position. Through a mental accommodation, the essential of the classical illusion (Lacan refuses to call it error — it is properly an illusion) was being reinstated. Everything Freud wrote aimed at re-establishing the exact perspective of the subject's eccentricity with respect to the ego.

### **Section 2**

The second section is the famous "materialist definition of consciousness." Lacan begins from a polemical remark. His audience's evident sense, the evidence of their psychological experience, is determined by a confusion of concepts of which they know nothing. We live at the level of concepts much more than we believe. The high, highly elaborated character of the phenomenon of consciousness is admitted as a postulate by everyone at this date of 1954\. He is sure there is not one person present who is not finally persuaded that, however partial the apprehension of consciousness, hence of the ego, may be, it is still there that one's existence is given. We think that in the fact of consciousness, the unity of the ego is, if not explored, then apprehended.

But what analytic experience puts in relief — and what leaves Freud as embarrassed as a fish with an apple — are *the illusions of consciousness*. In his 1895 sketches, Freud could not — although it would have been easy — situate the phenomenon of consciousness exactly within his already elaborated schema of the psychic apparatus. Much later, in the metapsychology, when he tries to explain the various pathological forms (dream, delirium, mental confusion, hallucinations) by disinvestments of systems, he always finds himself before a paradox when it comes to making the system of consciousness function, and he says there must be special laws. *The system of consciousness does not enter into his theory.* Freud's psycho-physical conception of investments in intra-organic systems is highly astute for explaining what happens in the individual; hypothetical as it is, what we have since gained in experience regarding the diffusion of nervous influx rather confirms the validity of Freud's biological construction. But for consciousness it does not work.

Lacan turns to philosophy. What gives consciousness its apparently primordial character? The philosopher seems to start from an indubitable given when he begins from the transparency of consciousness to itself. But Lacan will not pursue the metaphysical investigation of the problem of consciousness. He proposes not a working hypothesis — he insists it is not a hypothesis — but a way of cutting the Gordian knot. Some problems must be abandoned without being resolved.

It is, once again, a matter of a mirror. What is the image in the mirror? The rays that return on the mirror situate, in an imaginary space, the object that is otherwise somewhere in reality. The real object is not the object one sees in the glass. There is therefore a phenomenon of consciousness as such — and Lacan proposes that this be admitted in order for him to tell a little apologue.

Suppose all men have disappeared from the earth. He says *men*, given the high value placed on consciousness. That alone is enough to pose the question: what remains in the mirror? But let us go so far as to suppose that all living beings have disappeared. Only cascades and springs remain — and flashes and thunder. Do the image in the mirror, the image in the lake, still exist? They do — for a very simple reason. At the high degree of civilization we have reached, which goes well beyond our illusions about consciousness, we have fabricated apparatus that we can without boldness imagine complicated enough to develop their own films, put them in little boxes, and store them in the refrigerator. With every living being having vanished, the camera can nonetheless record the image of the mountain in the lake, or that of the Café de Flore crumbling away in complete solitude.

The philosophers will have all sorts of clever objections. Lacan asks his audience to continue paying attention. Now the men return. It is an arbitrary act of Malebranche's God — since He sustains us at every instant in our existence, He can easily have suppressed us and put us back into circulation a few centuries later. Men will perhaps have to relearn everything, and especially to read an image. No matter. What is certain is that as soon as they see on the film the image of the mountain, they will also see its reflection in the lake. They will also see the movements that took place in the mountain, and those of the image. Things can be pushed further. If the machine is more complicated, a photoelectric cell aimed at the image in the lake might have triggered an explosion — for something to appear effective, an explosion must always be unleashed somewhere — and another machine might have recorded the echo or collected the energy of that explosion.

This, Lacan proposes, is to be considered essentially as a phenomenon of consciousness — one that will have been perceived by no ego, that will have been reflected in no egoic experience, every kind of ego and ego-consciousness being absent at that epoch. One might say: the ego is somewhere — in the camera. No, there is not the shadow of an ego in the camera. But, on the contrary, Lacan will gladly admit that the *je* is in there for something — not in the camera itself, but in the symbolic order through which the camera was built. For it is insofar as he is engaged in a play of symbols, in a symbolic world, that man is a decentered subject. And it is with this same play, this same world, that the machine is constructed. The most complicated machines are made only of words. Speech is first of all this object of exchange by which one recognizes one another — because you said the password, we don't crack each other's skulls. The circulation of speech begins this way, and inflates to the point of constituting the world of the symbol that permits algebraic calculations. *The machine is structure as detached from the activity of the subject. The symbolic world is the world of the machine.*

The question opens of what, in this world, constitutes the *being* of the subject. Some are very worried to see Lacan referring to God. Yet it is a God we grasp *ex machina*, unless we extract *machina ex Deo*. The machine assures the continuity through which men temporarily absent will have the recording of what has happened in the interval of the phenomena of consciousness properly speaking. And here Lacan can say *phenomena of consciousness* without entifying any cosmic soul, any presence in nature. At the point we have reached — perhaps because we have engaged fairly well in the fabrication of the machine — we no longer confuse symbolic intersubjectivity with cosmic subjectivity. At least, he hopes so.

The apologue is forged not to develop a hypothesis but to perform an act of salubrity. Just to *begin* to pose the question of what the ego is, one must detach from what may be called the religious conception of consciousness. Implicitly, modern man thinks that everything that has happened in the universe since its origin is made to converge toward this thinking thing, this creation of life, this precious unique being, the summit of creatures, in which there is the privileged point called consciousness. This perspective leads to so delusional an anthropomorphism that one must begin to disabuse oneself of it. The silliness of scientistic atheism is new in humanity. As one defends oneself within science against everything that may recall recourse to the Supreme Being, one rushes elsewhere, taken by vertigo, to do the same thing — to prostrate oneself. There, nothing more is to be understood, everything is explained: consciousness must appear, the world and history converge on this marvel that is contemporary man, you, me, running through the streets.

The purely sentimental, truly incoherent atheism of scientistic thought pushes it by counter-blow to make consciousness the summit of phenomena. It tries — like one makes a too-absolute king into a constitutional king — to maintain consciousness as the masterpiece of masterpieces, the reason of all, perfection. But these epiphenomena serve no purpose. When one approaches the phenomena, one always proceeds as if one were not taking them into account. This very care not to take them into account marks well that if one does not destroy their scope, one becomes a cretin — one can no longer think of anything else.

Lacan adverts briefly to vitalism, embryology, and behaviorism. In embryology, the intervention of a formative form in the embryo is immediately taken to imply consciousness — as if there were a little demon in the embryo. One stops organizing what is manifest in the phenomenon, because one believes that whatever is superior implies consciousness. Yet we know that consciousness is tied to something altogether contingent — as contingent as the surface of a lake in an uninhabited world — the existence of our eyes or ears. Behaviorism reacted with taboos: let us observe total conducts, never mind consciousness. But this bracketing has not been very fertile. Consciousness is not the monster they believed it to be. Excluding it, chaining it, brings no real benefit. And in fact, under the name of molar behaviorism, it has been quietly reintroduced — because, following Freud, the behaviorists have learned to handle the notion of *field*. What progress behaviorism has made is due to its consenting to observe a series of phenomena at their proper level — total conducts considered in an object constituted as such — without breaking its head over what the elementary, lower or higher, apparatuses were. There is nonetheless in the very notion of conduct a certain castration of human reality. Not because it does not take consciousness into account — consciousness is of absolutely no use to anyone — but because *it eliminates the intersubjective relation*, which founds not simply conducts but actions and passions. This has nothing to do with consciousness.

Lacan asks his audience to consider — for the duration of this introduction — that consciousness is produced each time there is given (and this is produced in the most unexpected and distant places from one another) a surface such that it can produce what is called an image. *This is a materialist definition.* An image means that energetic effects starting from a given point of the real — imagine them of the order of light, since light is what most manifestly makes an image — come to be reflected at some point on a surface, come to strike the same corresponding point in space. The surface of a lake can just as well be replaced by the *area striata* of the occipital lobe, since the *area striata* with its fibrillar layers is altogether similar to a mirror. One does not need the entire surface of a mirror — if that means anything — to perceive the contents of a field or a room; one obtains the same result by manipulating a tiny piece. Likewise any little piece of the *area striata* serves the same use and behaves as a mirror. All sorts of things in the world behave as mirrors. It suffices that the conditions be such that to one point of a reality there corresponds an effect at another point, that a biunivocal correspondence be established between two points of real space.

Lacan corrects himself: he has gone too quickly in saying *real space*. There are two cases — either the effects are produced in real space or in imaginary space. He has just shown what happens at a point of imaginary space, in order to plunge ordinary conceptions into embarrassment. One can thereby grasp that everything that is imaginary, everything properly illusory, is not for all that subjective. There is a perfectly objective, objectifiable illusory; one does not need to make all his honorable audience disappear for this to be understood.

### **Section 3**

The third section turns the materialist definition back onto the ego. What then is the *moi* in this perspective? *The ego is indeed an object*. The ego that one supposedly perceives within the field of clear consciousness as that field's unity is precisely that vis-à-vis which the immediacy of sensation is put into tension. This unity is not at all homogeneous with what happens on the surface of this field, which is neutral. Consciousness as a physical phenomenon is precisely what generates this tension.

The whole dialectic that Lacan had given as an example under the name of the *mirror stage* is founded on the relation between, on the one hand, a certain level of tendencies experienced (let us say, at a certain moment of life) as disconnected, discordant, fragmented — and something of which always remains — and, on the other hand, a *unity* with which the subject becomes confused and apparelled. This unity is what the subject knows itself for the first time as unity — but as an *alienated, virtual unity*. It does not partake of the inertia of the consciousness-phenomenon in its primitive form; it has, on the contrary, a vital — or *counter-vital* — relation with the subject. Man seems here to have a privileged experience. Perhaps something of this order exists in other animal species; this point is not crucial. Let us not fabricate hypotheses. This dialectic is present in experience at all levels of the structuration of the human ego, and that suffices.

To make this dialectic vivid, Lacan reaches for an image he has not yet used: the *blind man and the paralytic*. Subjectivity at the level of the ego is comparable to this couple introduced by fifteenth-century imagery — and no doubt not without reason — in a particularly emphatic way. The subjective half before the mirror experience is the paralytic, who cannot move on his own except in an uncoordinated, clumsy fashion. What masters him is the image of the ego, which is *blind* and carries him. Contrary to appearances — and this is the whole problem of the dialectic — it is not, as Plato thinks, the master who rides the horse (the slave); it is the contrary. The paralytic, from whom this perspective is built, can identify himself with his unity only in *fascination*, in the fundamental immobility by which he comes to correspond to the gaze under which he is taken — the blind gaze.

A second image: the snake and the bird, fascinated by the gaze. Fascination is absolutely essential to the phenomenon of the constitution of the ego. It is insofar as it is fascinated that the uncoordinated, incoherent diversity of primitive fragmentation takes on its unity. Reflection too is fascination, blockage. Lacan will show this function of fascination — even of terror — under Freud's pen, precisely apropos of the constitution of the ego.

A third image, which prefigures the cybernetic models that will come later in the seminar. If machines could embody what is at stake in this dialectic, Lacan would propose the following model. Take one of those little turtles or foxes — the kind contemporary scientists amuse themselves with, the small homeostatic machines that have something resembling desires. Suppose such a machine is constituted so as to be incomplete: it will block, will only definitively structure itself in its mechanism, when it perceives — by any means whatsoever, a photoelectric cell, say, with a relay — another machine altogether similar to itself, with the single difference that the other will already have perfected its unity in the course of what can be called a *prior experience* (a machine can have experiences). The movement of each machine is thus conditioned by the perception of a certain stage reached by another. This is what corresponds to the element of fascination.

The circle that establishes itself is the constitution of the human object as such. Insofar as the unity of the first machine is suspended on that of the other — the other gives it the model and the very form of its unity — what the first machine will direct itself toward will always depend on what the other directs itself toward. The result is the impasse-situation that is the constitution of the human object. It is entirely suspended on this dialectic of *jealousy-sympathy* exactly expressed in traditional psychology by the *incompatibility of consciousnesses*. This does not mean that one consciousness cannot conceive another, but that an ego entirely suspended on the unity of another ego is strictly incompatible with that other on the plane of *desire*. An object apprehended, desired: either he or I will have it — it must be one or the other. And when the other has it, it is because it belongs to me.

This rivalry constitutive of knowledge in its pure state is evidently a virtual stage. There is no knowledge in the pure state, since the strict community of the ego and the other in the desire for the object opens onto something else: *recognition*. Recognition very evidently supposes a *third*. For the first machine, blocked on the image of the second, to come to an agreement — for them not to be forced to destroy each other at the point of convergence of their desire (which is in sum the same desire, since at this level they are only one and the same being) — the little machine would have to be able to inform the other, to tell it: *I desire this*. This is not possible. Granting that there is an *I*, this transforms itself immediately into *you desire this*. *I desire this* means: *you, other, who are my unity, you desire this*.

One might think that here we find that essential form of the human message according to which one receives one's own message back from the other in an inverted form. *Believe nothing of the kind.* What Lacan has just recounted is purely mythical. There is no means by which the first machine can say anything whatsoever, since it is *before* unity; it is immediate desire, it has no speech, it is no one. It is no more someone than the reflection of the mountain in the lake. The paralytic is aphonic; he has nothing to say. For something to be established, there would have to be a *third*, who installs himself inside the machine, the first one, for example, and pronounces an *I*. But this is altogether unthinkable at this level of experience.

This third is nonetheless what we find in the unconscious. But precisely, it is in the unconscious — it must be situated *there* for the ballet of all the little machines to be established, above them, in that elsewhere where Lévi-Strauss had said the system of exchanges, the elementary structures, hold. *In the system conditioned by the image of the ego, the symbolic system must intervene*, for an exchange to be established — something that is not knowledge but recognition.

From this it follows that the ego cannot in any case be anything other than an imaginary function, even though it determines at a certain level the structuration of the subject. It is as ambiguous as the object itself can be — of which it is, in some way, not only a stage but the identical correlate. The subject posits itself as operating, as human, as *je*, from the moment the symbolic system appears. And this moment is deducible from no model belonging to the order of individual structuration. To put it another way: for the human subject to appear, the machine, in the information it gives, would have to count itself, as one unit among others. This is precisely the only thing the machine *cannot* do. To be able to count itself, it would have to no longer be the machine that it is: anything can be done, except that a machine include itself as an element in its own calculation.

The next session, Lacan promises, will present things from a less arid angle. The ego is not only a function. From the moment the symbolic world is founded, it can itself serve as a symbol — and this is what we have to deal with. Since one wants the ego to be the subject, since one unifies it as function and as symbol, today's work has had to strip the ego of its symbolic, fascinating status that makes us believe in it. The next time, that status will be restored, and the close relation of all this to our practice will be seen. The date: 8 December 1954\.

## **V. Homeostasis and Insistence**

*Idolatry. The subject's self-counting. Heterotopy of consciousness. The analysis of the ego is not the analysis of the unconscious reversed.*

The fifth session takes up immediately where the fourth left off and develops two of its key themes: the subject's "self-counting" within a structure of exchange that requires the symbolic third, and a polemical clarification of what is at stake in Freud's metapsychological turn. The session is articulated around the encounter with Serge Leclaire's exposition of the previous evening, which had attempted a triangular schema of the subject — and which Lacan uses as an occasion to warn against *idolatry*, the constant temptation to *entify* the subject as a substance. The session's conceptual core is the distinction between *homeostasis* (the regulative function of the pleasure principle) and *insistance* (Lacan's preferred rendering of *Wiederholungszwang*, the compulsion to repetition). The session thereby installs the framework within which Freud's 1920 turn will be read: the analysis of the ego is *not* the analysis of the unconscious reversed.

Lacan opens with a self-deprecating image. He is glad that Freud's works are at hand and that he is not forced, by some divine intervention, to fetch them on a Sinai and thereby abandon his audience too quickly. What he constantly sees reproduced in close reading of Freud is something that, without being quite the adoration of the golden calf, is nonetheless an *idolatry*. The aim of his teaching is to pry the audience away from this tendency once and for all. He hopes one day to dissipate the penchant for too-pictorial formulations. Leclaire's exposition the previous evening, while not quite a prostration before the calf, had something of that order. The need to make images certainly has its value in scientific exposition as elsewhere, but not as much as is often thought — and nowhere does it conceal more traps than in the domain of subjectivity. *The difficulty in speaking of subjectivity is precisely not to entify the subject*. In trying to make his construction stand up, Leclaire had presented his model as a pyramid resting solidly on its base rather than on its point, and had thereby made some idol of the subject; he could not have done otherwise than represent it.

### **Section 1**

The first section resumes the dialectic of the previous session at the moment when the subject seizes its unity. The fragmented body finds its unity in the image of the other, which is its own anticipated image — a dual situation that sketches a polar but non-symmetric relation. This dissymmetry already indicates that the theory of the ego in psychoanalysis in no way joins up with the scholarly conception of the ego, which on the contrary rejoins a certain naive apprehension that is properly the psychology, historically datable, of modern man.

Lacan had stopped his audience at the moment when he was showing that this subject is, in sum, *no one*. The subject is no one: he is decomposed, fragmented. And he becomes blocked, aspirated by the deceptive yet realized image of the other — or, equivalently, by his own specular image. There he finds his unity. Taking up a reference from the most modern of mechanistic exercises that have such importance in the development of science and thought, Lacan had represented this stage of the subject's development in a model that has the merit of in no way idolifying the subject.

At the point where the previous session left things, the subject was *nowhere*. Two little mechanical turtles, one blocked on the image of the other through a regulating part of its mechanism. The first machine was suspended on the unitary functioning of the second and captivated by its movements — hence a circle whose essential linkage is given by this dyadic imaginary relation. The consequences for desire: what could the desire of a machine be, if not that of drawing on energy sources? Machines can really only feed themselves, as Grey Walter's little creatures do. Self-reproducing machines have not been built, nor even conceived — the schema of their symbolic has not even been established. The only object of desire one can suppose for a machine is its energy source. Since each is fixed on the point where the other goes, there will necessarily be collision somewhere.

Now suppose the machines are equipped with some sound-recording apparatus, and that a great voice — one may well imagine someone overseeing their functioning, a *legislator* — intervenes to regulate the ballet that has been until now only a round-dance and could lead to catastrophic results. *It is a matter of introducing a symbolic regulation*, whose schema is given by the unconscious mathematical sub-jacency of the exchanges of the elementary structures. The comparison stops there, for the legislator is not to be entified — that would be one more idol.

Leclaire intervenes. He has tended to idolify the subject, he admits, because he thinks it necessary, that one cannot do otherwise. Lacan replies, with mock severity, that Leclaire is a little idolater — he, Lacan, has come down from Sinai and broken the Tablets of the Law. Leclaire continues: in refusing this very conscious entification of the subject, Lacan tends to displace the idolification to another point — at which moment it will no longer be the subject but the other, the image, the mirror.

Lacan acknowledges the seriousness of the objection. Leclaire is not the only one to raise it. His transcendentalist preoccupations lead him to a certain substantialist idea of the unconscious; others have a critical-idealist conception, but they too think Lacan is bringing back what he expels. More than one in the audience comes from a traditional philosophical formation for which the grasp of consciousness by itself is one of the pillars of a worldview. This is something not to be treated lightly. Lacan had warned the previous time that he was taking the step of cutting the Gordian knot, taking the part of radically neglecting an entire perspective. Someone present (Lacan declines to identify him) had remarked after the previous session that, having severely mistreated consciousness, Lacan was bringing it back in the form of the voice that re-establishes order and regulates the ballet of machines.

This voice, Lacan's deduction of the subject demands that it be situated somewhere in the interhuman play. To call it the voice of the legislator would be an idolification — of a high order, certainly, but characterized. Is it not rather, Lacan suggests, the voice Valéry evokes in the *Cimetière marin* — the voice that knows itself when it sounds, becoming no one's voice as long as there are only waves and woods? It is of language that Valéry speaks here. And perhaps, indeed, this voice must in the last analysis be recognized as the *voice of no one*.

This is why Lacan had moved to the position of saying that we must demand that *the machine itself* take the ordering speech. And then, hurrying somewhat as one does at the end of a discourse where one must both close out and prepare a resumption, he had proposed: suppose the machine could count itself. For the mathematical combinations that order objectal exchanges (in the sense Lévi-Strauss had given) to function, *each of the machines in the combinatory must be able to count itself*.

### **Section 2**

The second section asks where this self-counting of the subject in its subjective function occurs — and answers: in the unconscious. This is one of the most manifest phenomena discovered by Freudian experience. Take the curious game Freud evokes at the end of the *Psychopathology of Everyday Life*: inviting the subject to say numbers at random. The associations that subsequently come to him raise significations that resonate so well with his remembrance and his destiny that, from the point of view of probabilities, his choice goes well beyond what could be expected from pure chance.

If the philosophers warn Lacan against the materialization of the phenomenon of consciousness — a materialization that would lose a precious foothold for grasping the radical originality of the subject in a Kantian or Hegelian world — Lacan would for his part warn the philosophers against an illusion not unrelated to the one made vivid by the Binet-Simon test, which is amusingly of its epoch. One thinks one can detect the mental age of a subject by inviting his assent to absurd sentences — among them: *I have three brothers, Paul, Ernest, and myself*. There is certainly an illusion of this order in believing that the subject's self-counting is an operation of consciousness, an operation tied to an intuition of consciousness transparent to itself. The model is not univocal, and not all philosophers have described it the same way.

Lacan does not pretend to critique the way Descartes proceeds, since there the dialectic is governed by an aim, the demonstration of God's existence, so that finally it is by arbitrarily isolating the *cogito* that one gives it a fundamental existential value. But it would not be difficult to show that, from an existentialist point of view, the seizing of consciousness by itself is at the limit unmoored from any existential grasp of the ego. The ego there appears only as a particular experience, tied to objectifiable conditions, within the inspection one believes to be simply the reflection of consciousness upon itself. The phenomenon of consciousness has no privileged character in such a grasp.

The aim is to liberate our notion of consciousness from every mortgage on the grasp of the subject by itself. Consciousness is a phenomenon — not contingent with respect to the deduction of the subject, but *heterotopic*, and this is why Lacan amused himself by giving a model in the physical world itself. Consciousness, he says, will always be seen to appear with great irregularity in subjective phenomena. In the perspective-reversal that analysis imposes, its manifestation always appears tied to conditions that are more physical, more material, than psychic.

Take the dream. Is the dream-phenomenon not in the register of consciousness? A dream is conscious. This imaginary shimmering, these moving images, are of the same order as the illusory side of the image one insists on apropos of the formation of the ego. The dream much resembles a reading in the mirror — a divinatory procedure among the most ancient, also usable in the technique of hypnosis. By fascinating himself on a mirror — preferably a mirror such as it has always been, from the beginning of humanity until a relatively recent period, more obscure than clear, a polished metal mirror — the subject can succeed in revealing to himself many elements of his imaginary fixations. So where is consciousness? In what sense should one seek it, situate it? Freud poses the problem in terms of psychic tension and seeks to know by what mechanisms the system of consciousness is invested and disinvested. His speculation in the *Entwurf* and the *Metapsychology* leads him to consider that it is a necessity of discourse to hold the system of consciousness as excluded from the dynamics of the psychic systems. The problem remains unresolved for him, and he leaves to the future the task of bringing the clarity that escapes him. He manifestly runs up against an impasse.

We are therefore confronted, Lacan says, with the necessity of a *third pole* — precisely what Leclaire had been trying to maintain in his triangular schema. A triangle is indeed needed. But there are a thousand ways of operating on a triangle. It is not necessarily a solid figure resting on intuition; it is just as much a system of relations. In mathematics, the triangle only begins to be truly handled when, for example, none of its edges enjoys any privilege. We are thus in search of the subject as self-counting. The question is where it is. That it is in the unconscious — at least for analysts — is what Lacan considers to have brought his audience to.

Pontalis intervenes — having, he says, recognized himself in the anonymous interlocutor who had remarked that Lacan was perhaps spiriting away consciousness at the beginning only to better recover it at the end. He had never said the *cogito* was an untouchable truth or that the subject could be defined by the experience of total self-transparency. He had never said consciousness exhausts subjectivity — which would in any case be difficult with both phenomenology and psychoanalysis. He had only said that the *cogito* represents a kind of *model* of subjectivity, that is, makes vivid the idea that there must be *someone for whom the word "as" has a sense*. This, Pontalis says, Lacan seemed to omit. In the apologue of the disappearance of men, Lacan had forgotten one thing: that men have to return for the relation between reflection and reflected thing to be grasped. If one considers the object in itself and the film recorded by the camera, this is nothing other than an object — not a witness, not anything. The same with the example of numbers said at random: for the subject to perceive that the numbers said at random are not so random, there must be a phenomenon — call it what one wants, but it does seem to be what is called consciousness. He does not see clearly why it is so important to demolish consciousness if it is only to bring it back at the end.

Lacan replies that the important thing is not to demolish consciousness; the seminar is not in the business of grand window-shatterings. What is at stake is the extreme difficulty in analytic experience of giving the system of consciousness a formulation in the order of what Freud calls energetic reference, of situating it in the interplay of the different psychic systems. The central object of the year's study is the ego. The ego must be stripped of the privilege it receives from a certain evidence which is, Lacan keeps emphasizing, only a historical contingency. The place this evidence has taken in philosophical deduction is one of its clearest manifestations. The notion of the ego draws its current evidence from a certain prestige given to consciousness as a unique, individual, irreducible experience. The intuition of the ego, insofar as it is centered on an experience of consciousness, retains a captivating character, from which one must extricate oneself to accede to our conception of the subject. Lacan is trying to draw his audience out of that attraction to allow them finally to grasp where, for Freud, the reality of the subject lies: *in the unconscious, excluded from the system of the ego, the subject speaks*.

### **Section 3**

The third section poses the decisive question. Between the two systems — the system of the ego (which Freud at one moment went so far as to say was all that was organized in the psyche) and the system of the unconscious — is there *equivalence*? Is their opposition of the order of a yes and a no, of a reversal, of a pure and simple negation? The ego no doubt tells us many things through *Verneinung* (negation, as in Freud's 1925 essay). Why not, while we are at it, simply read the unconscious by changing the sign on everything that is told? One has not quite gone that far, but one has come close.

The introduction by Freud of his new topography was understood as the return of the good old ego — texts, including those of the best analysts, testify to this, up through Anna Freud's *Mechanisms of Defense* written a decade later. It was a veritable liberation, an explosion of joy: *Ah, there he is back\! We will be able to occupy ourselves with him again, not only have we the right, it is recommended\!* This is how Miss Freud expresses herself at the start of the *Mechanisms of Defense*. The fact of occupying themselves with anything other than the ego had been felt by analysts — so strange the experience was for them — as a kind of prohibition on occupying themselves with the ego.

Freud, of course, always spoke of the ego. This function had always extremely interested him, *as exterior to the subject*. Do we, in the analysis of resistances, have the equivalent of what is called the analysis of material? Are operating on the moves of the ego and exploring the unconscious of the same order? Are the two systems complementary? Are they the same up to a change of sign? Are the unconscious and what contravenes its revelation like the inside and outside? If so, it is legitimate to speak — as one analyst, Eldorado, has dared to — of an *unconscious egology*.

Lacan refers to his very pretty article in the *Psychoanalytic Quarterly*, volume VIII, which puts forward as the essential keystone of this egology the *rid principle*. This is a new principle in analytic theory, found under a thousand forms, since it is what currently guides most analysts' activity. *To rid* means to get rid of something, to avoid. This new principle would govern from top to bottom all the subject's manifestations. It would preside over the most elementary stimulus-response process — the frog brushing off the bit of acid placed on its leg by a reflex whose spinal character can be easily demonstrated by cutting off its head — as well as over the reactions of the ego. References to consciousness are abandoned. This is an extremist position, particularly useful because it spells out coherently ideas usually veiled. But if there is something Freud wants to say at the moment he introduces his new topography, it is precisely the contrary. He intends to recall that between the subject of the unconscious and the organization of the ego there is not only absolute dissymmetry, but radical difference.

Lacan asks his audience to read Freud — three weeks until the next session. And while adoring the golden calf, they should keep a little book of the law in hand: read *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* with the little key of the introduction Lacan is giving. They will see that either it has no sense whatsoever, or that sense is exactly what he says.

Lacan now reconstructs the argument of *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* in its conceptual core. There is a principle from which we have set out, says Freud: that the psychic apparatus, as organized, lies between the pleasure principle and the reality principle. Freud, of course, is not given to idolification. He never believed there was no pleasure principle in the reality principle; if one follows reality, it is precisely because the reality principle is a *delayed pleasure principle*. Conversely, if the pleasure principle exists, it is in conformity with some reality — and this reality is *psychic reality*.

If the psyche has a sense, if there is a reality called psychic reality — in other words, if there are living beings — it is insofar as there is an internal organization tending up to a certain point to oppose the free, unlimited passage of forces and of energetic discharges such as can be supposed, on a purely theoretical level, to intersect in inanimate reality. There is a closed enclosure within which a certain equilibrium is maintained, by the effect of a mechanism now called *homeostasis*, which dampens and tempers the irruption of quantities of energy coming from the external world. This regulation — call it the *restitutive function* of psychic organization. The frog's leg gives a primitive idea: not only discharge but movement of retraction, which testifies to a still very primitive functioning of a principle of restitution, of equilibration of the machine.

Freud does not use the term *homeostasis*; he uses *inertia*, and there is in this an echo of Fechnerism. Lacan adverts to the strange two-facedness of Fechner. On the one hand, Fechner is the psychophysicist who affirms that only physical principles can permit one to symbolize psychic regulations. But there is another face of Fechner, less well known, which is singular: he goes very far in universal subjectification and would certainly have given a realist reading to Lacan's little apologue of the previous session — which was very far from his intentions. Lacan was not saying that the reflection of the mountain in the lake was a dream of the cosmos, but one could find that in Fechner.

Discharge and return to the position of equilibrium — this law of regulation holds for both systems, Freud posits. But he is led at the same time to ask: what is the relation between the two systems? Is it simply that what is pleasure in one is unpleasure in the other, and vice versa? If the two systems were the inverse of one another, one would arrive at a general law of equilibrium, and *then* there would indeed be an analysis of the ego that would be the analysis of the unconscious reversed. This, retaken theoretically, is the problem Lacan was posing.

It is here that Freud perceives that something does not satisfy the pleasure principle. He perceives that what comes out of one of the systems — that of the unconscious — has a quite particular *insistance*. Lacan introduces this term — *insistance* — because it expresses well, in a familiar way, the sense of what has been translated into French as *automatisme de répétition*, *Wiederholungszwang*. The word *automatism* resonates in French with a whole neurological ancestry. That is not how it must be understood. It is a matter of compulsion to repetition, and this is why Lacan thinks he is doing concrete work by introducing the notion of *insistance*.

This system has something disturbing about it. It is dissymmetric; it does not fit. Something escapes the system of equations and the evidences borrowed from the forms of thought of the energetic register as they were instaurated in the middle of the nineteenth century. The previous evening, Lacan adds, Professor Lagache had hastily produced the statue of Condillac. Lacan can only invite his audience to reread the *Treatise on the Sensations*. First, because it is an absolutely delightful read, of inimitable period style. They will see that Lacan's primitive state of a subject found everywhere — which is in some sense the visual image — has an ancestor. In Condillac, the scent of the rose seems an entirely solid starting point from which one can without the slightest apparent difficulty — like a rabbit from a hat — derive the entire psychic edifice.

The leaps in Condillac's reasoning leave us dumbfounded, though that was not the case for his contemporaries — Condillac was not delirious. Why, one may ask, does he not formulate the pleasure principle? Because, as La Palice would say, *he has not the formula* — because he is from before the steam engine. The age of the steam engine and its industrial exploitation, of projects of administration, of balance sheets, was needed before one would ask: *what does a machine yield?* In Condillac, as in others, more comes out than is put in: they were metaphysicians. Whatever one thinks — and the discourse Lacan is holding is not generally colored by a progressive tendency — there are nonetheless emergences in the order of the symbol. It was noticed at a certain moment that to pull a rabbit out of a hat, one has always to have put it in first. This is the principle of energetics, and this is why energetics is also a metaphysics.

It is the principle of homeostasis that obliges Freud to inscribe everything he deduces in terms of investment, charge, discharge, of energetic relations between the different systems. Now, he perceives that there is something that does not function within this. *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* is this — neither more nor less. Freud first attaches himself to a very local point, the well-known phenomenon of the repetition of dreams in traumatic neuroses, which contravenes the rule of the pleasure principle insofar as it is incarnated at the dream level in the principle of the imaginary realization of desire. Why the devil, Freud asks, is there an exception in this case? But it is not a single exception that can put into question something as fundamental as the pleasure principle, which is the principle of regulation allowing the inscription, in a coherent system of symbolic formulations, of the concrete functioning of man considered as a machine. This principle is not deduced from his theory; it is at the foundation of his thought insofar as in his epoch one thinks in that register. If one reads the text, one will see that none of the various exceptions Freud invokes seems by itself sufficient to put into question this principle. But the exceptions, taken together, seem to him convergent.

Lacan returns to Leclaire and the rabbit. Are we playing furet here? In any case Freud plays it. The phenomenon on which analysis is founded is this: aiming at remembrance, whether or not we encounter it, we find reproduction in the form of the transference of something that manifestly belongs to the other system. Leclaire intervenes once more — feeling somewhat singled out — to protest that he is not the one who put the rabbit in the hat: he pulled it out, certainly, but did not put it in. As for the subject of the unconscious, he had said he was figuring it, although in all rigor, like Jehovah, it ought to be neither figured nor named. He had nonetheless figured it knowing what he was doing. The idolification that Lacan accuses him of he feels Lacan is displacing onto the side of the other.

Lacan replies — with what is by now a familiar diplomatic acknowledgment — that he recognizes and even pays homage to the fact that Leclaire did things knowing what he was doing. What he had done was very controlled. As for the reef Leclaire was foreseeing, it is more than avoidable — already avoided. Leclaire: he simply has the feeling that this phenomenon of avoidance is produced every time one speaks of the subject. Lacan: avoidance — what is meant? Leclaire: *ridence*, the very one. Lacan: please, do not let us get lost. It is not the same avoidance.

The session closes by returning to the conceptual armature. There is a *restitutive function*, which is that of the pleasure principle. But there is also a *repetitive function*. How are they articulated? The subject can reproduce indefinitely an experience whose qualities one discovers by remembrance. God knows the trouble one has grasping in what way it satisfies the subject. Lacan explained this years before, apropos of the Wolf Man. What is this insistence of the subject to reproduce? Reproduce what? Is it in his conduct? In his fantasies? In his character? Even in his ego? All sorts of things, of extremely different registers, can serve as material and elements for this reproduction. The reproduction in the transference within the treatment is obviously only a particular case of a much more diffuse reproduction, encountered in what is called character analysis, analysis of the total personality, and other such nonsense.

Freud asks what, from the point of view of the pleasure principle, the inexhaustible character of this reproduction means. Is it produced because there is something out of order, or does it obey a different, more fundamental principle? Lacan leaves the question open — what is the nature of the principle that regulates what is at stake here, namely the subject? Is it assimilable, reducible, symbolizable? Is it *something*? Or can it be neither named nor seized but only *structured*? This will be the theme of the next term. The date: 15 December 1954\.

## **VI. Freud, Hegel, and the Machine**

*The death instinct. Freud's rationalism. The alienation of the master. Psychoanalysis is not a humanism. Freud and energy.*

The sixth session is one of the most ambitious sessions in the seminar's first half. Lacan situates the Freudian death instinct against the horizon of Hegel's philosophy of consciousness, and against the historical emergence of the modern machine — the clock, the steam engine, the calculating apparatus — that conditions the very possibility of Freudian metapsychology. Hyppolite had given an exposition on the *Phenomenology of Spirit* the evening before, and Lacan uses this as the occasion to develop his thesis that psychoanalysis is *not* a humanism. The session also makes a crucial methodological correction: Freud's rationalism is not the abdicating rationalism of progressive philosophy but a rationalism that refuses to stop before the opaque or the ineffable.

The session opens with the question Lacan had left hanging: what is the *Wiederholungszwang*, the compulsion to repetition (Lacan again rejects *automatism* in favor of *compulsion*)? He recalls that Freud had detached this *Zwang* from his earliest writings, which were only revealed later — the *Entwurf*. The pleasure principle, defined there, is a principle of constancy. There is another principle, with which the analytic theoreticians are as embarrassed as a fish with an apple: the *Nirvana principle*. Hartmann simply identifies the three — constancy, pleasure, Nirvana — as if Freud had never moved from a single mental category. One must ask why Freud would suddenly have called the beyond of the pleasure principle the Nirvana principle. The answer, Lacan suggests, lies in the dissymmetry between the two systems. If the primary and secondary processes were the simple inverse of one another, working on one would automatically work on the other; analysis of the ego and analysis of the unconscious would be the same task with reversed signs. Freud writes *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* precisely to explain that one cannot stop there. The manifestation of the primary process at the level of the ego — the symptom — is experienced as unpleasure, suffering, and yet it returns. This alone should arrest us.

### **Section 1**

The first section opens with Mannoni's question on the ambiguity of the *Wiederholungszwang* — does Freud maintain two senses of repetition, one as protection against trauma and one as a regressive movement toward an earlier state? Lacan accepts the ambiguity and ties it to Pontalis's earlier remark. The restitutive tendency and the repetitive tendency are interlaced, and Freud's thought does not oscillate so much as circle back on itself: each time he advances toward the new principle, he stops to ask whether it is not simply the restitutive tendency at work, and each time the data force him to admit that something escapes — something that on the plane of individual psychology presents itself as gratuitous, paradoxical, enigmatic, and is properly repetitive. The massive fact of reproduction in the transference finally imposes the decision to admit the compulsion to repetition as such.

Hyppolite presses the harder question. *Why* does Freud call it the *death instinct*? The phenomena beyond the pleasure principle are heterogeneous; the leap to the term "death instinct" opens perspectives that seem strange — return to matter, for example. Mannoni proposes "anti-instinct" as a better term. Hyppolite continues: once the name is in place, it leads Freud to discover further phenomena, to open perspectives not implied in what had pushed him to baptize it as death instinct in the first place. Lacan agrees. There is a "prodigious enigma" in the return to matter. To Bejarano's worry that Freud seems to identify self-preservation with death, and that this is as specious as identifying heat with cold, Hyppolite offers a more careful reading: in Freud's later articulation the life instincts tend to form increasingly bound organic groups, while the death instinct tends to return to the elements. The two are mixed in the unconscious; what is grave is their separation. Just as in love there is a component of aggression — without which there would be only impotence — together with a component of libido, and the two together make human love possible, so the death instinct emerges when the compound decomposes.

Lacan endorses Hyppolite's reading but locates it at the level of the immediate, the puppet-show. What interests Freud is the strings that move the puppet. This brings him to the major thesis of the session: is psychoanalysis a humanism? It is the same question as whether the *autonomous ego* of the Americans is in line with the Freudian discovery. What is the share of autonomy in man? Freud either constitutes a revolution on this point, or he does not. The third question follows: what is genuinely new from Hegel to Freud, if we place them in the same register?

Hyppolite then offers the rich suggestion that there are *two men* in Freud: a rationalist who thinks humanity can be rationalized — and this is the side of the ego, of *The Future of an Illusion* — and a more profoundly speculative figure, infinitely detached from curing men, hungry for a knowledge of altogether different depth, who emerges in the discovery of the death instinct. Lacan refuses the duality. There is no conflict in Freud — one could speak of conflict only if Freud's rationalism were incarnated in a *dream of rationalization*. But however far Freud pushed his dialogue with Einsteinian utopianism in *The Future of an Illusion* or *Civilization and its Discontents*, he never abdicates before the opaque. *Freud's thought deserves to be qualified, at the highest degree, as rationalist, in the full sense of the term, from one end to the other.* This text around which the seminar circles makes present the most living, most actual demands of a reason that does not abdicate before anything — that does not say "here begins the opaque and the ineffable." Freud enters, and even when he seems to lose himself in obscurity, he continues with reason. There is no prostration, no retreat to the mountain. Hyppolite reformulates more carefully: by rationalism he did not mean a new religion; on the contrary, the *Aufhebung* is a religion against religion. Lacan: the antithesis is precisely the death instinct. It is a decisive step in the grasp of a reality far surpassing what is called reality in the *reality principle*. *The death instinct is not a confession of impotence, not a stopping before the irreducible or the ineffable last word — it is a concept.*

### **Section 2**

The second section turns to the comparison with Hegel. Lacan takes Hyppolite's previous-evening exposition on the *Phenomenology of Spirit* as the framework. Hegelian *Bewusstsein* is much closer to *knowledge* than to *consciousness*. Lacan would have asked, had the audience been less reserved: what is the function of *non-knowledge* in Hegel? In the Hegelian perspective, the realization, the end of history, is *absolute knowledge*. The whole progress of the phenomenology of Spirit, Lacan suggests, is the audience itself — they are there for that, this is what they are doing even when they do not think it. The strings of the puppet again. The progress of the *Phenomenology*, he proposes, is an increasingly elaborated *mastery*. Hyppolite resists the simplification: "It depends what you put into mastery." Lacan invites the contestation, will not round the corners, will not slide his term in but show how it strikes against Hegel.

Hyppolite then offers a critical clarification of his own position. He is not a Hegelian, probably is against. He raises a major hermeneutic possibility: absolute knowledge may not be a moment in the sequence of experiences arrived at after Napoleon and the rest, but rather *immanent at each stage* of the Phenomenology — the consciousness in the field misses it; to see the field is absolute knowledge. Heidegger's reading, tendentious as it is, makes this possible. So one never gets past Hegel. Lacan accepts the qualification: even on this reading, absolute knowledge in Hegel is incarnated in a *discourse*. What is at stake in this discourse — present, on this reading, from the Neanderthal idiots onward — is that discourse closes on itself, that it is entirely in agreement with itself, that everything expressible in discourse is coherent and justified.

It is here that Lacan stops. The Hegelian completed discourse — the incarnation of absolute knowledge — is the *instrument of power, the scepter and the property of those who know*. Nothing implies that everyone participates in it. When the savants have managed to close human discourse, they possess it; those who do not have it must play jazz, dance, amuse themselves — the libidinous, the kindly, the gentle. *This is what Lacan calls elaborated mastery.* Even in absolute knowledge there remains a last ontological division in man. Hegel surpassed a certain religious individualism by showing that the reality of each human is in the being of the other; reciprocal alienation results, *irreducible, without issue*. What could be stupider than the primitive master? — a true master. The Germans advancing on Toulon to grab the fleet is a true history of masters. *Mastery is entirely on the side of the slave*, who elaborates his mastery against the master. This reciprocal alienation must last until the end. The elaborated discourse will be little compared to those amusing themselves with jazz in the corner café. The masters will aspire to join them; the others, considering themselves wretched nothings, will believe the master is happy in his enjoyment — when of course he is wholly frustrated. This is the limit Hegel brings us to.

*Hegel is at the limits of anthropology. Freud has stepped out of them. His discovery is that man is not entirely in man. Freud is not a humanist.*

### **Section 3**

The third section explains why. Freud is a doctor — but a doctor born a century after Hegel, when the meaning of "doctor" has changed. Freud is not a doctor in the line of Aesculapius, Hippocrates, or Saint Luke; he is a doctor more or less as we all are, which is to say no longer a doctor in the long tradition. It is a strange thing, an incoherence, to say that "man has a body." For us it makes sense; it probably always made sense, but for us more than anyone, because, being Hegelian without knowing it, we have pushed extremely far the identification of man with his accumulated knowledge. The Cartesian cut between mind and body cannot be undone by Thomist or Aristotelian declarations of unity. The doctor today regards the body as a man would regard a dismantled machine. This is the perspective from which Freud set out, and it was his ideal: pathological anatomy, anatomical physiology, the workings of the little complicated apparatus incarnated in the nervous system.

This decomposing perspective is troubling, scandalous; a whole tendency of thought (Gestalt theory and others) tries to return to the benevolence of nature, the pre-established harmony. Of course, Lacan adds, nothing proves the body is a machine, and there is every chance it isn't. But this is not the question. The important thing is that *this is how the question was approached*. The one who approached it this way was Descartes — and Descartes was not alone; many things had to be in place. In particular, a machine had to exist that not only worked on its own but could incarnate, strikingly, something thoroughly human. The machine in question, Lacan says, is *the clock*. Aragon describes it in the *Paysan de Paris* in the way only a poet can — a thing that pursues a human hypothesis whether man is there or not. Lacan recommends reading Descartes' *De l'homme* (cheaper than the *Discourse on Method*, which is dear to dentists) and checking that what Descartes seeks in man is the clock.

This machine is not what the common run takes it to be. It is not purely and simply the contrary of the living, the simulacrum of the living. That it was made to incarnate something called time — the mystery of mysteries — should put us on the path. That at the same epoch a certain Pascal set out to construct a modest adding machine indicates that *the machine is tied to radically human functions*. It is not a simple artifice like the chairs and tables around which we live without noticing they are our own portrait. The machines go much further on the side of what we really are than even those who construct them suspect. Hegel believed himself something like the incarnation of Spirit in his time, and dreamed Napoleon was the *Weltseele*; both of them, however, completely missed the importance of the phenomenon then dawning — the steam engine. Watt was not far off, and there were already things working on their own, little bombs in the mines.

The machine, Lacan claims, *incarnates the most radical symbolic activity in man*, and was necessary for the questions to be posed at the level at which we pose them. There is something in Freud spoken of that is not spoken of in Hegel: *energy*. This is the major preoccupation, the dominant one, and from a speculative point of view it is more important than the purely homonymic confusion between consciousness in Hegel's time and unconsciousness in Freud's. *Between Hegel and Freud, there is the advent of the world of the machine.* Energy is a notion that can appear only from the moment there are machines. Slave-owning societies never thought to establish equations between the cost of feeding slaves and what they produced in the *latifundia*. The notion of yield required machines. One then saw that machines tend to degrade — slaves too, but one assumed it natural that they aged and died. And further: that living beings *maintain themselves on their own*, that they represent homeostats.

From here begins to dawn modern biology — characteristically a biology that never invokes any notion of life. Vitalist thought is foreign to biology. Bichat, the prematurely dead founder of modern biology whose statue ornaments the old Faculty of Medicine, expressed it clearly: a new period had begun, and life would henceforth be defined in relation to death. This converges with what Lacan is explaining: the decisive character of the reference to the machine for founding biology. Biologists believe they are devoted to the study of life. There is no reason to. Their fundamental concepts have an origin that has nothing to do with the phenomenon of life, which remains in its essence completely impenetrable.

We will take biology by antiphrasis. *Freudian biology has nothing to do with biology.* It is a manipulation of symbols in view of resolving energetic questions, as the homeostatic reference manifests. Freud was no biologist — no more than any of us — but he placed the emphasis on energetic function throughout his work. If we know how to reveal the sense of this energetic myth, we see what was implied from the start in the metaphor of the human body as machine. We see manifest a certain *beyond* of the interhuman reference — what is properly the *symbolic beyond*.

Freud set out from a conception of the nervous system as always tending to return to a point of equilibrium. He set out from this because it was the necessity imposed on any doctor of his scientific age. Lacan asks Anzieu to look at the *Entwurf* and prepare a report. Freud tried to build, on this homeostatic basis, a theory of the functioning of the nervous system, showing that the brain operates as a buffer-organ between man and reality, an organ of homeostat. And he comes up short — runs aground — on the *dream*. He perceives that the brain is a *dream-machine*. And in the dream-machine he rediscovers what had been there from the start without being noticed: that it is at the level of the most organic and most simple, the most immediate and least manipulable, the most unconscious, that sense and speech reveal and develop themselves entirely. Hence the complete revolution of his thought, the passage to the *Traumdeutung*. It is said that he abandons a physiologizing perspective for a psychologizing one. This is not what is at stake. He discovers the functioning of the symbol as such, the manifestation of the symbol in the dialectical and semantic state, in its displacements — the puns, the wordplays, the laughs functioning all by themselves in the dream-machine. He had to take a position on this discovery, accept it or misrecognize it. It is such a turn that he did not even know what was happening to him. The session ends mid-sentence; the next session, "Le circuit," will continue. The date is implied as 12 January 1955 (the third week of the new term).

## **VII. The Circuit**

The seventh session, "The Circuit," continues the same arc. It develops two intertwined matters: a reading of pedagogy and tradition as the transmission of a *symbolic legacy*, and a sustained meditation on what the cybernetic notion of the *circuit* contributes to the analytic understanding of repetition. The session moves between concrete clinical material, the polemic against ego psychology, and the construction of conceptual scaffolding for the seminar's central thesis: that repetition is a symbolic, not a biological, phenomenon — that *insistence* belongs to the order of the signifier, not to the order of need.

Lacan begins by extending the historical genealogy from the prior session. The pedagogical procedures of ancient and modern teaching are of an absolutely heterogeneous register — Lucretius, he says, was clear and rather gay, but the relation between teacher and disciple has changed registers in a fashion that mirrors what is at stake in the analytic relation. Tradition is not the transmission of content from full master to empty pupil; it is the installation of a structure within which the subject finds himself already located, the structure that Lacan will increasingly call the symbolic order. The aside on Lucretius — and on the way "from time to time" the conscious tradition lets pass something genuinely new — prepares the larger thesis that *the symbolic order has its own dynamics*, which neither the conscious intention of teachers nor the receptivity of students explains.

The conceptual heart of the session is the development of the circuit-figure to think repetition. Lacan now formalizes what was already implicit in the earlier sessions: a cybernetic machine is structured by feedback, by a return of the output to the input, so that its trajectory is conditioned not by external stimulus alone but by the loop of its own previous states reincorporated. The clinical phenomenon of repetition — the way an analysand reproduces, despite himself, the same scenarios with new objects — is best modeled, Lacan claims, not by a biological tendency (which would push toward homeostasis and the cessation of repetition) but by such a closed circuit, in which the law of the system is *its own self-perpetuation as structure*. The structure repeats because *its repetition is what it is*. The Freudian observation in *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* — that the repressed material returns with a regularity untouched by the displeasure it brings — is captured by this model.

Lacan returns at length to the *fort-da* example. The eighteen-month-old child does not throw and retrieve the bobbin to *master* the mother's absence in any naïve sense; the action *is* the symbolization of absence, the inscription of presence and absence in the structure of the signifier (the two phonemes that accompany the throw and return). Lacan's reading anticipates what he will later say more crisply: the child is not staging a psychological scene but installing himself in the order of the signifier. From this moment, the loss of the object is no longer a deprivation that calls for compensation; it is the *condition* of the subject's existence in the symbolic register. Repetition, then, is not the persistence of an unmet need but the insistence of a chain of signifiers that has its own laws — that goes on regardless of what the subject feels about it.

The polemic against the analysis of resistances continues. Lacan reproaches the school of Anna Freud and her American successors for treating the analysand's repetitions as something to be overcome by ego-strengthening — that is, by suggestion — rather than as the very material in which the subject's truth is articulated. To interpret a resistance is not to dislodge it by pointing it out; it is to read what the resistance says, to hear the position of the subject in the structure it occupies. The session sketches what will later become a major Lacanian formula: the resistance is on the side of the analyst, not the analysand — meaning that what blocks the work is the analyst's failure to take up the position from which the symbolic material can be heard. The repetition is not the analysand's enemy but his speech.

Toward the close of the session Lacan returns to the schema of *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*. He repeats his demand that the audience read the text in its entirety and ties it back to Hyppolite's previous remarks. Freud's rationalism, he insists once more, is not a project of rationalization. The introduction of the death instinct is not the irruption of the irrational into the rational; it is the recognition that *the rational extends further than rationalization is willing to follow*. The repetition compulsion is rational — it has a structure, a regularity, it can be modeled; what is irrational is the demand that it be brought into the pleasure principle. The session ends with the announcement of the next section, which will turn to Freud's earliest text, the *Entwurf*, and to the apparatus there sketched.

## **Note on the scope of this commentary**

This commentary covers the opening movement of *Seminar II*: the two-session **Introduction** (Chapters I–II) and the complete section **Beyond the Pleasure Principle, Repetition** (Chapters III–VII). These seven sessions form a self-contained unit in the architecture of the seminar — Lacan's own first major section break falls after "The Circuit," before the turn to the Freudian schemas of the psychic apparatus (the *Entwurf*, the *Traumdeutung*, and the Irma dream) that occupies Chapters VIII–XIV, and before the explicit thematization of the symbolic and the big Other in Chapters XV–XXI and the concluding "Final" sessions.

Read together, these seven sessions accomplish a single sustained argument. They establish the seminar's central distinction between the imaginary ego (*moi*) and the subject of the unconscious (*je*); they relocate the analytic concept of the ego as an *object* with an imaginary function rather than as the seat of the subject; they install the tripartite framework of symbolic, imaginary, and real; they read Freud's 1920 metapsychological turn as the recovery of the decentering of the subject against the regressive pull of ego psychology; and they reconstruct the death instinct and the compulsion to repetition not as biological speculation but as the insistence of the symbolic order — the discourse of the Other — which has its own laws and runs its own circuit independent of the subject's lived experience and adaptive needs. The recurrent interlocutors (Hyppolite, Mannoni, Leclaire, Pontalis, Riguet) and the recurrent reference points (the *Meno*, Lévi-Strauss, Hegel, the cybernetic machine, Condillac, energetics and information theory) are the materials through which this argument is built.

The commentary throughout reconstructs Lacan's reasoning and situates it within the development of his thought; it is intended as a reader's companion and does not substitute for the seminar itself, which should be consulted directly (Jacques Lacan, *Le Séminaire, Livre II: Le moi dans la théorie de Freud et dans la technique de la psychanalyse*, texte établi par Jacques-Alain Miller, Éditions du Seuil, 1978; English translation by Sylvana Tomaselli, Cambridge University Press / Norton, 1988).

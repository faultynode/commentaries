# **Fifth Logical Investigation**

## **Edmund Husserl, *On Intentional Experiences and Their "Contents"***

*(Logische Untersuchungen, Vol. II/1 \= Husserliana XIX/1, pp. 352–529)*

## **Introduction**

In the opening paragraph Husserl reminds the reader of what the Second Investigation accomplished: it laid bare the sense in which a *species* in general is "ideal," and with it the specific sense in which *meanings* (Bedeutungen) are ideal — the sense relevant to pure logic. The point is then sharpened by a correlational claim. Just as every ideal unity has corresponding real possibilities and, eventually, real actualities, so the ideal meanings have corresponding *acts of meaning* (Akte des Bedeutens): meanings *in specie* are nothing other than moments of these acts grasped ideally, i.e., abstracted out as a species. But precisely this correlation opens new questions. There is a whole *genus* of psychic experiences (Erlebnisse) in which the highest genus "meaning" takes its origin, and there are *lower species* of these experiences in which the essentially different *kinds* of meaning unfold. So the task becomes a deeper and wider answering of the question concerning the *origin* of the concept "meaning" and its essential differentiations — deeper than anything the earlier Investigations offered. Bound up intimately with this is a further set of questions: meanings are supposed to lie in *meaning-intentions* (Bedeutungsintentionen) that can enter into a definite relation to intuition. Husserl has spoken repeatedly of the *fulfillment* (Erfüllung) of such intentions.

The thread is picked up: meaning-intentions are fulfilled through corresponding intuition, and the highest form of this fulfillment is supposed to be given in *evidence* (Evidenz). Hence a task arises — to describe this remarkable phenomenological relation and to determine its role, that is, to clarify the cognitive concepts grounded in it. Husserl stresses that for analytic investigation these tasks and the earlier tasks bearing on the essence of meaning (specifically the logical presentation and the logical judgment) cannot be separated at all. The phenomenology of meaning and the phenomenology of knowledge are one continuous problem-field.

The third paragraph announces a deferral and a precondition. The present Investigation will *not yet* take up those tasks directly; before one can attack them, a much more general phenomenological inquiry is required. The reason is laid out in a chain: "acts" are supposed to be the experiences of meaning; what is properly *meaningful* (das Bedeutungsmäßige) in any individual act is supposed to lie precisely in the *act-experience* (Akterlebnis) and not in the object, and to lie in whatever makes that experience an "intentional" experience "directed" at objects. Likewise the essence of fulfilling intuition lies in certain *acts*: thinking and intuiting are supposed to be acts that differ as acts, and the self-fulfilling itself is supposed to be a relation belonging specifically among the act-characters. But here is the difficulty: nothing in descriptive psychology is more disputed than talk of "acts." Doubt — if not outright quick rejection — may well attach to every place in the previous Investigations where the concept of act served to characterize and express Husserl's view. It is therefore an important precondition for solving the indicated tasks that this concept be clarified before all others. Husserl anticipates the result: the concept of *act* in the sense of *intentional experience* (intentionales Erlebnis) demarcates an important *generic unity* within the sphere of experiences grasped in phenomenological purity, so that classifying the meaning-experiences under this genus does in fact yield a valuable characterization of them.

The fourth paragraph adds two further components to the investigation of the phenomenological essence of acts as such. First, the clarification of the difference between *act-character* (Aktcharakter) and *act-content* (Aktinhalt). Second, and in this latter respect, the demonstration of the *fundamentally different* meanings in which one speaks of the "content" of an act. The word "content" is flagged from the outset as harboring a manifold of senses that must be sorted out.

The fifth paragraph widens the field once more. The essence of acts as such cannot be adequately discussed without entering, to a fairly considerable extent, into the phenomenology of "presentations" (Vorstellungen). Husserl invokes the familiar proposition that every act is either itself a presentation or has presentations as its underlying basis. But this very proposition raises a question: *which* of the very different concepts of presentation is to be brought in? Hence the separating-out of the phenomena that flow into one another and underlie the equivocations here becomes an essential part of the task. The equivocity of "presentation" is thus marked at the outset as a central obstacle.

## **The sixth and final paragraph of the Introduction states the *point of attachment* for the whole inquiry. Husserl proposes — not inappropriately, he says — to attach the treatment of the problems just roughly indicated (to which several others will intimately attach themselves) to the descriptive-psychological distinction of several concepts of *consciousness* (Bewußtsein) that flow into one another. He notes the customary phrasings: psychic acts are often called "activities of consciousness," "relations of consciousness to a content (object)," and sometimes "consciousness" is defined outright as a collective expression for psychic acts of every kind. The ambiguity of "consciousness" will therefore be the doorway through which the analysis of intentional experience is entered — which is exactly the program of the First Chapter.**

## **Chapter One: Consciousness as the Phenomenological Subsistence of the Ego and Consciousness as Inner Perception**

### **§1. The manifold ambiguity of the term "consciousness"**

The first paragraph situates the term "consciousness" within the practice of psychology. There, talk of *consciousness* and likewise of *consciousness-contents* and *consciousness-experiences* (ordinarily one says simply "contents" and "experiences") arises chiefly in connection with the *separation* of psychic and physical phenomena — the boundary by which, on one side, the phenomena belonging to the domain of psychology and, on the other side, those belonging to the domain of the physical sciences are supposed to be marked off. Husserl observes that the problem posed to us, namely to delimit the concept of *psychic act* according to its phenomenological essence, hangs very closely together with this separation question, precisely because the concept of psychic act *grew up* in exactly this context, as the supposed delimitation of the psychological domain. He then makes a discriminating point: a certain concept of consciousness has a legitimate application to the *correct execution* of this delimitation, whereas the determination of the concept "psychic act" yields *another* concept. In any case, what is needed is to distinguish several concepts that are *factually related* and that, on account of this relatedness, are easily run together. The whole section is thus framed as an exercise in conceptual disentangling.

The second paragraph announces the plan: in what follows, three concepts of consciousness will be discussed as the ones relevant to the present interests. Husserl then enumerates them. *First*, consciousness as the entire *real* (reell) phenomenological *subsistence* (Bestand) of the empirical ego — the interweaving (Verwebung) of the psychic experiences in the unity of the *experience-stream* (Erlebnisstrom). *Second*, consciousness as the *inner becoming-aware* (Gewahrwerden) of one's own psychic experiences. *Third*, consciousness as a collective designation for any kind of "psychic acts" or "intentional experiences." These three are the senses around which the chapter is organized; the first and second occupy this opening chapter, while the third is the genuine theme that opens out into the rest of the Investigation.

The third paragraph guards against the impression that these three exhaust the ambiguity. It hardly needs saying, Husserl writes, that the equivocations of the term in question are not exhausted by them. He recalls, by way of example, the turns of phrase current especially in non-scientific usage: of something "entering consciousness" or "coming to consciousness," of a "heightened" or "depressed" self-consciousness, of the "awakening of self-consciousness" — the last expression occurring in psychology too, but in a quite different sense than in common life — and the like. The point is that ordinary language carries still further senses that lie outside the three he has isolated for analysis.

The fourth paragraph draws a methodological consequence from this pervasive ambiguity. Because *all* the terms that could possibly serve to mark off these distinctions are themselves many-meaninged, the *unambiguous* determination of the concepts to be set off from one another is possible only on an *indirect* path. That path consists in juxtaposing expressions of *like* meaning and contrasting them with expressions to be *separated off*, together with suitable paraphrases and elucidations. Husserl announces that he will therefore have to make use of these aids — a frank acknowledgment that, where terminology is irremediably equivocal, conceptual clarity has to be won by circumlocution and contrast rather than by stipulative definition alone.

### **§2. First: Consciousness as the real (reell)-phenomenological unity of the ego's experiences. The concept of experience**

Husserl begins by setting out a *juxtaposition* of definitions. When the modern psychologist defines his science as the science of *psychic individuals* taken as concrete consciousness-unities, or as the science of the *consciousness-experiences* of experiencing individuals, or of their *consciousness-contents*, the placing of these terms side by side in this context fixes a certain concept of *consciousness* and at the same time certain concepts of *experience* (Erlebnis) and *content* (Inhalt). Under these latter titles the psychologist means the *real occurrences* — Wundt, Husserl notes, rightly calls them *events* — which, changing from moment to moment in manifold connection and interpenetration, make up the *real* (reell) consciousness-unity of the respective psychic individual. In this sense, perceptions, phantasy- and image-presentations, acts of conceptual thinking, surmises and doubts, joys and pains, hopes and fears, wishes and volitions, just as they run their course in our consciousness, are "experiences" or "consciousness-contents." And along with these experiences in their wholeness and concrete fullness, the parts composing them and their abstract moments are also experienced; they too are real consciousness-contents. Crucially, it does *not* matter whether these parts are somehow articulated on their own, whether they are delimited by acts specially directed at them, and in particular whether they can be objects of "inner" perceptions grasping them in their consciousness-existence or not. The first concept of consciousness, then, is the totality of the really-immanent (reell) constituents of the experiential stream, whether or not those constituents are ever separately noticed.

The second paragraph adds the decisive methodological qualification. This concept of experience, Husserl points out at once, can be grasped *purely phenomenologically* — that is, in such a way that all *relation to empirical-real existence* (to human beings or animals of nature) remains *switched off* (ausgeschaltet). The experience in the descriptive-psychological sense (the empirically phenomenological) then becomes experience in the sense of *pure phenomenology* (the footnote refers the reader to *Ideen I* of 1913). Husserl insists that the clarifying examples to follow can and must convince one that this demanded switching-off always stands within our freedom, and that the "descriptive-psychological" demonstrations first carried out on the examples are to be taken "purely" in the indicated sense and, further, to be understood as *pure essential insights* (a priori). And so, naturally, in all related cases. This is Husserl's signal that, although the analysis proceeds in psychological dress, its real yield is *eidetic*: claims about the essence of experience, not contingent facts about minds in nature.

The third paragraph gives the governing example: outer perception. The *sensation-moment color* (Farbenmoment), which forms a *real* (reell) component of a concrete seeing — in the phenomenological sense of the visual perceptual appearance — is just as much an "experienced" or "conscious content" as the character of perceiving and as the full perceptual appearance of the colored object. By contrast, the object *itself*, although perceived, is *not* experienced or conscious; and the coloring perceived *on* it is likewise not experienced. If the object does not exist — if the perception must be assessed critically as deception, hallucination, illusion, and the like — then the perceived, seen color, the color *of the object*, also does not exist. These differences between normal and anomalous, correct and deceptive perception do not touch the inner, purely descriptive (phenomenological) character of the perception. While the *seen color* — the color appearing-with on the appearing object as its property and posited as present in one with it — if it exists at all, certainly does *not* exist *as experience*, there *corresponds* to it in this experience (in the perceptual appearance) a *real* component: the *color-sensation* (Farbenempfindung), the qualitatively determinate phenomenological color-moment which in perception (or in a component specially belonging to it, the "appearance of the objective coloring") undergoes an objectivating *apprehension* (Auffassung). Here the whole later doctrine is already in germ: the immanent *sensation* and the transcendent *property* are not the same item viewed twice; the one is lived through, the other is the correlate of an apprehension performed *on* the sensational material.

The fourth paragraph attacks a popular conflation. Not infrequently the two — color-sensation and the objective coloredness of the object — are run together. Especially in recent days a way of speaking is fashionable that treats the one and the other as *the same*, merely considered under different "points of view and interests": considered psychologically or subjectively it is called *sensation*; considered physically or objectively it is called *property of the outer thing*. Against this Husserl needs only to point to the easily grasped difference between the objectively *uniform* red of a ball, seen as evenly colored, and the — precisely then, in the perception itself, indubitable and even *necessary* — *adumbration* (Abschattung) of the subjective color-sensations. The same red, seen as homogeneous over the surface, is given through a continuously varying, perspectivally shaded manifold of sensations; the difference between the steady objective property and its shifting subjective presentation recurs for every kind of objective property and the corresponding sensation-complexes. The phenomenon of adumbration thus refutes the "two viewpoints, one thing" doctrine outright.

The fifth paragraph carries the result from individual determinations to *concrete wholes*. The assertion that the difference between the content conscious *in* perception and the outer object perceived in it (perceptually meant) is a mere difference of *mode of consideration* — a difference whereby the *same appearance* is considered once in subjective connection (among the appearances referred to the ego) and once in objective connection (among the things themselves) — is *phenomenologically false*. Husserl then isolates the offending equivocation, which cannot be emphasized too sharply: the word "appearance" (Erscheinung) is used to designate *both* the *experience* in which the appearing of the object consists (e.g., the concrete perceptual experience in which the object is supposedly itself present to us) *and* the *appearing object as such*. The deception this equivocation breeds vanishes the moment one renders phenomenological account of *what of the appearing object as such is really (reell) to be found in the experience of appearance*. The thing-appearance (the experience) is *not* the appearing thing (what supposedly stands "over against" us in bodily selfhood). As belonging to the consciousness-connection, we *experience* the appearances; as belonging to the phenomenal world, the things *appear* to us. The appearances themselves do not appear — they are *lived through* (erlebt). This sentence states the whole asymmetry that Husserl is after.

The sixth paragraph multiplies and orders the distinct relations that the careless word "consciousness-content" tends to fuse. If we appear to *ourselves* as members of the phenomenal world, then physical and psychic things — bodies and persons — appear in physical and psychic relation to our *phenomenal ego*. This relation of the *phenomenal object* (which one also likes to call a consciousness-content) to the *phenomenal subject* (the ego as empirical person, as thing) must be kept apart from the relation of the *consciousness-content in our sense* (the experience) to *consciousness* in the sense of the *unity of consciousness-contents* (the phenomenological subsistence of the empirical ego). In the first case it is a question of the relation of *two appearing things*; in the second, of the relation of an *individual experience* to the *experience-complex*. Conversely, the relation of the appearing person-ego to the externally appearing thing must be distinguished from the relation between the *thing-appearance as experience* and the *appearing thing*. When we speak of this last relation, we are only bringing ourselves to clarity that the *experience* is not itself what is "intentionally" present "in" it — just as we establish that the predicates of the appearance are not at the same time predicates of what appears in it (the appearance of a red ball is not itself red and round). And yet a further, new relation is the *objectivating* relation, which we ascribe to the *sensation-complex experienced in the appearance* with respect to the *appearing object*: when we say that in the act of appearing the sensation-complex is experienced but is in a certain way "apprehended," "apperceived," and that in this phenomenological character of *animating* (beseelend) apprehension of the sensations consists what we call the *appearing of the object*. The apprehension is what breathes objective reference into otherwise mute sensational material; this animation-schema (sensation \+ apprehension \= appearance of an object) is one of the load-bearing structures of the entire Investigation.

The seventh paragraph generalizes the lesson and states the immediate purpose. Similar *essential distinctions* to those just found necessary in the case of perception — distinctions serving to separate what *in* the perception is genuinely experience (what really composes it) from what is in it only in an improper, "intentional" sense — must also be drawn for the *other* "acts." These distinctions will soon have to be treated more generally. For the moment Husserl's only aim is to block, from the outset, certain misleading directions of thought that could confuse the simple sense of the concepts to be clarified. The section thus closes by flagging itself as preparatory ground-clearing for the systematic account of intentionality still to come.

### **§3. The phenomenological and the popular concept of experience**

The first paragraph announces a second contrast to forestall confusion: *our* concept of experience does *not* coincide with the *popular* one, and here once again the distinction just indicated between *real* (reell) and *intentional* content does its work. The point is that "experience" in everyday speech and "experience" in the phenomenological sense are governed by opposite logics regarding what counts as belonging "to" the experience.

The second paragraph develops this through an example. If someone says, "I lived through (erlebt) the wars of 1866 and 1870," then what is called "lived through" in *that* sense is a complex of *outer events*, and the experiencing consists of perceptions, judgings, and other acts in which the events come to objective appearance and frequently become objects of a certain positing referred to the empirical ego. The *experiencing consciousness* in the phenomenological sense that is decisive for us does *not* contain these events, nor the things involved in them, within itself as its "psychic experiences," as its real (reell) components or contents. What it *does* find in itself, what is really present in it, are the relevant acts of perceiving, judging, and so on, together with their changing *sensation-material* (Empfindungsmaterial), their *apprehension-content* (Auffassungsgehalt), their *positing-characters* (Setzungscharaktere). So "experiencing" here means something wholly different from there. To "experience" the outer events meant: to *have* certain acts of perceiving, of knowing (however that be determined), directed at those events. And this *having* at once furnishes an example of the entirely different "experiencing" in the phenomenological sense, which says no more than that certain *contents are components in a consciousness-unity*, in the phenomenologically unified consciousness-stream of an empirical ego. This stream is itself a real whole that composes itself really out of manifold parts, and each such part is called "experienced." In this sense, what the ego or consciousness *experiences* is precisely its experience, and *between the experienced (conscious) content and the experience itself there is no difference at all*: the *sensed*, for example, is nothing other than the *sensation*. But if an experience "*relates*" to an object distinct from itself — as outer perception relates to the perceived, or nominal presentation to the named object — then *this object*, in the sense to be fixed here, is *not* experienced or conscious, but precisely *perceived*, *named*, and so forth. The decisive equation (experienced content \= experience) holds only for the really-immanent constituents; the transcendent object falls outside it.

The third paragraph legitimates and analyzes the word "content." This state of affairs, Husserl says, fully justifies the talk of *contents*, which here is thoroughly *proper*. The normal sense of the word "content" is *relative*: it points quite generally to an *encompassing unity* that possesses its content in the totality (Inbegriff) of the parts belonging to it. Whatever in a whole can be grasped as a part and truly *really constitutes* it belongs to the content of the whole. In the usual descriptive-psychological talk of "contents," the *tacit reference-point* — that is, the corresponding *whole* — is the *real consciousness-unity*. Its content is the total aggregate of the *present* "experiences"; and by "contents" in the plural one then understands these *experiences themselves*, i.e., everything that, as a *real part*, constitutes the respective phenomenological consciousness-stream. The first concept of consciousness is thereby pinned down precisely: it is the part–whole relation of the immanent constituents to the lived stream that contains them.

### **§4. The relation between experiencing consciousness and experienced content is no phenomenologically peculiar kind of relation**

The first paragraph draws a deflationary conclusion about the *ego*. After the foregoing account it is clear that the relation in which we *think* the experiences as belonging to an *experiencing consciousness* (or experiencing "phenomenological ego") points back to *no peculiar phenomenological finding* of its own. The "ego" in the sense of ordinary talk is an *empirical object* — one's own ego just as much as another's, and any ego just like any arbitrary physical thing, a house or a tree. However much scientific elaboration may modify the concept of the ego, so long as it keeps clear of fictions the ego remains an *individual thingly object* which, like all such objects, has phenomenally no other unity than the one given to it through its unified phenomenal properties and grounded in their own content-subsistence. If we separate off the ego's *body* from the empirical ego and then restrict the *pure psychic ego* to its *phenomenological content*, it *reduces to the consciousness-unity* — that is, to the real experience-complex which each of us, for his own ego, finds in part *with evidence* as present in us, and assumes, for the supplementary part, on good grounds. The phenomenologically reduced ego is therefore *nothing peculiar* hovering above the manifold experiences; it is *simply identical with their own connection-unity*. (A footnote records that in the first edition Husserl called the whole consciousness-stream the "phenomenological ego.") This is the famous "no-ego" or "bundle" position of the first edition.

The second paragraph explains *how* this self-constituting unity comes about without any extra principle. In the *nature of the contents* and in the *laws* to which they are subject, certain *connection-forms* are grounded. These run in manifold ways from content to content, from content-complex to content-complex, and finally a *unified content-totality* constitutes itself which is nothing other than the phenomenologically reduced ego itself. The contents have, like contents in general, their *lawfully determined* ways of going together, of *fusing* into more comprehensive unities; and in thus becoming and being one, the phenomenological ego or consciousness-unity has *already* constituted itself, without there being any need, beyond this, for a *peculiar ego-principle* that would bear all the contents and unite them all over again. Here, as everywhere, the *accomplishment* of such a principle would be *unintelligible*. Husserl appends a candid authorial footnote (added in the second edition) acknowledging that he *no longer endorses* the opposition to the doctrine of the "pure ego" voiced in this paragraph, referring the reader to the relevant sections of *Ideen I*. The section thus stands as a self-corrected document: the first-edition rejection of a pure ego is retained in the body, while the later Husserl flags his change of mind in the margin.

### **§5. Second: The "inner" consciousness as inner perception**

The first paragraph closes off the first concept and opens the second. After the considerations of the three preceding sections, the sense of the terms *consciousness*, *experience*, *content* is fixed — more precisely, a descriptive-psychological sense, and upon phenomenological "purification" a purely phenomenological one. Husserl resolves to hold fast to this sense henceforth, unless other concepts are expressly indicated. He then introduces the second concept: a *second* concept of consciousness expresses itself in the talk of *inner consciousness*. This is the "*inner perception*" (innere Wahrnehmung), which is supposed to *accompany* the actually present experiences — whether in general or in certain classes of cases — and to be referred to them as its *objects*.

The second part of the first paragraph analyzes the *evidence* usually ascribed to inner perception. That evidence indicates that one then understands inner perception as *adequate* perception (adäquate Wahrnehmung) — perception which imputes nothing to its objects that is not itself intuitively presented and *really* (reell) given in the perceptual experience, and which conversely presents and posits them exactly as intuitively as they are factually experienced in and with the perception. Husserl then states the general essence of perception as such: *every* perception is characterized by the *intention* of grasping its object as present *in bodily selfhood* (in leibhafter Selbstheit). Perception *corresponds* to this intention *in distinguished perfection* — it is *adequate* — when the object is really and in the strictest sense "bodily" present in the perception itself, grasped *without remainder* as what it is, and so *really enclosed* in the perceiving itself. From this it is self-evident, indeed evident from the *pure essence* of perception, that adequate perception can be only "*inner*" perception, that it can go only upon experiences given *simultaneously* with it and belonging together with it to *one* consciousness — and, strictly weighed, this holds only for experiences in the purely phenomenological sense. The argument is an essential one: only what is really contained in the lived stream can be grasped without imputation, and only the simultaneous immanent experience satisfies this condition.

The second paragraph guards against the converse and recommends a terminological reform. On the other side, one can by no means say conversely, simply and in psychological idiom, that *every* perception directed at one's *own* experiences (which by natural word-sense would be called inner) must be an *adequate* one. Given this ambiguity of the expression "inner perception," it would be better to maintain a *terminological distinction* between *inner perception* (as perception of one's own experiences) and *adequate (evident) perception*. With this distinction the *skewed* epistemological — and also psychologically exploited — opposition between *inner* and *outer* perception would also disappear, an opposition that is *foisted upon* the genuine opposition between *adequate* and *non-adequate* perception, the one grounded in the pure phenomenological essence of such experiences. (A footnote refers to the Appendix on inner and outer perception.) The real distinction is between adequate and inadequate giving, not between an "inner" faculty and an "outer" one.

The third paragraph diagnoses how the first two concepts of consciousness become entangled, with Brentano as the example. A close *relation* between the two concepts of consciousness treated so far comes about, in some researchers such as Brentano, through their believing themselves entitled to grasp the *being-conscious* (or being-experienced) of contents in the *first* sense at the same time as a being-conscious in the *second* sense. In this latter sense, what is conscious or experienced is what is *inwardly* — and with Brentano this always means at the same time *adequately* — perceived; whereas conscious in the first sense meant whatever is present *as experience in the consciousness-unity* at all. The equivocation that *pushes* toward understanding consciousness as a kind of *knowing*, and indeed of *intuitive* knowing, may here have recommended a conception burdened with all-too-severe drawbacks.

The fourth paragraph names the most serious of these drawbacks: the *infinite regress*. Husserl recalls the regress arising from the circumstance that *inner perception is itself an experience* and therefore requires a *new* perception, for which the same again holds, and so on without end — a regress that Brentano tried to resolve by distinguishing between *primary* and *secondary* directions of perception (so that the inner perception of an act is not a separate further act but a secondary moment of the same act). Since Husserl's aim here is purely phenomenological establishments, he must leave theories of this kind to one side so long as the *necessity* of assuming a *continuous action* of inner perception cannot be demonstrated *phenomenologically*. The section thus suspends Brentano's solution rather than refuting it, on the methodological ground that nothing is to be posited that phenomenological inspection does not show.

### **§6. Origin of the first concept of consciousness from the second**

The first paragraph asserts the priority of the second concept and lays out the path from it to the first. It is unmistakable, Husserl says, that the *second* concept of consciousness (inner \= adequate perception) is the "more original," indeed the "in-itself earlier." In a scientifically ordered manner one can advance from it, the *narrower* concept, to the *first* and *wider* one by the following consideration. If we lay claim to the *cogito, ergo sum* — or rather the simple *sum* — as an evidence entitled to assert its validity against all doubt, then it is self-evident that the "ego" here cannot be the *empirical* ego. But since on the other hand we must concede that the evidence of the proposition *I am* cannot depend on knowledge and acceptance of the ever-questionable philosophical concepts of the ego, we may best say: in the judgment *I am*, the evidence hangs upon a *certain core* of the empirical ego-presentation, a core not delimited in conceptual sharpness.

The second paragraph asks what belongs to this core. Raising the further question of what may belong to this conceptually unfixed and therefore *unsayable* core — what, that is, makes up at any moment the *given* in the empirical ego with evident certainty — it lies nearest at hand to point to the *judgments of inner (= adequate) perception*. Not only the *I am* is evident, but countless judgments of the form *I perceive this or that* — namely insofar as I do not merely *mean* but am *assured with evidence* that the perceived is also *given* as what it is meant to be; that I myself grasp it as what it is. For example: *this pleasure that fills me; this phantasy-appearance now hovering before me*, and the like. All these judgments share the fate of the judgment *I am*: they are not perfectly conceptually graspable and expressible; they are evident only in their living, but not adequately communicable, *intention*. The adequately perceived — whether or not it comes to expression in such vague assertions — now makes up the epistemologically *first* and absolutely secure domain of what the *reduction of the phenomenal empirical ego to its purely phenomenologically graspable content* yields at the moment in question. Conversely, it will be correct that in the judgment *I am*, the adequately perceived precisely constitutes, under the "ego," the *core* that *first of all* makes possible and grounds the evidence. (Here a long second-edition footnote, in brackets, candidly registers Husserl's later self-correction: the empirical ego is a transcendence of the same dignity as the physical thing; if switching off this transcendence and reducing to the purely phenomenologically given left *no* pure ego as residue, then there could be no genuine adequate evidence "I am" — and since this adequate evidence really does exist, the assumption of a *pure ego* cannot be evaded, for it is precisely the ego grasped in the performance of the *cogito*\-evidence.)

The third paragraph extends the secure domain through *retention* and *recollection*. To this domain there now accrues a *further* one when we reduce, to its *past phenomenological content*, all that the *retention* essentially attached to perception attests as having-been just present to us, and likewise all that *recollection* (Wiedererinnerung) attests as belonging to an earlier experiential actuality — when, that is, by reflection "*in*" retention and memory we go back to the *reproductively phenomenological*. We proceed in the same way with what we may *assume*, on empirical grounds, as *coexisting* with the adequately perceived of each moment, or as having coexisted with that reflective subsistence of retention and recollection, and indeed as hanging together with it in *continuously unified* fashion. By "continuously unified connection" Husserl means the unity of the concrete phenomenological *whole*, whose parts are either *moments* that mutually found (and so demand) one another in coexistence, or *pieces* that by their own nature found *unity-forms* in coexistence — forms that really belong to the content of the whole as moments really inhabiting it. And the unities of coexistence pass *continuously* over into one another from moment to moment; they constitute a *unity of change* — that of the *consciousness-flux* — which for its part demands continuous persistence or continuous alteration of at least one moment essential to the unity of the whole and therefore inseparable from it as a whole.

The fourth paragraph identifies *immanent time* as that all-pervading form. This role is played above all by the *form of presentation of the time immanent to the consciousness-flux* as a temporally appearing unity — not the time of the thingly world, but the time that appears *with* the consciousness-flux itself, in which it flows. Every time-point of this time presents itself in a *continuous adumbration*, so to speak, of "time-sensations"; every actual *phase* of the consciousness-flux possesses — insofar as a whole *time-horizon* of the flux presents itself in it — a *form* overarching all its content, a form that remains *continuously identical* while its content constantly changes. This is Husserl's compressed gesture toward the structure of inner time-consciousness: a standing form across flowing material.

The fifth paragraph states the upshot and the *widening* of the concept of experience. This, then, makes up the phenomenological content of the *ego* — the empirical ego in the sense of the *psychic subject*. The reduction to the phenomenological yields this *really self-enclosed, temporally self-developing unity of the "experience-stream."* The concept of *experience* has thereby *widened*: from the "inwardly perceived" and in that sense conscious (the second concept) to the concept of the "*phenomenological ego*" that *intentionally constitutes* the empirical ego (the first concept). The genesis is thus complete: starting from the adequately self-given (the second, narrower concept), and adding retention, recollection, and lawfully connected coexistence, one arrives at the full immanent stream (the first, wider concept). (A textual footnote records that the sentence which would have followed, together with the whole of §7, was *struck* in the B-edition; the next section therefore survives only from the first edition.)

### **§7. Mutual delimitation of psychology and natural science**

*(This section was struck in the second edition and survives from the first; it is printed in smaller type.)*

The first paragraph defines the task of psychology and sets the empirical ego over against the physical thing. Psychology has — *descriptively* — to study the ego-experiences (or consciousness-contents) according to their essential *kinds* and *complexion-forms*, in order then — *genetically* — to seek out their coming-to-be and passing-away, the causal forms and laws of their formation or transformation. The consciousness-contents are for it *contents of egos*, so it also has the task of investigating the real essence of egos (no mystical, but only an empirically grounded in-itself), the composition of psychic elements into egos, and further their development and decay. Over against the empirical egos stand the empirical physical things, the *non-egos*, likewise unities of coexistence and succession and with the claim of *thingly existence*. To us, who *are* egos, these are given only as *intentional unities* — that is, as unities *meant*, presented, or judged in psychic experiences. But they are not on that account themselves *mere presentations*, any more than are the egos foreign relative to us, of which the same holds. The physical things are given to us, they stand before us, they are *objects* — that is, we have certain perceptions and judgments adapted to them, which are "directed at these objects." To the *system* of all such perceptions and judgments there corresponds, as intentional *correlate*, the physical world.

The second paragraph refines the correlate by reference-frame and marks an asymmetry between psychic and physical givenness. One would distinguish more closely according to whether we consider the system of these judgments in single individuals, in a community of individuals (as a judgment-system common to them), or in the unity of science: the world of the individual ego, the world of the empirical social community, possibly the world of an ideal community of knowers — the world of (ideally completed) science, the world *in itself*. The psychic experiences and the egos too document themselves, in their being and lawful connections, only in science as a system of objectively valid presentations and judgments, and they are given only as *target-points* of intentional experiences in egos. But in a certain *narrower sphere* they are truly given *as what they are*, whereas this *never* takes place for physical things at all. The Berkeley–Hume doctrine, which reduces the appearing bodies to *bundles* of "ideas," does not do justice to the fact that, even if the *elementary* ideas of these bundles are psychically realizable, the bundles themselves — the intended *complexions* of the elements — never were and never will be really present in any human consciousness as complex ideas. No body is *inwardly perceivable* — not because it is "physical," but because, e.g., the three-dimensional spatial form is *adequately* intuitable in no consciousness. And adequate intuition is the same as inner perception. The fundamental defect of phenomenalist theories is that they do not distinguish between the *appearance* (as intentional experience) and the *appearing object* (the subject of the objective predicates), and therefore identify the experienced sensation-complex with the complex of objective marks. In any case, the objective unities of psychology and those of natural science are *not identical* — at least not as they await scientific elaboration as first givennesses.

The third paragraph turns to whether the two sciences will ultimately appear as separate, and counsels caution. Whether psychology and natural science will, in completed development, present themselves as separate depends on whether on both sides it really is a matter of *separate*, or at least relatively independent, realities (independence here not meaning that the two-sided realities must be separated by mystical abysses or wholly unheard-of differences). Better perhaps to reverse the question: whether such a separation obtains can be taught only by the *progress* of the two sciences. What is certain is that, according to their *starting-points* — the originary sphere of facts they undertake to work on — and also in their ascending advance, they are to a considerable extent *independent* of one another.

The fourth paragraph addresses phenomenalism's possibility while insisting it cannot decide the boundary question in advance. The possibility that phenomenalism sets up as a grounded theory is not excluded (in Husserl's estimation it has not gotten beyond vague, though by no means worthless, trains of thought): that the *objective grounds* of all talk of physical things and events lie in mere lawful *correlations* established among the psychic experiences of the manifold consciousnesses. But the separation of the sciences would *not* be abolished by accepting this theory. The distinction of *experiences* (consciousness-contents) from the *non-experiences* presented (and even perceived, or held to exist judgmentally) in experiences would remain, as before, the *foundation* for the division of the sciences as research-fields — that is, for the kind of division that alone can come into question at the present stage of development. To the demand for a "*psychology without a soul*" — a psychology abstaining from all metaphysical presumptions about the soul (abstaining because these could become insights only in completed science) — there corresponds the demand for a "*natural science without bodies*," a natural science that for now refuses all theories about the metaphysical nature of the physical. But the phenomenalist theory is *itself* such a metaphysically prejudging theory. It may *not* precede the question of the division of the two sciences. This division must rest on *purely phenomenological* ground; and in this respect Husserl believes the foregoing discussions well suited to settle the much-disputed question satisfactorily, since they employ alone the *most fundamental phenomenological difference* — that between *descriptive content* and *intended object* of perceptions and of "acts" in general.

The fifth paragraph credits earlier thinkers while faulting their inconsistency. This difference, of course, has not escaped the psychologists. We already find it in Hobbes, Descartes, and Locke; one can say that all the greater thinkers of modern times have occasionally touched on or treated it. Unfortunately, they do this only *occasionally*, instead of *beginning* with this difference and taking the most exact account of it at every step — in other words, instead of making it the *foundation of scientific epistemology and psychology*. Only thus does the way of speaking and thinking become scientifically correct (the text breaks off into a fragmentary line, the passage being one of the struck portions). The methodological moral is that the intentional difference, intermittently noticed by the tradition, must be installed as the *systematic basis* of the whole enterprise.

### **§8. The pure ego and consciousness-ness (Bewußtheit)**

The first paragraph introduces the doctrine of the *pure ego* through Natorp, whom Husserl quotes at length. Hitherto, Husserl notes, he has not thought of the *pure ego* (the ego of "pure apperception") at all — the ego which, according to researchers close to Kant but also some empirical ones, is supposed to provide the unitary *reference-point* to which all consciousness-content as such refers in an entirely unique way. To the *fact* of "subjective experiencing" or consciousness this pure ego is supposed to belong essentially. "*Being-conscious is relation to the ego*," and what stands in this relation is consciousness-content. Natorp's formulations are cited: "Content we call everything that is referred to an ego only in consciousness, whatever its further character may be." This relation is, for all however manifoldly changing content, evidently *one and the same*; it is really what constitutes the *common and specific* element of consciousness. Natorp marks it off, to distinguish it from the *total fact* of consciousness, by the special expression *consciousness-ness* (Bewußtheit). The ego, as the *subjective relation-center* to all contents conscious to me, stands *incomparably* over against these contents; it does not have to them a relation of the *same kind* as they to it; it is not conscious *to* its contents as the content is conscious to it. It shows itself precisely as equal only to itself, in that *other things* can be conscious to it, but *it itself* can never be conscious to another. It cannot itself *become content* and is in nothing like what may be content of consciousness. (A footnote inserted into the running text adds Husserl's distinction: the consciously appearing in the narrower sense is *appearing*, hence psychic *phenomenon*; but by far the greater part of what is conscious in the wider sense is not properly appearing, since one may not assert that everything psychic is perceived or even perceivable — so the definition of psychology as science of psychic *phenomena* designates only the nearest points of attack, not the whole object-domain.)

The second paragraph continues Natorp's argument about the *indescribability* of the ego. The ego therefore cannot be more closely described at all; for everything by which we might try to describe the ego, or the relation to it, could be taken only from the *content* of consciousness, and so would not strike the ego itself, or the relation to it. Otherwise put: every *presentation* we might make of the ego would make it an *object*; but we have already ceased to think it *as ego* in thinking it as object. To *be-ego* means not to be object, but, over against all objects, to be *that for which something is object*. The same holds of the relation to the ego: *being-conscious* means *being-object for an ego*, and this being-object cannot itself in turn be made an object.

The third paragraph reports Natorp's conclusion that consciousness-ness is a basic, underivable fact. "The fact of consciousness-ness, although the *basic fact* of psychology, can indeed be ascertained as present, made noticeable by *separating-out* (Aussonderung), but it cannot be *defined*, nor *derived* from anything else."

The fourth paragraph begins Husserl's critique. Impressive as these expositions are, on close consideration he cannot *confirm* them. How should we *establish* that "basic fact of psychology" if we do not *think* it, and how should we think it without "making objects" of ego and consciousness as objects of the establishing? This would hold even if we could relate to this fact only through indirect, symbolic thoughts; but according to Natorp it is supposed to be a "basic fact," which as such must surely be given to us in *direct intuition*. Indeed Natorp expressly teaches that it can be "ascertained as present and made noticeable by separating-out." But is the ascertained, the noticed, not *content*? Does it not there become *objective*? A *narrower* concept of object may perhaps be excluded; but it is the *wider* concept that matters first. Just as the turning of *noticing* upon a thought, a sensation, a stirring of unease makes these experiences *objects of inner perception* without thereby making them objects in the sense of *things*, so that relation-center "ego," and every determinate relation of the ego to a content, *as noticed*, would also be *objectively given*. Natorp's own admission that the ego can be "noticed" thus undercuts his claim that it can never be objective.

The fifth paragraph states Husserl's central negative finding (in its first-edition voice). He must confess that he is *simply unable to find* this primitive ego as a necessary relation-center. What alone he is able to *notice*, hence to *perceive*, is the *empirical ego* and its *empirical relation* to those of its own experiences or outer objects that have, at the given moment, become objects of a special "turning-toward," while "outside" as "inside" much remains over that *lacks* this relation to the ego. (A famous second-edition footnote reverses this: "Meanwhile I have learned to find it — that is, learned not to let myself be misled, by worries about the aberrations of ego-metaphysics, in the pure grasping of the given." This footnote is the recantation that pairs with the one at §6.)

The sixth paragraph offers Husserl's alternative analysis. He can find no other way to clarify the matter than to subject the empirical ego, with its empirical relation to objects, to a *phenomenological analysis*; and then the conception advanced above results necessarily. We separated off the ego-body, which appears as a physical thing like any other, and considered the *spiritual ego* empirically bound to it and appearing as belonging to it. Reduced to the *phenomenologically actually given*, this ego yields the *complex of reflectively graspable experiences* described above. This complex stands to the psychic ego *analogously* as the "side falling into perception" of a perceived outer thing stands to the whole thing. The conscious intentional relation of the ego to its objects Husserl can understand only thus: that to the phenomenological total subsistence of the consciousness-unity there belong precisely such *intentional experiences* in which the ego-body, the ego as spiritual person, and so the whole empirical ego-subject (the ego, the human being) is the *intentional object* — and that such intentional experiences at the same time make up an essential phenomenological *core* of the phenomenal ego.

The seventh paragraph announces the transition and answers the objection about objectivity. With this we stand before the *third* concept of consciousness, demarcated precisely by the *acts* or *intentional experiences*, which the next chapter will analyze. Whoever disputes the peculiarity of intentional experiences — whoever will not acknowledge what we hold most certain, that being-object, phenomenologically speaking, *lies in certain acts* in which something appears or is thought as object — will indeed be unable to understand how *being-object* can itself in turn become objective. For us the matter is quite clear: acts "direct themselves" upon the peculiarity of acts in which something appears; or acts direct themselves upon the empirical ego and its relation to the object. The phenomenological *core* of the (empirical) ego is here formed by acts that "bring objects to consciousness" for it; "in" them the ego "directs itself" at the relevant object.

The eighth paragraph rejects the claim that the ego–content relation is *without differences*. Husserl cannot see how the assertion can hold that the relation of the ego to the consciousness-content is *bare of all differences*. For if by "content" the *experience* (the real constituent of the phenomenological ego) is understood, then the way in which the contents *fit into* the experience-unity depends entirely on the *particularity* of the contents, just as with the fitting of parts into wholes in general. But if by "content" some *object* is meant — at which consciousness directs itself as perceiving, imagining, remembering or expecting, conceptually presenting or predicating — then there are all the more *obvious* differences, which emerge already in the mere stringing-together of the expressions just used. Either way, the alleged uniform, difference-free relation does not exist.

The ninth paragraph defends *self-perception of the empirical ego*. Perhaps one takes offense at Husserl's claim that the ego *appears to itself*, has *consciousness and specifically perception* of itself. But the self-perception of the empirical ego is the everyday matter that offers understanding no difficulties. The ego is perceived just as well as any outer thing. That the object does not fall into perception with all its parts and sides matters here as little as there; for it is *essential* to perceiving to be a *supposed grasping* of the object, not an *adequate intuiting*. The perceiving itself, though it belongs to the ego according to its phenomenological subsistence, naturally does not — like so much else that is "conscious" but not noticed — fall into the *grasping glance* of perception, somewhat as the *ungrasped yet appearing moments* of a perceived outer thing do not fall into the perception. All the same, the ego there and the thing here are called *perceived*; and perceived — conscious in the manner of *bodily self-presence* — it indeed is.

## **The tenth paragraph is the second-edition *Addendum* explicitly relativizing the whole quarrel. It is to be expressly emphasized, Husserl says, that the position taken here on the question of the pure ego (the position he no longer endorses) *remains irrelevant for the investigations of this volume*. However important this question is otherwise, and as a purely phenomenological question, the most comprehensive problem-spheres of phenomenology — those concerning, in a certain generality, the *real content* of intentional experiences and their *essential relation* to intentional objects — can be subjected to systematic investigation without taking any position at all on the ego-question. The present investigations restrict themselves exclusively to such spheres. With regard to the fact that so significant a work as the recently appeared first volume of the second redaction of Natorp's *Introduction to Psychology* engages with the above expositions, Husserl has not simply struck them. The chapter thus closes with a methodological firewall: the result of the entire Fifth Investigation does not stand or fall with the ego-doctrine debated in this opening chapter.**

## **Chapter Two: Consciousness as Intentional Experience**

The chapter opens with a brief transitional paragraph. The analysis of the *third* concept of consciousness — which now coincides with the concept "psychic act" according to its phenomenological essential composition — requires more extensive treatment. In connection with it, the talk of *conscious contents*, especially of contents of our presentations, judgments, and so on, also gains a *manifold* meaning, which it is of the greatest importance to sort out and investigate most exactly. This sets the agenda: define the act, then untangle the senses of "content."

### **§9. The significance of Brentano's delimitation of "psychic phenomena"**

The first paragraph credits and qualifies Brentano's class-distinction. Among the class-delimitations of descriptive psychology none is more remarkable, and in philosophical respect more significant, than the one Brentano carried out under the title of "*psychic phenomena*" and used for his well-known division of phenomena into psychic and physical. Husserl is careful to say he cannot endorse the *conviction* that guided the great researcher here and expressed itself already in the chosen terms — namely, the conviction of having won an *exhaustive classification* of "phenomena" by which the research-fields of psychology and natural science would be separated and the disputed question of their correct determination settled in quite a simple way.

The second paragraph presses the criticism. It may well be, Husserl grants, that a good sense can be supplied to the definition of psychology as the science of *psychic* phenomena and the coordinate definition of natural science as the science of *physical* phenomena; but it can be contested on serious grounds that the concepts of Brentano's distinction are the ones that figure homonymously in those definitions. It could be shown that by no means all psychic phenomena in the sense of a *possible* definition of psychology are likewise such in Brentano's sense (psychic acts), and that on the other side, under the equivocally functioning title "physical phenomena," a good part of *genuinely psychic* phenomena is to be found. (A footnote indicates that Husserl's divergent conception does not move in the direction of the *restrictions* Brentano himself, aware of the inadequacy of the plain determinations, thought necessary; the matter is treated in the Appendix at the volume's close.)

The third paragraph rescues the *value* of Brentano's conception from its classificatory purpose. Indeed, the worth of Brentano's conception of the concept "psychic phenomenon" does *not* depend at all on the ends he pursued with it. A *sharply delimited class* of experiences here confronts us, which embraces everything that, in a certain *pregnant* sense, characterizes psychic, conscious existence. Husserl illustrates *per impossibile*: a real being lacking such experiences — one that merely *had* contents of the kind that sensation-experiences are, but was incapable of *interpreting them objectively* or otherwise of making objects present through them, and so all the more incapable of relating in further acts to objects, of judging them, rejoicing or grieving over them, loving and hating, desiring and abhorring — such a being no one would any longer wish to call a *psychic* being. (A footnote remarks that we could no longer say *experienced*, since the very concept of experience has its origin in the domain of the psychic "acts.")

The fourth paragraph extends the point with the example of *soulless* things and the bearing on normative sciences. If one finds it questionable whether such a being, a mere sensation-complex, is even thinkable, it suffices to point to the *phenomenal outer things*, which present themselves consciously through sensation-complexes but by no means *appear* as such complexes, and which we call *soulless* beings or *bodies* precisely because they lack all psychic experiences in the sense of those examples. And if we leave psychology aside and enter the circle of the narrower philosophical disciplines, the *fundamental importance* of this class of experiences attests itself in this: that only the experiences belonging to it come into consideration for the highest *normative* sciences. For in them alone — provided we grasp them in phenomenological purity — are to be found the *concrete foundations* for the abstraction of the fundamental concepts that play their systematic role in *logic, ethics, aesthetics*: namely as concepts that build up the ideal laws of these disciplines. In naming logic, Husserl has at the same time recalled the special interest that prompts the closer consideration of these experiences. The class of *intentional* experiences is thus singled out as the bearer of everything that matters for the a priori normative disciplines.

### **§10. Descriptive characterization of acts as "intentional" experiences**

The first paragraph turns from value to *essence* and recalls Brentano's procedure. It is time to determine the essence of Brentano's class-delimitation, hence the essence of the concept of consciousness in the sense of *psychic act*. Guided by the classificatory interest mentioned above, Brentano carries out the relevant investigation in the form of a *mutual separation* of the two main classes of "phenomena" he assumes, the psychic and the physical. He obtains *six* determinations, of which from the outset only *two* can come into consideration for us, since in all the rest certain deceptive equivocations — which render Brentano's concepts of phenomenon (especially physical phenomenon), and of inner and outer perception, untenable — play a destructively contributing role.

The second paragraph presents the first and decisive determination: *intentionality*. Of the two preferred determinations, the one directly *exhibits the essence* of psychic phenomena or acts. It presses itself upon us unmistakably in arbitrary examples: in perception something is *perceived*, in image-presentation something is *imaged*, in assertion something is *asserted*, in love something is *loved*, in hate something *hated*, in desire something *desired*. The *common* element to be grasped in such examples Brentano has in view when he says (quoting the *Psychologie*): every psychic phenomenon is characterized by what the medieval scholastics called the *intentional* (also *mental*) *in-existence* of an object, and what we — though with not entirely unambiguous expressions — would call the *relation to a content*, the *direction upon an object* (by which here no *reality* is to be understood), or *immanent objectivity*. Each contains *something* as object in itself, though not each in the same way. This "manner of relation of consciousness to a content" is, in presentation the presenting one, in judgment the judging one, and so forth.

The third paragraph notes the use Brentano makes of this and disengages Husserl's own interest. Brentano's attempt to classify psychic phenomena into *presentations, judgments, and emotive movements* ("phenomena of love and hate") is grounded on this manner of relation, of which Brentano distinguishes three fundamentally different (and possibly manifoldly specifying) kinds. Whether one regards Brentano's classification as correct, and whether one even accords it the foundational significance for the whole treatment of psychology that its ingenious author claimed, does not matter here. Husserl keeps in view only *one* point as important for himself: that there are *essential specific differences* of the intentional relation, or briefly of the *intention* (which makes up the descriptive generic character of the "act").

The fourth paragraph enumerates the specific differences of intention. The *manner* in which a "mere presentation" of a state of affairs means this its "object" is different from the manner of the *judgment* that holds the state of affairs true or false. Different again is the manner of *surmise* and *doubt*, the manner of *hope* or *fear*, of *pleasure and displeasure*, of *desiring and fleeing*; of the *decision* of a theoretical doubt (judgment-decision) or of a practical doubt (volitional decision in a deliberative choice); of the *confirmation* of a theoretical opinion (fulfillment of a judgment-intention) or of a volitional opinion (fulfillment of a volitional intention); and so on. Certainly most, if not all, acts are *complex* experiences, and very often the intentions themselves are *manifold*: emotive intentions build themselves upon presentation- or judgment-intentions. But it is beyond doubt that in *dissolving* these complexes we always come upon *primitive* intentional characters that, according to their descriptive essence, *cannot be reduced* to psychic experiences of another kind; and again it is beyond doubt that the unity of the descriptive genus "intention" ("act-character") exhibits specific differences grounded in the *pure essence* of this genus and thus preceding empirical-psychological *facticity as an a priori*. There are *essentially different* kinds and sub-kinds of intention.

The fifth paragraph reinforces irreducibility with the *aesthetic* example. It is impossible to reduce all differences of acts to differences of the *interwoven* presentations and judgments, with mere assistance of elements not belonging to the genus *intention*. Thus *aesthetic approval or disapproval* is a manner of intentional relation that proves itself, evidently and essentially, *peculiar* over against the mere presenting or theoretical judging of the aesthetic object. The aesthetic approval and the aesthetic predicate can indeed be *asserted*, and the assertion is a judgment and as such includes presentations; but then the aesthetic intention, like its object, is *object* of presentations and judgments — it itself remains essentially different from these theoretical acts. To bring a judgment "to valuation" *as cogent*, an emotive experience *as high-minded*, certainly presupposes analogous and related, but not *specifically identical*, intentions. So likewise in the comparison between judgment-decisions and volitional decisions.

The sixth paragraph fixes the essential definition of the act and gives its purely phenomenological reading. The intentional relation, understood *purely descriptively* as an *inner peculiarity* of certain experiences, Husserl takes as the *essential determinacy* of the "psychic phenomena" or "acts," so that in Brentano's definition — that they are "such phenomena as intentionally contain an object in themselves" — he sees an *essential definition* whose "reality" (in the old sense, i.e. *real content*) is naturally secured by the examples. Put in other words and at the same time purely phenomenologically: the *ideation* performed on exemplary single cases of such experiences — performed so that every empirical-psychological apprehension and existence-positing remains out of play and only the really phenomenological content of these experiences comes into consideration — gives us the *purely phenomenological generic idea* "intentional experience" or "act," and then further its pure species. (A footnote explains that within psychological apperception this pure concept *modifies* into the concept of the *psychic state* of an animal being, and accordingly the analyses gain now phenomenological, now psychological significance depending on whether the psychological apperception is switched off or in.)

The seventh paragraph confirms that *not all* experiences are intentional. That not all experiences are intentional is shown by the *sensations* and sensation-complexes. Any piece of the sensed visual field, however filled with visual contents, is an experience that may contain manifold part-contents in itself, but these contents are *not* objects intended by the whole, *intentional in it*. Sensational material, in other words, is *experienced* but does not, of itself, *intend* anything.

The eighth paragraph promises further clarification and reasserts the *eidetic* status of the findings. The considerations to follow will more exactly clarify the fundamental difference between the one and the other talk of "contents." And everywhere one will convince oneself that what comes to grasp, in exemplary analysis and comparison, on the two-sided contents is insightable in *ideation* as a pure *essential difference*. All the phenomenological establishments aimed at here are to be understood — even without special emphasis — as *essence-establishments*.

The ninth paragraph introduces Brentano's *second* valuable determination: the founding role of presentation. Brentano formulates it thus: psychic phenomena "either *are* presentations or *rest on* presentations as their basis." "Nothing can be judged, but also nothing can be desired, nothing hoped or feared, if it is not *presented*." Under "presentation" in this determination is naturally understood not the presented *content* (object), but the *presenting*, the *act*.

The tenth paragraph explains why this second determination is *not* a suitable starting-point, even while honoring its importance. What makes it unsuitable as a point of departure is that it *presupposes* a concept of presentation which, given the manifold and not at all easily distinguished equivocations of this term, would first have to be *worked out*. Here, however, the discussion of the concept *act* forms the natural beginning. All the same, this second determination expresses an *important* proposition — one rich in stimulus for further research according to its content — to which Husserl will have to return. (This is the proposition of the founding role of presentations, the central theme of Chapters Four and Five.)

### **§11. Warding off terminologically suggested misinterpretations: a) The "mental" or "immanent" object**

The first paragraph rejects Brentano's *terminology* while keeping his essential determination. While we hold fast to Brentano's essential determination (intentionality), the indicated divergences from his convictions compel us to *reject his terminology*. We will do well to speak neither of *psychic phenomena* nor of *phenomena* at all where the experiences of the class in question are concerned. The first term has justification only on Brentano's standpoint, where this class is supposed (in the main) to delimit the research-field of psychology; on *our* standpoint, all experiences whatever are in this respect of equal standing. As for the term *phenomenon*, it is not only burdened with very disadvantageous ambiguities but also imputes a *very doubtful theoretical conviction*, which we find expressly stated in Brentano: that every intentional experience is precisely a *phenomenon*. Since "phenomenon" in the prevailing usage (also adopted by Brentano) designates an *appearing object* as such, it follows that every intentional experience not only *relates* to objects but is itself an *object* of certain intentional experiences — one thinks especially of those experiences that bring something to appearance in the most special sense, namely *perceptions*: "Every psychic phenomenon is object of inner consciousness." But Husserl has already said that he has serious reservations about assenting to this proposition.

The second paragraph turns to the *circumlocutions* and the two misinterpretations they invite. Further objections strike the expressions Brentano uses *parallel* to the term "psychic phenomenon," or paraphrastically, and that are otherwise current. It is at all events very questionable, and often enough misleading, to speak of perceived, phantasied, judged, desired objects (in perceiving, presenting fashion, etc.) as "*entering consciousness*," or conversely of "*consciousness*" (or "*the ego*") "entering into relation" with them in this or that way, of their being "taken up into consciousness," and so on; and likewise to speak of the intentional experiences as "*containing something as object in themselves*." Such expressions suggest two misinterpretations. *First*, that it is a matter of a pure real *process* or a real *relating* playing out *between* consciousness or ego and the "conscious" thing. *Second*, that it is a matter of a *relation between two things equally to be found really (reell) in consciousness* — act and intentional object — something like a *nesting* of one psychic content within another. If the talk of a *relation* cannot be entirely avoided here, the *expressions* must at least be avoided that positively *invite* the misinterpretation of the relation as a psychologically real one, or one belonging to the *real content* of the experience.

The third paragraph examines the *second* misinterpretation and locates its source. It is recommended especially by the expression *immanent objectivity* for the essential peculiarity of intentional experiences, and likewise by the equivalent scholastic expressions *intentional* or *mental in-existence* of an object. The intentional experiences have the peculiarity of *relating* to presented objects in various ways. They do this precisely in the *sense of intention*. An object is in them "*meant*," "*aimed at*," in the manner of presentation, or at the same time of judging, and so on. But in this lies nothing other than that certain *experiences* are present which have a character of intention, and specifically of presenting, judging, desiring intention. There are *not* (apart from certain exceptional cases) *two things* present in the manner of experience — the object is *not* experienced and *alongside* it the intentional experience directing itself at it; nor are there two things in the sense of *part* and *encompassing whole*; rather, only *one* thing is present, the *intentional experience*, whose essential descriptive character is precisely the relevant *intention*. According to its specific particularization, the intention makes up *fully and alone* the presenting of this object, or the judging of it. If this experience is present, then *eo ipso* — and this, Husserl emphasizes, lies in its own *essence* — the intentional "relation to an object" is accomplished, *eo ipso* an object is "intentionally present"; for the one and the other say *exactly the same*.

The fourth paragraph draws the consequence for *non-existent* objects, with the Jupiter example. Naturally such an experience can be present in consciousness with this its intention without the object *existing at all*, and perhaps without its being able to exist; the object is *meant*, i.e., the meaning of it is experience, but it is then merely *supposed* and in truth *nothing*. If I present the god Jupiter, this god is *presented object*, "immanently present" in my act, has "mental in-existence" in it — however the misleading idioms otherwise run. I present the god Jupiter: that means I have a certain presentation-experience, the presenting-of-the-god-Jupiter accomplishes itself in my consciousness. Dissect this intentional experience in descriptive analysis as one will — *something like the god Jupiter* one naturally cannot *find* in it. The "immanent," "mental" object therefore does *not* belong to the descriptive (real) subsistence of the experience; it is thus in truth not immanent or mental at all. It is, to be sure, also not *extra mentem*; it is *not at all*. But this does not prevent the presenting-of-the-god-Jupiter from being *real*, an experience so constituted, a determinate way of being-disposed, that whoever experiences it in himself can rightly say he presents to himself that mythical king of the gods of whom this and that is fabled.

The fifth paragraph generalizes: existence makes no *phenomenological* difference. If, on the other hand, the intended object *exists*, nothing need be changed in *phenomenological* respect. For consciousness the given is *essentially the same* whether the presented object exists, or is fictitious and perhaps even nonsensical. I present *Jupiter* no differently than *Bismarck*, the *Tower of Babel* no differently than *Cologne Cathedral*, a *regular thousand-sided figure* (Tausendeck) no differently than a *regular thousand-faced solid* (Tausendflächner). (A footnote sets aside the eventual *positing-characters* that make up the conviction of the being of the presented, and re-emphasizes that all presupposition of a natural reality with human beings and animals can be switched off, so that these considerations are to be understood as reflections on *ideal possibilities*.)

The sixth paragraph reverses the relation between *immanent* and *intentional* contents. If the so-called immanent contents are rather merely *intentional* (intended), then on the other side the *truly immanent* contents — those belonging to the *real* subsistence of the intentional experiences — are *not intentional*: they build up the act, they make the intention possible as its necessary points of support, but they are not themselves *intended*, they are not the objects presented in the act. *I do not see color-sensations but colored things, I do not hear tone-sensations but the song of the singer*, and so on. (A footnote refers to the Appendix at the chapter's close on the supposedly self-evident distinction between immanent and transcendent objects, oriented by the old schema "inwardly conscious image — extra-conscious being-in-itself.")

The seventh paragraph extends this from presentations to *founded* acts. What holds of presentations holds also of the other intentional experiences built upon them. To present an object to oneself — e.g., the Berlin Palace — is, we said, a descriptively so-and-so determined kind of being-disposed. To *judge* about this palace, to *rejoice* at its architectural beauty, to cherish the *wish* to be able to do this, and the like, are *new* experiences, phenomenologically characterized in a new way. All have in common that they are *manners of objective intention*, which in normal speech we can express only by saying that the palace is perceived, phantasied, presented in an image, judged, that it is object of that joy, that wish, and so on.

The eighth paragraph recommends abandoning "immanent object" in favor of "intentional object." It will require still more extensive investigation to bring out what justifies the figurative talk of the object presented in the presentation, judged in the judgment, and how the objective relation of acts is to be understood at all; but as far as we have penetrated, it is clear that we do well to *avoid entirely* this idiom of immanent objects. It is, moreover, easily dispensable, since we have the expression *intentional object*, which is not subject to similar reservations.

The ninth paragraph diagnoses the broader confusion and explains Husserl's terminological caution. In view of the *impropriety* of speaking of the intentional "being-contained" of the object in the act, it is unmistakable that the parallel and equivalent idioms — that the object is *conscious*, *in consciousness*, *immanent to consciousness* — suffer from a very *harmful equivocation*; for "consciousness" here means something quite different from what it can mean according to the two earlier-discussed meanings of consciousness. The whole of recent psychology and epistemology is thrown into confusion by these and closely related equivocations. Given the predominant influence of the psychological way of thinking and its terminology, Husserl would do ill to set his own terms in conflict with those of present-day psychology. Since the *first* concept of consciousness — which, taken empirically-psychologically, designates as conscious the experience-stream belonging to the real unity of the psychic individual together with all the moments really constituting it — shows the tendency to *prevail* in psychology, Husserl has already decided in the previous chapter to *prefer* this concept (only abstracting from the properly psychological, hence in phenomenological purity), and so he must use the idioms of consciousness in the sense of *inner perception* and in the sense of *intentional relation*, where not entirely avoid them (which is scarcely feasible), then with the necessary caution.

### **§12. b) The act and the relation of consciousness or of the ego to the object**

The first paragraph addresses the *first* misinterpretation — the relation as one between consciousness/ego and the thing — and shows how natural reflection feeds it. It stands similarly with the first-mentioned misinterpretation, as though consciousness on the one side and the conscious thing on the other entered into relation with one another in a *real* sense. Instead of "the consciousness" one often says outright "the ego." Indeed, in *natural reflection* it is not the *individual act* but the *ego* that appears as the *one* relation-point of the relation in question, whose *second* point lies in the object. If one then attends to the *act-experience*, the ego seems necessarily to relate to the object *through* it or *in* it; and on this latter conception one might even be inclined to *insert* the ego into *every* act as an essential and everywhere identical point of unity. With this we would after all fall back into the previously rejected assumption of a *pure ego* as relation-center.

The second paragraph refutes this by appeal to *living-in* the act. But if we *live*, so to speak, *in* the relevant act — if, e.g., we are absorbed in a perceptual contemplation of an appearing process, or in the play of phantasy, in reading a fairy tale, in carrying out a mathematical proof — then *nothing* is to be noticed of the ego as relation-point of the accomplished acts. The *ego-presentation* may be "in readiness," may press forward with special ease or rather newly accomplish itself; but only when it *really accomplishes itself* and posits itself in one with the relevant act do "we" relate "ourselves" to the object in such a way that something *descriptively exhibitable* corresponds to this self-relating of the ego. What then *descriptively* lies before us in actual experiencing is a *correspondingly composite act*, which contains the *ego-presentation* as one part and the respective presenting, judging, wishing of the relevant thing as a *second* part.

The third paragraph concedes the *objective* correctness of the ego-relation while denying its descriptive immanence. Naturally it is, *objectively* considered (hence also from the standpoint of natural reflection), correct that the ego relates intentionally to an object in *every* act. This is a pure self-evidence, provided the ego counts for us as nothing more than the "consciousness-unity," the respective "bundle" of experiences, or — in empirically real and natural construal — the continuous, thingly unity that constitutes itself intentionally in the consciousness-unity as the personal subject of the experiences: the ego that has its "psychic states" in them, that accomplishes the relevant intention, perception, judgment. If an experience of such-and-such intention is present, then *eo ipso* the ego has this intention.

The fourth paragraph spells out the equivalence of the two idioms. Thus the proposition "the ego presents an object, it relates in presenting fashion to an object, it has it as intentional object of its presentation" says *the same* as the proposition "in the phenomenological ego, this concrete complexion of experiences, a certain experience named, according to its specific peculiarity, 'presenting of the relevant object' is really present." Likewise the proposition "the ego judges about the object" says as much as "there is in it a so-and-so determined judgment-experience present." In the *description* the relation to the experiencing ego is not to be circumvented; but the respective *experience itself* does *not* consist in a complexion that would contain the ego-presentation as a part-experience. The description accomplishes itself on the basis of an *objectivating reflection*; in it the reflection upon the ego links with the reflection upon the act-experience into a *relating act*, in which the ego itself *appears* as relating itself, by means of its act, to the act's object. Evidently an *essential descriptive change* has thereby occurred. In particular, the *original* act is no longer simply *there*; we no longer *live in* it, but *attend to* it and *judge about* it.

The fifth paragraph states the conclusion. The misunderstanding must therefore be kept away — and is now also excluded by the consideration carried out — that the *relation to the ego* is something belonging to the essential subsistence of the intentional experience itself. (A footnote again refers to the second-edition Addendum to Chapter One and to *Ideen I*, marking once more Husserl's later reservation.)

### **§13. Fixing our terminology**

The first paragraph settles the terms. After these critical preparations Husserl fixes his own terminology, chosen so that contested presuppositions and disturbing ambiguities remain as far as possible excluded. He will therefore *avoid entirely* the expression *psychic phenomenon*, and wherever exactness is required will speak of *intentional experiences*. "Experience" is here to be taken in the phenomenological sense fixed above. The determining adjective *intentional* names the common *essential character* of the experience-class to be delimited, the peculiarity of *intention*, the relating-to-something-objective in the manner of *presentation* or in some analogous manner. As a *shorter* expression, to accommodate others' and his own linguistic habits, Husserl will use the word *act*.

The second paragraph flags the first reservation about "intention": its association with *attending*. These expressions are not entirely without reservations. We often speak of an *intention* in the sense of *specially heeding* something, of *attending* (Aufmerken). Yet *not always is the intentional object a noticed, attended one*. At times several acts are simultaneously present and interwoven, but attention "activates itself" in one of them in a distinguishing way: we *experience* all of them simultaneously, but in this *one* we are, as it were, absorbed. All the same, with regard to the historically transmitted and (since Brentano) again much-used talk of *intentional objects*, it is perhaps not inappropriate to speak of intention in a *correlative* sense — especially since for intention in the sense of *attending* (which we will have grounds *not* to let count as a peculiar act) we have precisely the term *attending*.

The third paragraph treats a *second* equivocation of "intention": the image of *aiming*, and the narrower vs. wider concept. The expression *intention* presents the peculiarity of acts under the image of *aiming* (Abzielen) and therefore suits very well the manifold acts describable, unconstrained and intelligibly, as theoretical or practical aiming. But this image does *not* suit all acts equally well; and attending more exactly to the examples assembled in §10, we cannot fail to notice that a *narrower* and a *wider* concept of intention must be distinguished. In the image, to the activity of *aiming* there corresponds, as correlate, that of *attaining* (Erzielen) — the shooting and the hitting. Exactly so, to certain acts as "intentions" (e.g., judgment-, desire-intentions) there correspond *other acts* as "attainments" or "*fulfillments*." That is why the image fits the former acts so perfectly; but the *fulfillments* are *also* acts, hence also "intentions," although they are (at least in general) not in turn intentions in that *narrower* sense which *points to a corresponding fulfillment*. The equivocation, once recognized, is harmless. Where the narrower concept is in question, this must be expressly said; otherwise the parallel expression *act-character* also helps to keep possible misunderstandings away.

The fourth paragraph defends the word "act" against the connotation of *activity*. As for the talk of *acts*, one may here of course no longer think of the original word-sense of *actus*: *the thought of activity must be unconditionally excluded*. But in the usage of a great number of psychologists the expression "act" is so firmly rooted, and on the other hand so worn and so clearly detached from its original sense, that — especially after this express reservation — we can unconcernedly retain it. If we do not wish to introduce wholly new artificial words foreign to all living linguistic feeling and all historical tradition, we can scarcely ever avoid drawbacks of the kind just discussed. (A footnote endorses Natorp's rejection of the "mythology of activities": only because consciousness is often or always accompanied by *striving* does it appear as a doing and its subject as a doer; Husserl agrees and defines the "acts" not as psychic activities but as *intentional experiences*.)

### **§14. Reservations against the assumption of acts as a descriptively founded class of experiences**

The first paragraph frames the section as a defense against objections. In all these terminological discussions we have already entered quite deeply into descriptive analyses of the kind demanded by our logico-epistemological interests. Before continuing them, it will be necessary to take into account certain *objections* concerning the *foundations* of our descriptions.

The second paragraph identifies the first objection: a group of researchers *disputes* the delimitation of the class outright. The original *manner of introduction* by Brentano, the *ends* he pursued with it, and probably some misinterpretations he committed have had a misleading effect, preventing the exceedingly *valuable descriptive content* of the delimitation from coming into its own. It is decidedly contested, e.g., by Natorp. When this excellent researcher objects, "I can indeed consider the tone for itself or in relation to other consciousness-contents, without further considering its existence for an ego, but I cannot consider myself and my hearing for itself, without thinking of the tone," Husserl finds in this nothing that could mislead him. That from the *hearing of the tone* the *hearing* cannot be detached, as if it were still something *without* the tone, is certain. But with that it is *not* said that a *twofold* is not to be distinguished: the *heard tone*, the perceptual object, and the *hearing of the tone*, the perceptual act.

The third paragraph grants Natorp's formula while insisting it permits further analysis. It is correct, Husserl says, when Natorp says of the heard tone: "Its existence for me — this is my consciousness of it. Whoever can catch his consciousness still otherwise than in the existence of a content for him — I cannot ... do likewise." But it seems to Husserl that the "existence of a content for me" is a matter that *permits and demands* a further phenomenological *analysis*. First, the differences in the *manner of noticing*: the content is there for me in a different way according to whether I merely *imply* it, without special prominence within a whole, or have it *set off*; and again whether I notice it only *in passing* or have it preferentially in view, having *specially aimed* at it.

The fourth paragraph isolates the *more important* difference: sensation vs. perceptual object, with the tone and the bird-song. More important for us are the differences between the existence of the content in the sense of the conscious, but not itself perceptual-object-become, *sensation*, and the content in the sense precisely of the *perceptual object*. The choice of the example of the tone *covers over* the difference a little, without abolishing it. "I hear" can in psychology mean "I *sense*"; in usual speech it means "I *perceive*": I hear the *adagio of the violinist*, the *twittering of the birds*. Different *acts* can *perceive* the same and yet *sense* something quite different — the same tone we hear once spatially near, another time far. So conversely: like sensation-contents "we apprehend" once thus and another time otherwise.

The fifth paragraph criticizes the *apperception* doctrine for misplacing the issue. Usually, in the doctrine of "apperception," one places predominant emphasis on the circumstance that, under presupposition of *like stimuli*, the sensed content is not everywhere the same, since by virtue of dispositions left over from earlier experiences what is really conditioned by the stimulus is *overgrown* by moments stemming from the actualization of those dispositions. But with such things one by no means suffices, and above all this is phenomenologically *not* what matters. However the contents present in consciousness (the experienced ones) may have *arisen*, it is *conceivable* that *like* sensation-contents are present in it and yet differently apprehended — in other words, that on the basis of the *same* contents *different objects* would be perceived. The *apprehension* (Auffassung) itself can never be reduced to an *influx* of new sensations; it is an *act-character*, a "*manner of consciousness*," of "being-disposed": we call the *experiencing of sensations* in this manner of consciousness the *perception* of the relevant object.

The sixth paragraph universalizes the finding eidetically. What was here established in the natural-scientific-psychological mode of consideration, within the frame of natural existence, yields, upon switching off everything empirical-real, its *purely phenomenological subsistence*. Looking to the pure experiences and their own essential content, we grasp *ideatively* pure species and specific states of affairs — here the pure species *sensation, apprehension, perception* in relation to the perceived, together with the essential relations belonging to them. We then also insight, as a general essential state of affairs, that the *being* of the sensed content is something quite other than the *being* of the perceived object, which is *presented* through the content but not *really conscious*.

The seventh paragraph sharpens all this with the *visual* example of the box. The matter becomes still clearer by a switch of example into the sphere of *visual perception*. I see a *thing*, e.g., this box; I do not see my *sensations*. I see continually this *one and the same* box, however it may be turned and twisted. I have thereby continually the *same* "consciousness-content" — if it pleases me to designate the *perceived object* as consciousness-content. With each turn I have a *new* consciousness-content, if I designate the *experienced contents* so, in a much more suitable sense. Thus *very different* contents are *experienced*, and yet the *same* object is *perceived*. Hence the experienced content, generally speaking, is *not* itself the perceived object. (It must be noted, Husserl adds, that actual being or non-being of the object is *irrelevant* for the own essence of the perceptual experience, and so also for its being perception *of* this so-and-so-appearing, as-such-meant object.)

The eighth paragraph analyzes the *identity-consciousness* that runs across the changing contents. That we further *suppose* ourselves to grasp perceptually *one and the same* object in the change of experienced contents is itself again something belonging to the *experience-domain*. We experience the "*identity-consciousness*," i.e., this *supposing* of grasping identity. What lies at the basis of this consciousness? Should not the answer be apt: that on both sides different sensation-contents are given, but that they are apprehended, apperceived "*in the same sense*," and that the *apprehension* according to this "*sense*" is an *experience-character* which *first of all* makes up the "*existence of the object for me*"? Further, that the identity-consciousness accomplishes itself on the basis of these two-sided experience-characters, as immediate consciousness that they both mean precisely *the same*? And is this consciousness not again an *act* in the sense of the definition, whose objective correlate lies in the designated *identity*? Husserl believes all these questions demand affirmative answer *with evidence*. Nothing can he find more evident than the difference here emerging between *contents* and *acts* — more specifically, between *perceptual contents* in the sense of *presenting sensations* and *perceptual acts* in the sense of the *apprehending intention* (further equipped with various superimposed characters); which intention, in unity with the apprehended sensation, makes up the full concrete act of perception.

The ninth paragraph forestalls a verbal objection about "content." Naturally, consciousness-contents, in the widest descriptive sense of *experiences*, are *also* the intentional characters and likewise the full acts; in this respect all differences we can establish at all are *eo ipso* differences of *content*. But *within* this widest sphere of the experienceable we believe we find the evident difference between *intentional experiences*, in which *objective intentions* constitute themselves — and indeed through *immanent characters* of the respective experience — and *such* in which this is *not* the case: contents which can function as *building-blocks* of acts but are *not themselves acts*.

The tenth paragraph supplies favorable *examples* for distinguishing act-characters, culminating in *expressions*. Favorable examples for further clarifying this distinction, and at the same time for mutually setting off different act-characters, are furnished by comparing perception with *recollection*, and both again with presentation through *physical images* (paintings, statues) or through *signs*. The very most favorable examples, however, are furnished by *expressions*. Imagine that certain figures or arabesques first affected us purely aesthetically, and now suddenly the understanding dawns that they might be *symbols* or *word-signs*. Wherein lies the difference? Or take the case of someone attentively *hearing* a word entirely foreign to him as a mere sound-complex, without even *suspecting* it to be a word; and compare it with the case where he later hears the word — having become familiar with its meaning — *with understanding* in the midst of a conversation, yet entirely without accompanying illustrations. Wherein lies, generally, the *surplus* of the understood but merely symbolically functioning expression over against the *thought-empty* word-sound? What makes the difference whether we simply *intuit* a concretum A, or apprehend it as "*representative*" for "*an arbitrary A*"? In these and countless similar cases the modification lies in the *act-characters*. All logical differences, and especially all differences of *categorial form*, constitute themselves in the *logical acts* in the sense of *intentions*.

The eleventh paragraph draws the methodological moral against the apperception-doctrine and defines *apperception* phenomenologically. In such example-analyses it emerges that the modern doctrine of apperception *does not suffice* — indeed, that it *overlooks* the points decisive for the logico-epistemological interest. It does not do justice to the *phenomenological* state of affairs; it does not engage at all in its analysis and description. But the differences of apprehension are above all *descriptive* differences; and *only such*, not any hidden and hypothetically assumed processes in the unconscious depths of the soul or in the sphere of physiological happening, concern the *critic of knowledge*. *Apperception* is for us the *surplus* that consists, in the experience itself, in its descriptive content over against the *raw existence* of the sensation; it is the *act-character* that, so to speak, *animates* (beseelt) the sensation and makes it the case, *according to its essence*, that we *perceive* this or that object — see this tree, hear that ringing, smell the scent of the blossom. The *sensations*, and likewise the acts "apprehending" or "apperceiving" them, are *experienced* here, but they *do not appear objectively*; they are *not* seen, heard, perceived with any "sense." The *objects*, on the other hand, *appear*, are *perceived*, but they are *not experienced*. (The case of adequate perception is excluded.)

The twelfth paragraph extends apprehension to *imagination* and unifies the terminology. Similar holds elsewhere too — e.g., regarding the sensations (or whatever we may call the contents functioning as foundations of apprehension) belonging to the acts of *simple* and *depicting imagination*. The *imaging* apprehension makes it the case that instead of a perceptual appearance we have rather an *image-appearance*, in which, on the basis of the experienced sensations, the *imaged object* (the centaur on the painted picture) appears. One understands at the same time that what, *in relation to the intentional object*, is called *presentation* (perceiving, remembering, imagining, depicting, signifying *intention* upon it) is called, *in relation to the sensations really belonging to the act*, *apprehension, interpretation, apperception*. (A footnote remarks that the much-debated dispute over the relation between perceptual and phantasy-presentation could lead to no proper result for lack of a phenomenological underpinning; that the act-characters are different, that, e.g., with *imageness* an essentially new manner of intention becomes experience, can — Husserl believes — be indubitably demonstrated.)

The thirteenth paragraph claims *evidence* for essentially different "manners of consciousness." With regard to the examples considered, Husserl also claims as *evidence* that there are in fact *essentially different* "*manners of consciousness*," namely of intentional relation to the objective. The character of *intention* is specifically different in the case of perception, of simple "reproductive" *re-presentation* (Vergegenwärtigung), of image-presentation in the usual sense of apprehending statues and paintings, and again in the case of *sign-presentation* and of presentation in the sense of *pure logic*. To every logically distinguished manner of *thinkingly* presenting an object there corresponds a *difference* in the intention. Husserl holds it incontestable that we know of all these differences only because we *intuit* them in the individual case (i.e., immediately, adequately grasp them), bring them comparatively under concepts, and so make them in turn, in various kinds of acts, objects of intuition and thought. At any time we can also, from them as intuited, grasp adequately, by *ideating abstraction*, the pure species individuating themselves in them and the specific essential connections belonging to them.

The fourteenth paragraph rebuts Natorp's leveling claim. When Natorp says, by contrast, "All the wealth, all the manifoldness of consciousness lies rather *exclusively* in the *content*. The consciousness of a *simple sensation* differs in kind, *as consciousness*, in nothing from the consciousness of a *world*; the moment of consciousness-ness is in both quite the same, the difference lies exclusively in the content" — it seems to Husserl that Natorp does *not keep apart* the various concepts of *consciousness* and *content*, indeed that he wishes to elevate their *identification* into an epistemological principle. In what sense Husserl himself teaches that all manifoldness of consciousness lies in the content, he has set out above: *content* is then *experience*, *really constituting* the consciousness; consciousness itself is the *complexion* of the experiences.

The fifteenth paragraph distinguishes *world* from *experience* and places the whole distinction at the gateway of epistemology. But the *world* is *nevermore* an experience of the thinker. *Experience* is the *meaning-of-the-world* (das Die-Welt-Meinen); the *world itself* is the *intended object*. For this distinction, Husserl expressly emphasizes, it is *indifferent* how one positions oneself on the questions of what the objective being, the true real in-itself-being of the world or of any object whatever, makes up, and how one determines objective being as "unity" relative to subjective being-thought with its "manifoldness"; likewise in what sense *metaphysically* immanent and transcendent being may be opposed. The matter here is rather a distinction that stands *before all metaphysics* and *at the gateway of epistemology* — one that therefore presupposes *no* questions as answered which epistemology is precisely first to answer. The intentional difference is thus the most elementary, presuppositionless starting-point of the whole critique of knowledge.

### **§15. Whether experiences of one and the same phenomenological genus (and especially the genus feeling) can be partly acts and partly non-acts**

The first paragraph poses the new difficulty. A new difficulty arises with respect to the *generic unity* of the intentional experiences. One could doubt whether the point of view of delimiting experiences into *intentional* and *non-intentional* is not merely *external*, such that the *same* experiences, or experiences of *one and the same* phenomenological genus, have now an intentional relation to the objective and now not. The corroborating examples for the one and the other conception, and partly the thoughts for resolving the doubt, have already been discussed in the literature — in connection with the disputed question whether the mark of intentional relation suffices to delimit the "psychic phenomena" (as the domain of psychology). The latter dispute concerned especially certain phenomena from the sphere of *feelings*. Since with the other feelings intentionality seemed evident, a *twofold* doubt was possible: either one became suspicious about these feeling-acts too, whether the intentional relation does not attach to them merely *improperly* — whether it does not rather belong directly and properly to the *presentations* woven into them — or one doubted only the *essentiality* of the intentional character for the *class* of feelings, granting this character to some and denying it to others.

The second paragraph sets the order of inquiry. We will first consider whether in the class of feelings there are at all *kinds* of experiences to which an intentional relation belongs *essentially*, and afterward see whether this relation can be *lacking* in other experiences of the same class.

**a) Whether there are intentional feelings at all**

The third paragraph affirms that many feelings *do* have intentional relation. With many experiences we generally designate as feelings, it is quite unmistakable that an intentional relation to the objective really belongs to them. So it stands, e.g., with the *liking* of a melody, with the *displeasure* at a shrill whistle. In general, every *joy* or *unjoy* — which is joy or unjoy *over* something presented — seems self-evidently to be an *act*. Instead of joy one can also say pleasurable *liking* of something, being *attracted* by it, being pleasurably inclined toward it; instead of unjoy, unpleasurable or painful *disliking* of something, being *repelled* by it.

The fourth paragraph states the opponents' thesis. The deniers of the intentionality of feelings say: feelings are *mere states*, not acts, intentions. Where they relate to objects, they owe this relation only to *complication with presentations*.

The fifth paragraph notes that this last point alone is no objection, citing Brentano. The latter point would in itself contain *no* objection. Brentano, who *defends* the intentionality of feelings, teaches on his own side, without contradicting himself, that feelings — like all acts that are not mere presentations — have *presentations as their basis*. Only on such objects can we relate ourselves feelingly as have become presented to us through interwoven presentations. A *difference* between the disputing parties emerges only through this: that on the one side one really wants to say the *feeling, considered in itself, contains nothing of intention*, points beyond itself to no felt object; only through *unification* with a presentation does it gain a certain relation to an object — but a relation determined only through this *connective* relation with an intentional relation, and not itself to be grasped as an intentional relation. This precisely is what the opposing party contests.

The sixth paragraph states the two construals. According to Brentano, two intentions are here *built upon one another*: the *founding* one furnishes the *presented*, the *founded* one the *felt* object; the former is detachable from the latter, but not the latter from the former. According to the opposed conception, there is here only *one* intention, the presenting one.

The seventh paragraph adjudicates by *phenomenological inspection*, favoring Brentano, and treats *association*. Attentive re-presentation of the matter in phenomenological inspection seems decidedly to favor Brentano's conception. When we turn with liking to a thing, or it repels us as displeasing, we *present* it. But we do not merely *have* the presentation and *in addition* the feeling, as something relationless to the matter in and for itself and then merely *associatively* attached; rather, liking or disliking *direct themselves* at the presented object, and *without* such direction they cannot be at all. When two psychic experiences — e.g., two presentations — enter into an *association* in the objective-psychological sense, there corresponds, to the objective dispositional regulation on the reproductively realized experiences, a phenomenologically *exhibitable associative unity-character*. Beside the intentional relation each has to its object, a *connection-relation* is also phenomenologically present: the one presentation, say that of Naples, "carries with it" that of Vesuvius, is peculiarly bound up with it, so that, with regard to the presented objects, we also say the one *reminds* us of the other (a sentence now understood as expression of a phenomenological occurrence).

The eighth paragraph shows that association does *not* confer an intention. One sees easily that, even if a *new* intentional relation is thereby in a certain way established, the one associative member does *not* thereby become the *object* of the other's intention. The intentional relations do *not* get tangled into one another in association. How then should association procure, for what is *not in itself intention*, the object from an *associated* intention? Moreover it is clear that this phenomenologically-associative relation is an *inessential* one, not to be placed on a level with the relation of *liking* to the *likeable*. The *reproducing* presentation is possible also *outside* this reproductive function. But a *liking* without a *likeable* is *not thinkable*. And not merely because we here deal with *correlative* expressions (as we say a cause without effect, a father without child is not thinkable), but because *the specific essence of liking demands the relation to a likeable*. Exactly so, the moment of *conviction* is a priori unthinkable except as conviction *of* something; again, no *desire* (according to specific character) without a desired, no *assenting* or *approving* without something to which the assent, the approval, applies. All these are *intentions*, genuine acts in our sense. They all "owe" their intentional relation to certain *presentations* underlying them. But in the sense of "owing" it lies, quite rightly, that they *themselves* now also *have* what they owe to the others.

The ninth paragraph rejects the *causal* construal of the founding-founded relation. The relation between *founding presentation* and *founded act* is by no means correctly described by saying that the one *effects* (bewirkt) the other. We do say the object *arouses* our liking, as in other cases a state of affairs *arouses* our doubt, *compels* us to assent, *stimulates* our desire. But the respective *result* of this apparent causation — the aroused liking, the aroused doubting or assenting — has *fully and wholly the intentional relation in itself*. It is *no* external causal relation according to which the effect, as what it is considered in itself, would be thinkable also *without* the cause, or the accomplishment of the cause would consist in the accession of something that could also *be for itself*.

The tenth paragraph deepens this into a *principled absurdity*, with the centaur-battle example. More exactly weighed, it is a *principled absurdity* to regard the intentional relation, here and in general, as a *causal* relation, hence to supply it with the sense of an empirical, substantial-causal necessity-connection. For the *intentional object*, apprehended as "effecting," comes into question only as the *intentional* one, not as really existing *outside* me and really, psychophysically determining my soul-life. A *centaur-battle* that I present to myself in a picture or in phantasy "arouses" my liking just as much as a beautiful *real* landscape; and even if I also apprehend the latter psychophysically as *real cause* for the state of liking effected in me, this is a *totally different* "causation" from the one in which I *intuit* the seen landscape — by virtue precisely of this manner of appearance, or precisely these appearing colors or forms of its "image" — as "source," "ground," "cause" of my liking. The being-liked, the feeling-of-liking, "belongs" to this landscape not as physical reality and not as physical *effect*, but, in the act-consciousness here in question, belongs to it *as so-and-so appearing*, possibly also so-and-so judged, reminding of this or that; as *such* it "demands," "awakens" such feelings.

**b) Whether there are non-intentional feelings. Distinction of feeling-sensations and feeling-acts**

The eleventh paragraph poses the second question and answers it, provisionally, with yes. The further question is whether, besides the kinds of feelings that *are* intentional experiences, there are *other* kinds of feelings that are *not*. This question too, it might at first seem, we must answer with a self-evident *yes*. In the wide sphere of the so-called *sensory feelings*, nothing of intentional characters is to be found. If we burn ourselves, the sensory *pain* is certainly not to be placed on a level with a conviction, surmise, volition, but with *sensation-contents* like roughness or smoothness, red or blue. Re-presenting such pains, or any sensory pleasures (the fragrance of a rose, the savor of a dish), we find that the sensory feelings are *fused* with the sensations belonging to these or those sense-fields quite as these are among themselves.

The twelfth paragraph shows that the apparent objective relation of sensory feelings is the same as that of *sensations*. In a certain way every sensory feeling — e.g., the pain of burning oneself — is *related* to the objective: on the one hand to the ego (more closely, to the burned limb), on the other to the burning object. But here the *uniformity with other sensations* shows itself again. Exactly so, e.g., the *touch-sensations* are related to the touching limb and the touched foreign body. Although this relation accomplishes itself *in* intentional experiences, no one will think of designating the *sensations themselves* as such experiences. The state of affairs is rather that the sensations here function as *presenting contents* of perceptual acts, or (as it is not quite unambiguously put) that the sensations here undergo an objective "interpretation" or "apprehension." They themselves are thus *not acts*, but with them acts *constitute* themselves, namely where intentional characters of the kind of perceiving apprehension *seize* upon them, lending them, so to speak, animation. In just this way the burning, stinging, boring pain — appearing from the outset fused with certain touch-sensations — seems itself to count as *sensation*, and at all events seems to function in the manner of other sensations, namely as *support* for an empirical, objective apprehension.

The thirteenth paragraph would seem to settle the matter — but raises the *genus* doubt. Against this surely nothing will be objectable, and so one might regard the posed question as settled: it seems proven that a *part* of feelings is to be reckoned to the intentional, the other to the non-intentional experiences. Yet here the doubt will stir whether the two-sided "feelings" really belong to *one genus*. We earlier spoke of "feelings" of liking or disliking, approval or disapproval, valuation or disvaluation — experiences evidently *related* to the theoretical acts of assent and rejection, of holding-probable and holding-improbable, or to the acts of deliberative judgment-decision and volitional decision. Into the evident *essential unity* of this genus, which embraces *exclusively acts*, one will *not* be able to fit those *pain- and pleasure-sensations*; they rather belong descriptively, according to their specific essence, *together with* the touch-, taste-, smell-sensations.

The fourteenth paragraph fixes the essential difference and warns against the equivocation of "feeling." In the fact that they are at best *presenting contents* or also *objects* of intentions, but not themselves intentions, an essential descriptive difference attests itself so great that we cannot seriously think of holding fast the unity of a genuine *genus*. To be sure, on both sides — the above-named acts of liking and these present sensations — there is uniformly talk of "feelings." But this circumstance cannot make us suspicious, as little as we let ourselves be deceived by the ordinary talk of *feeling* (Fühlen) in the sense of *touching* (Tasten) regarding the tactile sensations. The mere word "feeling" spans two essentially different genera.

The fifteenth paragraph aligns this with Brentano while qualifying. Already Brentano, in his discussion of the question of the intentionality of feelings, points to the equivocation discussed here. He distinguishes — if not in expression, then in sense — *pain- and pleasure-sensations* (feeling-sensations) from *pain and pleasure in the sense of feelings*. The contents of the former — or, as Husserl would say outright, the former themselves — count for Brentano (in his terminology) as "physical," the latter as "psychic" phenomena, and thus as belonging to essentially different upper genera. This conception seems to Husserl perfectly apt; he only doubts whether the *predominant* meaning-tendency of the word "feeling" does not aim at those *feeling-sensations*, and whether then the manifold *acts* designated as feelings do not owe this name to the feeling-sensations *essentially woven into them*. (A footnote: Husserl here, as elsewhere, *identifies* pain-sensation and the "content" of the pain-sensation, since he recognizes no special sensation-*acts* at all; he therefore cannot assent to Brentano's doctrine that acts of the genus *presenting*, in the form of acts of feeling-sensation, lie at the basis of feeling-acts.)

The sixteenth paragraph applies the distinction to *joy*, analyzing its strata. This distinction must be kept constantly in view and made fruitful in analyzing all complexions of feeling-sensations and feeling-acts. Thus the *joy* over a happy event is certainly an *act*. But this act — not a mere intentional character, but a *concrete* and *eo ipso complex* experience — comprises in its unity not only the *presentation* of the joyful event and the act-character of *liking* referred to it; rather, to the presentation a *pleasure-sensation* attaches, which is apprehended and localized on the one hand as *feeling-excitement* of the feeling psychophysical subject, and on the other hand as *objective property*: the event appears as if surrounded by a *rosy shimmer*. The event thus *pleasure-colored* is only then the *foundation* for the joyful turning-toward, for the liking, being-charmed, however one may name it. Likewise a sad event is not merely *presented* according to its thingly content, but appears *clothed* with the *coloring* of sorrow. The same displeasure-sensations that the empirical ego refers to itself (as ache in the heart) and localizes are, in the *feeling-determined apprehension of the event*, referred to the event itself. These relations are *purely presentational*; an essentially *new* manner of intention lies only in the hostile *being-repelled*, in the *active disliking*.

The seventeenth paragraph shows the *separability* of feeling-sensation from act. The pleasure- and pain-sensations can *endure* while the act-characters built upon them *fall away*. When the pleasure-arousing facts have moved into the background, when they are no longer apperceived as feeling-colored, indeed perhaps are no longer intentional objects at all, the *pleasure-excitement* can yet continue for a longer time; it is then possibly *itself* felt as agreeable: instead of functioning as *representative* of an agreeable property on the object, it is now merely referred to the *feeling subject*, or is itself a presented and pleasing *object*.

The eighteenth paragraph extends to *desire and willing* and offers two construals of "dark" strivings. Similar would be carried out in the sphere of *desiring and willing*. If one finds a difficulty in that not every desire seems to demand a conscious relation to a desired — since we are often moved by a dark longing and urge, driven toward an *unpresented* end-goal — and especially if one points to the wide sphere of *natural instincts*, which at least originally lack the conscious goal-presentation, we would answer: *Either* mere *sensations* are present here (we could speak, by analogy, of *desire-sensations*, without having to assert they belong to an essentially new genus of sensations) — experiences that really *lack* intentional relation and are therefore generically *foreign* to intentional desire; *or* we say it is a matter of intentional experiences, but of such as are *characterized as indeterminately directed intentions*, where the "indeterminacy" of the objective direction has not the meaning of a *privation* but designates a *descriptive character*, indeed a *presentation-character*. So too the presentation we accomplish when "something" stirs, when "it" rustles, when "someone" rings — the presentation accomplished *before* all utterance and verbal expression — is an "indeterminately" directed one; and the "indeterminacy" here belongs to the *essence* of the intention, whose determinacy it precisely is to present an indeterminate "something."

The nineteenth paragraph notes that either construal may fit and refuses generic community. Naturally, for some cases the one and for others the other conception may fit; and so here too we would concede, between the intentional and non-intentional drives or desirings, *no* relation of *generic community*, but only a relation of *equivocation*. The word "desire," like "feeling," spans heterogeneous strata.

The twentieth paragraph notes that classification follows *concrete complexions*. It is also to be noted that our classifying talk follows the *concrete complexions*, and that the *total character* of these unities can appear determined now by *sensation-moments* (e.g., pleasure- or drive-sensations), now by the *act-intentions* supported on them. Accordingly the *expressions* will orient themselves in formation and application now to the sensation-contents, now to the act-intentions, and so give occasion to the equivocations in question.

The twenty-first paragraph is an *Addendum* on *intensity*. In the self-evident tendency of this conception it lies *to ascribe all differences of intensity primarily and properly to the founding sensations*, but to the concrete acts only in a *secondary* sense — insofar, namely, as their concrete total character is *co-determined* by the intensity-differences of their sensation-foundation. The *act-intentions* — those non-self-sufficient moments that first confer on the acts their essential peculiarity as acts, characterizing them specifically as judgments, feelings, and so on — would be *in themselves intensity-less*. (Husserl adds that this would require more thorough analyses.)

### **§16. Distinction between descriptive and intentional content**

The first paragraph introduces the central distinction. Having secured our conception of the essence of acts against objections, and having granted them, in the character of intention (consciousness-ness in the single descriptive sense), essential generic unity, Husserl now introduces an important phenomenological distinction, readily intelligible after the foregoing: the distinction between the *real* (reell) content of an act and its *intentional* content. (A long footnote records a momentous later qualification: in the first edition "phenomenological" and "descriptive" were used exclusively for *real* experiential subsistence; but it becomes increasingly perceptible — and from here on — that the description of the *intentional objectivity as such* (taken as it is conscious in the concrete act-experience) represents *another direction* of purely intuitive, adequate description, which must *also* be called phenomenological. Pursuing this yields necessary and important extensions and improvements; Husserl refers to *Ideen I* and especially its account of *noesis* and *noema*.)

The second paragraph defines *real* content. By the *real phenomenological content* of an act we understand the *total aggregate* of its parts — indifferently whether concrete or abstract — in other words, the total aggregate of the part-experiences really *building it up*. To exhibit and describe such parts is the task of *purely descriptive psychological analysis* carried out in an experiential-scientific attitude. This analysis aims generally to dissect the inwardly experienced experiences in and for themselves, as they are really given in experience — without regard to *genetic* connections, but also without regard to what they *mean* outside themselves and what they may be *valid* for. The purely descriptive psychological analysis of an articulated *sound-formation* finds *sounds* and *abstract parts* or *unity-forms* of sounds; it does *not* find such things as *tone-vibrations*, *hearing-organ*, and the like; nor, on the other hand, anything like the *ideal sense* that makes the sound-formation a *name*, or even the *person* who may be named by the name. This example sufficiently clarifies what is meant. Naturally we know of the *real* contents of acts only through such descriptive analysis. That thereby — through imperfect clarity of intuitions, imperfect adaptation of the describing concepts, in short, through defective method — all sorts of "invented sensations" (Volkelt's phrase) can creep in, is not to be denied; but this concerns only the *correctness* of the relevant analyses in the individual case. What *is* evident — and this alone matters here — is that intentional experiences contain distinguishable *parts and sides*.

The third paragraph makes the *eidetic turn* explicit. Let us now make the turn from the psychological-experiential attitude into the *phenomenological-ideal-scientific* one. We switch off all experiential-scientific apperceptions and existence-positings; we take the inwardly experienced or otherwise inwardly intuited (e.g., of mere phantasy) according to its *pure experiential subsistence* and as a mere *exemplary substratum* for *ideations*; we *intuit ideatively* general essences and essence-connections out of it — ideal experience-species of various levels of generality and ideally valid essence-cognitions, which thus hold *a priori*, in unconditioned generality, for ideally possible experiences of the relevant species. So we win insights of *pure phenomenology* (here turned toward the real subsistences), whose description is thus thoroughly *ideal-scientific* and pure of all "experience," i.e., of co-positing of *real existence*. Whenever in simple speech we speak outright of *real* (and in general of phenomenological) analysis and description of experiences, it is always to be noted that the *attachment* of the discussions to the *psychological* is a mere *transitional stage*, that nothing of the empirically-real apprehensions and existence-positings belonging to it remains operative — in a word, that everywhere *purely phenomenological essence-validity* is meant and pretended.

The fourth paragraph defines real vs. intentional content and announces the *three* concepts. *Content* in the real sense is the simple application of the most general concept of content — valid in all domains — to the intentional experiences. When we now oppose to the real content the *intentional* content, the word already indicates that now the *peculiarity* of the intentional experiences (or acts) as such is to come into question. But here *various* concepts present themselves, all grounded in the *specific* nature of the acts and capable of being meant (and often *are* meant) in the same way under the phenomenological title *intentional content*. We will have to distinguish, for now, *three* concepts of intentional content: the *intentional object* of the act; its *intentional matter* (Materie) (in contrast to its *intentional quality* (Qualität)); and finally its *intentional essence* (Wesen). (A footnote explains why Husserl prefers "intentional" to "real" \[real\] for the object: "real" would sound much better beside "intentional" but carries the thought of a *thingly transcendence* which the reduction to real (reell) experiential immanence was precisely meant to switch off; we do well to reserve "real" consciously for the thingly.)

### **§17. The intentional content in the sense of the intentional object**

The first paragraph defines the first concept and recalls its non-immanence. A first concept of intentional content needs no elaborate preparations: it concerns the *intentional object* — e.g., when we present a house, precisely *this house*. That the intentional object in general does not fall into the *real* content of the relevant act, but rather *differs* from it entirely, has already been discussed. This holds not only of acts relating to "outer" things, but partly also of acts relating intentionally to one's *own present experiences* — as when I speak of my actually present experiences belonging to the *background* of consciousness. Only in those cases does *partial coincidence* enter where the intention really goes upon something *experienced in the intentional act itself*, as in acts of *adequate perception*.

The second paragraph distinguishes the *object as intended* from *the object which is intended*. With respect to the intentional content understood as object of the act, the following is to be distinguished: the *object as it is intended*, and outright the *object which is intended*. In every act an object is "presented" *as so-and-so determined*, and as such it is possibly target-point of *changing* intentions — judging, feeling, desiring. But cognition-connections (actual or possible) *external* to the real subsistence of the act itself can — through the way they close together into the unity of an intention — apportion to the *identically* presented object *objective properties* that the intention of the present act does not touch at all; or manifold *new* presentations can arise, all of which, precisely by virtue of the objective cognition-unity, may claim to present the *same* object. In all of them the *object which is intended* is then *the same*, but in each the *intention* is different; each *means the object in another way*. Thus the presentation "Germany's Kaiser" presents its object *as Kaiser*, and indeed as Germany's. This same one is the son of Kaiser Friedrich III, the grandson of Queen Victoria, and has many other properties here not named and presented. Accordingly one could, with respect to a given presentation, quite consistently speak of the *intentional* and *extra-intentional* content of its object; yet even without special terminology there are suitable, unmistakable expressions here, e.g., "the intended of the object."

The third paragraph introduces a *more important* distinction: the objectivity at which the whole act aims vs. the objects of its part-acts. Connected with the distinction just treated is another, still more important: the distinction between the *objectivity at which an act, taken fully and wholly, directs itself*, and the *objects at which the various part-acts direct themselves* which build up the same act. Every act relates intentionally to an objectivity belonging to it. This holds for simple as for composite acts. *However an act may be composed of part-acts*, so long as it is an act *at all*, it has its *correlate in an objectivity*. And it is *this* objectivity of which we say, in the *full* and *primary* sense, that the act relates to it. The *part-acts* too (if they are really not mere parts of the act but *acts* dwelling in the complex act as parts) relate to objects; these will in general *not* be identical with the object of the whole act, though they can occasionally be so. Naturally one can in a certain way say of the whole act too that it relates to these objects, but this holds only in a *secondary* sense; its intention goes upon them only insofar as it builds itself out of acts that *primarily* intend them. Or, seen from the other side: they are its objects only insofar as they help to *constitute* its proper object in the way it is intended. They function, say, as *relation-points* of relations by means of which the *primary* object is presented as *correlative* relation-point.

The fourth paragraph illustrates with "the knife on the table" and the judgment about the knife. The act corresponding to the name *the knife on the table* is evidently composite. The object of the *total* act is a *knife*, the object of a *part-act* is a *table*. But insofar as the former means the knife *as just lying on the table*, hence presents it in this *positional relation* to the table, one can also say, in a *secondary* sense, that the table is intentional object of the *nominal* total act. Again, to illustrate another important class of cases, in the proposition *the knife lies on the table*, the knife is indeed the object "about" which judgment is made or "of" which assertion is made; but it is nevertheless not the *primary* object — not the *full* object of the judgment, but only that of the *judgment-subject*. To the *whole judgment* there corresponds, as full and whole object, the *judged state of affairs* (Sachverhalt), which, as identically the same, can be presented in a *mere presentation*, *wished* in a wish, *asked* in a question, *doubted* in a doubt. In this latter respect, the wish "the knife should lie on the table" — corresponding to the judgment — indeed concerns the *knife*, but in it I wish not the knife but *this, that the knife lie on the table*, that the matter so stand. And this state of affairs is evidently not to be confused with the relevant judgment, or even with the *presentation* of the judgment — I do not wish the judgment or any presentation. Likewise the corresponding *question* concerns the knife, but what is *asked* is not the knife (which would make no sense) but the *lying-on-the-table* of the knife — it is asked *whether it be so*.

The fifth paragraph recommends a terminological caution. So much, provisionally, on the first sense of the talk of intentional contents. In view of the ambiguity of this talk, we will do best, in all cases where the *intentional object* is meant, *not* to speak of intentional *content* at all, but precisely of the *intentional object* of the relevant act.

### **§18. Simple and composite, founding and founded acts**

The first paragraph announces the program for the further senses of "intentional content." We have so far learned only *one* meaning of the talk of intentional contents. Its further meanings will arise in the following investigations, in which we wish to fix in view some important *peculiarities of the phenomenological essence of acts* and clarify the *ideal unities* grounded in them.

The second paragraph distinguishes *composite acts* from merely act-composed experiences, using the machine analogy. We attach to the already-touched distinction of *simple* and *composite* acts. *Not* every unified experience composed of acts is on that account already a *composite act*, just as not every arbitrary chaining-together of machines is a *composite machine*. The comparison clarifies what is still required. A composite machine is a machine *itself composed of machines*, and indeed this connection is such that the *accomplishment* of the total machine is a *total accomplishment* into which the accomplishments of the *part-machines* flow. Similarly with composite acts. Each part-act has its particular intentional relation, its unified object, its way of relating to it. But these manifold part-acts *close together* into a *total act* whose *total accomplishment* consists in the *unity* of the intentional relation. And here too the single acts contribute through their single accomplishments: the unity of the presented objectivity and the whole manner of the intentional relation to it constitutes itself *not beside* the part-acts, but *in* them and at the same time *in the manner of their connection* — which brings about the *unified act* and not the mere unity of an experience in general. The object of the total act could not appear as such, as it factually appears, if the part-acts did not make their objects present in their way: their overall function is precisely to *present* parts of the object, external relation-members to it, relation-forms, and the like. The same holds of those act-moments which, beyond making-present, make up the *qualitative* element of the part-acts and their unity into the *quality* of the total act, and so determine the specifically distinct *manners* in which the several objectivities are "taken up into consciousness."

The third paragraph gives *predication* as example. The unity of *categorical* or *hypothetical* predication can serve as example. Here the total acts clearly articulate into part-acts. The *subject-member* of the categorical assertion is an *underlying act* (subject-positing) on which the *predicate-positing* — the according or denying of the predicate — builds itself. Likewise the *presupposition* of the hypothetical assertion constitutes itself in a clearly delimited part-act on which the *conditioned positing of the consequent* is built. And the respective total experience is evidently an *act*, a *judgment*, with a *total objectivity*, namely a *state of affairs* (Sachverhalt). As the judgment is not *beside* or *between* the subject- and predicate-acts, the presupposing and inferring acts, but *in* them as the *pervading unity*, so on the *correlative* side the *judged state of affairs* is the *objective unity* which, as what it here appears, builds itself up out of subject and predicate, out of the presupposed and the thereupon posited.

The fourth paragraph treats more complex *founding* and the strict concept of foundation. The state of affairs can also be *more complicated*. On such a many-membered act (whose members may themselves be articulated) a *new* act can build itself — e.g., on the *establishing* of a state of affairs a *joy*, which is thereby joy *over* the state of affairs. The joy is *not* a concrete act *for itself* and the judgment an act *lying beside* it; rather, the judgment is the *founding* act for the joy, it determines its content, *realizes its abstract possibility* — for *without* such founding, joy cannot be at all. (A footnote: foundation is here meant in the *strict sense* of the Third Investigation, and the term is used everywhere only in this strictness.) Again, *judgments* — be they surmises, doubts, questions, wishes, volitional acts — can *found*; and conversely, acts of the latter kind can appear as *foundations*. So there are *manifold combinations* in which acts close together into total acts; and even the most fleeting consideration teaches that in the manner of *interweaving* and of *foundation* of acts by underlying acts (which make them possible in the concretion), *remarkable differences* obtain, of whose systematic investigation (even a descriptive-psychological one) scarcely the most meager beginnings are so far to be found.

### **§19. The function of attention in complex acts. The phenomenological relation between word-sound and sense as example**

The first paragraph announces the example and a further observation about *activity*. How far the differences extend in this respect an example will show — one no less interesting than those above — namely the already-considered *whole of expression and sense*. It will also illustrate an observation no one can miss here: that, as regards the *activity* with which the acts of a complexion assert themselves, very considerable differences are possible. Normally the act-character that *spans* the unity of all part-acts, having them all under itself — indifferently whether it is a proper act-intention, as in the example of joy, or a unity-form running through all parts — will unfold the *greatest activity*. In this act we *live preferentially*; in the subordinate acts only according to the *significance* of their accomplishment for the total act and its intention. And to speak of differences of significance in accomplishment is evidently itself only another expression for a certain *preferring*, which benefits some part-acts and not others.

The second paragraph turns to the example: word-sound vs. meaning. It concerns the unity of the acts in which an *expression* (taken as sensory word-sound) constitutes itself, with the *quite other* acts in which the *meaning* constitutes itself — a connection evidently essentially different from the further unity of the latter acts with those in which they find their nearer or farther *fulfillment* through intuition. And not only the manner of connection is essentially different, but also the *activity* with which the several acts are accomplished. The expression is, say, perceived, yet in this perceiving "our interest does not live"; if not distracted, we attend rather to *the signified* than to the *signs*; to the *sense-conferring* acts belongs the *predominant activity*. As for the possibly accompanying acts of evidencing, illustrating, or otherwise functioning *intuitions*, woven into the unity of the total act, they claim the ruling "interest" in *varying* degree. They can *predominate*, as in the perceptual judgment or the analogously built image-judgment, where we wish only to *express* the perception or imagination in which we live, or as in the law-judgment fully illumined by evidence; they can *recede* and finally appear quite incidental, as in cases of imperfect or wholly improper *illustration* of the ruling thought — fleeting phantasms to which scarcely any interest attaches. (In the extreme case one may doubt whether the accompanying intuitive presentations still belong to the unity of the expressive act, or are mere *companions*, coexisting but not knit into one act with it.)

The third paragraph announces closer analysis, given the value of the matter for the work. By virtue of the own value that the fullest possible clarification of the matter has for us in the case of expressions, Husserl wishes to carry out some points more closely.

The fourth paragraph treats *expression* as physical object. Expression and sense are *two objective unities* presenting themselves to us in certain acts. The expression in itself — e.g., the written word — is, as already set out in the First Investigation, a *physical object* just like any pen-stroke or ink-blot on paper; it is "given" to us in the same sense as any other physical object, i.e., it *appears*; and that it appears means, here as there, nothing but that a certain act is experience in which the sensation-experiences are "apperceived" in a certain way. The acts in question are naturally *perceptual* or *phantasy* presentations; in them the expression in the *physical* sense constitutes itself.

The fifth paragraph argues that the sense-conferring acts fuse with the word-perception into *one* total act. What makes the expression an expression are, as we know, the acts *attached* to it. They are not *externally* beside it, merely simultaneously conscious; they are rather *one* with it, and so one that we can scarcely avoid conceding that the connection of the several acts (for under the title "expression" we naturally mean, in convenient looseness, the act-unity *presenting* it) really yields a *unified total act*. Thus an assertion, a statement, is a strictly unified experience, and indeed of the genus *judgment*, as we like to say outright. We find in ourselves not a mere *sum* of acts, but *one act*, on which we distinguish, as it were, a bodily and a spiritual side. Likewise an express *wish* is not a mere being-beside-one-another of expression and wish (where not, in addition, a judgment about the wish — which is admittedly disputed), but a *whole*, an *act*; and we call it outright a *wish*. The physical expression, the word-sound, may count as *inessential* in this unity. So it is, insofar as any other word-sound could have stood in its place in the same function — indeed it could even drop away entirely. But once it is *there*, in the function of word-sound, it *fuses* with the adjoined acts into one act. It is also certain that the connection here is in a certain way quite *inessential*, since the expression — the appearing word-sound (the objective written sign) — is *not* to count as a component of the objectivity meant in the total act, and in general not as something belonging to it "objectively" (sachlich), determining it in any way.

The sixth paragraph distinguishes the *word-constituting* contribution from the *founding* contribution, while affirming an *intentional* unity. The contribution that the *word-sound-constituting* acts make to the total act (say the assertion) is of a *characteristically different* kind from the contribution of the *founding* acts in the examples discussed above (e.g., the part-acts belonging to predicative members in full predications). On the other hand we must not fail to recognize that a *certain intentional connection* between word and thing is *present* in all this. In that, e.g., the word *names* the thing, it appears in a certain way again *one* with it, as something *belonging to it* — only, of course, not as objective part or objective determinacy. So *objective relationlessness* does not exclude a certain *intentional unity* corresponding, as correlate, to the connection of the corresponding acts into one act. As confirmation may serve the recollection of the *hard-to-eradicate inclination* to *exaggerate* the unity between word and thing, to impute to it an objective character, even in the form of a *mystical* unity. (A footnote refers to the deeper analysis in the Sixth Investigation, §6 ff.)

The seventh paragraph identifies which acts determine the total act's *character*. In this connected act — comprising expression-appearance and sense-giving acts — it is evidently the *latter* acts, or the act-unity ruling in them, that essentially *determine* the character of the total act. Accordingly we name the *expressive* and the corresponding *non-expressive* experience with the same name: judgment, wish, and so on. In the complexion, then, the one acts *prevail* in a peculiar way. We expressed this on occasion thus: when we normally accomplish an *expressing* as such, we *do not live* in the acts that constitute the expression *as physical object*; not to this object belongs our "interest"; rather we live in the *sense-giving* acts, we are exclusively *turned toward* the objective that *appears* in them, we have aimed at *it*, we mean *it* in the special, pregnant sense. We also indicated how a special *turning-toward* the physical expression is possible, but also essentially *changes* the character of the experience — it then ceases to be an "expressing" in the normal sense of the word.

The eighth paragraph identifies attention as the underlying phenomenon and gives Husserl's central thesis about it. Evidently we here deal with a case of a general and — despite all efforts — not yet sufficiently clarified fact: the fact of *attention* (Aufmerksamkeit). Surely nothing has so obstructed the correct insight as the *failure to recognize* the circumstance that attention is a *distinguishing function* belonging to *acts* in the precise sense of *intentional experiences*, and that therefore there can be no talk of its descriptive understanding so long as one *confuses* being-experienced (in the sense of the simple existence of a content in consciousness) with *intentional objectivity*. *Acts* must be there in order that we can "*live*" in them, possibly "*be absorbed*" in their accomplishment; and in doing this (in modes of accomplishment to be more closely described) we *attend to the objects* of these acts — we are turned toward them in passing or primarily, possibly thematically occupied with them. The one and the other is *the same*, only expressed from different sides.

The ninth paragraph criticizes the prevailing view that attention picks out *experienced contents*. By contrast, one speaks of attention as though it were a *title for modes* of *preferring elevation* accruing to the respectively *experienced contents*. At the same time one speaks as though these contents (the respective experiences themselves) were that of which, in normal speech, we say we are *attentive* to. The *possibility* of an attention to *experienced contents* Husserl does not deny; but where we are attentive to experienced contents, they are precisely *objects of an ("inner") perception*, and perception here is not the mere existence of the content in the consciousness-connection, but rather an *act* in which the content becomes *objective* to us. And so it is *intentional objects* of some acts whatever, and *only* intentional objects, to which we are and can be attentive. With this the normal idiom harmonizes — over whose real sense the briefest reflection could have given information. According to it, the respective objects of attention are objects of (inner or outer) perception, memory, expectation, or also states of affairs of a scientific consideration.

The tenth paragraph refines the harmless self-evidence against the dangerous equivocation. Certainly, attention can be spoken of only where "we have in consciousness" that to which we are attentive. What is not "consciousness-content" cannot be noticeable, attended to, made a theme of consciousness. This is self-evident, but the equivocation of the word "consciousness-content" now becomes *dangerous*. The self-evidence by no means says that the direction of attention is necessarily one upon consciousness-contents in the sense of *experiences*, as though *things* and other real or ideal objects that are *not* experiences could *not* also be noticed; rather it says that *some act must underlie* in which that to which we are to attend becomes, in the widest sense of the word, objective or presented to us. This *presenting* can be *non-intuitive* as well as intuitive, an ever-so-inadequate one as well as an adequate one.

The eleventh paragraph leaves open whether attention is itself an act or a *mode of accomplishment*. In another respect it would be worth considering whether the *preferring* that an act experiences over others simultaneous — in that we "live in it" and are thus primarily or secondarily turned toward its objects, possibly "specially occupied" with them — is *itself to count as an act* (which would consequently make all *prevailing* acts *eo ipso* complex), or whether, under the title attention, it is rather a matter of mere *modes of accomplishment* (Vollzugsmodi) of the acts (to be more closely described in their peculiar particularizations) — *as is doubtless the case*. Husserl thus leans toward the latter view: attention is a mode of how acts are carried out, not a separate superadded act.

The twelfth paragraph states the limited aim of the section. But Husserl did not wish to carry out a "theory" of attention here, only to discuss the important *function* it plays as an *elevating factor* of act-characters in composite acts, and through which it essentially *influences* the phenomenological *formation* of the latter.

### **§20. The difference of the quality and the matter of an act**

The first paragraph introduces the *quality/matter* distinction. In a quite different direction from the last-treated difference (between acts we *live in* and acts that *run alongside*) lies a most important and at first quite self-evident difference: that between the *general character* of the act — which marks it as merely *presenting*, or as judging, feeling, desiring — and its "*content*," which marks it as *presentation of this presented*, as *judgment of this judged*. Thus the two assertions "2 × 2 \= 4" and "Ibsen counts as chief founder of modern realism in dramatic art" are, *as assertions*, of one kind: each is qualified as *assertion*. This common element we call the *judgment-quality* (Urteilsqualität). But the one is judgment of *this*, the other a judgment of *another* "content"; we speak here, to distinguish from other content-concepts, of the *judgment-matter* (Urteilsmaterie). Similar distinctions between *quality* and *matter* we perform for all acts.

The second paragraph clarifies what *matter* is *not*, and how it is isolated. The title "matter" does *not* concern a division and collecting reunion of *components* of the act, such as subject-act, predicate-act; on that reading the unified total content would be the act itself. What we have in view is something quite different. *Content in the sense of matter* is a *component* of the concrete act-experience which it can have in *common* with acts of *quite other quality*. It therefore emerges most clearly when we set up a series of *identities* in which the act-qualities *change* while the matter remains *identically the same*. No great arrangements are needed. We recall the usual talk that *the same content* can be once content of a *mere presentation*, another time content of a *judgment*, again content of a *question*, a *doubt*, a *wish*. Whoever *presents* that there are intelligent beings on Mars presents the same as whoever *asserts* there are intelligent beings on Mars, and again as whoever *asks* whether there are intelligent beings on Mars, or *wishes* would that there were intelligent beings on Mars. The equality of "content" amid difference of act-quality finds its visible *grammatical* imprint, and so the harmony of the grammatical formations can indicate the direction of our analysis.

The third paragraph asks what "same content" means and shows that *object-sameness* is insufficient. What does "same content" mean here? Evidently the *intentional objectivity* in the various acts is the same: one and the same state of affairs is presented in the presentation, posited as valid in the judgment, wished in the wish, asked in the question. But with this remark we do *not* suffice, as the following consideration will bring out. For the *really phenomenological* consideration the objectivity itself is *nothing*; it is, generally speaking, *transcendent* to the act. *Indifferent in what sense and with what right its "being" is spoken of, indifferent whether it is real or ideal, whether truly existent, possible, or impossible, the act is "directed at it."* If one asks how it is to be understood that the non-existent or transcendent can count as intentional object in an act in which it is not at all, there is no other answer than the one already given and indeed fully sufficient: the object is an *intentional* one, that is, an *act is there* with a definitely characterized *intention*, which in this definiteness makes up precisely what we call the intention upon this object. The relating-to-the-object is a peculiarity *belonging to the own essential subsistence of the act-experience*, and the experiences that show it are called (by definition) intentional experiences or acts. *All differences in the manner of objective relation are descriptive differences of the relevant intentional experiences.*

The fourth paragraph shows that object-relatedness does *not* exhaust the act's essence; two independent variations cross. It is to be noted that the peculiarity attesting itself in the phenomenological essence of the act — to relate to a *certain* objectivity and no other — cannot *exhaust* the whole phenomenological essence of the act. We just spoke of differences in the *manner of objective relation*. But under this are gathered *fundamentally different* and *wholly independently varying* differences. Some concern the *act-qualities* (objectivities being now intentional in the manner of presented, now of judged, asked). With this variation a *second* crosses, wholly independent of it: the variation of *objective relation* — one act can relate to *this*, another to *that* objective, indifferently whether the acts are of like or different quality. *Every quality is combinable with every objective relation.* This second variation thus strikes a *second side* of the phenomenological content of the act, *different from quality*.

The fifth paragraph notes that even quality \+ object-direction does *not* fix the act, anticipating a *third* variation. On closer view we soon notice that here still *another* variation-possibility, independent of quality, can be shown, with respect to which "different manners of relation to the objective" can well be spoken of; and at the same time it strikes us that the twofold variation just performed is *not yet* perfectly suited to set off clearly, from quality, what we must define as *matter*. According to it we would distinguish two sides on each act: the *quality*, marking the act as e.g. presentation or judgment, and the *matter*, conferring the determinate *direction* on the objective (making it the case that the presentation presents precisely *this* and nothing else). That is doubtless correct, yet in a certain respect *misleading*. For at first one is inclined to interpret simply: matter is what in the act confers the direction precisely upon *this and no other object* — so that the act is *unambiguously* determined by its qualitative character and the object it is to intend. Just this supposed self-evidence proves *incorrect*. For it is easy to see that, *when we fix at the same time the quality and the objective direction, certain variations are still possible*.

The sixth paragraph gives the decisive *examples* (equilateral/equiangular triangle, a+b / b+a). Two acts identically qualified — e.g., as presentations — can appear directed at the *same* objective, and indeed *with evidence*, *without* the acts agreeing according to their full *intentional essence*. Thus the presentations *the equilateral triangle* and *the equiangular triangle* are *different in content*, and yet both, as can be evidently shown, are directed at the *same* object. They present the same object, but still "*in different ways*." Similar holds for presentations like *a length of a \+ b* and *a length of b \+ a* units, and self-evidently for assertions which, otherwise meaning-identical, differ only by such "*equivalent*" concepts. So too in comparing differently equivalent assertions, e.g., *there will be rainy weather* and *the weather will become rainy*. But if we take a series of acts like the judgment *it will rain today*, the surmise *it will probably rain today*, the question *will it rain today?*, the wish *if only it would rain today\!* — this exemplifies the possibility of *identity* not merely as regards objective relation in general, but also *as regards the manner of objective relation understood in the new sense* — a manner *not* prescribed by the quality of the act.

The seventh paragraph defines *matter* fully. The quality determines only *whether* what is, in a definite way, already "made present" is intentionally present as *wished, asked, posited judgmentally*, and the like. Accordingly the *matter* must count for us as that in the act which *first of all confers on it the relation to an objective*, and this relation in *so perfect a determinacy* that through the matter *not only the objective in general which the act means, but also the manner in which it means it*, is firmly determined. (A footnote warns of unavoidable ambiguities of "determinacy"/"indeterminacy": e.g., the indeterminacy of the perceptual presentation regarding the back-side of the object, or of "particular" assertions vs. singular ones, has a quite different sense from the one here in question.) The matter — to clarify still further — is the peculiarity lying in the phenomenological content of the act which determines *not only that the act apprehends the respective objectivity, but also as what it apprehends it*, what marks, relations, categorial forms it ascribes to it *in itself*. It lies in the matter of the act that the object counts for the act as *this* and no other; the matter is, so to speak, the *sense of objective apprehension* (Auffassungssinn) founding the quality but indifferent to its differences. *Like* matters can never give a *different* objective relation; but *different* matters can well give the *same* objective relation. The latter is shown by the examples above; the differences of *equivalent but not tautological* expressions concern the matter. To such differences there corresponds naturally no conceivable *partitioning* of the matter (as if one piece corresponded to the same object, another to the different manner of presenting it). Evidently objective relation is a priori only possible as a *determinate manner* of objective relation; it can come about only in a *fully determinate matter*.

The eighth paragraph adds the *abstractness* of quality and matter. The *act-quality* is doubtless an *abstract moment* of the act, simply unthinkable detached from *any* matter. Or should we hold an experience possible that would be *judgment-quality* but not *judgment of a determinate matter*? With that the judgment would lose the character of an intentional experience, evidently belonging to it as essential. Similar will hold for *matter*: a matter that would be neither matter of a presenting, nor of a judging, will be held unthinkable. Quality and matter are *mutually non-self-sufficient*: each demands the other to be a concrete act.

The ninth paragraph flags the persisting *ambiguity* of "manner of objective relation." On the *double sense* of the talk of the *manner of objective relation* — which after these considerations refers now to differences of *quality*, now of *matter* — attention is henceforth to be paid; we will meet it through suitable turns drawing on the terms *quality* and *matter*. That the same talk has still *other* important meanings will emerge later.

### **§21. The intentional and the meaning-essence**

The first paragraph announces a *third* concept of intentional content. The closer investigation of the relevant, quite difficult problems Husserl defers for the moment, turning at once to a new distinction in which a once-again-new concept of the act's "intentional content" — to be separated from its full descriptive content — accrues to us.

The second paragraph defines the *intentional essence*. In the descriptive content of every act we distinguished *quality* and *matter* as two *mutually demanding* moments. Taking both together again, it seems at first that we have thereby only restored the relevant act. But on closer view another conception forces itself on us, according to which the *two moments, brought to unity, do not make up the concretely complete act*. In fact, two acts can be alike both in respect of their quality and in respect of their matter, and yet still be descriptively *different*. Insofar as quality and matter must count for us (as we shall hear) as the thoroughly *essential* and therefore never-dispensable components of an act, it would be suitable to designate the *unity of both* — which makes up only a *part* of the full act — as the *intentional essence* (intentionales Wesen) of the act. Holding fast to this term and the conception belonging to it, Husserl at the same time introduces a *second* term. Insofar as it is a matter of acts that function, or could function, as *meaning-conferring* acts in expressions (whether all can do so will have to be investigated later), there shall more specifically be talk of the *meaning-essence* (bedeutungsmäßiges Wesen) of the act. Its *ideating abstraction* yields the *meaning* in our ideal sense.

The third paragraph justifies the definition through a new identity-series. To justify this conceptual determination, the following new series of *identifications* can serve. We say generally and in a good sense that an individual at different times, or several individuals at the same or different times, can have *the same* presentation, recollection, expectation, make *the same* perception, utter *the same* assertion, cherish *the same* wish, *the same* hope. (A footnote: in the ideative grasping of the phenomenological essential differences, everything empirically psychological in the exemplifications becomes irrelevant and drops out.)

The fourth paragraph distinguishes "same presentation" from "same object." To have the *same* presentation says also, but does *not* say the *same* as, to present the *same object*. The presentation I have of Greenland's ice-deserts is certainly *another* than the one Nansen has of them; but the *object* is the same. Likewise the ideal objects *straight* and *shortest line* are *identical*, but the presentations (with suitable definition of "straight") are *different*.

The fifth paragraph rules out *individual* identity and *complete likeness* as the meaning of "same presentation." The talk of the same presentation does not mean *individual identity* of the acts, as if my consciousness had grown together with another's. It means just as little the relation of *complete likeness* — indistinguishability with regard to all inner constituents, as if one were a mere duplicate of the other. We have the *same* presentation of a thing when we have presentations in which the thing presents itself not merely *in general* but *as exactly the same*; i.e., in the same "apprehension-sense" or on the basis of the *same matter*. In "essence" we then have in fact the same presentation despite other phenomenological differences. The significance of such essential identity emerges most clearly when we think of the function of presentations as *foundations* for higher acts.

The sixth paragraph gives an *analytic* criterion of essential identity, for presentations and judgments. Equivalently, we can designate this essential identity thus: two presentations are *in essence the same* if, on the basis of *each* of them, taken purely for itself (hence *analytically*), *exactly the same* and nothing else could be asserted about the presented thing. Similarly for the other act-kinds. Two judgments are *essentially the same judgment* if everything that would hold of the judged state of affairs according to the one judgment (purely on the basis of the judgment-content itself) would also have to hold of it according to the other, and nothing else. Their *truth-value* is the same — and it is so, evidently, when "the" judgment, the *intentional essence* as unity of judgment-quality and judgment-matter, is the same.

The seventh paragraph shows the intentional essence does *not* exhaust the act, using *phantasy*. The intentional essence does *not* phenomenologically *exhaust* the act. For example, a phantasy-presentation qualified as mere imagining changes, in the respect considered, *inessentially* if the *fullness and vividness* of the sensory contents co-building it increase or decrease; or, referred to the object, if the object appears now with greater clarity and distinctness, now dissolves in nebulous blurredness, fades in its colorings. Whether one assumes here intensity-changes, whether one denies in principle the *likeness* of the sensory phantasms here occurring with the sensations within perception or not — at all events the *absolute qualities, forms*, etc., matter little, provided only the *intention* of the act, its "meaning" so to speak, remains unchanged. In all these phenomenologically considerable changes of the *fictionalizing* phantasy-appearance, the object itself can stand before consciousness continually as the *one and same unchanged, equally determined* one (identity of *matter*); we ascribe the changes not to it but to the "*appearance*," we "*mean*" it as constantly persisting; and we mean it so *in the manner of mere fiction* (identity of *quality*). By contrast, the matter *changes* in the course of the unified presentation of an object giving itself *as altered* (notwithstanding the overarching unity-form, to which corresponds the identity of the "self"-altering in the intentional object); and similarly when new *marks* enter into the apprehension of an unchangedly conscious object, marks that previously did not belong to the intentional content of the object, to the object of *this* presentation as such.

The eighth paragraph extends the result to *perception*. With perception it stands no differently. Here too, when we jointly make "the same" perception or merely "repeat" the made one, it is a matter only of the *identical unity of the matter*, and so of the intentional essence — which by no means excludes a *change* in the descriptive content of the experience. The same holds for the changing *share* that phantasy has or can have in perception, in the making-present of the perceived. Whether *phantasy-presentations* of the *back-side* of this tobacco-box lying before me come to life at all, and how they then behave as to fullness, steadiness, vividness, does *not* touch the *essential content* (the apprehension-sense) of the perception — that in it which, suitably understood, explains the fully justified talk of *the same* perception over against a plurality of phenomenologically *different* perceptual acts. In all this the object is *presupposedly* perceived as the same, equipped with the same determinacies, namely "*meant*" or "*apprehended*" and *posited* in perceiving fashion.

The ninth paragraph shows perception and phantasy can *share* matter, so that *intuitive* species-differences are *not* determined by intentional essence. A perception can have its *matter* in common with a *phantasy-presentation*, provided this presentation apprehends the object or state of affairs imaginatively "as exactly the same" as the perception apprehends it perceptively, so that the one imputes to it objectively nothing the other does not also impute. Since the presentation can also be like-qualified (recollection), we see already that the *species-differences of the intuitive acts* are *not* determined by the intentional essence. The difference between perceiving and phantasying lies *outside* quality and matter.

The tenth paragraph generalizes to *all* act-kinds and to *meaning*. Analogously for acts of every kind. Several persons cherish *the same* wish if their wishing intention is the same. With one the wish may be fully express, with another not; with one intuitively clear as regards the founding presentation-content, with another more or less non-intuitive. In every case the identity of the "essential" lies evidently in the two moments distinguished above: in the same act-quality and the same matter. The same we claim also for the *expressive* and specifically the *meaning-conferring* acts, in such a way that their *meaningful* element — that in them which forms the *real* phenomenological correlate of the ideal *meaning* — *coincides with their intentional essence*.

## **The eleventh paragraph confirms by the earlier identity-series and leaves room for descriptive differences. To confirm the conception of the meaning-essence (meaning *in concreto*), Husserl recalls the *identity-series* by which (in the First Investigation) the unity of *meaning* was separated from the unity of *objectivity*, and the frequent examples of expressive experiences serving to illustrate the general conception of the intentional essence. The *identity* of "the" judgment or "the" assertion lies in the *identical meaning* that repeats itself in the manifold single acts precisely *as the same* and is *represented* in them by the *meaning-essence*. That a wide *latitude* remains open here for very considerable descriptive differences regarding *other* components of the acts, Husserl has set out at length.**

## **Appendix to Paragraphs 11 and 20: Toward a Critique of the "Image-Theory" and of the Doctrine of the "Immanent" Objects of Acts**

The first paragraph names two errors to be avoided. Of *two* fundamental and well-nigh ineradicable errors one must guard oneself in the phenomenological interpretation of the relation between act and object: (1) the error of the *image-theory* (Bildertheorie), which believes it has sufficiently clarified the fact of presenting (enclosed in every act) by saying: "outside" is, or at least under circumstances is, the thing itself; in consciousness is, as its proxy, an *image* (Bild).

The second paragraph refutes the image-theory's central oversight. Against this it is to be remarked that this conception entirely *overlooks* the most important point: that in image-presenting we *mean*, *on the basis of* the appearing "*image-object*" (Bildobjekt), the *depicted* object (the "*image-subject*," Bildsujet). But the *imageness* of the object functioning as image is evidently no inner character (no "real predicate"), as if an object were *pictorial* the way it is, e.g., red and ball-shaped. Whence comes it, then, that we get *beyond* the "image" alone given in consciousness and are able to relate it *as image* to a certain consciousness-foreign object? The reference to *similarity* between image and thing gets us no further. Similarity is, at least when the thing really exists, doubtless present as an objective fact. But for the consciousness that (presupposedly) has *only* the image, this fact is *simply nothing*; it can therefore not serve to clarify the essence of the presenting — more closely, the *depicting* — relation to the object external to it (the image-subject). The similarity between two objects, however great, does *not* yet make the one the *image* of the other. Only through the capacity of a presenting *ego* to *use* the similar as image-representative for something similar — to have the one merely intuitively present and yet *mean* the other in its stead — does the image become image at all.

The third paragraph locates imageness in a *peculiar intentional consciousness*. In this can lie only that the image *as such* constitutes itself in a peculiar *intentional consciousness*, and that the *inner* character of this act — the specific peculiarity of this "manner of apperception" — makes up not only what we call image-presenting in general, but, according to the particular and likewise *inner* determinacy, also further what we call the image-presenting of *this or that determinate object*. The reflective and relating talk that opposes image-object and image-subject points *not* to two *really appearing objects in the imaginative act itself*, but to *possible* cognition-connections, accomplishing themselves in *new* acts, in which the image-intention would *fulfill* itself and so the *synthesis* between image and re-presented thing would realize itself. The crude way of speaking of *inner images* (in contrast to outer objects) may *not be tolerated* in descriptive psychology (and still less in pure phenomenology). The painting is image only for an *image-constituting consciousness* which, through its (here perception-founded) imaginative apperception, first confers on a primary, perceptually appearing object the "*validity*" or "*meaning*" of an image.

The fourth paragraph derives the infinite-regress argument against the image-theory of perception. If the apprehension *as image* already presupposes an object intentionally given to consciousness, it would evidently lead to an *infinite regress* to let *this* itself be again constituted through an image — hence to speak seriously, regarding a simple *perception*, of a "perceptual image" dwelling in it, "by means of" which it relates to the "thing itself." On the other hand, one must learn to see thoroughly that in *every* case some "*constitution*" of the presentational object *for* consciousness, and *in* it, in its own essential content, is needed; that an object is therefore *not* presented for consciousness by an "content" somehow similar to the transcendent thing *simply being* in consciousness (which, exactly weighed, dissolves into sheer absurdity), but rather that *in the phenomenological essence of consciousness itself* all relation to its objectivity is enclosed, and *can* in principle only be enclosed therein — and indeed *as* relation to a "transcendent" thing. This relation is a "*direct*" one when it concerns a *simple* presenting, a *mediate* one when it concerns a *founded*, e.g. *depicting* presenting.

The fifth paragraph rejects the spatial picture of image-in-consciousness. One may not speak and think as though the so-called "image" were related to consciousness as the picture is related to the *room* in which it is set up, and as though with the substruction of an *interpenetration of two objects* even the least would be made intelligible. One must rise to the fundamental insight that *solely* through a *phenomenological essence-analysis* of the relevant acts is the desired understanding to be attained — here, of the act-experiences of "*imagination*" in the old very wide sense (Kant's and Hume's *Einbildungskraft*) — and first that it makes up their (a priori) essential peculiarity that in *them* "an object appears," and indeed now simply, directly appears, and now in such a way that it counts not for itself but as "*image-re-presentation*" of an object similar to it. It is not to be overlooked that the *representing image-object* itself again, like every appearing object, constitutes itself in an act (one founding the image-character in the first place).

The sixth paragraph extends the critique to the *sign-theory*. Evidently this exposition transfers, *mutatis mutandis*, to the representation-theory in the wider sense of the *sign-theory* (Zeichentheorie). Sign-being too is no real predicate; it likewise needs a *founded act-consciousness*, the recourse to certain novel act-characters that are the *phenomenologically alone decisive* and, with regard to this predicate, the *alone really phenomenological*.

The seventh paragraph adds a general objection to all such theories. All such "theories" are moreover hit by the objection that they simply *ignore* the *fullness* of the *essentially different manners of presentation* that can be exhibited, within the classes *intuitive* and *empty* presentation, through purely phenomenological analysis.

The eighth paragraph turns to the *second* error: the alleged real difference between immanent and transcendent objects. It is a grave error to make a *real* (reell) difference at all between the "merely immanent" or "intentional" objects on the one side and the possibly corresponding "real" and "transcendent" objects on the other — whether one then interprets this difference as one between a sign or image really present in consciousness and the signified or depicted thing; or whether one in any other way *substitutes* for the "immanent" object some real consciousness-datum, even the content in the sense of the meaning-giving moment. Such errors, dragging themselves through the centuries (one thinks of the ontological argument of *Anselm*), have their hold in the *equivocation* of the talk of *immanence* and of similar idioms.

The ninth paragraph states the famous *analytic* thesis. One need only pronounce it, and everyone must acknowledge it: *that the intentional object of the presentation is the same as its real and, in the given case, its outer object, and that it is absurd to distinguish between the two.* The transcendent object would not be the *object of this presentation* at all if it were not its *intentional* object. And this is obviously a merely *analytic* proposition. The object of the presentation, of the "intention," *is and means* the *presented, the intentional* object. If I present God or an angel, an intelligible being-in-itself or a physical thing or a round square, then this here-named and *transcendent* is precisely *meant*, hence (only with another word) *intentional* object; and it is indifferent whether this object *exists*, whether it is fictitious or absurd.

The tenth paragraph clarifies what "merely intentional object" does and does not mean. That the object is a "merely intentional" one means, of course, *not*: it *exists*, but only *in* the *intentio* (hence as its real component), or there exists in it some *shadow* of it; rather it means: the *intention*, the "*meaning*" of a so-constituted object, *exists* — but *not* the object. If, on the other hand, the intentional object *does* exist, then not merely the intention, the meaning, but also the *meant* exists. (A footnote reminds us that "meaning" here does not exactly mean *attending to* the object, or being thematically occupied with it, though such is also comprised in the general talk of meaning.) Enough about these self-evidences, still so misinterpreted today by not a few researchers.

## **The eleventh paragraph reserves the legitimate distinction. What has just been set out does *not*, of course, exclude that (as already touched) a distinction is drawn between the object *simpliciter*, which is in each case intended, and the object *as* it is thereby intended (in what *apprehension-sense* and possibly in what "*fullness*" of intuition), and that to the latter title *its own analyses and descriptions* belong. The thesis of identity holds for the object *which* is intended; it leaves room for the rich descriptions of the object *as* intended.**

## **Chapter Three: The Matter of the Act and the Underlying Presentation**

### **§22. The question concerning the relation between matter and quality of the act**

The first paragraph frames the concluding consideration of the general inquiries. The general investigations bearing on the *phenomenological structure* of intentional experiences in general Husserl closes with a consideration of no small importance for clarifying the *guiding problems* specially belonging to the domain of *meaning*. It concerns the *relation of quality and matter*, and the *sense* in which every act *needs* a "*presentation*" as its *basis* and also *includes* such. Here we strike at once on *fundamental difficulties* hitherto scarcely heeded and at all events not formulated. This gap in our phenomenological cognitions is the more perceptible since one must judge that *without filling it* there can be no talk of a real understanding of the *essential structure* of intentional experiences — and so also of *meanings*.

The second paragraph reaffirms that quality and matter are *inner* moments. Quality and matter we had distinguished as two *moments*, two *inner constituents of all acts* — surely rightly. When we designate an experience as a *judgment*, it must have an *inner determinacy*, not some externally attached mark, that distinguishes it as judgment from wishes, hopes, and other act-kinds. This determinacy it has in common with all judgments; what distinguishes it from every other (or "essentially" other) is above all (apart from certain moments to be investigated later) the *matter*. And the matter too represents an *inner moment* of the act.

The third paragraph notes that this is shown by *comparison*, not directly, and poses the section's question. This shows itself not so much *directly* — for one will not easily be able, in the isolated single judgment, to lay quality and matter analytically apart — as by the way of *comparison*: with a view to the corresponding *identities* in which we set qualitatively different acts beside one another and now find in each act, as a common moment, the *identical matter*, somewhat as in the sensory domain the like intensity or color. The question *is only* this: *what* this identical is, and *how* it relates to the *quality-moment*. Whether it is a matter of two *disjoint*, though *abstract*, components of acts — as, say, color and shape in sensory intuition — or whether they stand in *another* relation, that of *genus* and *difference*. This question is the more important since the matter is supposed to be that in the act which confers on it the *determinate objective relation*. To gain the fullest possible clarity about the essence of this relation is — recalling that all thinking accomplishes itself *in acts* — of *fundamental* epistemological interest.

### **§23. The conception of matter as a founding act of "mere presenting"**

The first paragraph recalls Brentano's proposition and unfolds its sense. The nearest answer is given by the well-known proposition Brentano co-used to determine his "psychic phenomena": that *every intentional experience either is a presentation or rests on presentations as its basis*. More exactly, the sense of this remarkable proposition is that in every act the intentional object is an object *presented in an act of presenting*, and that, where it is not from the outset a matter of a "*mere*" presenting, a presenting is always so peculiarly and intimately *interwoven* with one or several further acts (or rather act-characters) that thereby the presented object stands at the same time as *judged, wished, hoped for*, and the like. This *manifoldness* of intentional relation accomplishes itself *not* in a connected *side-by-side and successively* of acts — whereby the object would be intentionally present anew, repeatedly, with each act — but in a *strictly unified act*, to which an object appears *a single time*, but in this single being-present is target-point of a *complex* intention. In other words: an intentional experience gains its relation to an objective at all only through this, that in it an *act-experience of presenting* is present which makes the *object present* to it. For consciousness the object would be *nothing* if it accomplished no presenting that made it precisely *object*, and so made it possible for it to become object of a feeling, desiring, and the like.

The second paragraph establishes the *non-self-sufficiency* of the founded characters. These new intentional characters are evidently *not* to be grasped as *full and self-sufficient acts*. They are not thinkable without the objectivating presentation-act, hence *founded* in it. A *desired* object (or state of affairs) that were not, in and with the desiring, at the same time *presented*, not only does not actually occur but is *simply unthinkable*. And so in every case. This is a state of affairs raising claim to *apriority*; the general proposition asserting it is an *essential law* evident with *evidence*. Accordingly, we are *not* to regard the accession of the *desiring* to the founding presentation as the accession of something that could, as what it here is, also *be for itself*, and above all could be already, for itself, *intention* upon an objective; rather we must regard it as the accession of a *non-self-sufficient* factor, which is intentional insofar as it really has relation to an objective and would a priori not be thinkable without such relation, but can *unfold* or *gain* this relation only through *intimate interweaving* with a *presentation*. This *latter*, however, is *more* than a mere act-quality; in contrast to the desiring-quality founded by it, it can very well *be for itself* as a "mere" presentation — i.e., subsist for itself as a concrete intentional experience.

The third paragraph specifies what counts as *mere presentations* (in Brentano's sense). As corroborating examples for the *mere presentations* (in Brentano's sense, we may assume) are to count: all cases of mere *imagining*\-presentation, in which the appearing object is *posited* neither as existent nor as non-existent and regarding which all other acts are *absent*; or also the cases in which we receive an *expression*, say an assertion-sentence, *understandingly*, without deciding ourselves in *belief* or disbelief. Especially in this contrast to the character of *belief* (belief), whose accession is supposed to *complete* the judgment, the concept of mere presentation is clarified — and it is known what important role precisely this contrast plays in recent judgment-theory.

The fourth paragraph applies the proposition to the matter/quality relation. Returning to our proposition, it lies very near (as touched at the outset) to apply the state of affairs expressed in it to the *interpretation* of the relation of *matter* and *quality*, and accordingly to determine it thus: the *identity of matter* amid changing quality *rests on the "essential" identity of the underlying presentation*. Otherwise put: where acts have the *same* "content" and differ, according to their intentional essence, only in that the one is a *judgment*, the other a *wish*, the third a *doubt* of precisely this content, there they possess "essentially" the *same presentation* as basis. If the presentation underlies a judgment, it is (in the present sense of *matter*) *judgment-content*; if it underlies a desiring, *desiring-content*; and so on.

The fifth paragraph corrects "the same presentation" to "the same matter." It was just said *essentially* the same presentation. It should not be said that matter and underlying presentation are really *one and the same*, since matter is a mere *abstract moment* of an act. Rather, the talk of an essentially same presentation concerns, by the earlier considerations, presentations of *one and the same matter*, which can naturally still be phenomenologically differentiated by moments irrelevant to the matter. Since the *quality* too is the same, all these presentations have the *same "intentional essence."*

The sixth paragraph draws the striking result: the presentation's essence is *bare matter*. The following state of affairs results. While every *other* intentional essence is a *complexion* of quality and matter, the intentional essence of the *presentation* is *bare matter* — or *bare quality*, as one wishes to call it. Otherwise put: only the circumstance that the intentional essences of all *other* acts are *complex* — and indeed so that they necessarily comprise a *presentation-essence* as one of their components — would now ground the talk of the difference between quality and matter; whereby under the latter title precisely this necessarily founding *presentation-essence* would be understood. Just for this reason, in *simple* acts, which *eo ipso* would be mere presentations, the whole difference would *fall away*. One would therefore also have to say: the difference between quality and matter designates no difference of *fundamentally different genera* of abstract moments of acts. Considered in and for themselves, the *matters themselves* would be nothing other than "*qualities*," namely *presentation-qualities*. What we designated as the intentional essence of the acts would be precisely the *whole qualitative* in them; this would in fact be the *essential* in them over against the contingently changing.

The seventh paragraph spells out the resulting picture of simple and composite acts. The state of affairs would then express itself thus. If an act is *simple*, hence mere presentation, its *quality* coincides with what we called intentional essence. If it is *composite* — and to this would belong every act different from a mere presentation, and besides them the composite *presentations* — then the *complex intentional essence* is nothing other than a *complex* of unitarily connected qualities grounding a unified total-quality; yet so that *every* primitive or complex quality in the formation that is not itself a *presentation-quality* must in turn be *founded* by a *presentation-quality*, which in this function would be the corresponding "*matter*," and in relation to the complex total act the *total-matter*.

### **§24. Difficulties. The problem of the differentiation of the quality-genera**

The first paragraph questions whether the foregoing conception is forced. As illuminating as this whole conception appears, and on however indubitable an *evidence* it rests, it is by no means of a kind that would *exclude other possibilities*. Certainly the indicated evidence (that of Brentano's proposition) *exists*, but the question is whether one does not *read into* it what does not lie in it at all. Striking at all events is the peculiar *privileging* of the *presentations* as the *single genus* of intentional experiences whose intentional essence (or, what would now say the same, whose intentional *quality*) could be a really *simple* one; and connected with this is the *difficulty* how the *last specific differentiation* of the various *genera* of *intentional essences* (briefly: *intentions*) is to be understood.

The second paragraph poses the differentiation problem for judgment. For example, when we judge, the full *judgment-intention* — the moment in the act of asserting corresponding to the *meaning* of the assertion-sentence — is supposed to be *complex*, built up out of a *presentation-intention* making the state of affairs present, and out of a *supplementary intention*, as the proper judgment-character, whereby the state of affairs stands *in the manner of the existent*. How does it stand, we ask, with the *last specific difference* of such *acceding* intentions? The highest genus *intention* specifies — indifferently whether immediately or mediately — into the species *judgment-intention*, which we must of course take *purely for itself*, in abstraction from the allegedly founding presentation-intention. Is *this species* already the *last specific difference*?

The third paragraph brings in a clear example of genuine differentiation: color → red → red-nuance. To keep clear concepts, let us draw a *secure* example of genuine *differentiation of a generic essence* into comparison. In the proper sense, the genus *quality* specifies into the species *color*, this again into the *red* lying under it, and indeed into the *determinate red-nuance*; this is the *last specific difference* — it admits no genuine differentiation lying *within* this genus. What is alone possible here is *interweaving* with other determinacies belonging to *other genera*, which are themselves again *last differences* with respect to their genera. This interweaving is still content-determining, but no longer *differentiating* in the genuine sense. So "the same" red can take on a *spread* (Ausbreitung) of this or that *shape*. The red-moment *changes*, but not *as quality*; it changes according to the moment of the new genus *spread* essentially belonging to it. It is grounded in the essence of color in general that it cannot be *without* spread.

The fourth paragraph asks whether *judgment-intention* is a last difference, and exposes the consequence for *presentations*. Returning to our case: how does it stand with the judgment-character acceding, in the concrete judgment, to the founding presentation? Is it *something wholly alike* in all judgments — is the species *judgment-intention* (ideally grasped, the *simple* species, not complicated with presentation) actually already a *lowest specific difference*? (A footnote sets aside the disputed sub-species "affirmative" and "negative" judgment.) We shall not be able to hesitate to assume this. But if we assume it and try, *consistently*, to assume it for *all kinds of intention*, we strike serious difficulties with the *presentations*. For if even within the species *presentation* no further differentiation is present, then the difference between *this* and *that* presentation *in specie* — e.g., the difference between the presentation *Kaiser* and the presentation *Pope* — does *not* concern the presenting *intention* as such. What, then, is the *differentiating* element of these presentations — or better, of these *intentional essences*, these *presentation-meanings*?

The fifth paragraph draws out the dilemma. They would then have to be *complexions*, formed out of the character (quality) "presentation" and a *second character of a quite other genus*; and since within the former all distinctness in objective relation would evidently have been lost, it would be this *second* character that introduced them into the *full meaning*. In other words: the intentional essence belonging to the presentation (in the examples: the *meaning*) could *not* be the *last specific difference* of presentation-intention in general; rather, to the last-differentiated presentation-intention a wholly *new* determinacy of *quite another genus* would have to accede. Every *presentation-meaning* would be a *complexion* of "presentation-intention" and "content" as two *interwoven ideal unities of different genus*. Recurring to our old names, we would have to say: if we regard it (as above) as self-evident that all kinds of intentions differentiate *in like manner*, then we must again resolve to *posit* an *essential difference* of *act-quality* and *matter*. The view according to which the matter (in our earlier determination) would be identical with the intentional essence of an underlying presentation, and this itself again with a mere *presentation-quality*, could *not* be maintained. The two options — bare matter vs. essential quality/matter difference — thus stand opposed, and the section ends by exhibiting the unresolved tension.

### **§25. Closer analysis of the two possibilities of solution**

The first paragraph anticipates the impatient objection and rebuts it. Many will here ask in astonishment why so much circumstantiality is needed, except to remove difficulties we have laid in our own path. Everything is supposedly quite simple: every presentation-act has of course the general act-character of the species *presentation*, and this admits no further genuine differentiation; what distinguishes presentation from presentation is naturally the "*content*" — the presentation *Pope* presents the Pope, the presentation *Kaiser* the Kaiser.

The second paragraph dismisses this as the complacency of those who have not made the relevant distinctions. With such "self-evidences" only one may content himself who has *never* made clear to himself the phenomenological differences here prevailing (on the side of the ideal unities, the specific ones), and above all has *never* performed the fundamental *separation* between *content as object* and *content as matter* (apprehension-sense, or meaning); and likewise one who, precisely at this point where so much depends on it, does not let the truth become operative that the *object*, in the proper sense, is "in" the presentation *nothing*.

The third paragraph re-derives the need for the analysis and frames the two options. The circumstantiality is therefore much needed. *Objects that are nothing in the presentation can also effect no difference between presentation and presentation* — hence in particular not the difference, so well-familiar to us from the own content of the respective presentations, regarding *what* they present. Taking this *what* as the "content" — to be distinguished from the intended object and dwelling in the presentation itself — the question is precisely *as what* we should understand it. We see here no other possibilities than the two already indicated, to be set out once more in the sharpest possible form. *Either* we assume that what makes up the changing intentional essence — and thereby the changing objective relation — in the *real* content of the presentation *is the presentation-quality itself*, which differentiates now thus, now otherwise.

The fourth paragraph spells out the *first* possibility (red/blue analogy). The presentations *Pope* and *Kaiser* (not Pope and Kaiser themselves) differ in *exactly analogous* manner as the colors *red* and *blue* differ (both thought as determinate differences, as "nuances"). The *general* is *presentation*; the *particular* is the last-differentiated presentation, fully determinate according to its meaning-essence. So in the comparison-case the general is *color*, the particular this and that determinate color, this nuance of red, that nuance of blue. That a presentation relates to a certain object and in a certain way it owes *not* to any *activity* upon the object existing outside it, in and for itself — as if it "directed" itself at it in any serious sense, or otherwise busied itself with it or at it, like the writing hand with the pen; it owes this not to anything remaining as it were *external* to it, but exclusively to its *own* particularity. This holds for *every* apprehension; the present view determines it thus: the respectively given presentation is, merely by virtue of its so-and-so *differentiated presentation-quality*, precisely a presentation that presents *this object in this way*.

The fifth paragraph spells out the *second* possibility (color/extension analogy). *Or* we assume — as the second possibility offered here — that the full intentional (or, in the examples, the full meaning-) essence, which the talk of the (ideally-one) presentation "Pope," or of the meaning of the word *Pope*, undergoes by *ideating abstraction*, is something *essentially complex*, decomposable into *two abstract moments*: the one the *presentation-quality*, the act-character of presenting taken purely for itself and everywhere alike; the other the "*content*" (the *matter*), which does *not* belong to the inner essence of that character as its *difference*, but precisely *accedes* to it and *completes* the full meaning. Now the one relates to the other as, in the comparison-case, the determinate *color* relates to *extension*. Every color is color of a certain extension; so every presentation is presentation of a certain content. On both sides the connection is *not* contingent but *necessary*, and indeed *a priori*.

The sixth paragraph clarifies the *complexion-form* (attributive parts) of the second possibility. The comparison also indicates how we wish — and on the present standpoint must — have the *kind* of complexion grasped. It is a *complexion-form* for which a quite suitable name is still lacking. Brentano and some researchers close to him speak here of the connection of *metaphysical parts*; Stumpf prefers the name *attributive parts*. The connections of inner *properties* into the unity of the phenomenal outer things give the typical examples on whose basis the idea of this complexion-form is to be conceived. Accordingly, it is to be well noted that the *supplementing character* — which, as the determining content, accedes to the pure character of the presenting *quality* (separable from the content only by abstraction) — must really be regarded as belonging to a *new genus*. For as soon as one wished to grasp *it* in turn as a *qualitative* character, the difficulties would *pile up anew* whose removal we are now laboring at, and only the *names* would have changed.

The seventh paragraph states the consequence of adopting the second possibility. Were we, then, to resolve to *separate* the "content" or "matter" *from the genus act-quality*, we should have to say: the qualitative character which in and for itself makes the presenting a presenting (and consequently the judging a judging, the desiring a desiring) has in its inner essence *no relation to an object*. But grounded in this essence is an *ideal-lawful* relation, namely that such a character cannot *be* without supplementing "*matter*," with which the relation to the object first comes into the full intentional essence and so into the concrete intentional experience itself. This transfers *eo ipso* to the *meaning-essence* of the expressive experiences — hence to that on whose account we speak, e.g., of the *same judgment* uttered by different persons. This *meaningful* element — ideally, the *meaning* — is, in the concrete judgment-experience, the act-character of *judging positing* (the abstract judgment-quality) in "attributive" *interweaving* with the "content" (the judgment-matter), whereby the relation to the "object" (the state of affairs) completes itself. And this judging positing is, one will then have to say, *a priori* not thinkable without a content at all, as little as a color without extension.

### **§26. Weighing and rejection of the proposed conception**

The first paragraph poses the decision. How are we to make our own decision between these contending possibilities, weighed with equal care?

The second paragraph exhibits the cost of the *first* possibility: presentation as anomaly. Adopting the *first* possibility, the *presentation* stands as an *offensive exception* in the series of intentional experiences. For while within the *essence-genus intentional quality* — which comprises as *coordinate species* the qualities *presentation, judgment, wish, will* — the species *presentation* still *differentiates* (into all the differences we call presentations of this or that "content," this or that matter), the *judgment-qualities, wish-qualities, will-qualities* are *last differences*; differences of content with them are only differences of the *presentation-qualities* complicating with, or "underlying," the respective quality. Nor can the matter be grasped otherwise. For it is *not* possible to produce the *uniformity* by also conceiving the distinguishing contents of the various judgments, and likewise of the various wishes, willings, as *differences of the quality-species judgment, wish, will*. For *different species cannot have the same last differences*. Is the drawback not replaced by a *new* one if we are now to assume *different species of the same level*, of which some still have last differences *under* them, but all others are themselves *already* last differences?

The third paragraph turns to the *second* possibility and its pressure to abandon Brentano's proposition. Befriending ourselves accordingly with the *second* possibility, it presses us — so it seems — at once to *further changes* of our conception. For have we still serious ground to hold at all to the proposition that every intentional experience either *is* a "mere" presentation or *implies* presentations as its necessary "basis"? Such a *privileging* of the presentations — as acts — such a *complication* of all acts that are not themselves presentations, looks almost like a *purposeless* assumption. If, in the sense of the now-decisive conviction, the "contents" (grasped as experiences of a *proper genus*) are united with the act-character of presenting only by *complication* (even the most intimate, that of positive inner properties), and if this manner of complication proves *here* capable of bringing about what we call *act of this content*, why should the matter stand *otherwise* with the *other* kinds of acts — or at least *have* to stand otherwise? The said complexion-form of *presentation-quality* and "content" conditions on the one side the whole: *presentation of this content*. Why should not, with other acts (e.g., judgment), the *same* complexion-form, in relation to judgment-quality and content, bring about the whole: *judgment of this content*?

The fourth paragraph concedes mediation in some cases but denies its universality. It may be that, through the *peculiarity* of some act-kinds, a *mediation* is lawfully demanded; it may occur that some act-qualities can appear only in *complexion*, such that other act-qualities (referred to the same matter — e.g., a presenting of this matter) necessarily underlie them in the act-whole, so that their attachment to the matter must be a *mediate* one. But that this *must* be so always and everywhere — above all that the act-kind of "*mere presenting*" plays so significant a role, and that now *every* act not itself a mere presenting could gain its matter *only through the medium* of such a presenting — this appears *not* self-evident and from the outset *not* even probable. The proposition is thus put in question, preparing the appeal to direct intuition in the next section.

### **§27. The testimony of direct intuition. Perceptual presentation and perception**

The first paragraph reverses the order of presentation, appealing to *intuition* but cautioning about its testimony. We close these arguments with what, in investigating descriptive disputes of this kind, must be the *first* thing: the "testimony of inner perception" — or, as Husserl prefers for good reasons to say, the testimony of *immediate intuitive essence-analysis* of intentional experiences. This *reversal* in presentation is admissible and under circumstances necessary. To the evidence of *rightly understood immanent essence-intuition* (falsely called "inner perception" in such cases) Husserl will accord all the honors due it in epistemological respect. But this does not prevent its testimony, once *invoked* — brought into conceptual form and asserted — from forfeiting much in force and so admitting *justified doubts*. With appeal to the *same* "inner perception," some arrive at *this*, others at the *opposite* view; some read *this*, others *that* into or out of it. So too in our case. Precisely the analyses carried out put us in a position to recognize this and to distinguish and assess, one by one, the *deceptions* arising from the *interpretation* of the givennesses of phenomenological essence-intuition. The same holds of the evidence of the *general propositions* growing on the basis of inner *intuition* of single cases — this evidence considered in contrast to the *interpreting* insertions.

The second paragraph (a long second-edition bracketed addendum) clarifies that phenomenological evidence rests on *essence-intuition*, not inner experience. Husserl explains why it is *falsely* called "inner perception" in the usual appeals to "the evidence of inner perception." On closer view, all such appeals serve the establishment of states of affairs that are either themselves *essence-relations* of the purely phenomenological sphere or mere *transferences* of such onto the sphere of psychological reality. Establishments of phenomenological states of affairs can *never* have their cognition-grounds in *psychological experience*, and specifically not in *inner perception* in the natural word-sense, but only in *ideating phenomenological essence-intuition*. This takes its *exemplary* point of departure from inner intuition; but, on the one hand, this inner intuition need *not* be actual inner perception or other inner experience (memory) — any inner, freely *fictionalizing* phantasy can serve it just as well, provided it has sufficient intuitive clarity, and indeed it serves *preferentially*. On the other hand, phenomenological intuition switches off from the outset every psychological and natural-scientific *apperception* and *real existence-positing* — all positings of psychophysical nature with real things, bodies, human beings, the own empirical ego-subject included, and in general everything transcendent to pure consciousness. This switching-off accomplishes itself *eo ipso* in that phenomenological essence-intuition, as *immanent ideation* on the ground of inner intuitions, orients the ideating gaze exclusively toward the *own real or intentional subsistence* of the intuited experiences and brings to *adequate* intuition the specific experience-essences individuating themselves in these singular experiences, and the (hence "a priori," "ideal") essence-relations belonging to them. It is of the greatest importance to bring this to perfect clarity and to convince oneself that it is mere *semblance* when, in epistemological discussions (and likewise in psychological ones of the kind that appeal to apodictic evidence in setting up general propositions about consciousness-data), one believes the *source* of the evidences lies in *inner experience*, specifically in inner perception — hence in *existence-positing* acts. This cardinal misinterpretation conditions that variety of *psychologism* which believes it can satisfy the demands of a pure logic, ethics, and epistemology, and overcome extreme empiricism, by speaking of apodictic evidence and even a priori insights without really leaving the ground of inner experience and psychology. In principle one thereby does *not* get beyond *Hume*, who recognizes the a priori in the form of *relations of ideas* but so little separates inner experience and ideation in principle that he nominalistically reinterprets the latter into *chance facts*.

The third paragraph turns to the case, granting the *evidence* of the founding role but querying the word "presentation." Going now into detail, it is *evident* that every intentional experience has a "presentation" as its basis: evident that we cannot *judge* without the state of affairs we judge about being present to us; and so with asking, doubting, surmising, desiring. But does "presentation" here mean the *same* as what we designate as presentation *outside* such connections? Could it not be that we succumb to the *enticements* of equivocation — especially when we let that evidence *grow into* the law that every act-experience is either a "mere presentation" or has "presentations" as its basis? What can make us suspicious from the outset is the circumstance that, if we really hold strictly descriptively to the experiences, an *analysis* of the acts that are not "mere presentations" into the part-acts allegedly *building them up* by no means everywhere *succeeds*.

The fourth paragraph contrasts a clear case of complexion (joy on perception) with a doubtful one. Let us set, beside one of the doubtful cases, a case of *genuine complexion* in the intentional manner of relation, with full identity of matter. I cannot rejoice over something without that over which I rejoice standing over against me in *some mode of being* — in the manner of *perception*, *memory*, possibly also of *judging* in the sense of asserting. Here the complexion is *quite unmistakable*. As I, e.g., rejoice perceivingly, the act-character of *joy* is grounded in the *perception*; this has its own act-character and, through its matter, at the same time produces the matter for the joy. The character of joy can fall away entirely, but the *perception* remains, in itself unchanged. It is thus indubitably a *component* in the concretely complete experience of joy.

The fifth paragraph poses the doubtful case: perception itself. *Perception* offers us at once an example of *doubtful* act-complexion. We distinguish here, as with all acts, *quality* and *matter*. The comparison with a corresponding *mere presentation* (say a mere phantasy) shows how the *same* object can be re-presented as the same (in the same "apprehension-sense") and yet in a quite other "*manner*." In perception the object seemed to be "*bodily*" present, in its own person, so to speak; in the phantasy-presentation it "merely hovers before" us, it is "*re-presented*" but not *bodily* present. However, this is *not* the difference here in question; it is a difference through moments concerning neither matter nor quality — just as is the difference between *perception* and *memory* of one and the same object presented in the same apprehension-sense. Comparing perception with a corresponding "mere" presentation *in abstraction* from such differences: by our conception an abstract common element, the *matter*, is given on both sides in *different* manner, in *different act-quality*. By the other (to us doubtful) conception, the matter underlying the perceiving is supposed itself to be again an *act-quality*, namely that of a *founding* act of mere presenting. Is anything of this to be found in the analysis? Can perception thereby be regarded as an *act-complexion*, and a *mere presentation* really be detached from it as a self-sufficient act?

The sixth paragraph entertains the *illusion* strategy. Perhaps one points here to the possibility of an exactly corresponding *illusion*, and means that this — after its deception is unmasked — is to be grasped as the *isolated mere presentation* which was woven into the perception and supplied its matter. The illusion, so long as not yet recognized as deception, was *simply perception*. Afterward, however, the *perception-character*, the act-quality of *belief*, fell away, and the *mere perceptual presentation* remained over. The same complexion is, further, to be assumed with *all* perceptions: everywhere the underlying *perceptual presentation* (whose *quality* makes up the matter of the perception) is supplemented by the *belief*\-character.

The seventh paragraph gives the *Panopticon* example. For closer consideration take a concrete example. Strolling in the *Panopticon* (wax museum), we encounter on the stairs an amiably waving, unknown *lady* — the well-known Panopticon joke. It is a *doll* that deceived us a moment. So long as we are caught in the deception, we have a *perception* as good as any other. We see a *lady*, not a doll. Once we have recognized the deception, the reverse holds: now we see a *doll* that *presents* a lady. This talk of presenting does *not* mean that the doll functions *as image* of a lady (the way wax figures "of" Napoleon, "of" Bismarck function as *depictions* in the same Panopticon). The perception of the doll-thing is thus *not* substrate of a *depicting* consciousness; rather, in one with the doll, the *lady* at the same time merely *appears*: two perceptive apprehensions, two thing-appearances, *interpenetrate*, coinciding (so to speak) according to a certain appearance-content. And they interpenetrate in the manner of *conflict* (Widerstreit), whereby the attending glance can turn now to the one, now to the other of the appearing but mutually being-cancelling objects.

The eighth paragraph rebuts the detachment-claim via the conflict-analysis. One might say: even if the original perceptual presentation here does not attain wholly *detached* existence, but appears in connection with a new one (the doll-perception), it still does not serve *as founding* for a *proper perception*: perceived is only the *doll*; it alone stands in "*belief*" as actually there. So the *detachment* has succeeded in a way fully sufficient for the present purpose. — But this detachment would suffice only if we had a *right* to speak of detachment here at all; in other words, if the *presentation of the lady* in the second case could really be assumed as *contained* in the *perception* of the same lady in the original case. But "presentation" there means (after the unmasking of the deception) as much as *perceptive consciousness "cancelled" in conflict*. This, *so qualified* as it there appears, naturally does *not* stick in the original perception.

The ninth paragraph isolates the genuine identity (matter) from the genuine difference (quality). Certainly both have a *common* element; they are, in our example (which in this respect could not be more favorably chosen), as *alike* as is possible at all between perception and corresponding presentation. Certainly both have (which so far-reaching likeness would by no means require) the *same matter*. It is the *same lady* who appears on both sides, and does so with *identically the same* phenomenal determinacies. But on the one side she stands before us *as reality*, on the other *as fiction* — bodily appearing and yet as something *null*. The difference lies on both sides in the *qualities*. It is, to be sure, "almost" as if she herself were there, a true and real person. The unusual *likeness* as regards matter and the other *extra-qualitative* constituents of the acts in fact arouses the *inclination* to *lapse* from the imageness-consciousness into the *perception*\-consciousness. Only the living *conflict* that this perception-tendency (belief-tendency), in its direction upon the waving lady, experiences from the side of the perception of the *doll* (the thing of wax) — which partially coincides with it but excludes it according to the other moments — and specifically from its *belief-quality*, hinders us from really yielding to this inclination.

The tenth paragraph draws the conclusion against the complexion-theory of perception. In all this the *difference* is of a kind that excludes the thought that this *presentation* could be *contained* in the perception. The *same matter* is once matter of a *perception* and another time matter of a mere *perceptive fiction*. Both at once can evidently *not* be united. A perception can *never* be at the same time *fiction* of the perceived; a fiction never *perception* of the fictioned. Accordingly, the descriptive analysis by no means seems to favor the view, which appears to many almost self-evident, that *every perception is a complexion* in which a *moment of belief* (making up the qualitative of perceiving) builds itself upon a *full* — hence own-quality-endowed — *act* of "perceptual presentation."

### **§28. Special investigation of the situation in the case of judgment**

The first paragraph transfers the doubt to judgment and fixes the sense of "judgment." A similar state of affairs we find with a class of acts that especially interests the *logician*: the *judgments*. We take this word here in the *predominant* meaning oriented to *assertions* (predications), and accordingly excluding *perceptions, memories*, and similar acts (despite the not inessential descriptive kinship). In the judgment a *state of affairs* (Sachverhalt) "appears" to us — or, more clearly, is intentionally objective to us. A state of affairs, even when it concerns a sensibly perceived, is *not* an object that could appear to us *sensibly* in the manner of a perceived (whether in "outer" or "inner sensibility"). In perception an object *gives itself* as *bodily existent*. We *call* it a presently *existent* one insofar as, on the basis of this perception, we pass the *judgment* that it *is*. In this judgment — which can persist as essentially the same even when the perception falls away — the "appearing," the intentionally conscious, is *not* the existent sensible *object*, but the *fact that it is*.

The second paragraph elaborates the forms of judging-meaning and isolates the state of affairs as its objective correlate. In the judgment it further seems to us that something is so-and-so constituted; and in general this *seeming* — to be understood not as doubting surmise but (in the normal sense of "judgment") as firm *meaning*, certainty, being-convinced — accomplishes itself, in content, in various *forms*: it is a meaning that *S is* or *is not*; that *S is p* or is not *p*; that either *S is p* or *Q is r*; and so on. The *objective* of the judging meaning we call the *judged state of affairs*; we distinguish it, in reflecting cognition, from the *judging* itself — the act in which this or that seems to us to be so or otherwise — exactly as in perception we distinguish the perceived object from the perceiving as act. Corresponding to this analogy, the dispute is now to be weighed here too: *whether* what makes up the *matter* in the act of judgment — that which *determines* the judgment to be judgment of *this* state of affairs — *lies in a founding act of presenting*. By virtue of this presentation the state of affairs would first be *presented*, and upon this presented the *judging positing* would relate, as the *new act*, or more exactly, as the *new act-quality built upon it*.

The third paragraph grants the *corresponding presentation* but questions its containment. That to every judgment (a priori, in essence-generality) there *corresponds* a presentation which has the *matter* in common with it — and so presents exactly the same in exactly corresponding manner as the judgment judges it — no one will doubt. Thus, e.g., to the judgment "the earth-mass is approximately 1/300000 of the sun-mass" there corresponds, as the "mere" presentation belonging to it, the act accomplished by someone who *hears* and *understands* this utterance but finds no motive to decide *judgingly*. We now ask: is this *same* act a *component* also of the *judgment*, and does the judgment differ merely by the *judging decision* that *accedes* as a *plus* to a mere presenting? For his part, Husserl *strives in vain* to find such confirmed in descriptive analysis. The *duality* in act-quality here demanded he misses entirely.

The fourth paragraph warns against arguing from the *word* "mere" and distinguishes lack from supplementable lack. Naturally one may not, in the supposition of analyzing, rather draw *arguments* from the *talk* of "mere presentation." The *mere* (the bareness) points, here as everywhere, to a *lack*; but not always is a lack to be remedied by a *supplement*. So we oppose to *perception* the "mere" *imagination*. The distinguishing element lies in a *privilege* on the side of perception, but *not* in a *plus*. Likewise, with the talk of mere presenting in contrast to judging, to the *lack* of the former there corresponds a *privilege* of the latter — namely the privilege of *judgmental decidedness* regarding the merely presented state of affairs.

### **§29. Continuation. "Recognition" or "assent" to the mere presentation of the state of affairs**

The first paragraph reports the opponents' new example: assent acceding to a hovering presentation. Perhaps others find that the complexion we *miss* comes clearly to light in certain cases. They recall the known experiences where, without our being at once judgmentally decided, the *mere presentation* hovers in us, to which only *subsequently* the *assent* (recognition, or rejection, repudiation) *accedes* as an evidently *new* act.

The second paragraph accepts the evidence but offers a different interpretation. We shall of course not doubt this evidence; but we may well undertake to *interpret* it and the whole situation *otherwise*. Certainly a *new* act attaches to the "mere presentation," follows it and then asserts itself in consciousness. But the question is *whether* the new act *really* wholly *contains* the old in itself — and more closely, whether the new simply *grows* out of the old in such a way that to it, as the mere presentation, the *specific judgment-quality*, the character of *belief*, *adjoins itself* and thereby completes the concrete judgment-experience (somewhat as to a perceptual act the act-quality of *joy* adjoins itself and so completes the concrete act of joy).

The third paragraph offers the alternative reading: quality-replacement, not addition. No doubt, in this emergence of the new act out of the old, an *identical* — and enclosed in it, what we called the *matter* — is *preserved*. But this identical need *not* be a *full* act of presenting, with the sole change the accession of a new quality *founded* by it. The process could also be interpreted thus: in the original act of mere presenting, the specific character of *presenting* is *replaced* (abgelöst) by the *judgment-character*, while the *identical* (with the matter enclosed in it) could subsist in an *abstract moment* that, for itself, makes up no full *act*.

The fourth paragraph deepens the description with the *example of assenting to another's judgment*, introducing question and deliberation. But we must be more exact. Only a *part* of the situation is described by the thought just tried; what is especially lacking is precisely what grounds the talk of *assent*. For a more careful description we lay at the basis an example where *assent* is spoken of by preference: we *assent* to a judgment another utters. His speech does not immediately awaken the *like-sounding* judgment on our side: to accomplish a like-sounding judgment, simply to *take over* a communication, is *not* to assent. Rather, assent requires that we first *understand* the assertion *without* ourselves judging — that the asserted is conscious to us as "*merely put forward*" (dahingestellt) — and that we now *weigh* or *deliberate* it. For evidently it is all these acts that are here in question in the *mere presenting* on which the assent builds itself. We *immerse* ourselves musingly in what the other means; what is at first merely put forward to us shall *not remain* put forward; we *put it in question*, we *intend a decision*. And then the *decision*, the recognizing *agreement* itself, *sets in*; we now judge ourselves and like-soundingly with the other.

The fifth paragraph shows the *matter*\-identity running through these acts, but notes something is still missing. In this judgment there certainly does *not* stick the prior "mere presentation," that act-series of musing putting-forth and putting-in-question. Rather, a *judgment* is given which is "like-sounding" on the one hand with the *judgment of the speaker* and on the other with the *musing question* — i.e., of the *same matter*; and so the assent accomplishes itself. I assent to the judgment, namely I judge exactly so, on the basis of the same matter. I assent to the question, namely I hold true exactly what was held questionable in the question; the act accomplishes itself again on the basis of the same matter. But on closer view the analysis is *still incomplete* — indeed the *specific* element of assent is still lacking. The *succession* of question and like-sounding judgment, or also of judgment and like-sounding judgment, does *not yet* make up the *whole*: *assenting* judgment to the question or to the judgment.

The sixth paragraph identifies the *transition-experience* of *fulfillment* as the missing element. Evidently a certain *transition-experience* mediates — rather, *connects* — the two distinguished members. The deliberating and questioning "intention" finds in the like-sounding *decision* its *fulfillment*, and in this fulfillment-unity of the *answering* (which has the phenomenological character of a *unity-moment*) the two acts are *not* a mere succession but *intimately, unitarily* referred to one another: the *answer fits* the *question*, the decision says: *So it is*, exactly as it stood before the eyes in the deliberating consideration.

The seventh paragraph treats *vacillating* deliberation and *negative* fulfillment. Where the deliberation is one *swaying up and down*, quite corresponding to the image of the *scales* — where question turns into counter-question and this again into that ("is it so or not?") — there the intention is *twofold*, and the whole deliberation-experience finds its *fulfillment* through *each* of the two possible decisions: it is so — it is not so. The fulfilling answer then concerns specially the half of the deliberating question corresponding to it. In the simpler case, by contrast, the decision *with opposed matter* has the character of *negative* fulfillment, of *disappointment*, so to speak. This transfers of itself to manifold (not merely yes/no) disjunctions: the negative fulfillment then lies in the decision *neither A nor B nor C*.

The eighth paragraph locates the source of "assenting judgment" in this tension-resolving fulfillment, with the communicative motive. Evidently in this fulfillment-experience referred to the deliberating question — this resolution of a kind of *tension* — lies also the *original source* for the talk of *assenting* judgment, assenting in relation to *another* judgment uttered by some speaker. The asserter, if he cannot reckon outright on a like-sounding judgment, represents the hearer as a *deliberator* and *wishes to obtain* his *assent*; he then grasps the *agreement* as *assent* even where the same judgment has set in *without* deliberation — especially since its *value*, when it has passed through deliberation, is more highly esteemed. The hearer in turn likes to present himself, even when he took no occasion for deliberation, as a *deliberator* and then *assenter*, in order to grant the other the *joy* of the won assent. So to simple agreement the *thought* of assent is often *imputed*, while *real* assent constitutes itself in the *complex experience* in which a perceived or presented judgment leads to a *putting-in-question*, which for its part finds its *fulfillment* (and in the opposed case its *disappointment, rejection*) in the corresponding actual judgment.

The ninth paragraph classes assent with *fulfillment* of wish, expectation, hope. After these considerations we must regard *assent* as a *transition-experience of quite similar kind* to the *fulfillment* of a *surmise*, an *expectation*, a *hope*, a *wish*, and the like "*aiming*" intentions. With *wish-fulfillment*, e.g., we have not the *mere succession* of wish-intention and the entering of the wished-for, but *unity* in the characteristic fulfillment-consciousness. Here too we find the *agreement* as regards *matter*; but agreement *alone* cannot do it, else it would bring *any* two acts of the *same matter* to fulfillment-unity. Only the *fulfillment-consciousness* coordinates (in essence-lawfully restricting manner) the *wish* that *S be p* and the *judgmental experiencing* that *it is p*, and gives the latter the relative character of the *fulfilling*, as it gives the wish itself the character of the (in the pregnant sense) *intending, aiming* act.

The tenth paragraph draws the moral against a "judgment-theory" of assent. This analysis makes it fully clear — and Husserl notes it for later investigations — that a "*judgment-theory*," or more aptly a purely phenomenological characteristic of judgment, that *identifies* the peculiar *quality of judging* with the *assenting* or *recognizing* (or with the *rejecting, repudiating*) of a *presented state of affairs* (or even of a presented *object*), is on a *wrong path*. *The acceding assent is not an act-quality acceding to a prior act of mere presentation*; what the analysis really finds is first the *mere presentation* (here comprising the interpenetration of acts of *having-put-forward*, putting-in-question, and deliberation), passing over, *by means of the fulfillment-character*, into a *judgment of the same matter*. The judgment is *not* for itself and in itself *recognition* of that first-given mere presentation; rather it is *recognizing, assenting only in such a fulfillment-connection*, only in it does it receive this relative predicate — just as the "presentation" (or deliberation) receives in it only the relative character of the "*aiming intention*" upon this assent. The *analogy* with other kinds of fulfillment (e.g. wish-fulfillment) is here very instructive: the entering of the wished-for fact — or rather the *belief* in this entering (it is not the objective entering but our *knowledge*, being-convinced of it that matters) — does not, taken for itself and in itself, have the character of *wish-fulfillment*, but has it only for the one who *wishes* and experiences his wish *as going into fulfillment*. No one will here describe the fulfillment-experience as a *mere accession* of a new act-quality to the original wish, or even think to interpret the end-goal of the process — the fulfilling conviction — as a complexion enclosing the wish as an underlying part-act.

The eleventh paragraph closes the section. After all this, the experience of *subsequent assent* to a mere presentation can *no longer serve as argument* to prove the (by us doubted) constitution of intentional experiences, at least in the domain of judgment.

The twelfth paragraph is an *Addendum* on the double sense of "question." Husserl has not overlooked that in the deliberation preceding an assent there is mostly also a *wish-intention* interwoven, directed at the judgment-decision. But it would be quite *incorrect* to *identify* the *answering fulfillment* of the so-called *theoretical question* (in which the *appearing-as-questionable* constitutes itself) with the fulfillment of the *wish founded in it* (the *wish-question*). "Question" is a *double-sensed* word: in the one sense a certain *wish* is meant, in the other an *act of peculiar kind* that every such wish *presupposes*. The wish goes upon "judgment-decision," i.e., upon a judgment that *decides* the question (and, where disjunctive, the *doubt*, the "two cases"). In short, the wish *aims* at the *answering* of the "question," which here is thus not itself the wish. Likewise that *doubt* is *no emotive act*: it is no act distinct from the theoretical question (merely occasionally interweaving with it), but precisely the *special case* of *disjunctive* questions, in the present theoretical sense.

### **§30. The conception of identical word- and sentence-understanding as "mere presenting"**

The first paragraph states the *general argument* from identical understanding. It now perhaps lies near to oppose to our doubt the following *general argument*: *the same words and word-formations preserve their identical sense in the most diverse connections and as expression-parts for quite different acts.* There must therefore correspond to them an *everywhere alike experience*, which can only be grasped as an *everywhere underlying presenting*.

The second paragraph elaborates the argument with examples. One person says, judging, *S is P*; another hears the same words and understands them *without himself judging*. The same words function in the same sense, are used and received with the same understanding. The distinguishing element is clear: in the second case the *mere understanding* of the words accomplishes itself, in the first still a *more*. The understanding is the same, but *we judge in addition*. Widening the circle of examples: various persons may *wish, hope, surmise, doubt* this same thing, that *S is p*, in the corresponding expressive acts. They all understand the common words; they all have in common with the *judger* what the latter has in common with the one *merely understanding* the "S is p." Evidently in the latter lies *isolated* what in the former appears still burdened with the character of conviction, wish, hope. The *mere understanding* is here the *mere presenting*, which gives the everywhere-alike *basis* for the series of acts of the same "matter." And the same conception transfers from the expressive acts to the non-expressive.

The third paragraph concedes the argument's force while restating the act-definition. This is certainly a *seductive* argument. Doubtless the talk of the same sense, the same word- and sentence-understanding, points to an *everywhere-alike* element in the various acts here coming to expression — indeed to something with respect to which we ascribe to ourselves, not only in these acts (of conviction, wish, hope) a "taking-position," a subjective *doing*, but which we also believe we appropriate to ourselves in and for itself in an *activity*, an active *accomplishing* of the understanding. However much of this may lead back to peculiar, really phenomenological characters, it is well to note that we did *not* define the concept of act by an *activity*, but wished to use the word simply as *abbreviation* for the expression *intentional experience*. Under the latter we understood *every concrete experience* that "intentionally relates" to an objectivity in the known "manners of consciousness," to be clarified only by examples.

The fourth paragraph reopens the *two possibilities* for the identical understanding. Thus that identical understanding leaves open, for interpretation, again the *two possibilities*. *Either* it is a matter of a *common element* that is *no complete act*, but well that in the relevant act which confers on it the *determinacy of objective relation*; this common element is then given in *various act-qualities*, whereby the full intentional essence of the respective acts completes itself. *Or* the common element consists in a *full intentional essence*, and so there underlies all acts of a coherent group a *proper act of understanding*, which then founds now these, now those further acts (or rather act-qualities); thereby would *grow*, e.g., the judgment (by *enrichment* of the mere presenting with the judgment-quality) or the wish (enrichment with the wish-quality).

The fifth paragraph denies that the "mere understanding" is a genuine *isolation* and gives the phantasy/memory analogy. At all events we can by no means regard it as *secured* that the alleged *isolation* of the founding presentation in the "mere understanding" of the assertion-sentence is *really* an isolation in the sense here claimed. On closer consideration it shows itself rather that this *experience* relates to the *actual judgment* *analogously* as the mere *phantasy-presentation* relates to the *exactly corresponding memory*. They are *different manners* of intentional relation to *one and the same* object conscious in the same "sense," and that says: they are *two acts of the same matter and different quality*. The one of them is *not really nested* in the other, such that it would there experience the mere *increment* of a new qualification.

### **§31. A last objection against our conception. Mere presentations and isolated matters**

The first paragraph states Husserl's positive thesis: the function is borne by *non-self-sufficient* moments. Whoever immerses himself here unprejudicedly in the descriptive relations — who lets himself be misled neither by prejudices nor by equivocations — will come with us to the conviction that the *presentations*, in the sense of the acts isolated as "*mere*" presentations and especially opposed to the *judgments* as specifically peculiar acts, do *not* play so dominant a role in cognition as one is accustomed to assume; and that what one *imputes* to them — namely, to make the intentional objectivity *present* in all acts — is performed by *non-self-sufficient experiences* that *necessarily belong to all acts*, because as *abstract moments* they belong to their *intentional essence*.

The second paragraph restates the opponent's argument once more. The opposing side lets itself be repeatedly seduced, in secret, by the following argument: in order that an intentional character *can* relate to an objective, this must become *present* to us. How can I hold a state of affairs true, wish it, doubt it, if I do not *present* it at all? The *making-present* is precisely the *underlying presentation*.

The third paragraph grants the truth of this but denies it is an objection. There is *objectively* nothing to object to here; what is said is *perfectly true* — only it is *no objection* against our conception. Certainly there dwells in every intentional experience a *component* or *side* that performs the *making-present* of the thing. But a component that is *itself a whole act* — that is precisely the *question*. And above all it is the question with reference to the case especially interesting us, that of *judgment* and the component dwelling in it as *presentation of the judged state of affairs*. It seemed to force itself on us as *irrefutable* that this component — as regards the essential moment in it that performs the "presenting" of the state of affairs — must be of an *essentially other genus* than the characters we otherwise designate as *act-qualities*: in other words, than the known characters to which the presented thing owes it that it is *judged, wished*, etc. To these characters we reckon also that of the "mere" presenting discussed above, but *not* the *identically remaining moment of the "content" or matter*, even if this (or the whole underlying act-component) is also called presentation or presenting.

The fourth paragraph entertains a final evasion: matter occurring *for itself*. One could finally try the following way out: having conceded that the "contents" are *not act-qualities*, it could yet appear *possible* that precisely the *same* contents — which in acts appear in supplementing interweaving with act-qualities — could under other circumstances appear also *for themselves*, in concrete experiences *free of all act-qualities*. And on this latter way would come about the *genuine* cases of *mere presentations*, as concrete experiences that are yet *not "acts"* at all — namely, provided we still hold to reckoning, to the concept of act, the co-presence of some act-quality.

## **The fifth paragraph rejects this: matter is *abstract* and cannot occur in isolation. On *attentive essence-consideration* of the relevant experiences, it seems *unavoidable* to grasp the mere presenting *really as an act*. The *interweaving* of matters with act-qualities is, as we convince ourselves on the basis of exemplary intuition, an interweaving of *abstract moments*. Hence *isolated*, the matter cannot *occur*; it can evidently gain *concretion* only in *supplementation* by arbitrary moments lawfully *delimited by the highest genus act-quality*. To be sure, the *mere understanding* — in general the mere "*having-put-forward*" — is something quite essentially other than the "*positing*" of *belief* or other "*position-takings*" of surmising, wishing. But *then* we have precisely to recognize, and phenomenologically fix, *differences within the total genus act-quality*. (A footnote refers to *Ideen I*, §109, on the "qualitative modification"/"neutrality-modification," noting that deeper insight there required *developments* of the doctrine of "act-quality" which leave the essential content of this chapter untouched but bring a *partial reinterpretation* of the results won.) Mere presenting is thus *not* a non-act bearing isolated matter, but itself an act-quality, a distinctive species within the genus — the *positionless* counterpart of belief and the other position-takings.**

## **Chapter Four: Study Concerning Founding Presentations with Special Regard to the Doctrine of Judgment**

### **§32. A double sense of the word presentation and the supposed evidence of the proposition of the foundation of every act by a presentation-act**

The first paragraph distinguishes the *first* sense of "presentation" (an act-quality). If we may regard the results of the last chapter as secured, a *double* concept of presentation is to be distinguished. *Presentation in the first sense* is an *act* (or a peculiar *act-quality*) as good as judgment, wish, question. Examples of this concept are all the cases where isolated words or whole sentences are, outside their normal function, *merely understood*: we understand assertion-, question-, wish-sentences without ourselves judging, asking, wishing; likewise every non-expressive "mere having-hover-before-us" of thoughts without any "position-taking"; likewise every "mere" phantasying.

The second paragraph distinguishes the *other* sense (act-matter, or representation). In the *other* sense, *presentation* would be *no act* but the *act-matter*, which makes up the one side of the intentional essence in every complete act — or, more concretely, this matter together with the other moments it needs for its full *concretion*, what we shall later call *representation* (Repräsentation). This "presentation" underlies, like every act, also the act of *presenting* (in the first sense). When it does, the *matter* — which as identical can function in various kinds of acts — is given *with* a peculiar act-quality "presenting," in a peculiar "manner of consciousness."

The third paragraph confirms the quality/matter analysis applies to mere presenting, and stresses *abstract moments*. Orienting the meaning of the talk of acts of *mere presenting* by the above examples, the possibility of performing the analysis into quality and matter phenomenologically — as with other acts — is *indubitable*. Exactly as with judgment we distinguish between the specific character of conviction and the *content* of the conviction, so here too between the peculiar being-disposed of that mere understanding (having-put-forward) and the *determinacy* that makes up the *what* of this understanding. The same holds however one chooses the circle of examples. But it is *constantly to be kept in view* that in the present analysis we speak not of a possible *partitioning* of the acts, but of a *distinction of abstract moments* or sides of these acts. They emerge in the comparing consideration; they are the moments lying *in the essence* of the acts themselves, which determine the possibility of ordering the acts into certain *series* of likeness and difference. The like or different *intuitively exhibitable* in such series — these are precisely those sides, like quality and matter. So no one can *decompose* any *movement* into direction, acceleration, but one can well *distinguish* these determinacies on it.

The fourth paragraph exposes the *supposed evidence* and locates the deception in the double sense. The proposition that every intentional experience is either itself a (mere) presentation or *has* a presentation as basis turns out, after the foregoing, to be a *supposed evidence*. The deception is grounded in the discussed *double sense* of presentation. In its first part the proposition speaks, rightly understood, of presentation in the sense of a certain *act-kind*; in the second, of presentation in the sense of the mere *act-matter* (completed in the manner indicated above). *This second part for itself* — the proposition that every intentional experience has a presentation as basis — would be a *genuine* evidence, provided *presentation* were interpreted as *completed matter*. The *false* proposition, combated by us, arises when *presentation* is here too interpreted as *act*.

The fifth paragraph cautions: perhaps another act-sense escapes the objections. But here a reservation counsels caution. Is there *only one* way of interpreting "presentation" as act? Does the proposition in question not perhaps admit *other* interpretations that remain untouched by our objections? In that case our exposition would be quite apt with reference to *that* concept of presenting it presupposed (following the usual elucidations of the word), but not with reference to *other* concepts of presentation and the new interpretations of the multiply shimmering proposition thereby attainable.

### **§33. Restitution of the proposition on the basis of a new concept of presentation. Naming and asserting**

The first paragraph raises the question of restitution. The question arises whether the proposition cannot, *on the basis of another concept of presentation*, be *fully* upheld.

The second paragraph recalls why the proposition was doubtful and introduces the *narrower* sense oriented to *naming*. To the unity of the act corresponds in each case the objective unity belonging to it, the unity of the (widest-sense) objectivity to which it "intentionally" relates. The proposition in question we found *doubtful* insofar as it understood by *presentation* a certain *act* supposed to relate to this *whole* objective unity of the respective act and to underlie it: the state of affairs that is meant in the judgment, wished in the wish, surmised in the surmise, was supposed to be necessarily *presented*, and indeed in a peculiar act of "presenting." There the title "presenting" comprised the "mere" presenting, an act-kind we exemplified by the mere understanding of torn-out words, or by the mere understanding of heard assertion-sentences toward which we "comport ourselves completely neutrally." But the proposition gains at once a *new* and *unobjectionable* sense if we underlie the term "presenting" with a *new* concept — namely the one that especially lies near insofar as the talk of *names* as *expressions of presentations* leads to it. We may then no longer demand that this presenting *intentionally span* the *whole* objective unity of the respective act. For we can comprise *under the title presentation every act in which something becomes objective to us in a certain narrower sense* — e.g., in the manner of *perceptions* (and parallel intuitions) grasping the objective *in one grip*, meaning it in *one ray of meaning*; or in the manner of the *one-membered subject-acts* in categorical assertions, the acts of *simple presupposing* functioning as antecedents in hypothetical assertions.

The third paragraph states the decisive descriptive difference: state of affairs in a judgment vs. in a nominal subject-act. If we accomplish a *judgment*, an act of predicating *closed for itself*, then something seems to us *to be or not to be*, e.g., *S is p*. But the *same being* that is here "present" to us becomes present to us in a quite *other way* when we say: *the p-being of S*. Likewise the state of affairs *S is p* comes to consciousness in a quite other way in a judgment in which we *simply assert* "S is p," and in the *subject-act* of another judgment, as when we say "the fact that S is p" — or simply "that S is p — has the consequence …, is gratifying, is doubtful." Likewise in the *antecedent* of a hypothetical or causal sentence: "*if* (or *because*) S is p," "in a disjunctive consequent: *or* it is S p." In all these cases the *state of affairs* — *not* the judgment — is objective to us *in another sense*, and accordingly present in *changed meanings*, than in the judgment of which it forms the full objective correlate. And it is then objective in a *similar* sense as the *thing* at which we glance in perception (or phantasy, or picture-viewing) in one *ray of regard* — although a state of affairs is *no thing* and in general nothing that could be perceived, imagined, depicted in the proper, narrower sense.

The fourth paragraph distinguishes *naming a judgment* from *naming a state of affairs*. With reference to the sentences *functioning as subjects*, Husserl said in parenthesis above that they are *not presentations of judgments* but of the corresponding *states of affairs*. This is well to note. Judgments, as concrete experiences, are of course — as good as things — objects of possible perception, imagination, and possibly a (non-physical) depiction. They can then function as *subject-objects* in judgments: this is the case of *judgments about judgments*. In their expression, if the judged judgments are not merely indirectly designated (e.g. as "this, your judgment"), a *sentence* will stand at the subject-place. But *not always*, where a sentence stands at such place, does it have the function (as here) of *naming a judgment*. To *judge about a judgment* is something other than to *judge about a state of affairs*; and accordingly it is something other to present (or name) *subjectively* a *judgment*, and again something other a *state of affairs*. When I say, e.g., "that S is p is gratifying," I do not mean that the *judgment* is gratifying. It is also indifferent whether by "judgment" one means the singular act or the sentence (the judgment in the specific sense). Gratifying is rather *this, that the matter so stands*, the objective *state of affairs*, the *fact*. This is also taught by the objectively equivalent (though meaning-modifying) turn "the p-being of S (the victory of the just cause) is gratifying."

The fifth paragraph restores the proposition in its new form. Laying the changed concept of presentation at the basis, and letting fall the claim that the presentation as *founding* act spans the *whole matter* of the founded, the previously rejected proposition — that every act not itself a presentation must be *founded* in a presentation — seems really to gain a *valuable content*, which we may well claim as *evidence*. More exactly we must now formulate it thus: *every act is either itself a presentation, or it is founded in one or several presentations*. Examples to which the first half applies are furnished by *one-membered (single-rayed)* acts of perception, memory, expectation, imagination — these would be the "mere" presentations. Examples for the second half are furnished by *judgments* (predications) and the mere presentations corresponding to them as counter-images (in the earlier word-sense). A judgment has *at least one* presentation as basis, as every fully uttered assertion contains at least one "name." If the prevailing view is correct, assigning to the simple judgment the normal form *S is p*, we would have as *minimum* even *two* presentations (two names). The *maximal* number is *unlimited*: arbitrarily many presentations are possible in a single judgment.

The sixth paragraph extends the proposition to all acts and states the last-founding thesis. The same seems to hold for *all other acts*, insofar as they are full and whole acts at all. The *wish* "may S be p," "may truth triumph," has its presentations in the *S* and *p*; "the truth" is object of a *simple* subject-positing, and the wishing grounds itself on the *triumphing* predicatively presented on it. So with all similarly built acts, and with the simpler acts grounding immediately on intuitions (e.g. a *joy* over something perceived). Finally we may add the proposition that the *last-founding* acts in *every* act-complexion are necessarily *presentations*.

### **§34. Difficulties. The concept of the name. Positing and non-positing names**

The first paragraph notes the new concept is not free of difficulties. The new concept of presentation is not free of difficulties. That those acts called to *last foundation* have a *common* element insofar as they make an objective "present" in a certain *pregnant* sense is unmistakable. But whether presentation in this sense designates an *essential genus* of intentional experiences — such that the generic unity is determined purely by the *act-quality*, and the acts excluded from the sphere of presentation would have to be of *qualitatively* other genera — is *not* thereby said. And it is not at all easy to decide *wherein* the commonality is supposed to consist.

The second paragraph turns to *names* and the unity of nominally expressible presentations. If one designates, as usually happens, *names* as *expressions of presentations*, then the present concept of presentation is essentially in question. At all events, all nominally expressible "presentations" form a *unity* we wish first to inspect. The various sense of the talk of *expressions* brings it about that here, under presentation, both the *nominal meaning-intentions* and the corresponding *meaning-fulfillments* can be meant. But both — the non-intuitive and the intuitive acts — fall here uniformly under the staked-out concept of presentation.

The third paragraph clarifies what a *name* is (with the article). Under *names* we may *not* understand mere *nouns*, which for themselves alone express no full act. To grasp clearly what names are and mean, we do best to look to *connections*, especially *assertions* in which names function in normal meaning. Here we see that words or word-complexions counting as names express a *closed act* only when they either present a *complete simple assertion-subject* (expressing a complete subject-act) or can, apart from syntactic formings, fill the simple subject-function in an assertion without change of their intentional essence. Accordingly, the mere *noun* — even together with an accompanying adjective- or relative-clause — does not make a full name; rather we must add the *definite* or *indefinite article*, which bears a very important meaning-function. *The horse; a bouquet; a house built of sandstone; the opening of the Reichstag* — but also expressions like *that the Reichstag is opened* — are names. (A footnote excludes conjunctive or disjunctive subject-multiplicities, such as "A and B are p," "A or B is p": the subject-function as such is *simple*.)

The fourth paragraph notes a remarkable difference: positing vs. non-positing names. In many — but evidently not all — cases the names (the nominal presentations) are of a kind that intends and names the object as a *really existent* one, *without* their being on that account *more* than mere names (without their being allowed to count as full *assertions*). The latter is already excluded by the fact that *assertions* can never enter the subject-place in *unmodified* meaning. Judgments can indeed function as *judgment-subjects* in the sense of *judged objects*, but never (without a certain change of sense) as *subject-acts* of other judgments, as "presentations."

The fifth paragraph illustrates positing names. Setting aside provisionally the cases where seemingly full assertions stand in the subject, names come into consideration like *Prince Heinrich, the Roland statue in the market, the postman hurrying by*. Whoever uses these names in *truthful* speech and *normal* sense "*knows*" that Prince Heinrich is a real person and no fabled being, that a Roland statue stands in the market, that the postman hurries by. More: the named objects stand before his eyes *otherwise* than imagined ones, and they appear to him not only *as existent* — he also *expresses* them as such. Nevertheless he *predicates* nothing of all this in the *naming* act; exceptionally he may express the being at least *attributively*, in the form "the *really existing* S" (as in opposed cases he might say "the supposed S," "the imagined S"). But the *positing* is, even in the grammatically enriched name, accomplished by that moment of the act expressed in the *definite article*, and only the *matter* is changed. At all events, even then it is *not asserted* that S exists; rather S is *attributively* presented as *really existing*, and besides *posited*, and therefore *named* in the form "the really existing S"; and *naming* is in sense not identical with *asserting*.

The sixth paragraph fixes the distinction of two kinds of names. Granting this, we have to distinguish *two kinds of names* (nominal acts): those that *accord the named the value of an existent*, and those that *do not*. An example of the latter, if one is needed at all, is offered by the nominal matter pure of every *existential consideration*, which really begins without any being-position-taking.

The seventh paragraph extends positing/non-positing across the whole sphere of presentation. A similar difference is found also with other founding acts, as the comparison of a hypothetical and causal *antecedent* teaches; this is not otherwise to be expected, since these acts are essentially *akin* to the nominal ones. In general, the difference between *positing* and *non-positing* acts extends over the *whole domain of presentation* in the present sense, which reaches far beyond that of the properly nominal presentations. In the sphere of the relevant *intuitive presentations* — which do not themselves function nominally but have the *logical calling* to *fulfill* nominal meaning-intentions — *positing* acts are: *perception, memory, expectation* (sensibly appropriating the objective in a *ray* of positing meaning). *Non-positing* is the corresponding *anomalous* perception, deprived of its being-valuation — e.g., the *illusion* kept free of all position-taking toward the reality of the appearing — and likewise every case of mere *phantasy*. To *every positing act there belongs a possible non-positing act of the same matter, and conversely.*

The eighth paragraph identifies this as a difference of *act-quality* and poses the genus-question. This characteristic difference is evidently a difference of *act-quality*, and so in the concept of presentation lies a certain *cleavage*. May we still speak of a *genus presentation* in the strict sense? May we assume that *positing* and *non-positing* presentations are *species* or *differences* of this one genus?

The ninth paragraph entertains an evasion: take positing acts as founded. The difficulty would be removed at a stroke if one could grasp the *positing* acts as *already founded*, and so assume that they themselves are *not* mere presentations but *founded* in presentations — that to the mere presentation the *positing-character* (about which one would then only have to consider whether it is not quite self-evidently *like* the character of judgment-quality) *accedes* anew.

The tenth paragraph rejects this evasion by the earlier analysis. After our above analyses, this conception appears *quite doubtful*. As little as from a *perception* an act of mere presentation can be *split off* — or from an *actual assertion* an act of merely understood but not judged assertion — so little, from the *positing* act of nominal meaning-intention, a *positing-less* one. The *analogy* of the nominal and propositional acts must necessarily be a *complete* one, since a priori to every *positing* and complete *nominal* act there corresponds a possible *self-sufficient assertion*, and to every *non-positing* one a *correlative* act of *modified assertion* (mere assertion-understanding). The analysis thus leads, in the wider sphere too, to the result that the *common* element of the positing and non-positing act *of like content* consists *not* in a full act but in a *mere act-matter*, given in the two cases in *different act-quality*. One can *merely understand* a name, but this mere understanding is *not contained* in the *positing* use of the name. Thus there is *no way* here to remove the cleavage in question within the class of presentations in the present sense of nominal acts.

### **§35. Nominal positing and judgment. Whether judgments can become parts of nominal acts at all**

The first paragraph returns to the relation between positing presentations and judgments. We return to the question of the kinship and the correct *relation* between *positing presentations* and *predicative judgments*. Perhaps one tries to regard the difference of the two act-kinds as an *inessential* one, and to say: the *positing name* is admittedly no *assertion* — no self-sufficient predication, no expression of a self-sufficient judgment. Yet it *offers* a *judgment*, only one meant to serve as *presupposition* or *basis* for another act to be built upon it. This function — *not altering* the *intentional content* of the judgment — is what determines the linguistic form distinctively. If someone says "the passing postman …," there lies in it the judgment "the postman passes." The nominal form is a *mere indication* for the *thetic* subject-function, pointing to the further predicate-positing.

The second paragraph rejects this externalization, siding with Bolzano. This way of *entirely externalizing* the difference — as though to the *identically remaining* judgment merely new acts attached, and the grammatical form of the name merely had the character of an *indirect indication* for the kind of this attachment — we can scarcely approve. Most logicians, among them such deep thinkers as *Bolzano*, have held the difference between *names* and *assertions* an *essential* one, and the *riper* science will grant them right. A *common* element can well subsist on both sides, but that the difference is a *merely external* one must be contested. More exactly, one must realize that *nominal acts and complete judgments can never have the same intentional essence*, and that therefore every transformation of the one function into the other — despite preservation of a common subsistence — entails *necessary changes* in this essence.

The third paragraph identifies what misleads: full assertions *can* function subjectivally as determining judgments. What misleads here is probably most the circumstance that in fact genuine *predications*, full *assertions*, can in a certain way function *subjectivally*. Even if they are not the *subject-acts* themselves, they yet *fit themselves into* these in a certain way, namely as *determining judgments* in relation to the otherwise already presented subjects. E.g., "the minister — he is just driving up — will make the decision." Instead of the parenthetical assertion it can also read, without change of sense, "the minister who is just driving up," or "the — just driving up — minister."

The fourth paragraph shows this conception is not universally apt (regress). But such a conception is *not everywhere* apt. The *attribution* may often present a *determinative predication*; but even if it always did so (which doubtless is not the case), it concerns only a *part* of the subject-name. After deleting all such determinative additions, a *full name* still remains over, to which it would be vain effort to *suppose* a merely subjectivally functioning judging. In our example the determining predication leans on the name "the minister," from which a *second* predication can no longer be detached. What should here be the underlying judgment, how would it run in self-sufficient form? Does "the minister" mean as much as "the — it is a minister"? Then "the" would be a full name and claim its own judgment for itself. But how does this express itself? Is it the judgment that, self-sufficiently grasped, ran "the exists"? But in that there sticks again the same subject "the," and so we would come to an *infinite regress*.

The fifth paragraph affirms genetic origin from judgments but insists on the essential difference (modification). It is indubitable that a great part of names — among them all *attributive* names — have, immediately or mediately, "*sprung*" from judgments, and according to this origin *point back* to judgments. But with this talk of *springing* and *pointing back* it is already said that the two are *different*. The difference is so sharply marked that we may not push it aside for theoretical prejudices or for the greater simplicity hoped for in the doctrine of presenting and judging. The *prior judging* is *not yet* the nominal meaning, which only *grows* out of it. What is given in the *name* as *precipitate* of the judgment is, *instead of* the judgment, a *modification sharply distinguished* from it. The *accomplishment* of the modified act no longer *contains* the unmodified one. Having experienced or insighted that the city *Halle lies on the Saale*, that *π is a transcendent number*, we continue: "the Saale-city Halle," "the transcendent number π." The *judgment* we thereby no longer *accomplish* — at least it is no requirement — and where it sets in *alongside*, it makes *no contribution* to the act of nominal *meaning*. And so in every case.

The sixth paragraph clarifies that judgment cannot *take over* an attributive function. We spoke above of judgments appearing in *determining* function; but this may not be taken quite strictly. For on closer view this function consists only in letting the *attribution* enriching the name *arise* before our eyes, so to speak. The *judgment itself* is *no attributive function* and can never *take over* such; it only *prepares the ground* from which the attributive meaning *grows phenomenologically*. Once this accomplishment is done, the judgment can again *fall away*, and the *attribute* with its meaning-function *remains*. In those exceptional cases we thus deal with *complexions*: the attributive function is *interwoven* with the predicative; the latter lets the former proceed out of itself, but wishes at the same time to come to validity *for itself alongside* — hence the normal expression in *parenthesis*. The ordinary cases of attributive function are free of this complication. Whoever speaks of *the German Kaiser* or *the transcendent number π* does *not* mean "the Kaiser — it is the Kaiser of Germany," or "π — it is a transcendent number."

The seventh paragraph supplies the important addition: the modification is a *phenomenological* essence-relation, not psychological. To full understanding of this an *important addition* is needed. The accomplishment of the "modified" act, we said, no longer *contains* the "original" one. But this does *not* exclude that the "original" judgment in a certain way "*logically*" "*lies in*" the "modified" act. The talk of *springing-from* and *modification* is by no means to be understood in an *empirical-psychological* and *biological* sense, but expresses a peculiar *essence-relation* grounded in the *phenomenological content* of the experiences. In the own essential content of the nominal, attributive presentation it lies that its intention "*points back*" to the corresponding judgment, that it gives itself, in itself, as "*modification*" of this judgment. To "*realize*" the sense of presentations of the type *the p-being S* (the transcendent number π) — to accomplish it in full distinctness and properness, hence to take the way of the *fulfilling exhibition* of what is "meant" by such an expression — we must, so to speak, *appeal* to the corresponding *predicative judgment*, accomplish it, and "originally" *derive* the nominal presentation from it. The same holds, *mutatis mutandis*, of *non-positing* attributive presentations: in "proper" accomplishment they phenomenologically *demand* predicative acts of *qualitatively modified* kind (the counterparts of actual judgments) in order to proceed originally from them. In the essence of the attributive presentation lies, then, phenomenologically a certain *mediacy*, to which the talk of *springing, being-derived*, and *pointing back* gives expression. So it comes that a priori the *grounding* of the validity of every nominal attribution leads back to that of the corresponding *judgment*, and that correlatively it must also be said that the *nominal object*, in its respective categorial grasping, is "*derived*" from the corresponding *state of affairs*, this being in its *truth* in itself *earlier* than that.

The eighth paragraph states the general conclusion: an essential difference of meaning-essence. After these expositions we may assert quite generally that *between names and assertions there subsist differences concerning the meaning-essence* — differences resting on "presentations" and "judgments" as *essentially different acts*. *As in the intentional essence it does not come to the same whether one grasps an existent perceivingly or judges that it is, so it also does not come to the same whether one names an existent as such or asserts (predicates) of it that it is.*

The ninth paragraph establishes the *ideal-lawful* correlation between positing names and judgments. Noting that *evidently* to every *positing name* a possible *judgment* corresponds (and to every attribution a possible predication, and conversely), there remains — after we have *denied* the *identity* of the acts as regards their essence — only the assumption that here *lawful*, and evidently *ideal-lawful*, connections subsist. As ideal-lawful, they mean not the *causal* proceeding or the empirical *coexistence* of the coordinated acts, but a certain ideal-lawful *operative belonging-together* of the relevant ideatively graspable act-essences, which have their "being" and their lawful "being-order" in the realm of *phenomenological ideality*, just as the pure numbers and the pure species of geometrical formations in the realm of arithmetical and geometrical idealities. Entering the a priori scope of the pure ideas, we can also say that "one," in pure, unconditioned generality, "cannot" accomplish the one (with respect to the specific meaning-essence) without "being able" to accomplish the coordinated ones; indeed, that also in the validity-logical respect coordinations subsist in the form of *lawful equivalences*, such that one cannot — reasonably — begin with "this S" without "potentially" thereby conceding that "there is S."

The tenth paragraph states the analytic incompatibility. In other words: that a *proposition with some positing names* holds, and the *being-judgments* corresponding to these names do *not* hold, is an *a priori incompatibility*. It is one from that group of "*analytic*" ideal laws grounded in the "*mere form*" of thinking — in the *categories*, as the specific ideas belonging to the possible *forms of proper thinking*.

### **§36. Continuation. Whether assertions can function as whole names**

The first paragraph poses the hardest case: assertion-sentences seemingly functioning as full names. Still one important class of examples we must weigh to test our conception of the relation between nominal acts and judgments: the cases where *assertion-sentences* are used not only in *determinative* intent (seeming, as actual assertions, to form parts of names) but seem outright to function *as names*, as *full and whole names*. E.g., "*that rain has finally set in* will gladden the farmers." The subject-sentence is — the concession seems here unavoidable — a *full assertion*. It is indeed meant that *rain has really set in*. The modified expression that the judgment has undergone through the form of a *subordinate clause* could here only serve to indicate the circumstance that the assertion stands in *subject-function*, that it is to furnish the *ground-act* for a predicate-positing to be built upon it.

The second paragraph flags the threat to Husserl's view. All this sounds very appealing. But if the contested conception found a real *support* in this class of cases, and were really *admissible* with them, the doubt would at once stir whether it must not, *despite* our objections, be maintained in the wider circle too.

The third paragraph analyzes the example: the *fact* is the subject, nameable in various ways. To the question of *what* the farmers would be glad about, one answers: "about *this, that* …" or "about *the fact that* rain has finally fallen." Thus the *fact*, the state of affairs posited in the *being*\-mode, is the *object* of the joy, the *subject* of which something is asserted. This fact we can *name* variously. We can — as with all other objects — simply say "this"; but we can also say "this fact," or, more determiningly, "the fact of the fallen rain," "the falling of the rain"; and among these, as in the example, "that rain has set in." It is clear in this juxtaposition that this *sentence* is a *name* exactly in the sense of all the other nominal expressions for facts, and that it does *not* essentially differ from other names in the sense-giving acts. Exactly as they, it *names*, and naming *presents*; and as other names name other things (things, properties), so it names (presents) a *state of affairs*, specifically an empirical *fact*.

The fourth paragraph poses the contrast between naming and asserting the state of affairs. What, then, is the *difference* between this *naming* and the *asserting* of the state of affairs in the self-sufficient assertion — in the example, "finally rain has set in"? It happens that we *first* simply assert and then refer to the state of affairs *namingly*: "finally is … — that will gladden the farmers." Here we can study the *contrast*; it is unmistakable. The state of affairs is on both sides the same, but it becomes objective to us in a quite *other way*.

The fifth paragraph analyzes the *judgment* as synthetic, multi-rayed consciousness. In the simple *assertion* we judge about the rain and its having-set-in; *both* are to us, in the pregnant sense, "objective," "presented." But we accomplish *not* a mere *succession* of presentations, but a *judgment*, a peculiar "*unity of consciousness*" that "*connects*" the presentations. And in this connection the consciousness of the *state of affairs* constitutes itself for us. To *accomplish the judgment* and to *become "conscious" of a state of affairs* in this "synthetic," "something-upon-something"-positing manner *is one and the same*. A *thesis* is accomplished and thereupon a *second non-self-sufficient thesis*, such that in the *founding of these theses upon one another* the *synthetic unity of the state of affairs* comes to intentional constitution. This *synthetic consciousness* is something quite *other* than the *setting-an-something-over-against-oneself* (so to speak) in a *single-rayed thesis*, in a possible *simple subject-act*, in a *presentation*.

The sixth paragraph analyzes the *naming* as single-rayed, pointing back to the judgment. Compare the way the *rain* becomes "conscious," and above all the *judgment-consciousness* (the *being-asserted* of the state of affairs) with the immediately adjoining *presentation-consciousness* (the *being-named* of the same state of affairs): "that will gladden the farmers." This *points*, as with a finger, to the asserted state of affairs; it thus *means* this *same* state of affairs. But this meaning is *not the judging itself*, which has gone before, has flowed off as a so-constituted psychic event; rather it is a *new and novel act* which, as *pointing*, *sets itself over against* the state of affairs (already synthetically, multi-rayedly constituted before) in a *single-rayed thesis* — and so has it as object in a quite *other sense* than the judgment. Accordingly, this state of affairs comes to consciousness "*more originally*" in the *judgment*; the intention directed at it in *one ray* presupposes the *multi-rayed* one and *points back* to it in its own sense. A priori, in every multi-rayed manner of consciousness there is grounded the *possibility* (as "ideal" essence-possibility) of being *transferred* into the *single-rayed* one, in which the state of affairs is "objective" or "presented" in the pregnant sense. (As, e.g., a priori in the ideal essence of a geometrical formation is grounded the possibility that "one" could *rotate* it in space, transform it by deformation into other formations.) At all events it is now fully clear: the "*manner of consciousness*," the way the object becomes intentional, is *on both sides different* — but that is only another expression for our dealing with "essentially" different acts, with acts of *different intentional essence*.

The seventh paragraph locates the *article*'s meaning-moment in the subjectival sentence too. Setting aside the actual *pointing*, the *essential* of that (emphasized in the example) lies also in the thought of the *mere sentence at the subject-place* (and at any other place in some connection demanding *presentations*), as it on the other hand necessarily *lacks* in the thought of the self-sufficient and proper assertion. *As soon as the meaning-moment underlying the definite article is alive, a presenting in the present sense has also been accomplished.* Whether language or dialect actually uses the article or not, whether one says "the man" or "homo," "Karl" or "the Karl," is here indifferent. That this meaning-moment is also *not lacking* in the subjectivally functioning sentence "that S is P" is easy to see. In fact, "that S is P" means as much as "this, that S is P," or, only slightly paraphrasing, "the fact, the circumstance, that S is P."

The eighth paragraph draws the general conclusion. After all this, the situation is by no means of a kind that would suggest speaking here of a *judgment*, an actual *predication*, that could be a *subject* or in general a *nominal* act. Rather we see with full clarity that *between sentences functioning as names of states of affairs and the corresponding assertions of the same state of affairs there subsists, as regards intentional essence, a difference* mediated only by *ideal-lawful* relations. *Never* can an *assertion* function as *name*, or a *name* as *assertion*, *without changing its essential nature* — i.e., without change of its meaning-essence and with it of the *meaning* itself.

The ninth paragraph qualifies: the acts are not *totally* foreign; equivocation explains preserved expression. This does not say the corresponding acts are descriptively *totally foreign* to one another. The *matter* of the assertion is *partially identical* with that of the nominal act; on both sides the same state of affairs is intended by means of the same termini, though in *different form*. Accordingly the great *kinship* of the expression-form is not accidental but grounded in the *meanings*. If occasionally, despite the changed meaning-function, the *expression* preserves itself unchanged, we deal with a special case of *equivocation* — belonging to the wide class of cases where expressions function in *anomalous meaning*. Evidently this anomaly, rooted in the pure essence of the meaning-domain, is of the kind of the purely *grammatical* anomalies.

The tenth paragraph extends the result to *causal* antecedents. So our conception can be carried through consistently everywhere: we distinguish everywhere *presentations* and *judgments*, and within the presentations *positing* (being-value-according) presentations from those that are *not*. We will then also not hesitate to *deny the judgment-character* to the *causal antecedents* (sentences of the kind "because S is p") and to bring them into the *same relation* to the *hypothetical antecedents* as we recognized between *positing* and *non-positing* names. The "because" may *point back* to a judgment asserting that S is p; but in the causal sentence itself this judgment is no longer *accomplished*, it is no longer *asserted* "S is p"; rather, on a simply "presenting" underlay — which, as *causal antecedent-thesis*, is in its own sense characterized as *modification* of a judging synthesis — a second, the *consequent-thesis*, is grounded ("accomplished thereupon"). The whole is a *new form of judging synthesis*, whose sense-content can be expressed (only slightly paraphrased) as: that the *being of the grounding state of affairs conditions* that of the consequent one. Only in the manner of *complexion* can here antecedent and consequent *in addition* function *as judgment*, as when we assert "S is p, and because that is, so is Q r." Here it is a matter not merely of synthetically establishing the consequence, but also of *having and holding* these two states of affairs "S is p" and "Q r" judgmentally, in the relating synthetic consciousness itself.

The eleventh paragraph notes that nominal presentations *represent* a wider class of thetic acts. The just-accomplished extensions show that the *nominal presentations* in the narrower and proper sense *represent* for us only a *wider but firmly delimited class* of "*thetic*," "*single-rayed positing*" acts. This must also be held fast in the following, even when we *bind* our considerations to *really nominal* presentations; accordingly the term *nominal presentation*, where it functions as a *class-term*, must be understood in a very *extended* sense.

## **The twelfth paragraph fixes the operative terminology for "judgment." Well to note is also the *terminology* decisive *here*, according to which by *judgment* the *meaning of a self-sufficiently closed assertion* is understood. That this meaning cannot become — without *inner modification* — the meaning of a *hypothetical* or *causal antecedent*, or a *nominal* meaning in general, is the *thesis* established above.**

## **Chapter Five: Further Contributions to the Doctrine of Judgment. "Presentation" as Qualitatively Unitary Genus of Nominal and Propositional Acts**

### **§37. The aim of the following investigation. The concept of the objectivating act**

The first paragraph recalls the result and reformulates the question. The investigations just carried out have *not yet* settled the question raised at the opening of §34. Our result is that "*presentation*" and "*judgment*" are *essentially different acts*. Therein — the ambiguity of words demanding repeated recourse to the operative concepts — "presentation" is meant in the sense of the *nominal act* and "judgment" in the sense of the *assertion*, indeed the *accomplishment* of the normal, self-closed assertion. *Naming* and *asserting* are thus not "merely grammatically" different but "essentially different," and this again means that the two-sided acts — whether meaning-conferring or meaning-fulfilling — are different *according to their intentional essence*, and in this sense as *act-kinds*. Have we thereby shown that presentation and judgment, that the acts conferring meaning and fulfilling sense on naming and asserting, belong to *different "basic classes"* of intentional experiences?

The second paragraph answers no: the basic-class distinction concerns only *qualities*. The answer must turn out *negative*. We must consider that the intentional essence builds itself out of the two sides *matter* and *quality*, and that the distinction of "basic classes" of acts refers, as is at once clear, *only to the act-qualities*. We must further consider that from our expositions it does *not even* follow that nominal and propositional acts must be *of different quality at all*, let alone of different *quality-genus*.

The third paragraph removes the offense: the difference is a difference of *matter*. One may not take offense at this last point. The act-*matter* in our sense is nothing *foreign* to the act or externally attached, but an *inner* moment, an inseparable side of the act-intention, of the intentional essence itself. The talk of "different manners of consciousness" in which the same state of affairs can become conscious must not deceive us. It points to *differently constituted acts*, but not on that account to differently constituted *act-qualities*. With *identical quality* (and by this we let ourselves be guided in conceiving the idea of matter) the same objectivity can still be conscious in *different ways* — think of equivalent positing presentations: they direct themselves, by means of *different matters*, at the same object. And so that essential *meaning-modification* in the passage of an assertion into the nominal (or an equivalent) function — on whose demonstration we laid weight above — may have no other content than that of a *change of matter*, with *identity of quality* (or at least, according to the kind of nominal modification, of the *quality-genus*).

The fourth paragraph confirms this by examining the matters themselves: categorial form. That the real situation is thereby described is shown by attentive consideration of the *matters* themselves. The supplementation — recognized as necessary in the discussed examples — by the nominally significant *article*, or by nominal expressions like "the circumstance that …," "the fact that …," in the case of a transference of the propositional meaning into the subject-function, points us to *places* where, on the *identically transferred* essential content of the matter, the *sense-reinterpretation* attests itself; where *apprehension-functions* appear that are lacking in the original assertion, or are represented in it by others. The two-sided agreeing *essential moments* undergo, as we can everywhere see, a *different "categorial forming."* Compare, e.g., the form *S is p* with its nominal modification *S, which is p*.

The fifth paragraph announces the result: a *generic community of quality*, and a new concept of presentation (objectivating act). On the other hand, the following considerations will make clear that, as regards the *qualities*, there subsists between nominal and propositional acts a *generic community*; and thereby we will at the same time reach the *delimitation of an again-new* — and (over against the last-considered) *wider and still more significant* — *concept of presentation*, through which the proposition of the grounding of every act in presentations will undergo a new and especially important interpretation.

The sixth paragraph fixes the new term: *objectivating act*. To keep the two present concepts of "presentation" distinct, Husserl will (without making definitive terminological proposals) speak, with reference to the narrower concept, of *nominal acts*, and with reference to the wider, of *objectivating acts* (objektivierende Akte). That under the former title are meant not merely acts attaching to nominal *expressions* as meaning-giving (or acceding to them as fulfillments), but *all similarly functioning acts*, independently of whether they stand in a grammatical function, scarcely needs emphasis after the whole introduction of the nominal concept of presentation in the last chapter.

### **§38. Qualitative and material differentiation of the objectivating acts**

The first paragraph recalls the positing/non-positing distinction among nominal acts. Within the nominal acts we distinguished the *positing* and *non-positing*. The former are, so to speak, *being-meanings*; they are either sensory *perceptions*, or perceptions in the wider sense of supposed being-graspings in general, or other acts which — even without supposing to grasp the object "itself" (bodily or even intuitively) — yet *mean* it as *existent*. The other acts leave the *being* of their object *put forward*; the object may, objectively considered, exist, but in them it is not meant in the being-mode, does not count as actual, is rather "*merely presented*." Here holds the *law* that to every *positing* nominal act there corresponds a *positing-less* one — such a "mere presentation" of the same *matter* — and conversely, this correspondence to be understood in the sense of *ideal possibility*.

The second paragraph identifies the *same modification* in judgments. A certain *modification*, we can also express it, leads every positing nominal act over into a *mere presentation* of the same matter. Exactly the *same modification* we find again with the *judgments*. To every judgment belongs *its* modification, an act which *merely presents* exactly what the judgment holds true — i.e., *without decision* over true and false, has it objective. Phenomenologically considered, the modification of the judgments is *completely alike* with that of the positing nominal acts. The judgments, as *positing propositional acts*, thus have their *correlates* in mere presentations as *non-positing propositional acts*. On both sides the corresponding acts are of the *same matter* but of *different quality*.

The third paragraph establishes generic community of quality across nominal and propositional. As with the nominal acts we reckon the positing and non-positing to *one* quality-genus, so also with the propositional acts the *judgments* and their *modified* counterparts. The qualitative differences are on both sides *the same* and are not to be claimed as differences of *upper* quality-genera. In the passage from the positing to the modified act we do *not* enter a *heterogeneous* class — as we do, say, in the passage from any nominal act to a *desiring* or *willing*. But as for the passage from a *positing nominal act* to an act of *asserting assertion*, we find *no occasion* to assume any *qualitative* difference at all. And likewise in comparing the corresponding "mere presentations." The *matter alone* (matter in the sense decisive for the present investigation) makes up the one and the other difference; it alone determines the unity of the nominal and the unity of the propositional acts.

The fourth paragraph delimits the genus *objectivating act*. Thereby a *comprehensive genus of intentional experiences* delimits itself, which comprises all the considered acts together according to their *qualitative essence* and determines the *widest* concept that the term "presentation" can mean within the total class of intentional experiences. Husserl designates this qualitatively unitary genus, taken in its natural breadth, as that of the *objectivating acts*. It yields, to put it in clear opposition: (1) through *qualitative* differentiation, the division into the *positing* acts (the acts of *belief*, of judgment in the sense of Mill and Brentano) and the *non-positing*, with respect to positing "modified" acts (the corresponding "mere presentations"); (2) through differentiation of the *matter*, the difference of the *nominal* and *propositional* acts — though here it remains to be considered whether this difference is not a *single* one among a *series* of equally entitled material differences.

The fifth paragraph identifies the deeper opposition: single-rayed vs. multi-rayed (thetic vs. synthetic) acts, with the *collective* synthesis as example. Surveying the analyses of the last chapter, the *really pervasive opposition* here forces itself forward as that between *synthetic, multi-rayed-unitary* acts and *single-rayed*, in-one-thesis positing (or putting-forward) acts. But it is to be noted that the *predicative* synthesis is only a *particularly privileged form* of synthesis (or rather a whole form-system) over against which stand other, often interwoven forms: the *conjunctive* and *disjunctive* synthesis. E.g., in the plural predication "A and B and C are p" we have a *unified* predication terminating in three predicative strata in the identical predicate p. "Upon" the ground-positing of A, the second of B, the third of C, the identically held-fast p is posited in the *one* three-stratum act. This act is articulated, as by a "*caesura*," into subject-positing and predicate-positing, such that the subject-member is in turn a unified *conjunction* of three nominal members. These are connected in the conjunction but by no means close together into *one* nominal presentation. But it holds of the "*conjunctive*" (more aptly: *collective*) synthesis, as of the predicative, that it *admits a nominalization* in which the *collectivum* already constituted by the synthesis becomes, in a new single-rayed act, the simply "presented" object, and so "objective" in the pregnant sense. The nominal presentation of the collectivum then again "*points back*," in its own sense (in its "matter" changed over against the original act), to the matter (or consciousness) originally constituting it.

The sixth paragraph generalizes: *nominalization* applies to all syntheses. In general we find, on closer consideration, what forced itself on us with the predicating syntheses (where we held only to the predicative primal-form, that of the "categorical" synthesis), again with *all* syntheses: with all, the fundamental *operation of nominalization* is possible — the transformation of the *synthetic multi-rayedness* into a "*nominal*" *single-rayedness* with the corresponding *back-pointing matter*. Accordingly, in the total consideration of the ideally possible "objectivating" acts, we come back to the basic difference of "*thetic*" and "*synthetic*," single-rayed and multi-rayed acts. The single-rayed are *unarticulated*, the multi-rayed *articulated*. Every member has its objectivating *quality* (its kind of position-taking to "being," or the corresponding qualitative modification) and its *matter*. At the same time the synthetic whole, as an objectivating act, has *a* quality and *a* matter — the latter now an *articulated* one. The analysis of such a whole leads, on the one hand, to *members*, on the other to the *synthetic forms* (syntaxes).

The seventh paragraph descends to *simple* single-rayed members, distinguishing them from *primitive* ones. The members lead further to *single-rayed* and *many-rayed* (themselves again articulated, synthetically unitary) ones — so, in the above example, the conjunctive subjects of plural predications, the conjunctive antecedent-connections of hypothetical predications, and the corresponding disjunctive bonds. Finally we come to *simple, single-rayed objectivating members* — but not on that account necessarily to *primitive* ones in the last sense. For the single-rayed members can still be *nominalized syntheses*: nominal presentations of states of affairs, or of collectives or disjunctives, whose members may themselves again be states of affairs. So in the *matter* there appear *back-relativities* (Rückbezüglichkeiten) of more or less complicated kind, and with them *articulations* and *synthetic forms* implied in a peculiarly modified and *mediate* sense. If the members are no longer back-relative, they are in this respect too *simple* — as is evident, e.g., with proper-name presentations, or with all one-membered (not unfolding in explicative syntheses) perceptions, phantasy-presentations. Such perfectly *simple objectivations* are free of all "categorial forms." Evidently the analysis of every (non-simple) objectivating act — provided it also pursues the *step-sequence* of back-pointings in the nominalizations enclosed in it — leads finally back to *such "simple" act-members, simple according to form and matter*.

The eighth paragraph notes the connection to logical grammar and combinatorial laws. The general consideration of possible articulations and synthetic formings leads to the lawfulnesses discussed as *purely logical-grammatical* in the Fourth Investigation. In this respect it comes essentially only to the *matters* (the objectivating act-senses) in which all *forms* in the structure of the objectivating syntheses are imprinted. Here belongs, e.g., the proposition that every *unitarily closed objectivating matter* (hence every possible *self-sufficient meaning*) can function as *member-matter* in every synthesis of every possible form — whence is understood also the particular proposition that every such matter is *either* a full *propositional* (predicative) one *or* a possible *member* of such. Taking the *qualities* into account, we can pronounce the proposition that, ideally grasped, *arbitrary objectivating matters are combinable with arbitrary qualities*.

The ninth paragraph confirms the free combinability and distinguishes *qualitative* from *conform* modification. Looking to the special difference of nominal and propositional acts (of particular interest in our context), the asserted possibility of combining arbitrary qualities with arbitrary matters is easily confirmed. In the analyses of the previous section it did not emerge from all sides, insofar as we dealt only with modifications of the *judgment* (the positing propositional act) into a nominal one. But it is unmistakable that *every judgment qualitatively modified into "mere" presentation* can also be transformed into a corresponding *nominal* act — e.g., "2 × 2 equals 5" (in the merely understanding, non-position-taking utterance) into the name "*that 2 × 2 equals 5*." Since we speak of *modification* also with such transformations of sentences into names that *leave the qualities untouched* — hence with *mere* transformations of propositional (and in general synthetic) matters into nominal ones — it is good to designate that quite differently kinded modification *concerning the qualities* (transforming positing names or assertions into positing-less ones) expressly as *qualitative modification*. Insofar as the matter alone (form-giving or grounding form-differences) is preserved, or shall be preserved (the name remains name, the assertion assertion, with all inner articulations and forms), we also speak of *conform modification* of the positing act. However, if the concept of conform modification is taken in natural generality — so that it extends to *every modification not touching the matter* of the act — then it is, as we shall further discuss, *wider* than the present concept of qualitative modification.

### **§39. Presentation in the sense of the objectivating act and its qualitative modification**

The first paragraph poses a worry: is the positing/non-positing modification class-defining or universal? Decisive for comprising the *objectivating* acts in one class was the circumstance that this whole class was characterized by a *qualitative opposition* — that, as to every *nominal belief*, so to every *propositional* act, every full *judgment*, there belongs a "*mere presentation*" as its *counterpart*. The worry now arises whether this *qualitative modification* is at all suited to *characterize a class* of intentional experiences, and whether it does not rather have its validity, in the *total sphere* of these experiences, as a *division-motif*. For the latter a near-lying argument offers itself: to *every* intentional experience whatever there corresponds a mere presentation — to the wish the mere presentation of the wish, to the hate the mere presentation of the hate, to the willing the mere presentation of the willing — quite as to actual naming and asserting the corresponding mere presentations.

The second paragraph distinguishes two fundamentally different things. Here one may not *mingle* fundamentally different things. To every *possible act*, as to every possible *experience* — indeed, quite generally, to every possible *object* — there belongs a *presentation* referring to it, and this can be qualified equally well as *positing* or as *non-positing* (as "mere" presentation). But at bottom it is *not one* but a *whole manifold* of differently kinded presentations, and this holds even if we restrict ourselves (as we shall tacitly have done) to presentations of the *nominal* type. This presentation can present its object *intuitively* and *thinkingly*, *directly* or *attributively mediated*, and all this in manifold ways. But it suffices for our purposes to speak of *a* presentation, or to single out any one of them (say the *imaginative*), since all kinds of presentations are everywhere possible in like manner.

The third paragraph distinguishes presentation-*of*\-an-act from the *qualitative counterpart* of that act. To every *object*, then, corresponds the *presentation* of the object: to the house the presentation of the house, to the presentation the presentation of the presentation, to the judgment the presentation of the judgment. But here it is to be noted that the presentation *of the judgment* (as set out above) is *not* the presentation of the *judged state of affairs*. And likewise, more generally, the presentation of a *positing* is *not* the presentation of the *object presented in the manner of positing*. The objects presented on the two sides are *different*. Hence, e.g., the *will* that wills to realize a *state of affairs* is *another* than the will that wills to realize a *judgment* or a *nominal positing* of this state of affairs. To the positing act its *qualitative counterpart* corresponds in a *totally other way* than the *presentation* of this act corresponds to it (and to any act whatever). The *qualitative modification* of an act is, so to speak, a *totally other "operation"* than the *production of a presentation* referring to it.

The fourth paragraph exhibits the contrast formally: iterability and scope. The essential difference of these two operations shows itself in this: that the latter — the *operation of presenting objectivation*, after the symbols *O, V(O), V\[V(O)\] …* (where O is some object, V(O) the presentation of O) — is *iterable in infinitum*, but the *qualitative modification* is *not*; and again, that *presenting objectivation* is applicable to *all objects whatever*, while the *qualitative modification* has a sense only for *acts*. And again: that in the one series of modifications the "presentations" are exclusively *nominal*, while in the other this restriction does not hold; finally, that *there* the *qualities* remain quite out of question (the modification essentially concerning the *matters*), while *here*, with qualitative modification, precisely the *quality* is the modified.

The fifth paragraph describes the qualitative modification concretely and its non-iterability. To every act of *belief* there corresponds, as counterpart, a "mere" presentation, which makes the *same* objectivity present in exactly the same way (on the basis of an *identical matter*) as that act of belief, and which distinguishes itself from it *only* by this, that it lets the presented objectivity, *instead of* positing it in the manner of being-meaning, rather be *put forward*. This modification cannot, of course, be *iterated*, as little as it would give a sense with acts not falling under the concept of belief. It thus creates, between acts of this quality and their counterparts, a *unique* connection. E.g., the positing *perception* or *memory* has its counterpart in a corresponding act of "*mere*" *imagination* of the same matter — e.g., in a perceptive *picture-viewing*, as in contemplating a *painting* that we let affect us merely aesthetically, without any position-taking about being or non-being of the depicted; or in the intuition of a "phantasy-image," as when we range in phantasy without any actual being-position-taking. Naturally the "mere" presentation does *not* here again have a counterpart; it is quite unintelligible what *that* should mean and accomplish. Once "believing" has transformed into "mere presenting," we can at most *return* to believing; but a modification *repeating and continuing in the same sense* does *not* exist.

The sixth paragraph shows the *iterability* of presenting objectivation by examples. It is otherwise if we *exchange* the operation of qualitative modification with that of *presenting (nominal) objectivation*. Here the possibility of *iteration* is evident. Most simply we show this in the relation of acts to the ego and their distribution over various time-points or persons. Once I *perceive* something, another time I *present to myself* that I perceive this, a third time I again present that I present that I perceive. Or another example: A is painted; a second painting *depicts* the first, a third then the second. Here the differences are unmistakable. They are not, of course, *mere* differences of sensation-contents, but differences of the *apprehending act-characters* (especially of the intentional *matters*), without which the talk of phantasy-image, painting, would be senseless. And these differences one grasps *immanently*, is *phenomenologically* certain of them, as soon as one accomplishes the corresponding experiences and reflectively turns to their intentional differences.

The seventh paragraph corrects the error of denying presentations of presentations. Whoever has made these relations clear cannot fall into the error of those who declare *presentations of presentations* phenomenologically undemonstrable, indeed mere *fictions*. Whoever so judges *mingles* the two distinguished operations; he *substitutes* for the *presentation of a mere presentation* the (indeed impossible) *qualitative modification* of this presentation.

The eighth paragraph affirms generic community via conform modification, and that an objectivating quality belongs to every act. We believe we may assume, as regards the qualities coordinated by *conform modification*, a *generic community*; and we hold it correct that one or the other of these qualities *belongs to all acts* out of which the unity of any qualitatively unmodified or modified judgment essentially builds itself, indifferently whether we look to the acts of mere *meaning-intention* or to those of *meaning-fulfillment*. (A footnote refers to *Ideen I* on the interpretation of "generic community" as a peculiar relation of "essence and counter-essence," and on the neutrality-modification.)

The ninth paragraph clarifies that the mere presentation of an act is itself the counterpart not of that act but of a positing presentation of it. It is self-evident that those *mere presentations of quite arbitrary acts* — which we distinguished above from the qualitative counterparts possible only with *positing* acts — are, *as mere presentations*, themselves such *counterparts*: only they are so not to their *originary* acts (which are rather their *presentation-objects*). The mere presentation of a *wish* is *not* the counterpart of the *wish*, but of *some positing act referring to it* — e.g., a *perception* of the wish. This *pair*, perception and mere presentation of the wish, is of *one genus*: both are *objectivating* acts; whereas the *wish itself* and its perception (or its imagination, or some other presentation referring to it) are of *different genus*.

### **§40. Continuation. Qualitative and imaginative modification**

The first paragraph weighs the terms "holding-true" and "imagining" acts. It lies very near to designate the *positing* acts as *holding-true* acts, their counterparts as *imagining* (einbildende) acts. Both expressions, however much may at first seem to speak for them, have their *reservations*, opposing especially the terminological fixing of the latter.

The second paragraph notes the over-extension of "holding-true." Of a *holding-true* the whole logical tradition speaks only with *judgments* (assertion-meanings). Now, however, *all* perceptions, memories, expectations — all acts of express-nominal *positing* — would be designated as *holdings-true*.

The third paragraph notes the difficulties with "imagination" and rejects its sense as fiction or false meaning. As for the word "*imagination*" (Einbildung), in usual speech it means a *non-positing* act; but it would have to *extend* its originary sense beyond the sphere of sensory imagination so far that its scope comprised *all* possible counterparts of holdings-true. On the other hand the word would also need *restriction*, insofar as the thought must remain excluded that imaginations are either conscious *fictions*, or *objectless* presentations, or even *false meanings*. We often enough receive what is *narrated* without deciding in any way on truth or falsehood. And even reading a *novel*, it is normally no different. We *know* it is an aesthetic *fiction*; but this knowledge remains, in the pure aesthetic effect, *out of action*. All *expressions* are in these cases — both on the side of meaning-intentions and of the arising phantasy-fulfillments — *bearers of positing-less acts*, of "imaginations" (in the sense of the considered terminology).

The fourth paragraph clarifies that the *judgments* in fiction undergo *qualitative* modification, not phantasy. This concerns also the *whole assertions*. The judgments are indeed in a certain way *accomplished*, but they do not have the character of *real* judgments; we do not *believe*, but neither do we deny or doubt, what is narrated; without any holding-true we let it affect us, accomplishing — instead of real judgments — mere "imaginations." But this talk may *not* be understood, as it just suggests, as though *in place of the real judgments phantasy-judgments* should step. Rather, instead of the *judgment* as the "holding-true" of its state of affairs, we accomplish the *qualitative modification*, the *neutral putting-forward* of the same state of affairs, which by no means is to be *identified* with a *phantasying* of this state of affairs.

The fifth paragraph rejects "imagination" as a term because positing/imagining and non-positing/non-imagining do not coincide. The name *imagination* is burdened with a drawback that *seriously* stands in the way of its terminological introduction: it points to an *imaginative* apprehension (a phantasy-apprehension, or one pictorial in the more proper sense), whereas we can *by no means* say all *non-positing* acts are *imagining*, all *positing* acts *non-imagining*. At least the latter is at once clear. E.g., an imagined sensory object can stand over against us equally well in the manner of *positing* (as existent) and in the modified manner (as imagined). And it can do this even while the *representative content* of its intuition *remains identical* — that, namely, which confers on the intuition not only the determinacy of relation to *this* object, but at the same time the character of an *imaginative representation* re-presenting the object in the manner of the phantasy-presentation or the depicting presentation. E.g., the appearance-content of a *painting* with its painted figures remains the same, whether we take them as presentations of *real* objects or let them affect us *purely aesthetically, without positing*.

The sixth paragraph queries whether perception too admits qualitative modification, with the stereoscopic example. Whether the parallel situation occurs *in purity* with *normal perception* appears doubtful — namely whether perception, with complete identity of its other phenomenological subsistence, can be *qualitatively modified* and so forfeit its normal *positing-character*; it is questionable whether the *perceptive* apprehension of the object as a "*self*" (and bodily) present one does not at once pass over into a *pure image-apprehension*, in which the object, analogously to the case of normal perceptive *imageness* (paintings), appears as *pictorial* and no longer as *self*\-given. Yet one can point here to various *sensory semblances*, like *stereoscopic* phenomena, which one can quite well take as "mere phenomena" (like aesthetic objects, without position-taking) and at the same time yet *as themselves* and not as images *for* something else. However, it suffices that the perception *can pass over into a corresponding imageness* (hence into an act harboring the same matter in itself, though in *different apprehension-form*) — also *without* change of its positing-character.

The seventh paragraph distinguishes the *two* conform modifications: qualitative and imaginative. We see that here *two kinds of conform modifications* can be distinguished: the *qualitative* and the *imaginative*. In both the *matter* remains *unchanged*. With identity of matter, it is precisely *not only* the *quality* that can still change in the act. Quality and matter we grasped as the "thoroughly essential," because *meaningful* and inseparable from any act; but we pointed out from the outset that still *other* moments are distinguishable in the acts. Precisely these come into consideration — as the next investigation will show more exactly — for the differences between *non-intuitive objectivation* and *intuition*, and again between *perception* and *imagination*.

The eighth paragraph treats the terminology of "judgment" as a logical-interest question. Once the descriptive relations are clarified, it is evidently a merely *terminological* dispute whether one restricts the word *judgment* (as we do, in the tradition's sense) to the (unmodified) *assertion-meanings*, or accords it the *whole sphere of belief* as its domain of application. That in the first case no "basic class" of acts — indeed not even a *lowest qualitative difference* — is fully spanned (insofar as the *matter*, to which on our concept both the "is" and the "is not" belong, is co-determining for the delimitation) does *nothing to the matter*. Since *judgment* is a *logical* term, only the logical interest and logical tradition have to decide what concept is to give it meaning. In this respect one will have to say that so fundamental a concept as that of the (ideal) *assertion-meaning* — as which it is the last unity to which everything logical must be referred back — must keep its natural and ancestral expression. The term *judgment-act* would thus be *restricted* to the corresponding act-kinds: the *meaning-intentions* of complete assertions and the *fulfillments* adapted to them and possessing the same meaning-essence. To designate *all* positing acts as judgments has the tendency to *veil* the essential difference separating the nominal and propositional acts (despite all qualitative community), and thereby to confuse a series of important relations.

The ninth paragraph extends the terminological reflection to "presentation" and leaves the choice open. Similarly with the term *presentation*. What logic should understand by it must be decided by its own need. Certainly account is then to be taken of the *excluding separation* between presentation and judgment, and of the circumstance that presentation wants to count as something possibly *building up* the full judgment. Whether one should then adopt that concept of presentation which *Bolzano*, comprising *all possible part-meanings of logical judgments*, laid at the basis of his treatment of the doctrine of science; or whether one should restrict oneself to the relatively self-sufficient meanings of this kind (phenomenologically: the closed judgment-members, especially the *nominal acts*); or further, whether one must rather, preferring another direction of division, grasp as presentations the mere *representation* (i.e. the total content of the respective acts remaining after abstraction from quality, containing in itself, of the intentional essence, only the *matter*) — these are *difficult questions, not to be decided at this place*.

### **§41. New interpretation of the proposition of presentation as basis of all acts. The objectivating act as primary bearer of the matter**

The first paragraph states the new interpretation. A number of researchers, in older and newer times, grasp the term *presentation* so widely that, with the "merely presenting" acts, it also comprises the "holding-true" acts and especially the *judgments* — in short, the *total sphere of objectivating acts*. Laying at the basis this important concept (imprinting a *closed quality-genus*), the proposition of the *presentation-basis* gains (as already announced) a *new and especially significant* sense, of which the previous one (building on the *nominal* concept of presentation) is merely a *secondary branch*. We may say: *Every intentional experience is either an objectivating act or has such an act as its "basis"* — i.e., it then has an *objectivating act* necessarily as a *component* in itself, whose *total matter* is at the same time, and indeed *individually identical*, its *total matter*.

The second paragraph justifies the term "objectivating act." All that we said in unfolding the sense of the still-unclear proposition we can claim here almost word for word, and thereby at the same time give the term *objectivating act* its *justification*. For since *no act* — or rather no *act-quality* not itself of the objectivating kind — can *appropriate its matter* except by means of an objectivating act interwoven with it into *one* unified act, the objectivating acts have precisely the *unique function* of making *present, first of all*, to all other acts the *objectivity* to which these are to relate in their new ways. The relation to an objectivity constitutes itself in general *in the matter*. But every *matter* is, our law says, *matter of an objectivating act*, and can only by means of such become matter of a *new* act-quality founded in it.

The third paragraph distinguishes *primary* and *secondary* intentions and notes the indifference of positing/non-positing. We have, so to speak, to distinguish *primary* and *secondary intentions*, of which the latter owe their *intentionality* only to the *founding* by the former. Whether, moreover, the primary objectivating acts have the character of the *positing* (holding-true, believing) or *non-positing* ("merely presenting," neutral) is *indifferent* for this function. Some secondary acts thoroughly *demand* holdings-true — e.g., joy and sorrow; for others mere *modifications* suffice — e.g., for the *wish*, for the *aesthetic feeling*. Very often the underlying objectivating act is a *complexion* comprising acts of *both* kinds.

### **§42. Further expositions. Fundamental propositions for complex acts**

The first paragraph states the qualitative complexity and foundedness of composite acts. To illuminate the remarkable situation more closely, Husserl adds the following. Every *composite* act is *eo ipso qualitatively complex*; it has as many qualities (whether of different or of the same species or difference) as there are individual acts distinguishable in it. Every composite act is further a *founded* act: its *total quality* is not a mere *sum* of the qualities of the part-acts but a quality whose *unity* is founded in these building-up qualities — just as the unity of the *total matter* is not a mere sum of the matters of the part-acts but, where a *distribution* of the matter over the part-acts takes place at all, *founded* in the part-matters. But there are essential *differences* in the way an act is qualitatively complex and founded in other acts, with respect to the various ways in which the qualities relate to one another, to the unified total matter, and to the eventual part-matters, and in which they gain unity through various elementary foundings.

The second paragraph states the law that every act must contain an objectivating quality. An act can be complex in such a way that its complex total quality is *decomposable into several qualities*, each of which has *one and the same matter* individually-identically in common — so, e.g., in the *joy over a fact*, the complexion of the specific quality of *joy* and that of the *holding-true* in which the fact is present to us. One might think that *each* of these qualities, with the exception of a *single arbitrary* one among them, could *fall away* while still a concretely complete act *remained*. One might further think that qualities of *arbitrary genus* could be connected with a single matter in the indicated manner. Our *law* says that all this is *not possible*: rather, in every such complexion and in every act whatever, an *act-quality of the genus of the objectivating* must *necessarily* be present, because a *matter* is not realizable at all except as matter of an *objectivating* act.

The third paragraph: non-objectivating qualities are always founded in objectivating ones; the detachability of a full objectivating act. Qualities of *other* genus are consequently *always founded in objectivating qualities*; never can they be connected with a matter *immediately and for themselves alone*. Where they appear, the total act is necessarily a qualitatively *many-formed* one (containing qualities of different quality-genera); and more closely, such that from it a *full objectivating act* is always (one-sidedly) *detachable*, which possesses the total matter of the total act also as *its* total matter. Acts that are *one-formed* in the corresponding sense need not, by the way, be *simple*. All *one-formed* acts are *objectivating*, and we may even *reverse*: all objectivating acts are one-formed — but objectivating acts can still be *complex*. The matters of the part-acts are then mere *parts* of the matter of the total act; in it the total matter constitutes itself in that to the part-acts belong *parts* of the matter, and to the unified element of the total quality belongs the unified element of the total matter. (The division can be an *explicit articulation*; but there can also occur, within *nominalized* matters, an *implied* articulation of every form otherwise admissible in free syntheses.)

The fourth paragraph illustrates with the assertion-sentence and restates the one-quality law. Every *assertion-sentence* offers a relevant example, whether it functions in *normal* meaning (as asserting) or in *modified* meaning. To the *members* there correspond underlying part-acts with part-matters; to the *connecting forms* (the "is" or "is not," the "if" and "so," the "and," the "or") there correspond *founded* act-characters, but at the same time *founded moments of the total matter*. In all this complexion the act is *one-formed*; we find also *only one objectivating quality* belonging to the total matter; and *more than one objectivating quality cannot be referred to a single matter taken as a whole*.

The fifth paragraph derives *many-formedness* from one-formedness. Out of such one-formedness *many-formedness* now grows — be it in that the objectivating total act *connects with novel qualities* referring to the total matter, or in that the new qualities *adjoin merely single part-acts* (as when, on the basis of a unified articulated intuition, *liking* sets in regarding the one member, *disliking* regarding the other). Conversely, it is self-evident that in *every* complex act containing act-qualities of *non-objectivating* kind (grounded however — on the total matter or on its parts), these act-qualities can all be, so to speak, *struck out*; there then remains a *full objectivating act* still containing the *whole matter* of the original act in itself.

The sixth paragraph derives that the *last-founding* acts are simple nominal acts. A further consequence of the lawfulness here prevailing is that the *last-founding* acts of *every* complex act (or the last-*implied* in the nominal members) must be *objectivating* acts. These are all of the kind of the *nominal* acts; and indeed the last-implied members are in every respect *simple* nominal acts, simple *connections of a simple quality with a simple matter*. We can also pronounce the proposition that *all simple acts are nominal*. Naturally the *converse* does not hold; not all nominal acts are simple. As soon as in an objectivating act an *articulated matter* appears, there is found in it also a *categorial form*; and to all categorial forms it is essential to constitute themselves in *founded acts*, as will be discussed more exactly (in the Sixth Investigation).

The seventh paragraph notes that "matter" here may be read as "representation." In the foregoing and following expositions, one need not understand by *matter* the mere *abstract moment* of the intentional essence; one could substitute for it the *whole* of the act, only under abstraction from the *quality* — what we shall call in the next investigation the *representation* — and everything essential would still stand.

### **§43. Retrospect on the earlier interpretation of the treated proposition**

The first paragraph explains why the nominal interpretation is a secondary consequence of the objectivating one. One now also understands why we could assert above that *Brentano's proposition interpreted on the basis of the nominal concept of presentation is a mere secondary consequence of the same proposition in the new interpretation*. If every act not itself (or not purely) objectivating is *founded in objectivating acts*, then it must of course ultimately be founded also in *nominal* acts. For every objectivating act is, as we discussed, either *simple* (hence *eo ipso nominal*) or *composite* (hence founded in simple, i.e. again *nominal*, acts).

The second paragraph contrasts the two interpretations and the two founding-kinds they mingle. The new interpretation is evidently *much more significant*, because only with it do the essential basic relations receive a *pure imprint*. In the *other* interpretation — though it asserts nothing incorrect — *two fundamentally different founding-kinds* are *mingled or crossed*: (1) the founding of *non-objectivating* acts (joys, wishes, willings) *in objectivating ones* (presentations, holdings-true), where *primarily* an act-quality is founded in *another act-quality*, and only *mediately* in a *matter*; (2) the founding of *objectivating acts in other objectivating acts*, where *primarily* an *act-matter* is founded in *other act-matters* (e.g., that of a predicative assertion in those of the founding nominal acts).

## **The third paragraph identifies the *different sources* of the universal nominal foundation. For so we can also view the matter: the circumstance that no matter is possible without objectivating quality must itself have the consequence that, where a matter is founded in other matters, an *objectivating act* of the first matter is also founded in *just such acts* of the latter matters. Thus the *fact that every act is at all times founded in nominal acts* has *different sources*. The *original source* lies everywhere in this: that every *simple* matter (including no further *material* founding) is a *nominal* one, and accordingly every *last-founding objectivating* act is a *nominal* one. But since all *other-kinded* act-qualities are founded in objectivating ones, the *last founding* by nominal acts transfers from the objectivating to *all acts whatever*.**

## **Chapter Six: Compilation of the Most Important Equivocations of the Terms Presentation and Content**

### **§44. "Presentation"**

The first paragraph announces the enumeration. In the last chapters we struck upon a *fourfold* (or fivefold) equivocation of the word *presentation*.

The second paragraph gives the first sense: matter / representation. *(1) Presentation as act-matter*; or, as we can also say in obvious completion, presentation as the *representation* underlying the act — i.e., as the *full content* of the act *excluding the quality*. This concept too figured in our expositions, though, given our special interest in the relation of quality and matter, it mattered to emphasize the *latter*. The *matter* says, as it were, *which* object is meant in the act, and with *what sense* it is thereby meant; the *representation*, however, also draws in the moments lying *outside* the intentional essence, which make it the case that the object is meant precisely in the manner of perceptive or imaginative *intuition*, or of mere non-intuitive *meaning*. (Comprehensive analyses of this follow in the first section of the Sixth Investigation.)

The third paragraph gives the second sense: "mere presentation" (qualitative modification of belief). *(2) Presentation as "mere presentation,"* as *qualitative modification* of some form of "belief" — e.g., as mere sentence-understanding, without inner decision in assent or rejection, without surmise or doubt.

The fourth paragraph gives the third sense: nominal act. *(3) Presentation as nominal act* — e.g., as subject-presentation of an assertion-act.

The fifth paragraph gives the fourth sense: objectivating act. *(4) Presentation as objectivating act* — in the sense of the act-class necessarily represented in every complete act, because every *matter* (or representation) must *primarily* be given as matter (representation) of such an act. This qualitative "basic class" comprises both the acts of *belief* (nominal and propositional) and their "*counterparts*," so that all presentations in the second and third senses above belong here too.

The sixth paragraph defers the systematic analysis and announces the further list. The closer analysis of these concepts of *presentation* (and the experiences they comprise), and the definitive establishment of their relation to one another, must remain the task of *further phenomenological* investigations. What Husserl wishes only to attempt here is an *appending* of *other* equivocations of the term — sharply keeping them apart being of fundamental importance for the logico-epistemological efforts. The phenomenological analyses forming the indispensable presuppositions for resolving these equivocations have so far been learned only partly in extenso; but what is still missing has been repeatedly touched and mostly so far indicated that the main points can be briefly designated. The enumeration continues.

The seventh paragraph gives the fifth sense: presenting (intuition) opposed to mere thinking (concept). *(5)* *Presenting* is often opposed to mere *thinking*. Decisive is then the same difference designated also as the opposition of *intuition* and *concept*. Of an *ellipsoid* I have a presentation, of a *Kummer surface* not; but through suitable drawings, models, or theoretically guided movements of phantasy I can win a presentation of it too. A *round square*, a *regular icosahedron* (twenty-faced) and similar a priori *impossibilities* are in this sense "unpresentable." Likewise a completely bounded piece of a *Euclidean manifold* of more than three dimensions, the number π, and similar formations free of all incompatibility. In all these cases of unpresentability "*mere concepts*" are given us — more exactly, we have *nominal expressions* enlivened by *meaning-intentions* in which the meant objects are "*thought*" in more or less indeterminate manner (especially in the indeterminate attributive form "an A," as mere *bearers* of definitely named attributes). To mere thinking is opposed "*presenting*": evidently the *intuition* conferring *fulfillment* — and indeed *adequate* fulfillment — on the mere meaning-intention. To *present something to oneself* now means: to *procure for oneself a corresponding intuition* of what was merely *thought* (hence meant, but at best only very inadequately *illustrated*).

The eighth paragraph gives the sixth sense: imagination opposed to perception (ordinary usage). *(6)* A very ordinary concept of presentation concerns the *opposition of imagination to perception*. This concept prevails in ordinary speech. If I *see* St. Peter's, I do not *present* it. But I do present it if I re-present it in the "*memory-image*," or have it before my eyes in the painted, drawn image.

The ninth paragraph gives the seventh sense: the physical image and the image-object. *(7)* Presentation was just now the concrete act of *imagination*. On closer view, the *image* as *physical thing* is also called presentation of the depicted, as in "this photograph presents St. Peter's." *Presentation* then further also means the *image-object* appearing here (as distinct from the *image-subject*, the *depicted object*): the thing appearing here in the photographic colors is *not* the photographed church (image-subject) but only *presents* it. These equivocations transfer to the "*imageness*" of the simple re-presentations in *memory* or *mere phantasy*. Naively, the appearing of the phantasied as such in the experience is interpreted like a *really being-inside* of an image in consciousness; the appearing, in the *how* of its appearance-manner, counts as an *inner image* and so, like a painted image, as "presentation" for the phantasied thing. One does not make clear that the inner "image," and its manner of "presenting" one and the same thing with other possible images, constitutes itself *intentionally* and cannot itself count as a *real moment* of the phantasy-experience. (A footnote refers to the critique of the image-theory in the Appendix.)

The tenth paragraph gives the eighth sense: representation as both prompting and proxy (sign). *(8)* In the equivocal talk of presentation, in all cases where an *image-relation* is supposed, the following thought also plays an effective role. The (often very inadequate) image "*represents*" the thing and at the same time *reminds* of it, is for it a *sign* — the latter such that it proves suited to *draw in* a *direct and more content-rich* presentation of it. The *photograph reminds* of the original and is at the same time its *representative*, in a certain way its *proxy*. Its image-presentation makes possible various judgments otherwise to be passed on the basis of perception of the original. Similarly there often functions a *sign content-foreign* to the thing — e.g., an *algebraic symbol*. It *arouses* the presentation of the signified (even an *intuitionless* one, like an integral), leads our thoughts to it; at the same time the sign can function "*representatively*," as *proxy*, in mathematical operations (one operates with it additively, multiplicatively, as if in it the symbolized were directly given). This way of speaking is rather crude, but it imprints the conception decisive for the talk of *presentation* in this sense — according to which *presentation* means as much as *representation* in the *double sense* of *presentation-prompting* and *proxy-standing* (Vorstellungsanregung and Stellvertretung). So the mathematician, drawing at the board, says "OX presents the asymptote of the hyperbola"; or, calculating, "x presents the root of the equation f(x) \= 0." In general the *sign* — whether *image-sign* or *name-sign* — is called "presentation" of the signified.

The eleventh paragraph distinguishes the present (object-level) representation from the primitive (content-level) one. The present talk of *representation* (which we do not wish to fix terminologically) refers to *objects*. These "representing objects" constitute themselves in certain acts and receive, through certain new acts of *out-pointing* presenting, the character of "*representatives*" for new objects. *Another and more primitive* sense of representation is the one indicated under point (1), where the representatives are *experienced contents* that undergo, in the representation, *objectivating apprehension*, and in this way (without themselves becoming objective) help an object become present to us.

The twelfth paragraph gives the ninth sense, and the confusion of perception/imagination with sensation/phantasm. *(9)* The difference between *perception* and *imagination* (the latter itself again showing significant descriptive differences) is repeatedly *mingled* with the difference between *sensations* and *phantasms*. The former is a difference of *acts*, the latter a difference of *non-acts* — namely of *experienced contents* which, in acts of perceiving or phantasying, receive *apprehension*. (If one wishes to call all contents representing in this sense *sensations*, one would have to distinguish terminologically between *impressive* and *reproductive* sensations.) Whether there are *essential* descriptive differences between sensations and phantasms at all — whether the usually adduced differences of vivacity, steadiness, fleetingness suffice, or whether one must recur to the two-sided *manner of consciousness* — cannot be entered into here. At all events it is certain that the (eventual) content-differences do *not* already make up the difference between *perception* and *imagination*, which is rather, as analysis teaches with indubitable clarity, a difference *of acts as such*. We cannot think of regarding the descriptively given in perception or phantasy as the mere *complexion* of the experienced sensations or phantasms. This too-ordinary mingling conditions that one understands by *presentation* now the *phantasy-presentation* (in senses 6 and 7), now the corresponding *phantasm* (the complexion of the representing contents of the phantasy-imageness), whence a new equivocation grows.

The thirteenth paragraph gives the tenth sense: the presented object called presentation. *(10)* By virtue of the *confusion* between the *appearance* (e.g., the concrete phantasy-experience or the "phantasy-image") and the *appearing*, the *presented object* too is called *presentation*. Likewise with perceptions, and so in general with presentations in the sense of mere or logically grasped intuitions — e.g., "the world is my presentation."

The fourteenth paragraph gives the eleventh sense: all consciousness-contents (Lockean ideas). *(11)* The opinion that all *consciousness-experiences* (contents in the real phenomenological sense) are conscious in the sense of *inner perception* or some other inner turning-toward (consciousness-ness, originary apperception), and that with this turning-toward *eo ipso* a *presentation* is given (consciousness or the ego *sets the content before itself*), led to designating *all consciousness-contents* as *presentations*. These are the *ideas* of English empiricist philosophy since Locke (with Hume, *perceptions*). To *have a presentation* and to *experience a content* are often used as equivalent expressions.

The fifteenth paragraph gives the twelfth: the logical concepts, especially Bolzano's "presentation in itself." *(12)* Within *logic* it is of great importance to keep the *specifically logical* concepts of presentation apart from others. That several concepts come into question here we have already mentioned in passing. As not yet touched, the *Bolzano* concept of "*presentation in itself*" should be specially named again — which we interpreted as *every self-sufficient or non-self-sufficient part-meaning within a full assertion*. With respect to all purely logical concepts of presentation, one must on the one hand distinguish the *ideal* from the *real* (e.g., the nominal presentation in the purely logical sense from the *acts* in which it *realizes* itself); on the other hand the mere *meaning-intentions* from the *experiences* offering them more or less adequate *fulfillment* (i.e., the presentations in the sense of *intuitions*).

The sixteenth paragraph gives the thirteenth: presentation as opinion (doxa). *(13)* Besides the enumerated equivocations — whose harmfulness everyone must experience who seriously immerses himself in the phenomenology of thought-experiences — there are probably still others, partly less considerable. Mentioned, e.g., may be the talk of presentation in the sense of *opinion* (δόξα). It is an equivocation that has grown through near-lying *transferences*, as we find with all related terms. Recall the verbally manifold but ever equivalent turn: "it is a widespread opinion, presentation, view, intuition, conception."

### **§45. "Presentation-content"**

The first paragraph notes the correlative ambiguity of "content." Self-evidently the expressions correlative to "presentation" are correspondingly *ambiguous*. This concerns especially the talk of "*what a presentation presents*," i.e., of the "*content*" of the presentation. That the mere distinction between *content* and *object* of the presentation — as *Twardowski*, following *Zimmermann*, advocated — does *not remotely suffice* (though it was meritorious to press here for firm distinctions at all) is clear already from the foregoing analyses. In the *logical* sphere (which these authors, without consciousness of the restriction, have in view), besides the *named object*, not merely *one* thing is to be distinguished as "content"; rather, *several* can and must be distinguished.

The second paragraph enumerates the senses of "content." Above all, by *content* (e.g., of the nominal presentation) the *meaning* as *ideal unity* can be meant: the presentation in a purely logical sense. To it there corresponds, as *real moment* in the *real content* of the presentation-act, the *intentional essence* with *presentation-quality* and *matter*. Further we distinguish, in the real content, the *detachable* components not belonging to the intentional essence: the "contents" which in the act-consciousness (the intentional essence) receive their *apprehension* — i.e., the *sensations* and *phantasms*. To these come, with some presentations, the again-ambiguous distinctions of *form* and *content*; especially important there is the distinction of *matter* (in a totally new sense) and *categorial form*, with which we shall have much to occupy ourselves. Connected with this is, e.g., the not-itself-univocal talk of the *content of concepts*: content \= aggregate of the "marks," distinguished from their *connection-form*.

The third paragraph illustrates Twardowski's difficulties. How questionable the unified talk of *content* is — in mere opposition of *act, content, object* — is shown by the difficulties and aberrations into which Twardowski falls: so in his talk of the "presentation-activity moving in a *double direction*," in his complete *overlooking* of the *meaning* in the ideal sense, in his *psychologistic* dissipation of evident meaning-differences by recourse to differences of *etyma*, in his treatment of the doctrine of "intentional in-existence" and the doctrine of *general objects*.

The fourth paragraph is the closing *Note* defending the presentation-act against its deniers. In recent times the view has often been expressed that between *presenting* and *presented content* there is no difference, or at least none phenomenologically demonstrable. How one positions oneself on this depends, of course, on what one understands by these words *presenting* and *content*. Whoever interprets them through the mere *having* of sensations and phantasms — and *overlooks* the phenomenological moment of *apprehension*, or does not reckon with it — certainly says rightly: there is no proper *act* of presenting; presenting and the presented are one and the same. That *mere having* of the content, as a mere *experiencing* of the experience, is *no intentional experience* (relating to an objective precisely through an apprehension-sense), and specifically also no *inner perceiving*; hence we too *identified* sensation and sensation-content. But can anyone who has ever kept the various concepts of presentation apart doubt that so-delimited a concept *cannot be held fast* — and has never been held fast — and that it has grown only through *misinterpretation* of the more original, *intentional* concepts of presentation? However the concept *presentation* may be determined, all are agreed that thereby a concept decisive not only for *psychology* but also for the *critique of knowledge* and *logic* (especially pure logic) is meant. Whoever concedes this and yet lays at the basis the above-designated concept has *eo ipso* already fallen into the *mingling*. For in epistemology and pure logic *this* concept has *no function* at all.

The fifth paragraph closes with the example against Ehrenfels. Only from the mingling can Husserl explain how so otherwise acute a researcher as *von Ehrenfels* occasionally meant that we cannot do without the assumption of a presentation-*act* distinct from the presentation-*content*, chiefly because otherwise we could give no psychological difference between the presentation of an object A and the presentation *of a presentation* of it — but that he had never been able to *directly* convince himself of the existence of that phenomenon. Husserl would say that a presentation-act *as such* becomes *directly intuitive* to us precisely where we *phenomenologically establish* this difference between *presentation* and *presentation of this presentation*. Were there no such cases, no argument in all the world could *indirectly* ground the justification of such a distinction. Likewise we have *directly established* the existence of a presentation-*act* when *we make clear to ourselves the difference between a mere sound-formation and the same sound-formation as an understood name*.

# ***Basic Problems of Phenomenology*** **(Winter Semester 1910/11)**

*Text Nr. 6 of Husserliana XIII, "Zur Phänomenologie der Intersubjektivität, Erster Teil."* 

## **First Chapter: The Natural Attitude and the "Natural Concept of the World"**

### **§ 1\. The I in the Natural Attitude**

Husserl opens by announcing the program of the semester: the course will occupy itself with the basic problems of a general phenomenology of consciousness, and it will study, in its main features, the fundamental constitution (*Grundverfassung*) of consciousness as such. This first sentence is deceptively modest. It already commits the lectures to two decisions that organize everything that follows. First, the object is "consciousness" taken quite generally, not this or that mental faculty, not perception or judgment in isolation, but the overall structural form of conscious life. Second, the aim is to grasp the *Grundverfassung*, the constitution or basic make-up of that life — its essential architecture rather than a catalogue of contents. The word "phenomenology" is used without fanfare, as though its sense were settled; but as the synopsis will immediately warn, Husserl intends in this course to keep open a question about what kind of phenomenology is in play, and not to assume in advance that it is the eidetic doctrine of essences he had been developing.

An editorial footnote attached to the title records the textual situation: what survives are only the manuscript sheets of the first weeks of the course (October–November 1910), to which classroom discussions were attached, and that thereafter Husserl lectured freely, without a written manuscript. This note matters for how one reads the whole. The careful, written opening chapters give way, in the surviving record, to a text whose later portions were reconstructed or left in a more provisional state, and it explains why the early sections on the natural attitude are so meticulously composed while the methodological core arrives with the density of working notes.

Husserl then inserts a synopsis of the course's whole arc — a compressed table of its leading moves. The point of departure is the natural concept of the world, taken as the starting point for a theory of knowledge, from which the very possibility of a phenomenology is to be raised. He flags at once a terminological caution: "phenomenology" here is *not* taken from the outset as a phenomenological doctrine of essence (*Wesenslehre*); rather the experiment is made of asking whether an *experiencing* phenomenology — one that is not a doctrine of essence — is possible at all. The synopsis then names the chain of problems to come: the evidence of the *ego cogito* in relation to the evidence of the unity of the stream of consciousness, hence the givenness of the phenomenological field; transcendence within immanence and the several distinct concepts of transcendence; the right (*Recht*, the warrant or legitimacy) of transcendent positing within immanence; the warrant of recollection and of expectation. He singles out as especially important the warrant of intentional relations and of expectation-intentions, by which an empirical transcendent positing is *turned* into systematic connections of consciousness. Then: objectivity as an index for transcendental subjectivity, and the warrant of "empirical" cognition within the sphere of consciousness. The phenomenological (and pointedly *not* eidetic) reduction thereby yields the possibility of going, within the subjective — the transcendental-subjective — beyond what is merely actually impressional, beyond the live now-content. The same is then applied to empathy (*Einfühlung*), with first elaborations; and transcendental reduction, understood as the switching-off of physical nature, yields besides the ego also the other ego and its stream — whence a doctrine of monads (*Monadenlehre*) and of the connection of monads. He notes a sharp asymmetry that will become decisive: recollection gives the self, whereas empathizing presentification and the memory of another's present do *not* give the self. And finally, as especially important, the essence of the unified phenomenological I: how one stream of consciousness phenomenologically closes itself off against every other — the principle of unity. This synopsis is, in effect, the skeleton of the entire 1910/11 breakthrough toward intersubjectivity, and it is worth reading slowly because nearly every later section is the unfolding of one of its clauses.

The lecture proper now begins. The investigations to be conducted, Husserl says, require a *totally different* attitude from the natural one within which natural-scientific and psychological knowledge is won. He states the cardinal thesis flatly: phenomenology is by no means psychology. It lies in a new dimension; it demands an essentially different attitude than psychology, than every science of spatio-temporal existence (*Dasein*). To make this out, an introduction is needed. The force of "new dimension" should not be softened. Husserl is not saying phenomenology studies a different region of the same world (the mental rather than the physical); he is saying it requires a change of *attitude* so radical that it cannot be located within the framework that all worldly sciences, psychology included, share. The introduction he promises is precisely the long descriptive detour through the natural attitude that occupies this first chapter: one must first see clearly what one is stepping out of.

He announces his method for that detour: he begins with a description of the various attitudes in which experience and knowledge can take place, and first of all of the natural attitude — the attitude in which we all live and from which we therefore set out when we carry through the philosophical change of regard (*Blickänderung*). The procedure is to describe, in a general way, the *Vorfindlichkeiten* of this attitude — the things one simply finds before oneself, the data of pre-philosophical life. Two methodological commitments are visible here. The "change of regard" is a redirection of the gaze rather than the discovery of a new object; and the description proceeds by laying out what is *found* (vor-gefunden), staying with the naïve self-understanding of natural life before any critique of it.

Husserl then describes that natural self-understanding from the inside. Each of us says "I" and, in so speaking, knows himself as an I; as such he finds himself before himself (*findet sich vor*), and finds himself always as the center of an environment (*Umgebung*). "I" means for each of us something different — for each the wholly determinate person bearing the determinate proper name, who lives through his perceptions, memories, expectations, fantasy-presentations, feelings, wishes, and volitions; who has his states and performs his acts; and further who has his dispositions, his innate predispositions, his acquired capacities and skills. Each I has its own such inventory. And — a subtle point — into this same circle belongs also the respective *finding-itself*, the very act of coming upon this and that, of which we are here speaking in general terms; the finding is itself part of what is found. Likewise the asserting belongs here: on the basis of an immediate finding of so-called experience, and on the basis of convictions, opinions, and conjectures which, whatever their origin, are *lived-experience* (*Erlebnis*) to the I, the I predicates of itself that it is the human being so-and-so named, the one who *has* such-and-such personal properties, actual experiences, opinions, purpose-settings, and so on. Husserl notes that the "having" differs according to what is had: a pain is *suffered*, a judgment is *performed*, while competence in life, fidelity, and truthfulness are *had* as "personal" properties. The descriptive richness here is doing real work: it shows that within the natural attitude the I appears as a thing among the things it possesses, an item in the world that "has" experiences the way it has a name.

But then comes the pivot that gives the section its philosophical edge. If the I finds itself as the haver, in various ways, of all that assertible content, it finds itself *on the other hand not as something of the same kind as what is had* (nicht als etwas diesem Gehabten Gleichartiges). The I itself is not a lived-experience but the experiencer; not an act but that which performs the act; not a character-trait but that which has the character-trait as a property. Husserl is marking, already within the natural description, a categorial difference between the I and everything that belongs to it as content — the difference between the having subject and the had. He adds the temporal dimension: the I finds itself and its I-experiences and dispositions *in time*, and knows itself not only as being now and having this and that, but also, through memory, as the *same* I who "just now" and in earlier time had these and those determinate experiences. Each thing had, and each thing had-had, has its time-place; and the I itself is an identical one *in* time, occupying a determinate position within it. With this the natural-attitudinal portrait of the I is complete in outline: a temporally persisting, self-identical center, set over against the stream of its experiences, properties, and acts, yet found as a worldly person bearing a name. The categorial non-likeness of the I to its contents, and its identity through time, are the two findings Husserl will carry forward.

### **§ 2\. The Lived-Body and the Spatio-Temporal Environment**

Husserl now turns the descriptive gaze from the I to the lived-body (*Leib*) and the spatio-temporality that surrounds it. Each I finds itself as *having* an organic lived-body. The lived-body, for its part, is no I but a spatio-temporal "thing," around which a thingly environment groups itself, extending off into the unlimited. At any given moment the I has a *limited* spatio-temporal environment which it immediately perceives, or of which it is immediately aware in retentional memory. But — and this is the decisive observation — each I "knows," is certain, that this environment posited as existing in immediate intuition is only the *intuited piece* of a total environment; it knows that the things continue on in endless space (Husserl writes "Euclidean," a word he later struck, presumably because it imported a determinate geometry into a description that should remain pre-theoretical), and likewise that the actually remembered stretch of existence is only a piece of an endless chain reaching back into an infinite past and forward into an endless future. The I knows that things are not *only* when they are perceived and did not exist *only* when they were perceived: things that are there are, were, and will be *in themselves* (*an sich*), even without being present in the actual experiential environment or recalled in it. And this holds for all of a thing's thingly properties — its rest and motion, its qualitative change and persistence. What Husserl is describing is the natural attitude's implicit metaphysics: the world is given as an in-itself that vastly overflows any actual perception, an infinite spatio-temporal horizon whose existence is simply taken for granted. He is not yet criticizing this; he is recording it as the deepest *Vorfindlichkeit* of natural life.

A methodological reminder follows, and it must be carefully noted: we are describing *only* what each I as such finds before it — what it directly sees or indirectly means with certainty — and this certainty is meant to be such that each I can transform it into an absolute evidence (again "absolute" is a word Husserl later deleted, softening the claim). Each I knows that in the singular case it can err, while the *universal* expressed in assertions of the highlighted kind is, or can become, evident to it. As describers, Husserl insists, we are not at all concerned at present with how matters stand regarding the *final truth* of all this; yet — and this is the careful balance of the natural-attitude description — nothing less than a *doubt* in this respect is to be expressed either. The describer neither endorses nor questions the world-certainty of natural life; he simply lays it out faithfully. This stance of neutral fidelity is the seed of what will later become the reduction, but here it is still only a descriptive discipline.

Husserl then corrects the order of his exposition: properly, before that last remark, he should have said the following. Each I not only perceives, not only has experiences that posit intuitive existence; it also has a more or less clear or confused *knowing* — it thinks, it predicates, and as a scientific human being it pursues science. It knows itself thereby as sometimes judging correctly and sometimes erring, as occasionally doubting and confused, and occasionally pushing through again to clear conviction. Yet it also knows, or is certain, that this existence-world nonetheless *is*, and that it itself is in the midst of this world, just as was described above. The point of this insertion is to widen the inventory of the natural attitude beyond mere perceiving: cognitive, predicative, and scientific life all belong to it, including the awareness of one's own fallibility — and none of this disturbs the underlying certainty of the world.

Finally Husserl flags a topic for further elaboration: the thing each I finds as "its lived-body" is distinguished before all other things precisely as *own-body* (*Eigenleib*). It is always and inescapably present in the actual perceptual sphere; it is perceived in its own peculiar way, still to be described more closely, and it functions as the *constant central member* of the way the thingly environment is apprehended. Everything that is not lived-body appears as *related to* the lived-body, having with respect to it a spatial orientation that is constantly conscious to the I — right and left, front and back — and likewise a temporal orientation: now, before, after. This is one of the seminal descriptions in Husserl's phenomenology of embodiment: the lived-body is not one thing among others but the orientational zero-point around which the entire field of things arranges itself. It cannot be absent from the perceptual field, and it supplies the standing system of coordinates by reference to which everything else has its place.

### **§ 3\. The Localization of Lived-Experiences in the Lived-Body**

Everyone relates his I-experiences too — his specific "I-possession" in general — to the lived-body, and thereby *localizes* them in it. He does this sometimes on the basis of direct "experience," immediate intuition, and sometimes in the manner of indirect experiential or analogizing knowledge. Husserl's central claim in this section is that this localization is *quite peculiar* and altogether different from the localization by which intuitively thingly parts and moments belong to a thing — whether sensuously intuitive or physical determinations. The examples are vivid and precise: joy and sorrow are not in the heart the way the blood is in the heart; tactile sensations are not in the skin the way pieces of its organic tissue are. He is careful to specify the standard by which he judges this: it follows from the *originally sense-giving representation* of the localization of the psychic — that is, from what immediate or mediate experience itself teaches about the matter — which of course does not rule out that the original sense is occasionally disregarded (as when one naïvely treats a feeling as literally seated in an organ). But he declines to linger here. The phenomenological observation is that mental life is experienced as *somehow* placed in the body and yet not as a physical ingredient of it; the "in" of psychic localization is sui generis, a relation of belonging-to and dependence rather than of spatial inherence.

Husserl closes the section by registering a further finding of natural experience: the I-experiences are acknowledged — on the basis of the experience each I makes and which governs its judging — as *dependent* (*abhängig*), within a certain not more closely determined extent, on the lived-body, on its bodily states and processes. This is the natural-attitude correlate of psychophysical dependency: ordinary experience already takes mental life to vary with bodily condition, without specifying how far that dependency reaches. Husserl notes it as a datum of the natural attitude, not yet as a thesis to be assessed; its eventual critical treatment (the question of how, and whether, such dependency survives the phenomenological reduction) is reserved for later.

### **§ 4\. Empathy and the Foreign I**

Here the lecture takes its first step toward the theme that will give the whole course its lasting importance: the other. Each I finds in its environment — and often in its *actual* environment — things that it regards as lived-bodies, yet sharply sets these over against its "own" body as *foreign lived-bodies* (*fremde Leiber*), such that to each such body there belongs again an I, but a different, *foreign* I. Husserl is exact about the mode of givenness involved. The I sees the foreign bodies as "bearers" of I-subjects, but it does *not* "see" the foreign I in the sense in which it sees itself, finds itself experiencingly. It posits them in the manner of *empathy* (*Einfühlung*) — a term Husserl later glossed as foreign-perception and foreign-experience — so that foreign experiencing and foreign character-dispositions too are "found"; but they are not given, not *had*, in the sense in which one's own are. This asymmetry is fundamental and will return at the methodological core of the course (the synopsis already flagged it: empathic presentification does *not* give the self). The other's mental life is genuinely accessible, genuinely "found," yet it is constitutively *not* given in the original, self-having way in which I have my own.

Husserl then fills out the content of what is empathically posited. The foreign I is an I that likewise has its "soul" — a phrase he later reworked to say that the I *is* the soul of its body — its actual consciousness, its dispositions and character-dispositions; that likewise finds its own thingly environment, including its own body as its own; and so forth. Crucially, the environment which the foreign I (standing quasi-perceptually over against us) finds is by and large *the same* as our environment, and the body which we apprehend in our environment as its body is *the same* one which it apprehends, from the inside, as its own-body. What holds of these mutually self-finding I's — each of which orders itself and the others into a shared environment — holds of the whole world. All I's grasp themselves as *relative midpoints* (Husserl later sharpened this to *orientation*\-midpoints) of the one and same spatio-temporal world, which in its indeterminate infinity is the total environment of each. And here Husserl states the principle of intersubjective spatial order: for each I, the other I's are not midpoints but *environment-points* (later refined to: they are *originarily given* not as midpoints but as environment-points). Each foreign I, according to the location of its body, occupies a different spatial and temporal position within the one and the same all-space and world-time. The natural attitude thus already contains a fully social cosmology: a single objective world, an indefinite plurality of embodied centers, each of which is a midpoint for itself and an environment-point for the others, all coordinated within one space and one time.

### **§ 5\. The Space-Phenomenon and the Correspondence of the Appearances of Different Subjects in Normality**

This long section develops the relation between the single objective world and the multiplicity of perspectival appearances through which it is given to different embodied subjects. Each I finds itself (Husserl later added: *originarily*) as midpoint, as the zero-point of the coordinate system — the *Nullpunkt* — from which it considers, orders, and knows all the things of the world, whether already known or not. In a later insertion Husserl underscores the foundational status of this center: it is the *ur*\-coordinate system through which all other coordinate systems first receive their sense. Yet each I grasps this midpoint as something *relative*: it changes its bodily place in space, and although it continually says "here," it knows that the "here" is each time a different location. Out of this Husserl draws a distinction that organizes the section: each I distinguishes *objective space*, as the system of objective space-places or locations, from the *space-phenomenon* — the manner in which space *appears*, with its "here and there," "front and back," "right and left." The same distinction holds for time. Objective space and time are one system; the phenomenal, oriented space and time of "here," "now," "front," "before" are perspectival presentations of that one system.

The same structure governs *things*. Each subject has around him the same world, and several subjects may see the same thing, the same piece of world; but each has his *own* thing-appearance, for the identical thing appears differently according to one's different position in space. The thing has its front and back, its top and bottom; my front of the thing may be the other's back. But it remains the *same* thing with the *same* properties. Husserl then articulates the laws of objective spatial order that underlie this: each thing can exchange its momentary location with any other in infinite objective space, but only through *continuous movement*; distinct things (and distinct parts) cannot occupy the same place at once, but they can interchange their places through continuous motion. This holds of the lived-body as well. And now comes the pivotal thought of the section. When a body exchanges its objective place with another's, the appearances which the corresponding I's have of their experienced things change *continuously*, and — in an ideal case — so that after the exchange of body-places the appearances themselves have been exchanged. Husserl names this an *ideal possibility* under the title of *normality* (an explicitly *ideal* normality): of two normal individuals, if they exchange their places (or think them exchanged) and are bodily in an ideal-normal condition, each would find in its consciousness *exactly* the same appearances that had earlier been realized in the consciousness of the other. If two people have "normal" eyes, they see the same thing when the same unchanged objects present themselves at the same objective place, which they can successively occupy; and each would always have had the same appearances had he seen from the other's place, provided all spatial relations of eye-position, and indeed eyes and whole body, were in equal "normal constitution." Husserl candidly calls these *idealizations* ("ideal talkings"). In practice, each subject simply assumes an *approximate* correspondence between his appearances and others', and registers *deviations* — under the title of illness and the like — as exceptions, or at any rate as possibilities. The deep claim here is that intersubjective agreement about a single objective world is underwritten by a rule of exchangeability of standpoints: appearances are not private and incommensurable but systematically convertible into one another as functions of bodily position and normal constitution.

Finally, Husserl describes how all of this enters communication: the I's — or, let us say, the human beings — reach mutual understanding about it. Each makes his experiences in relation to things that appear to him now thus, now so, judges on the basis of those experiences, and exchanges these judgments in mutual understanding with others. And here he marks a phenomenologically crucial point about the *direction* of natural judgment. When a subject has no occasion to reflect on the appearances — when he is turned *straightforwardly* (*geradehin*) toward the object in experiencing — he judges not about the appearances but about the *things*. Describing a thing, he takes it as the one and same object, the unchanged thing endowed with unchangeable qualities, and asserts it as such, *even though* all the while, moving head and eye and whole body through space, he continually has different appearances — now a far-appearance, now a near-appearance, now the front-appearance, now the back-appearance. The natural attitude is thus characterized by a constitutive *self-forgetting* of the appearances in favor of the thing: the manifold of subjective presentations is constantly lived through but never thematized, precisely because the gaze runs straight to the identical objective thing. This straightforward, thing-directed life is exactly what the later turn of regard will have to interrupt in order to bring the appearances themselves into view.

### **§ 6\. Recapitulation of the Preceding Expositions**

Husserl devotes a full section to recapitulating the description of the natural attitude, and the recapitulation deserves the same attention as the original, since it tightens and partly reformulates the analysis. He recalls that the previous lecture began to describe the natural attitude by trying to describe, in a general way, its *Vorfindlichkeiten*; and he proposes this time to recapitulate in detail. The first move is a shift of grammatical person that is more than stylistic. Whereas the description had spoken of "each I," Husserl now says it is best to speak in the *singular*, in the first person: I posit myself as being, as being this one here, as being with such-and-such determinate content; I posit myself as experiencing this and that, as having such states and acts. (In a later revision Husserl replaced "posit" with "find before me," and struck the clause "but I do not posit myself" — adjustments that move the description away from the language of active positing toward that of simple finding.) But — and the old categorial point returns — I do *not* find myself as a state or act. I find myself, further, not merely as experiencing subject but also as the subject of personal properties, as a person of a certain character, as having certain intellectual and moral dispositions; and this I find in a quite *different way* from my experiences.

The recapitulation then re-states the temporal structure: I find myself and what is mine as enduring in time, as changing or not changing during its duration, and I distinguish the flowing *now* from the "just-now" (*Soeben*) still given in retention; and in recollection I find myself again as the *same* who was earlier and still is now. Next the lived-body: I find that I have a body, and the body is a thing among the other things I likewise find; and this too I find in time — in the now the now-existing body as mine, in the just-now the just-elapsed body, in recollection the recollected body — and throughout, it belongs to me. And at every time-point I find some changing thingly environment. This environment is partly *immediate* (given, or having been given, in immediately positing intuition) and partly *mediate* — co-posited (*mitgesetzt*), prior to all inferential thinking, along with the environment actually intuited. In the mode of co-positing, the environment is the so-called *infinite* one: indeterminately posited thinghood in endlessly continuing space and time. Husserl describes how one renders this co-positing clear to oneself in symbolic, analogizing intuition and then posits it analogically as continuing, indeterminate, possible thingly environment, in the sense of "so, roughly, it goes on further." This is a precise account of how the infinite world-horizon is given in natural experience: not perceived, but co-meant by analogical anticipation, as the "and so on" of perception.

Husserl then recapitulates the descriptions of things as things, of the special character of the lived-body over against other things, and — importantly — of the sense of those *Vorfindlichkeiten* that bear the title "foreign body as bearer of foreign I." These foreign I's, with their experiences and personal properties, are "found" in a quite *different* way from the own I: not through self-perception and self-recollection, but through *empathy*. He recapitulates the differences of orientation in which all things, including the body, appear to the I — how to each space-place of the I (to which the body's current space-place belongs in a certain self-presentation) there belong thing-appearances in which the thing and the space of things present themselves from that subjective place — and likewise the difference between time and time-appearance. He recalls that by way of empathy all this is ascribed to the foreign I's too; that in the normal case the orientations distinguished from I to I stand in a certain *correspondence*, answering to the necessarily different space-places that different I's find as their relative location; and that, normally, when the relative space-places of the I's are exchanged, so too are their orientations and thereby their thingly appearances. And he points back to the fact that this whole conception rests on an *Idea* — an ideal of correspondence — over against which deviations are possible under the title "normal and anomalous perceiving," deviations that refer back to a different functioning of the body. The recapitulation thus consolidates the central results of the first chapter: the categorially distinct, temporally identical I; the body as thing and as orientational center; the foreign I given only through empathy; and the ideal of intersubjective correspondence grounded in the exchangeability of embodied standpoints.

### **§ 7\. The Natural Attitude as the Attitude of Experience. The Problem of the Evidence of Experiential Judgments**

Husserl now names what has been described. What was designated by the term "finding before oneself" (*vorfinden*), and what lies *prior* to all inferential, let alone scientific, thinking, is nothing other than what, in the pregnant sense, is called *experiencing* (*erfahren*). Hence the natural attitude *is* the attitude of experience. The I experiences itself and makes experience of things, of bodies, and of foreign I's. This attitude of experience is the natural one insofar as it is the *exclusive* attitude of the animal and of pre-scientific man. With this identification Husserl has fixed the sense of "natural attitude" precisely: it is life in and from experience, the attitude in which everything is simply met with as existing.

He then opens the problem that gives the section its title — the problem of *evidence*. Of course I judge when I describe what is experienced, the simply found. But these purely descriptive judgments are, as such, *mere expressions* of the experiences, of the found-nesses, and as such they are in a certain sense *absolutely evident* — evident precisely as mere expressions, just as even the faithful description of a *fiction* manifestly has this evidence. The point is subtle: insofar as a judgment merely articulates what is found, just as found, its adequacy to the finding can be perfect, regardless of whether the finding is veridical. But when the I describes the found or experienced in its individual determinacy, this is *posited as being* (*als seiend gesetzt*), and the judgment then has, over and above the evidence belonging to the adequacy of the expression (which can be complete), the evidence of the *experience-thesis* (*Erfahrungsthesis*) — and this, generally speaking, is an *imperfect* evidence. Everyone knows that "experience can deceive": one has a right to assert in following experience, yet what is experienced "need not really be." Husserl thereby separates two evidences that the natural attitude runs together: the perfect evidence of the description *as* expression of what is found, and the imperfect, defeasible evidence of the positing of the experienced *as actually existing*.

The decisive contrast follows. The assertions by which *we ourselves* have descriptively carried out the givenness of the experiential attitude raise a claim to *absolute* evidence. It is indubitably true that we *find* such things; with indubitable, absolute truth I assert and see that I *find myself* as the one having this and that, as midpoint of an environment, and so on — and this is indubitably true whether I assert that I here and now experience this determinate thing, or whether I assert indeterminately and generally that I perceive and have perceived things in a thing-environment. A footnote in Husserl's own voice makes the stakes explicit: this, naturally, is the evidence of the *pure cogito with the pure I*. A further evidence is that I am not only certain of finding just this and that, but also that "I am" and that "a world is," and that found-nesses of the described type belong, according to their general type, in the connection of the I — even though, with respect to the determinate singular, doubt and error remain possible. Tellingly, Husserl later marked the clause "that a world is" as *questionable*, and where the text asks what *kind* of evidence this is and says he will not decide here, a footnote adds: "apparently empirical evidence after all." These marginal hesitations are the very fault-line along which the *cogito* will later be separated from the world: the evidence of "I am" and the evidence of "a world is" will prove to be of fundamentally different ranks, and the second will lose the absoluteness the natural description was tempted to grant it.

Husserl ends the section by fixing what may be held fast independently of these unresolved questions: that *experience has its right* — more precisely, that judging "on the basis of experience" within the natural attitude has its self-evident legitimacy. At the lowest level stands simple descriptive judging; then, at a higher level, the inductive scientific judging of the descriptive sciences; and finally that of the exactly objective sciences, which goes *beyond* what is immediately experienced and infers to the non-experienced, yet remains always dependent on its ultimate ground of right — on immediate experiential givennesses. This graded structure of cognitive warrant, all of it rooted in experience, sets up the next section's survey of the experiential sciences.

### **§ 8\. The Experiential Sciences: Physical Natural Science and Psychology. The Natural Concept of the World**

Husserl now moves from pre-scientific experience to the *sciences* that grow out of it. When the human being not only describes the experienced but also *knows* it scientifically, he pursues experiential science (*Erfahrungswissenschaft*); and this is the science of the natural attitude. He then divides the field. Under the first heading, the scientific investigation of the (in a later insertion: *merely physical*) things — those special givennesses of the natural attitude — is the affair of *physical natural science*. Its objects are things precisely in the sense in which they are experiential givennesses: things given as being *in themselves*, with their determinate place and extension in objective space, their determinate position and duration in objective time, changing or not changing. And Husserl at once reinserts the crucial distinction won earlier: things are *not* appearances, but the *identical* that appears to me or to any other I in manifold appearances according to the subjective position of those I's and their normal or abnormal bodily constitution. The thingly makes up only one piece of the total givenness.

Under the second heading, human beings make experiences about themselves, their fellow human beings, and other experiencing organic beings (the animals and other ensouled beings). Through empathy and the empathic understanding of expression they enter not merely into practical intercourse but also observe one another for cognitive purposes, gaining — in the form of self-perception and self-recollection, and equally in the form of empathy-experience and the theorizing built upon it — so-called *psychological* knowledge; and likewise knowledge of a *psychophysical* kind, concerning the dependency-relations of the psychic (one's own as well as the foreign) upon the body. Psychology and psychophysics thus both stand on the experiential ground already described, with empathy supplying the access to the foreign that self-perception supplies to the own.

Husserl then draws an exact parallel — one that a later marginal note prefaces with "One would now like to say:" — between the two sciences, in order to expose a profound asymmetry within the parallel. Just as physical natural science describes and causally explains *things* (the things with objective properties, changes, and states that appear *in* the physical appearances, but *not* the physical appearances — the experiences — themselves), so psychology describes and causally explains human *personalities* with their changing states, acts, and dispositions, and not the appearances in which they appear, now thus, now so, to themselves and others. But here the situation is different in a decisive respect: in a certain way *all* appearances — the physical appearances as well as the self-appearances and foreign-appearances of the psychic — belong within the frame of psychology. Husserl reasons carefully. Even though describing *how* an other appears to me, or I to an other, or I to myself, is something other than describing my I itself or the foreign person itself, nonetheless the *consciousness* in which I am objective to myself is, like every consciousness, an *I-experience*; and so too is the consciousness in which an other stands over against me. Again the thing/appearance distinction: the thing is no thing-appearance; the thing is what it is whether or not I perceive it, whether or not I have the relevant perceptual appearance; the thing is *physical* and not psychical. But the *having* of the perceptual appearance, as well as the thinking of the thing grounded upon it, is something falling within the frame of psychology. And should closer consideration show that a distinction must be drawn between the *having* of the appearance of the appearing (say, in the perception of a thing) and the appearance *itself* (the appearance had in this so-called consciousness), then even the appearance, insofar as it is a *had* appearance, would fall into psychology — for it is only through this content that the "having" is differentiated. The upshot is that physics and psychology stand asymmetrically: physics studies things and brackets appearances, whereas psychology, because every appearance is a moment of consciousness, finds *all* appearances — physical and psychic alike — falling within its domain. This near-coincidence of psychology with the field of consciousness is exactly what will make the distinction between psychology and phenomenology so delicate and so important.

Husserl then specifies the *sense* in which all this belongs to psychology — namely, the sense it has within the described *natural* apprehension. The psychological I belongs to *objective* time, the very time to which the spatial world belongs, the time measured by clocks and chronometers; and this I is spatio-temporally bound to the body, on whose functioning the psychic states and acts depend — depend in their objective, i.e. spatio-temporal, existence and being-thus. In the natural sense, all the psychic is spatio-temporal. Husserl handles this claim with conspicuous care, and his own hesitations show in the deletions. Even if one declares it absurd — and, he adds (later striking the words), "perhaps rightly" — that the psychic I and its experiences have *extension* and *place*, the I nonetheless has *existence in space*, namely as the I *of* the relevant body, which has its objective place in space; and therefore everyone says, naturally and rightly, "I am now here and later there." The same holds of time: it may be no less absurd to assign the (later qualified: *purely psychic*) I and its experiences *in themselves* a place in the time determined by the Earth's motion and measured by physical apparatus, yet everyone says, naturally and rightly, "I am now, and in this same now the Earth has such-and-such a place in its orbit." On this basis the classification of psychology, and of the psychophysics inseparably bound to it, *as natural science* (a phrase Husserl later glossed with the alternative "world-science") becomes intelligible: every science of existence in the one space and the one time is natural science. And *nature* is the unitary totality — more exactly, the lawfully unitary *whole* — of all spatio-temporal existence, of all that has place and extension in the one space and position and duration in the one time. This whole Husserl names the *world* or *all-nature* (*Allnatur*). In this world there are *not two separate worlds*, things and souls. Experience knows only *one* world — since souls are souls *of bodies*, and since the world is the experiential world and as such refers back to I's that themselves, like all other I's, have an experiential place within the world. Husserl thus secures the monism of the natural world-concept: the natural attitude does not posit two realms but one spatio-temporal totality in which the psychic is everywhere embodied and worldly.

He closes the section by breaking off deliberately. The description could obviously be carried much further along all the indicated lines, and enriched with new ones; it could be shown, too, that philosophical interests of the *highest dignity* demand a complete and all-sided description of the so-called *natural concept of the world* (*natürlicher Weltbegriff*), the concept belonging to the natural attitude — and, conversely, that an exact and deep description of this kind is no easy matter but requires extraordinarily difficult reflections. Yet here, he says, there shall be no talk of such philosophical interests — even though the lectures' own undertaking is *attuned* to precisely those interests. For the immediate purposes the rough beginnings suffice. The aim was only to *indicate* what the natural attitude is, by a general and brief characterization of what is found in it as *world in the natural sense* — a world that is nothing other than the infinite object of the natural and psychological sciences, both the exactly describing and the theoretically (and then causally) explaining. With this, the first task of the introduction — securing the natural attitude as the point of departure — is complete, and the way is open to ask after the *new* attitude that phenomenology requires.

### **§ 9\. Empirical or Natural Attitude and Apriori Attitude. Ontology of Nature and Formal Ontology**

This section opens the question that drives the whole methodological enterprise: is any *new* attitude possible over against the natural world-apprehension already described, in which nature or world becomes the visible and knowable field? A footnote records that Husserl later struck everything from here to the end of §9 — a fact worth keeping in view, since the section develops a *first* answer (the eidetic or apriori attitude) that he eventually judged to fall short of, or to be misplaced within, the path toward the genuinely phenomenological attitude. As a paragraph of the original text, however, it must be read in full and on its own terms.

Husserl begins from an apparent objection. Does not nature embrace *all* actual being? Certainly — *if* by "actual" we again mean what exists in space and time. But not if we recall that correct and insightful judging also bears on objects that have *no existence*. Thus pure geometry speaks of geometric figures, pure arithmetic of numbers; and the figures of pure geometry — as the possible formations of pure space — and the numbers of arithmetic — as the pure numbers of the number-series — are no things and in no sense facts of nature. (A marginal note labels this the *eidetic attitude*.) Accordingly, Husserl says, one will be able to claim: over against nature — the world of factual spatio-temporal existence, the "empirical" world — there are *ideal worlds*, worlds of ideas, that are unspatial, untemporal, unreal, and yet *are*, just as numbers in the number-series are; subjects of valid scientific assertions just as much as the things of nature. Correspondingly one must distinguish between the natural or *empirical* attitude and, on the other hand, the non-empirical, *apriori* attitude. In the one, existence-objectivities come to givenness; in the other, *essence*\-objectivities — in the one, nature; in the other, ideas.

Against this distinction, Husserl says, nothing can of course be objected. It is plainly a different attitude when, in perception or memory, we have a color given as a moment *in* a thing and *mean* it perceivingly and rememberingly, and when we so to speak turn otherwise and grasp only the *idea* of this color, the corresponding color-species, as a pure givenness. It is one thing to perceive a singular tone of quality *c* as the tone of the violin now sounding, and another, in a changed attitude and on the basis of this (exemplary) tone-appearance, to form the *idea* "quality *c*," which is a single one in the ideal and unique series of tone-qualities; or to see four strokes, and, on the other hand, while seeing the four strokes, to be turned not to *them* but to the single idea, the number 4, the idea here exemplarily illustrated. Such ideas now function as *objects* and at the same time make possible assertions concerning corresponding indeterminately and generally thought *singularities* — singularities merely thought and not posited as existing — assertions that have the character of *unconditioned generality*, as the arithmetical ones do. Every idea has, as such, the property that to it corresponds a so-called *extension* (*Umfang*), but a *pure* extension of singularities, singularities with respect to which no existence-positing whatever is performed. Hence pure arithmetic, pure geometry, pure phoronomy, pure tone-theory, and the like contain *no* assertion about real existence: whether or not anything existent exists, the propositions of these disciplines hold — they hold as *pure* propositions.

Husserl then dwells on the difficulty of *seeing* this purity. A step or a resolve belongs to seeing and holding fast the *purity* of the apriori, i.e. its *freedom from existence* (*Daseinsfreiheit*). Natural scientists and mathematicians love to lay an *empirical* sense under the mathematical propositions. But if they judge and ground such propositions with the sense that the indeterminately left units of counting *stand for* really existing things, processes, and the like — only in that indeterminate generality of thought which would span any arbitrary empirical existence — *then* mathematics, precisely as they construe it (and likewise every similar science), belongs from the outset to the *sphere of nature*. The thought of the *pure idea*, and with it of pure, wholly unconditioned generality, lies in fact far from the natural empirical attitude. Over against such an interpretation of the mathematical, one needs first of all the *exclusion* of every existence-positing — even of the indeterminate kind — in order to grasp the apriori, the ideal essences, in their existence-purity, and so to grasp the over-empirical, unspatial, untemporal idea.

Yet, Husserl immediately adds, this is really an inexact way of putting it. Whoever has once *beheld* the ideal in its purity, whoever has once carried out judgments in pure or "strict" generality, does not first *need* to set out from empirical generality and perform a special act of excluding empirical existence. One grasps the idea and the pure generality precisely *in a proper attitude*, in a proper, differently directed *seeing and meaning*. On the other hand, it is to be noted that it is one thing to *have*, grasp, and mean the pure apriori, and another, afterward — reflecting on the sense of what is grasped and asserted — to interpret it *correctly*, to take it as what it gives itself to be. The mathematician may very well, and generally does, judge in strict generality, and yet empiristic prejudices mislead him into *interpreting* the purely grasped, after the fact, empirically. So, then: ideas, essences, we grasp in the apriori attitude. To this belong the idea of space and the ideas of spatial shapes, the ideas *of* spatialities which are not themselves spatial. In real space, in nature, there is no idea of space, no idea of triangle; and likewise, in real time, there is not the idea "time," which is rather itself an untemporal being, an idea. The essence-attitude — ultimately that of *intuitive ideation* — thus brings a new, *existence-free* sphere to givenness, and in a certain sense may already be designated a *philosophical* attitude. The transition from the impure apriori of an empirically restricted mathematics to the strict apriori of pure mathematics is, Husserl insists, of great philosophical significance and an indispensable step for the establishing of genuine philosophy: whoever has not made it can never scale the heights of true philosophy.

But — and here the section turns toward its own self-limitation — were matters to rest with this new attitude, we should have no more than the natural sciences on the one side and, on the other, the mathematical and other apriori sciences grasped in their purity; or rather only *those* apriori sciences which the point of departure from the natural sciences suggests, and which at first constitute themselves merely as *instruments* of natural-scientific research. Husserl articulates the resulting articulation of disciplines. To *nature as fact* he opposes *nature as idea*. To nature as fact the natural sciences in the ordinary, empirical sense refer; to nature as idea, the *pure* natural sciences. This yields the sciences of the ideas constitutive *for* the idea of nature: geometry, pure theory of time, pure theory of motions and of the possible deformations in the thingly as such — the last corresponding roughly to *Kant*'s idea of a pure natural science. These disciplines, answering to the idea of nature, Husserl gathers under the title *ontology of nature*.

Of an essentially *different* character is another group of apriori disciplines whose truths the natural sciences must often employ: pure logic of asserting propositions, pure theory of probability, pure arithmetic, and finally pure theory of manifolds (*Mannigfaltigkeitslehre*). These do *not* belong to the idea of nature; they do not lay out the apriori that constitutes the idea of nature. The existence-freedom of arithmetic concerns not only every actual positing of real existence but also *every positing of the idea of nature*, every appeal to the idea of things, properties, and so on. The "one" of arithmetic is *something whatever* (*Etwas überhaupt*), and under it falls not only the thingly and spatio-temporal but anything whatever — be it an idea, be it even a number. If formal logic treats of the truth of propositions, then the idea "proposition," in its unconditioned generality, embraces not merely arbitrary propositions with a natural-scientific content but propositions with *any* content, e.g. a purely arithmetical one. It can be shown, Husserl says, that this group of disciplines may also be interpreted as a *universal apriori ontology* — an ontology referring to *thought being as such* (*gedachtes Sein überhaupt*).

Husserl restates the contrast cleanly. *Pure natural science*, or better *ontology of nature*, would be the title for all disciplines belonging to the idea of nature, i.e. to the ideas constitutive for it: the ideas of space and time, hence pure spatial theory (geometry), pure theory of time, pure kinematics, the pure disciplines of the possible deformations of spatial formations. But to the idea of the *thing* — which has not only its duration and geometric shape but *real properties* and *real changes* standing in *causal* connections — there belong further apriori laws which, as such, do not bear on the facticity of existing things but on the idea of thinghood as such. Here one strikes upon the Kantian "*pure natural science*," which Kant famously separates from geometry, pure chronometry, and the other aforementioned disciplines. But of this discipline it must be said that it has *in fact* not exercised the functions one should expect: historically it has not been developed and applied as an apriori auxiliary discipline of the natural sciences — as, so to speak, a *mathematics of thinghood*. It has remained, properly, a *desideratum*, advancing no further than slight beginnings. For the natural sciences as they stand, only isolated propositions belonging to it serve — for example, the impenetrability of material things; or that a thing can change its place only by *moving*, i.e. only in continuous change of place; further the *causal law*, according to which every change of property can proceed only according to empirical laws, according to natural laws — though about this principle, as ultimately about the others too, much is disputed, insofar as some are inclined to take even these principles (which would belong to the Kantian pure natural science) as *empirical* laws, which others decidedly deny. Whoever, however, has learned to let full intellectual honesty hold sway, whoever has once learned to vindicate as *givenness* what is beheld in the essence-attitude — even in reflection, against all confusing misunderstandings and especially fashionable theories — will conduct himself here just as in the matter of the aforementioned mathematical disciplines, which are, and must be acknowledged as being, ideally related to pure space, pure time, pure motion, and so on.

Husserl then returns to the second group of disciplines to characterize them more sharply: a group of essentially differently constituted, in part likewise "mathematical" disciplines that came to flower and to a *pure* development only in the last century or, fully, only in most recent times, and that likewise play their role as instruments of the existence-sciences. He has in view, first, pure and formal logic of asserting propositions and the wholly purely grasped theory of probabilities or possibilities. To the former belongs — this may suffice as illustration — the entire *syllogistic*, which under the hands of mathematicians has lately likewise taken on mathematical form. The pure theory of probability has so far remained mixed with existence-restrictions; only a few yet uphold the idea of a wholly existence-free probability theory. Closely akin to syllogistic logic, he must not forget to mention, are pure arithmetic and pure theory of manifolds. All these disciplines do *not*, like geometry, belong to the idea of nature; they concern nothing of what constitutes the idea of nature according to its specific essence. The existence-purity of arithmetic, for example, means not only that every actual positing of real existence (physical or psychic) remains switched off, but that *nothing* of the special essence-content of the idea of a nature whatever is in question — hence no idea, even in ideal manner, of the spatial, the thingly, of thingly properties. The "one" of arithmetic means *anything whatever*, and when one further unit is spoken of, only *some other something whatever* is meant, thought as indeterminately and generally distinct from that first something whatever. Whether physical or psychic existents are at issue — even in pure generality — or again ideas, makes no difference: everything and anything can be *counted*, even, e.g., numbers (which are nothing thingly), even space and time, as when I say they are two pure forms of every possible nature whatever. Likewise with formal logic in the narrow sense: if it treats of propositions as such, there is no talk of propositions specially related to nature or to anything else.

All the disciplines of this second group hang intimately together, and so closely that one may gather them all under the idea of a *formal, unconditionally general ontology*. Over against it stands the much more restricted, because *materially* determined, idea of the *ontology of nature* — physical and psychic. With this circle of apriori disciplines, Husserl concludes — as he had already intimated — we do *not* yet have the higher and proper *storey* of the philosophical problematic. We shall have to go further, and first to the question whether the philosophical disciplines we have struck upon are the *only* apriori disciplines. This last question — pointing beyond both the ontology of nature and formal ontology toward something still higher — is exactly where the struck-out section breaks off and where the lecture, in its surviving form, will redirect itself toward the properly phenomenological problem.

### **§ 10\. The Apriori of Nature, the Natural World-Concept, and Natural Science. Avenarius' "Critique of Pure Experience"**

Before going further, Husserl undertakes what he calls an instructive *excursus*: a principled engagement with the positivism of the Avenarius school, which sees the task of a theory and critique of pure experience in the exclusion of all "metaphysical" admixtures from the world-concept and in the restitution of the "natural" world-concept of pure experience. (An editorial note records that two leaves of Husserl's manuscript are missing here, and refers the reader to a parallel appendix on "Immanent Philosophy — Avenarius.") The interest of the excursus is to set the *description* of the natural world-concept, with which the course began, over against this most broadly conceived ontology of nature.

Husserl first reflects on the epistemic status of his own opening description. It was general and in a certain way evident; yet it *was* a description, and as such it *posited the existence* of what it described. Each of us, in giving it, says: "I am and find myself in a spatio-temporal environment, among things and other people; I have appearances of all this and find them related to the privileged thing 'my body'." These are *facts*. The same when, on the basis of memory, I say "I was, and was in an environment," or "with the other bodies I's are connected, as I am, related to the same environment." Whether the singular facts I have before my eyes really obtain may, one will say, be doubtful. Does any evidence then remain — the evidence we claimed for the description? Husserl answers by locating evidence in an easily isolated *restriction*. It is evident that I can at any time say: I *have* such perceptions, memories, convictions; I have self-perception and self-grasping of myself as the familiar person; I have perception of an environment. And further it is evident that my judgments — insofar as they are, as we oriented them, *pure expressions* of the perceived as such, the remembered as such — exclude every possible error *to that extent*, in that they mirror with purely descriptive expressions the mere *sense* of the relevant perceptions, memories, and other experiential certainties. It may be that I deceive myself that *the thing is there*; but that I *perceive*, and that the perception is precisely perception of a thing within a spatial environment, is *doubtless*. (A footnote restates the point exactly: the description is evident *so long as* it faithfully expresses the objective sense of the relevant *cogitationes*.)

Husserl then formulates a *conditional* evidence of the highest importance. It is evident that *if* what is posited in the natural attitude is actual — that is, if the perceptions and memories can be *legitimated*, so that the objective sense they carry holds up objectively in its validity — *then* what such a sense demands a priori must *also* hold objectively. The general expressions with which I describe perception as perception-in-general, memory as memory-in-general, and correlatively the general expressions I use of the perceived as such — speaking of persons and things, of experiences, dispositions, things, thingly properties, spatial extension, temporal duration — these general expressions designate a *general sense* to which every empirical truth is manifestly bound. It may be that I deceive myself, in a given case, that a thing I believe myself to have before me really *is*, or *is as* it appears. But it *appears*; and before I so much as raise the question whether it really is and how it is in reality, I know *in advance* that it can only be in *the* sense in which a thing with properties is — for it is *as that* that it appears perceptually, and the question whether the appearing is becomes thereby the *determinate* question: is *this thing*? Husserl restates the thought once more in terms of fact: it is a fact, in the description, that I am generally convinced not only that I (purportedly) find this and that — myself in a spatio-temporal environment among other things and other psychic beings — but also that this is, *generally spoken, true*, even if in the singular case I deceive myself about the particulars assumed within the world.

What philosophical questions this *thetic* evidence — this general evidence positing the world-fact as such and in generality — may direct at us, Husserl leaves undecided for now. But an evidence it *is*. Now suppose that, within the frame of this evidence, one operates with particular empirical theses — positing the particular things of experience and judging about them experientially, as common life and natural science alike do. Then it is wholly doubtless that, already in general and no less in particular, all possible experiential knowledge is *bound* to the *sense* with which these theses are carried out. Natural science is nothing other, and wants to be nothing other, than the science *of nature*; it therefore presupposes as valid, prior to any closer methodical working of the experientially given, what the general sense of *nature as experiential givenness whatever* prescribes to it — what already comes to general expression in the very words the description of the natural attitude employs: thing, property, change, cause, effect, space, time, and, on the other side, person, experience, act, appearance, disposition. (A footnote concedes a gap: what is still missing is the clarification of the sense in which apriori invariance is distinguished from the empirical — the empirical type — and the reduction of empty meanings to the full meanings of possible experience.) From this Husserl draws the formula that is the heart of the section: *all natural science, insofar as it presupposes the thesis of the natural world-view and within this frame and sense investigates being, is a priori bound to the real ontology*. (A second footnote insists this be understood rightly: the standing thesis of experience, with its standing sense, runs on within the frame of an experience that keeps itself concordant; the evidence of the thesis remains throughout an *experiential* evidence, which is reservational and necessarily remains so. That the idea of nature can find application to *given* nature presupposes given nature; but whether nature really is there, really exists, remains forever under reservation.)

On this basis Husserl can grant the Avenarian project its legitimate kernel. *If* it is true, as the positivists and especially Avenarius have repeatedly maintained, that natural science as it factually stands is thoroughly permeated and falsified by skewed interpretations offending against the natural world-view — already shot through with auxiliary concepts that, useful as their functions are within scientific method, as factually defined and interpreted carry a *surplus* of thoughts violating the basic schema of the natural world-view — then it is an important, indeed for the attainment of definitive natural knowledge an indispensable task, to exercise *critique*; and this critique may quite correctly be called "*critique of pure experience*." Pure experience would then be that experience and experiential knowledge which (to remain in the jargon of positivism) excludes all "metaphysics" — where "metaphysics" means, for our orientation (which is of course not the positivistic one), nothing other than *suppositions that do not hold to the fundamental sense* of the natural world-thesis, or to the sense of "experience." (A footnote presses the question of what this "fundamental sense" of world- or nature-experience is, and answers: all world-thinking rests on "pure experience"; if we subtract all thoughts, without asking whether right or wrong, and hold to the experienced world purely as experienced, we can circumscribe the pure experiential sense in most original general concepts.) The task is thus to exercise the needed critique on the concepts of natural science by first cleanly laying out the general sense of the natural thesis that grounds all natural science, and thereby fixing the *measure* of critique. Only so can a really concordant world-concept of concrete content be worked out from natural science, and the factual natural science be reshaped into a "pure" experiential science. Husserl summarizes: *the "ontology" of nature unfolds, in its disciplines, the pure formal-general sense of the natural thesis* — the givenness of the natural attitude as such — while the question of what *justifies* a thesis of such sense-content, like the further question of what justifies a given natural science in its particular theses, *lies outside its lines*.

Husserl now guards against a decisive misunderstanding, and this is the philosophically richest stretch of the section. The talk of the *natural world-concept* does *not* and *may not* mean a world-concept that every human being remarkably and factually brought into the world — say as a hereditary deposit of millions of years of animal development, the precipitate of ever more perfect, ever more thought-economical adaptation of animal and finally man to natural conditions; nor a world-concept that historical humanity, or even the individual, *empirically* formed and that could and would have been formed *otherwise* had the anthropological, historical, cultural circumstances been different. To be sure — Husserl grants, judging within the natural attitude — every experience and group of experiences that any human being of this world has belongs *itself* to this world and arose in it according to empirical laws with empirical necessity under the given circumstances. But *however* the actual experiences in which humans have the world-concept as unitary content have arisen, *as long as* there is talk of a world in which there are humans who have consciousness *of* the world, who have experiences including empirical-existence-positing perceptions — *as long as that talk keeps its sense, so long the natural world-concept holds absolutely and a priori*. This apriority does not mean that another thesis than that of the natural world is in *every* sense impossible; it does not mean that other perceptions of individual unities and other experiences than those we call thing-perceptions, human-perceptions, and the like are simply unthinkable — on which Husserl here expressly *suspends* all judgment. Rather it means: *starting from the fact* of the natural attitude, from the fact of the thesis of the nature graspable and generally characterizable within it, and from the fact that this thesis has its indubitable right, *every natural-scientific assertion* — as an assertion scientifically determining the particular posited in that thesis — *is senseless if it offends against the sense of that thesis according to its general sense-determining content*. (A footnote glosses "fact" so as to include the anticipation that the thesis, performed by each for himself, *will keep itself up* in the concordance of experiences — an anticipation grounded in the unity-trait of world-experience itself; and a further footnote clarifies that the natural world-concept is *not* the one humans formed before science, but the world-concept that constitutes the sense of the natural attitude before *and after* science — a sense that must, however, first be *worked out* in the basic concepts of ontology.)

The consequence is sharp. It is *senseless* to speak of the possibility that humans, in the course of their experience, or higher animals, in the course of their ever more perfect adaptation, could have *rationally* worked out a *different* world-concept as legitimate — as if this world-concept were, for humans-in-nature or animals-in-nature, something *contingent*, like the particular and general-lawful facts the natural sciences treat in their textbooks. This is senseless, Husserl says, because in speaking of humans and of nature and of what is possible *in* nature, we have *presupposed* nature and humans, and thereby presupposed what makes nature whatever "possible" — that is, we have presupposed the *sense* of nature, precisely the natural world-concept. *In the world nothing can be that abolishes the sense of the talk of world, because it presupposes that sense (that essence).* It is therefore, Husserl continues, fundamentally wrong to frame the problem as Avenarius — if Husserl recalls rightly — or at least his school framed it: we can describe the world-concept we all have, or humanity had, *before* science, and then ask whether man, in pursuing natural science, has *occasion*, indeed experiential occasion, to *abandon* this world-concept. Such a way of posing the question is perverse, because it sets up as *possible* that experience might bring occasions to modify the natural world-concept — *rationally*, note well — whereas the analysis has taught that this supposed possibility is *countersense* (*Widersinn*), in the sharpest meaning of the word.

A paragraph that Husserl later struck refines the modal point with great care, and it is worth preserving as part of the original text. Granting that it is countersense to claim *implicitly* that *in* the world a human being could, with rational right, *find that another world than this is the actual world* — it is, on the other side, *no* countersense to claim that perhaps another world might *exist at all*; indeed perhaps even that, *outside any connection* with this world, the world of the natural attitude or of experience, there might be still another world, totally differently constituted, Euclideanly spaceless and the like. *That* is no countersense. For in so claiming we do *not* assert what makes the countersense — namely that humans, or beings in principle of the same kind, body-endowed and so on, could *find* and *scientifically know* such a world, or that natural science (the science on the ground of the natural world-concept, the science that with its first word, so to speak, posits things, space, time) could be *compelled by experience* to abandon the natural world-concept. The great problems hanging on this *meaningful* possibility of other worlds, and on the ultimate question of the *facticity* of this world and its natural thesis, cannot be entered into here. But, Husserl concludes, we draw near to this sublime sphere when we now turn back to the question of the attitudes that *deviate* from the natural attitude and that may, perhaps, interweave with it. With this the first chapter closes: the natural world-concept has been secured as an *a priori, sense-constituting* structure to which all natural science is bound, and the way is cleared for the question of the *new* attitude — the question to which the second chapter gives the answer of the phenomenological reduction.

## **Second Chapter: Fundamental Consideration: The Phenomenological Reduction as the Gaining of the Attitude toward Pure Lived-Experience**

### **§ 11\. The Sphere of Knowledge in the Subjective Sense, and Empirical and Rational Psychology**

The chapter that contains the methodological heart of the course opens with a question that grows directly out of the survey of apriori disciplines. Are the apriori disciplines we have found *all* those that offer themselves in the essence-attitude? Is the domain of the apriori, along the path taken, *completely* delimited? Husserl reminds us how that path was determined: everything seen so far — singular, general, and more narrowly apriori — was determined by the *point of departure from the natural attitude*. In that attitude we looked toward the natural world, nature in the widest sense — *this looking is the natural attitude* — and that yielded the apriori *of nature*, unfolded in real ontology. We looked, further, toward the sciences in general (of nature, and of apriori nature such as geometry), and recalled that in all asserting there is to be found something like a *form* — in the proposition as the supposed state-of-affairs as such — and likewise forms in the connections of propositions, and forms in the manner of number, combination, manifold. In all this, Husserl notes, we were in a certain sense *objectively directed* (*gegenständlich gerichtet*): we spoke, after all, of *formal ontology*. The apriori concerned a *form of objectivity* as objectivity of scientific thinking in general — objectivity as variously grasped in concepts, predicatively to be determined, theoretically to be posited as true or probable.

Is everything thereby concluded? Are there not *new directions of regard*? Husserl now turns the gaze, for the first time, *reflectively*, toward thinking itself. How does it stand with reflection on the *thinking itself*, and on all the *experiences* that, in the connection of thinking, are of significance for the *normative adjudication of right* — for instance, the manifoldly changing perceptions we make of a thing, which underlie the simple experiential judgment and by faithfully orienting itself to which the experiential judgment wins its *logical value of right*? How does it stand with the whole sphere of *knowledge in the subjective sense*, as distinct from what is meant in it, its *objective* sense, to which we have already granted its right? And this question, Husserl stresses, is to be posed in the *widest* compass — not only with reference to the empirical sphere but also to any apriori sphere whatever. The decisive widening of the problematic is thus: alongside the *objective* correlate of cognition (the thing, the truth, the state-of-affairs), there stands the *subjective* side — the cognizing experiences themselves, the perceivings and meanings — and *this* side has not yet been made a theme.

Husserl then confronts an objection that seems already to have answered the question — the objection that the subjective falls under psychology. By speaking of knowledge "in the subjective sense," he seems to have given the answer: *all* the subjective belongs itself to the natural sphere, and more narrowly to the sphere of *psychology*. As a fact of the relevant empirical subject, generally as a fact of cognizing experiencing in the human world, the subjective belongs of course to psychology *as natural science*. But — Husserl asks — does it not also admit of an *apriori* consideration? Certainly. Just as there is, and self-evidently is, an apriori with respect to the *physical thing* — an apriori that says nothing other than what belongs to the general *sense* of empirical thing-positing — so there is also a *psychological apriori*: that which lays out what belongs to the *essence* or *sense* of empirical "soul"-positing, of the positing of humans, of the positing of experiences as experiences of humans. And here Husserl ventures a deliberately provocative claim. Against the crass empiricism that has ruled among psychologists for some decades, it may seem to many incredible that he should dare to reawaken the long-buried idea of a *rational psychology*. But, he says, he cannot help it: from the standpoint of the matter itself — which, once seen, is absolutely evident — it cannot be said otherwise. It is downright self-evident that a *pure psychology* must run *parallel* to a pure natural science. There must, at any rate, be *some* groups of propositions that lay out the *sense* lying in I-experience, in the experience of the psychic, and that draw their evidence from being *transposed into* the perfect givenness of the relevant modes of the psychic.

Husserl unfolds this parallel with a precise methodological description of how essential insight into the psychic would proceed — a description that already contains, *in nuce*, the method of eidetic variation and intuitive fulfillment. Exactly as we make clear to ourselves what the *thing* as such is according to its essence, by thinking ourselves into perception-connections in which a thing comes steadily to ever more perfect givenness while constantly preserving its identity; or what *causality* as such is, by transposing ourselves into the connections in which the dependencies of changes of things show themselves step by step and in continued confirmation — exactly so must the *essence of an I-character* show itself in certain experience-connections into which we intuitively transpose ourselves, perhaps in *fingiert* (feigned, imaginatively constructed) but thoroughly clear connections of givenness, in which precisely what we call the *character* of a human being would show itself and bring itself to ever new confirmation, just as the kind of this objectivity demands of itself. And the same when we want to show what belongs to the *essence of experiences* insofar as they are experiences of experiencing persons — insofar as they belong to those persons as acts or states, and with them have their objective time-place. With this Husserl has opened the door through which the entire methodological turn will pass: the subjective is not merely a worldly fact for empirical psychology but has its own *essential structure*, accessible by intuitive transposition into clarified givenness — and the question now becomes whether this subjective essence-sphere can be secured *purely*, free of the existence-positing of the natural and psychological world.

### **§ 12\. The Problem of the Switching-Off of the Empirical as well as of the Essence of Nature. The Linking of the I to the Body**

Husserl begins from what the natural attitude continually delivers: *empirical* I's, I's or souls as human personalities in time; experiences as objectively temporally determined experiences of such body-belonging empirical I's; and among these, thing-appearances as appearances had at some determinate time by this or that psychic individual. (An editorial note of considerable interest reports that the following leaves were extracted by Husserl from this 1910/11 course and inserted into the manuscript of his summer-semester 1912 lecture "Introduction to Phenomenology," which served as a basis for *Ideas I*; beside the next paragraph Husserl wrote the date "26 November 1910." The passage thus stands at the very source of the formulation of the reduction in *Ideas I*.) On this basis Husserl poses what he himself calls an at-first unintelligible question: can we gain an attitude such that the *empirical* — the peculiar feature of the givenness of the natural attitude — remains entirely switched off, and indeed so that *also its essence as essence of nature* remains switched off, while on the other hand *components* are preserved that enter into the essence of nature, or into nature itself *in individuo*? The question is the seed of the reduction: to switch off not only factual existence but the very *sense* "nature," and yet to retain something — the lived-experiences themselves — that had been *embedded in* nature.

To make the question intelligible, Husserl analyzes the linking of the I to the body. In the natural attitude the I is experienced as a *member of nature*, an object within spatio-temporal existence, and is so experienced precisely *by virtue of* its experiential linking to the thingly body. The I experiences, but the experiences are experienced as standing in relation to the body, in a certain order of levels still to be described. First come the *sensory* experiences: the perceptions of things (the body included) with their sensation-components — sensation of color, of tone — have a peculiar relation to the appearing thing "own-body"; likewise the specific *body-sensations* belonging to eye-movement, hand-movement, and the like, which are *localized into* the body and its parts; and likewise the sensory feelings. Intimately interwoven with these are the higher, *founded* psychic experiences. To the body posited as existing there relates the whole *manner of distribution* of the perception-experiences that the I has of the manifold of possible things of its environment; and this manner of distribution belongs to *its* body, whereas to the foreign body, and to the perception-groups empathically ascribed to it, belongs a *corresponding but different* manner of distribution — and *other* perception-groups, since the perceptions one subject has, the other does not, and conversely. This last observation is quietly important: the indexing of perception-distributions to distinct bodies is what individuates one stream of consciousness from another, and it will resurface when the multiplicity of monads is at stake.

Husserl notes that the clarification and scientific description of these complicated connections is an extraordinarily important and difficult matter, and contents himself here with holding fast the essential point: the manifold experiences have a *perceptually appearing relation* to the body. The body, *as a thing*, has the *primary* ordering into objective time and objective space; the first objective time is *thing-time*. With this, the body and the bodily have their time, and thereby so does everything that is *ordered to* the body and localized in it — though already in a secondary sense — and in further consequence what appears as the higher psychic functions in the same now (and appeared in the same past). The picture is thus of a *graded* dependence: the body anchors the psychic in objective space and time, primarily as thing, secondarily through the localization of sensations, and at a third remove through the higher founded experiences. It is precisely the *contingency* of this graded linking that the next section will exploit.

### **§ 13\. The Resolvability of the Empirical Connection of "res cogitans" and "res extensa." The "distinctio phaenomenologica"**

Husserl states the thesis of the section at once and emphatically: *obviously all linking to the body is something that can be detached from the body again*. The body stands there *as a thing*. It does not belong to the essence of a thing that it is, so to speak, a *sensing* thing — that it reacts to a pinprick with pain, to a tickle with itching; nor does this belong essentially to the essence of the particular *shaping* of a thing that makes it a body. It is a *facticity* that such an appearing thing *is* a body; it is *experience* that it is connected with the psychic. The modal asymmetry is exact. A thing that were not spatial, a thing that had no real properties, would be *nonsense*. But that *no* thing whatever — not even the well-known human body — should be a sensing thing is *no* nonsense. Thing, *res extensa*, is *factually res cogitans* insofar as *cogitationes* are in a certain way experientially connected with it; but *in itself* the *cogitare* has nothing to do with any *res extensa*. The essence of the *cogitatio* and the essence of the *extensio* have, in principle, *as essences*, nothing to do with one another. (Husserl notes that "extensio" is to be taken in the full compass of the thingly essence — not mere geometric extension but thinghood as such.) This is the phenomenological transformation of the Cartesian dualism: not a metaphysical claim that two substances exist, but an *eidetic* claim that the essence "consciousness" and the essence "thing" are mutually independent, so that their factual connection is just that — factual.

Husserl confirms the thesis from the other side. In the essence of a pain or a pleasure no relation to a thing is given at all; in the essence of the sensations of color and tone, in the essence of experiences of perceiving, judging, desiring, questioning, there lies again *no essential relation* (Husserl later specified: no *real* connection) to a thing — as if a linking to a thing were essentially necessary for the being of such *cogitationes*. And if that is so, then we can *cut through* the empirical relation between *cogitatio* and *res*, without thereby performing an *abstraction* in the sense of a *distinctio* of essentially non-self-sufficient and inseparable moments of a concretum — that is, without treating consciousness as a dependent moment that cannot be thought apart from the thing. The cut is legitimate precisely because the essences are mutually independent.

Husserl reinforces this by an analogy with causality that also forestalls an objection. Just so, on the occurrence of a cause, we can not merely *think* but *intuitively represent* that the experientially belonging effect does *not* occur. The connection is necessary in the *empirical* sense but not in the *ideal* (essential) sense: the essence of the cause-thing is no non-self-sufficient essence, as though it were necessarily connected with the essence of the experientially belonging effect-thing. Husserl heads off the apparent contradiction with his earlier admission that causality of changes belongs to the essence of every thing. To the essence of *thing-experience*, he repeats, belongs that *every thingly change stands under causal laws*. But this does *not* say that it lies in the essence of an experienced change that *precisely the experientially found cause* belongs to it. Hence not all necessity in the realm of experience is *essence*\-necessity — otherwise all the natural sciences would be apriori sciences. And so, by the same token, the connection of *experience* and the *human being who has the experience* is a "*contingent*" (*zufällig*) one. The lawfulness of causality in general is essential; the particular causal tie, and the particular tie of an experience to a body, is empirical and severable.

From this the *distinctio phaenomenologica* follows. We can therefore, *without countersense*, cut through the empirical connection between the experience and all thingly existence. But what does this cut mean? Husserl raises the natural objection against himself: is it not true that experiences *are* experiences of experiencing humans, that they have relation to the body and an ordering into nature? Can I change that? It is just so, certainly. *But* — and here is the precise sense of the reduction as it first emerges — we can *consider the experiences in and for themselves*, without considering them in their empirical relation. We can *switch off* every natural positing (every positing of natural existence) in the sense that we carry out scientific considerations *in which we simply make no use of any positing of nature*, and which therefore retain their validity *whether or not* there is a nature, a spiritual-bodily world at all. The *distinctio phaenomenologica* is thus not a denial that experiences belong to embodied humans, nor a metaphysical separation of mind from body; it is a *methodical abstention* from the use of the natural thesis, grounded in the eidetic independence of consciousness from thinghood — and its hallmark is that its results stand fast regardless of the existence of nature. With this the concept of the reduction is essentially in place, and the next section turns to *why* the lived-experience enjoys a privilege of being that underwrites this independence.

### **§ 14\. The Privilege-of-Being of the Lived-Experience over against the Natural Object. Empirical (Transcendent) Perception and the Perception of the Pure Lived-Experience**

This is one of the pivotal sections of the course, for it grounds the legitimacy of the *distinctio phaenomenologica* in a difference in the very *mode of being* of lived-experience and natural thing. Husserl states the thesis: in fact an experience has *in itself* its being, of which we can say that it is what it is *even if* the talk of a spatio-temporal nature with bodies and spirits were a meaningless fancy. And, looked at more closely, the being of the experience has herein a *tremendous privilege* over the existence of a natural object. To make this out, Husserl proposes to bring to clearest consciousness the opposition between *empirical* being and *phenomenological* being, and correlatively between *empirical* perception and *phenomenological* perception, as the acts that prescribe sense to the one and the other being.

He begins with the thing. We speak of things as having a *being-in-itself* (*An-sich-sein*) over against knowledge, over against consciousness, and with evident right. Things are immediately given in experience, thought and determined in experiential thinking. But if the thing exists, it is what it is even if the experience that experiences it does not exist, even if the experiential thinking that objectively-validly determines it does not exist. His example is stark: if all humans sleep, if a geological catastrophe destroys all humans and living beings, then no one is on Earth who thinks and determines the Earth, its bodies, and its upheavals — yet the Earth *is*, with everything it is. This is *correct*, Husserl grants, when spoken in the manner of natural science: the independence of the thing from being known is a genuine sense-moment of the thing.

But — and here the privilege of the experience emerges by contrast — knowledge of thinghood has an *unremovable disadvantage*. However well-grounded a thing may be experienced as existing, and however it may confirm its existence in the course of further experience and let itself be scientifically determined, it forever remains, for knowledge, to a certain extent *mere existence-pretension* (*Daseinsprätention*). The ever-so-good right can, in the progress of experience, turn out to be insufficient and outweighed by a better right. Husserl insists this belongs to the *essence* of appearance, to the sense of experiential objectivity as such. We need only transpose ourselves into an experiencing and weigh the sense in which the experienced gives itself as being, and we have the evidence that experienceable being *in principle does not go up in its being-perceived* (*percipi*) but is, over against it, an *in-itself*. And this in-itself *does* come to givenness — and yet *in principle it is never given in an absolute way*. Its meaning is forever only meaning in the sense that it always requires a demonstration (*Ausweis*) that can never be finally given. Thus the in-itself-being of the thing remains, over against knowledge, *forever pretension*, insofar as we never really get free of the experiential consciousness. Whether the once-performed experience-thesis can be legitimately maintained always depends on the further course of experiencing. In experiential thinking, then, something is posited to which *being-experienced is in itself contingent*; and yet, as soon as we cut off all further being-experienced, the experiential positing of that in-itself *hangs in the air*, since it is finally not demonstrated and in principle not demonstrable. And this hangs together with the fact that experience, *by its very sense, posits transcendence*.

Husserl then specifies *why* the thing is given only inadequately: it is given *through adumbrations* (*Darstellungen*), through "appearances." The thing is in experience given and yet again *not* given. Every single experience, and likewise every connected, finitely closed experience-*series*, gives the experienced object in a *principle-incomplete* appearance — one-sidedly, or many-sidedly, but never *all*\-sidedly, according to all that the thing "is." Complete experience of a thing is something *infinite*. To demand a complete experience of an object in a finitely closed act — or, what is the same, a finitely closed perception-series that means the thing in a complete, final, conclusive way — is a *countersense*, something *excluded by the essence of experience*. Husserl admits this is here only an assertion whose full grounding he cannot give, but one that the hearer can see by transposing himself into the sense of a thing-perception: the perceived thing is always given perspectivally, with a horizon of co-meant but unfilled sides, and the filling can go on without end.

With this the contrast is prepared. *Quite otherwise* than with simple (Husserl later said: *natural*) experience, and first of all with *empirical* perception, it stands with that perception of *pure experiences* which *cuts off all empirical apprehension* of them and takes them *according to their pure sense in themselves*. To exhibit this, Husserl gives the celebrated example of the feeling. Let us look at a feeling we are just now experiencing and grasp it *purely in itself*. We do not draw into our grasping the "*empirical apperception*" — that is, we do not apprehend the feeling as the feeling-*state* in which *we*, these empirical persons, these human beings, find ourselves under the momentary psychophysical circumstances. We draw in nothing of nature; we omit every drawing-in of the feeling into psychophysical nature, every positing of it as something dependent on our bodily states, taking its place in the objective time measured by clocks. All that we leave aside. And then — this is the decisive result — *not a Nothing remains*, but there remains the *feeling in itself*, which in itself is what it is, whether the whole of nature is or is not, and which is *not touched at all* even if we thought the whole of nature annulled. The reduction does not impoverish the field; it discloses a being that stands wholly on its own.

Husserl then stages an objection that is really a sharpening. One could say: what is this strange *wanting-to-cross-out* the empirical apperception, since it is, after all, *there* and *always* there? If I look at the feeling — if I perform reflection in *Locke's* sense — the feeling stands there as *my* feeling, as the pleasure *I* feel, as the pain that hurts *me*. The objection grants that reflection finds the feeling already empirically apperceived as a state of an embodied I. Husserl's answer is a model of phenomenological discrimination. *Certainly*, the empirical apprehension is there and is a *component* of the reflection. But now we direct our attention, on the one hand, to the feeling *in itself*, and on the other hand, to the *apprehension* interwoven with it, likewise *in itself*. It is obviously *two different things* to *perform* the empirical apprehension and live in it — thereby meaning the feeling in this or that relation to me, the empirical person, with his body — and, on the other hand, to *grasp and mean the feeling in itself*; and again, to grasp and mean the *interwoven apprehension itself*, with all that it in itself is and makes up. This apprehension *implies* the I-apprehension: I find myself as this human being who just now stands at the lectern in this lecture hall, and find myself in the relevant feeling-state. But this perceptual apprehension is itself a *being* that I can grasp and posit *in and for itself* — and this being is *another* than the being whose positing is the very *function* of the apprehension, namely the being posited *as* the perceived I-thing, the I-person in this lecture hall. And here Husserl performs the decisive thought-experiment. Let us *fingiere* that what the empirical I- and environment-perception posits here is *false* — that I am not the one I posit myself as, that this body is not at all, or not as just posited, that the environment is in truth not. Well — *then this re-valuation of the perception changes nothing whatever about its own being*, the being that I take and posit, in the reflecting gaze, *as a being in itself*. The apprehension's *positing of* the world may collapse; the apprehension *itself*, as a lived-experience, stands untouched.

Husserl draws the conclusion that defines the reduction. I can thus grasp and posit the feeling *in and for itself*; and if I find, *as one with it*, an apprehension and positing that relates it as a psychic state to the natural object "human" and thereby orders it into nature, then I grasp and posit — in a *new act* — precisely this apprehension and positing *in itself*. But "in itself" here means: I make the empirical apprehension an *object* in and for itself, *yet I do not now make it my own* (*zu eigen*). That is to say, I now *refrain* from positing further what it posited, and from making any use of what it posited as actuality. This is the exact phenomenological sense of the reduction as it first appears in Husserl's lecturing: not the destruction or denial of the empirical apprehension, but the *turning of it into an object* while *withholding participation* in its positing — looking *at* the world-positing instead of living *in* it, and so retaining the lived-experience in the absolute, presuppositionless givenness that the next sections will name the *phenomenological attitude*.

### **§ 15\. The Phenomenological Attitude. Delimitation of the Phenomenological Beholding or Perception of the Pure Lived-Experience over against the Inner Perception of the Psychic Lived-Experience**

Husserl now generalizes the result won on the single example of the feeling. In *this* way we can proceed with respect to *all* experiences. We can give ourselves, quite generally, a *new kind of attitude* that switches off every empirical, transcendent attitude. From now on we accept *no single* object posited in the empirical attitude as actuality; we let ourselves be given no single thing that is to be given in the empirical attitude. We no longer *perform* any empirical attitude, no natural, naïve positing of things, of nature in the widest sense. Every empirical act that may, so to speak, press itself upon us, or that we have just performed, we set *in brackets* (*in Klammern*) — we accept what it offers us as being in no way at all. Instead of *living in* its performance, and afterward naïvely holding fast its positing with its sense, we *look at it itself*, making it and whatever it offers us in itself into an *object* — an object that is no nature and contains nothing of nature-positing any longer. Husserl puts the resulting relation to experience pointedly: *so we appropriate all experience to ourselves*. Not in that we *make* experience and, living in it, pass experiential judgments, pursue experiential theories and sciences; rather, we *take up* every experiential act, every experiential judgment, every complete or incomplete experiential cognition into *our* realm, as the *pure existence* that it itself is. What is *completely omitted*, by contrast, is all co-positing of that existence which the experience, *as* experience, pretends of itself to posit. The image of *bracketing* and the formula of "appropriating" the act while withholding its world-positing are, in nuce, the *epoché* of *Ideas I*, here making one of its earliest appearances.

The attitude thus described is called, over against the natural attitude, the *phenomenological attitude*. Husserl fixes the correlative terminology. If "experience" (*Erfahrung*), in the natural attitude, is the title that brings the objectivities of the natural attitude to givenness — a title, that is, for *every giving consciousness* of the natural attitude — then *phenomenological beholding* or *seeing* (*Schauung*, *Erschauung*) may be the title comprising the *giving acts of the phenomenological attitude*. That the realm of phenomenological objectivities is one *completely separate* from the realm of nature, he says, need hardly be stated, since "object of nature" means precisely *experienceable* being, being determinable on the basis of experience — whereas the phenomenological object is precisely not posited as existing in nature.

Husserl reserves the articulation of the phenomenological realm for later and stays with the task of clarifying the *peculiarity* of the phenomenological attitude. He issues a crucial warning, one that separates his enterprise sharply from the empirical psychology of his day: the phenomenological beholding — more closely, the *perceiving grasp* of those phenomenological objectivities indicated by the examples — must *not be confused* with *Lockean reflection*, or, as one tends to say in German, *inner perception* (also *self-perception*). By the latter one understands the perception of one's *own* psychic experiences according to their own content. But it is clear that this perception is *empirical* perception, and *remains* empirical perception so long as not *every* empirical positing has been switched off. Consequently, Husserl insists, not only must every positing of the rest of nature — things in space and time — remain out of play, *including the positing of one's own body and of the psychophysical relation of experiences to it*; but *also* the positing of the *empirical I*, which is thought as a person connected with the body — and *not only every foreign but also one's own empirical I*. This is the radical point that distinguishes phenomenological reduction from mere introspection: even *my own* empirical ego, as a worldly person, is bracketed. Only when one carries out the *phenomenological reduction* consequently and completely, and in the immanent description of the psychic experience *no longer apprehends and posits it as a state, as the "experience" of the experiencing I and as a being in objective time*, does one gain the *pure* experience as the object of phenomenological perception — and only then does one perform genuine phenomenological perception in its *radical* difference from empirical perception. Inner perception still posits a worldly soul; phenomenological perception posits nothing worldly at all.

### **§ 16\. Descartes' Fundamental Consideration and the Phenomenological Reduction**

Husserl situates his method historically, in a passage of lasting interest for the self-understanding of phenomenology. The *first* philosopher who carried out a phenomenological reduction — though he performed it only to surrender it again at once — was *Descartes*. It is a most remarkable fact, Husserl says, that the *fundamental consideration* opening the entire developmental course of modern philosophy was nothing other than the *staging of a phenomenological reduction*. Remarkable, because herein in truth lies the *beginning of all genuine scientific philosophy* and the *springing-point of all genuine philosophical problems*. The *correlate* of phenomenological perception is the *cogitatio* in the Cartesian sense — for which we may say: the *pure*, as opposed to the *empirical*, consciousness. A *cogitatio*, a consciousness, is any sensing, representing, perceiving, remembering, expecting; any judging, inferring; any feeling, desiring, willing. These are all well-known matters — all that everyone, as he says, immediately *sees* "in himself," and sees in such a way that he cannot doubt it at all.

But Husserl at once marks the difference between this familiar self-evidence and the *pure* givenness he intends. All that the empirical psychologist claims as the psychic *experience* of the respective human or animal I-consciousness first becomes *cogitatio in the absolute sense* — in the sense of *pure phenomenological givenness* — *through* the phenomenological reduction. And only then is the givenness pure and absolute also in the sense that the simple positing of it as a *This*, as a being, leaves open possible doubt *in no direction* — indeed in such a way that doubt here would lose all sense. Precisely *this* — indubitability — was what mattered to Descartes, Husserl notes, *whereas for us it is not the main thing*. The intention toward a *reform of all the sciences*, one that would make possible their construction as absolutely valid science and exclude every deceptive appearance, every establishment of pseudo-sciences, is significant enough; and in the last analysis philosophy surely is nothing other than the *intention toward absolute knowledge*. *But* whether and how knowledge in the phenomenological attitude can *serve* to ground absolute knowledge in general — including absolute knowledge in the *experiential* sphere — cannot be made out, or even so much as understood, from the outset. And here Husserl delivers his verdict on Descartes: Descartes' own procedure *failed*, because, *without* an investigation of the sense of absolute science and *without* the establishment of a systematic phenomenology — of whose very existence he had no inkling — he believed himself entitled to venture the grounding of absolute science straightaway. Descartes reached the field of pure consciousness and immediately abandoned it, pressing on to prove God and the external world, precisely because he lacked the systematic discipline that would have shown what that field contains and demands.

Husserl therefore narrows his own aim, distinguishing it from the Cartesian ambition. *We* are not interested here in *absolute universal science*, but in *science within the phenomenological attitude*. He leaves it to other considerations to determine *whether* and *how far* science in such an attitude may be called "absolute," and how far, *beyond that*, absolute science is possible at all. The methodological modesty is deliberate: the course will explore what the phenomenological attitude *discloses* and what cognition is possible *within* it, bracketing the grand epistemological question of whether phenomenology can found the totality of knowledge — a restraint that is itself a lesson drawn from Descartes' premature collapse of the reduction.

### **§ 17\. Independence of the Phenomenological Judgment from the Natural Judgment**

Husserl names the chief difficulty of the whole method: it is the *cognition of the essence of the phenomenological attitude itself*, and the *prevention of every false delimitation* of it. He clarifies it by a concrete enactment. When he now perceives this room with the persons present in it, what does the phenomenological attitude offer? In the natural attitude, he perceives these things; in doing so they stand before him; a unity of space encompasses them and his body, to which he assigns his I, "this well-known I"; and about all this he has asserted, passing perceptual judgments and forever passing new ones. That is the natural attitude. *Now* he, as it were, turns the gaze and performs a *new* attitude. The people, the benches "*still stand there*." But upon this he does *not* ground the judgment that they stand there; he does not assert *about* these things, does not investigate what may hold for them. He has, to be sure, just judged, and he may perform anew the judgment "there stand these benches"; *but he switches this judgment off* — he does not take what it presents as true among the truths he *admits* in the new attitude. What *does* belong to his new sphere is the judgment *as* a "this here," with its own properties — namely that it judges about benches in "this room," about things in space, in nature — yet *so that he thereby merely describes what this judgment judges*, what it posits as true, *while he does not himself take up the as-true-posited as such*. The object of phenomenology is thus the *judging* with its sense and its positing-character, not the judged state of affairs in its claimed truth.

Husserl states the resulting rule of validity. As *being* there now holds for him *exclusively* what he posits in his *admitted* judgments, and *not* what he posited in the non-admitted ones, or may posit again. He is careful to forestall two misreadings. He does *not* thereby say that the bracketed being holds for him as *non*\-being; nor even that he *doubts* such being, or somehow suspects it. Rather, he *omits every position-taking* on it. That the judgment is a judgment about these benches can be meant in such a way that he performs *not the slightest assertion* that these benches are, that the judgment with its positing is right or not. The reduction is neither denial nor doubt but *abstention*.

To make this stance intuitive, Husserl draws an illuminating parallel with *critical re-examination*. We behave quite similarly when someone casts doubt on one of our judgments, or when we ourselves feel the need for critical reflection and wish to re-examine a judgment "*without prejudice*." "Without prejudice" does *not* mean that we have become wavering, or that we have already surrendered our judgment. We may be entirely firm in our conviction, judging afterward exactly as before. *And yet*, without prejudice, we examine — which here too means: we *switch off* the judgment for the new consideration; we do not accept the judged as truth within it; we make *no use* of what it asserts as true. And Husserl points out the danger of failing in this: if we forget it, slipping unawares (or with other, merely verbal turns of phrase) back into the original attitude, and make use, wholly or partly, of the asserted content during the re-examination and grounding, we fall into a well-known error — the *circulus vitiosus*. Hence the critical attitude is in fact *akin* to the phenomenological one. Where it is first to be examined whether a *supposed* being may hold as *actual* being, there the supposed stands *in question*, and we may not treat it as already actual and true; if we do hold it for such, we must switch off, *bracket*, this holding-for-actual. The epoché is thus the rigorous generalization of the methodological honesty already required by any presuppositionless critique.

From this Husserl derives the *independence* thesis that titles the section. It is evident that judgments of which I make no use — better, propositions that I *in principle* take as no premises for any establishments within a scientific circuit — are *without influence* on those establishments. It is therefore *absolutely certain* that my establishments remain, in their truth, *unaffected* by whether those bracketed judgments hold or not. So if, as phenomenologist, I switch off all empirical judgments in the ordinary sense, my *phenomenological* assertions remain unaffected — unaffected if, as a naturally thinking human being, I again pass empirical judgments and lend my trust to natural science; and equally unaffected if, as a dogged skeptic, I doubt the truth of the empirical judgments, or even reject them, whether with right and sense or not. From the standpoint of phenomenology these are *private affairs* that do not concern it, precisely because it has switched them off. And switched off, along with them, is *every judgment that explicitly or implicitly posits the existence of the phenomenologist himself as a member of nature*. The phenomenologist's own worldly existence is among the bracketed.

It is worth recording that Husserl later (probably in 1921\) appended a substantial *critical* qualification to the two sentences asserting unaffectedness — a note he reworked into a separate appendix. There he warns that one cannot put the matter quite so simply. By analogy with geometry: if I make use of only certain propositions and do not judge about other domains of propositions, it does *not* follow that the ones and the others are, *as truths* (actual or hypothetical), *independent* of one another. The real question is *in what sense* phenomenological and ontological judging are independent, and what their independence can legitimately mean — for certain *dependencies* do obtain. If I posit that a thing exists, or, more generally, posit the possible existence of a thing as such, a *rule* is thereby prescribed for every I's possible experiencing consciousness. Nonetheless I can construct, describe, and judge with evident truth about a whole *system* of concordant experience related to an object that I treat *purely as an intentional object*, without judging in the least about the thing *simpliciter*; and likewise I can, *eidetically*, consider "a thing in general" purely as an intentional possibility of a possible and concordant experiencing, constructing the possible concordant experience-systems, *without* judging *ontologically* about the possibilities of things in general. Conversely, I can pursue ontology and, in the purely ontological attitude, never strike upon a phenomenological judgment. It is even possible that I judge falsely ontologically and rightly phenomenologically, or the reverse. *But* — and this is the correction — an independence of the *truths themselves* does *not* obtain, by virtue of the binding essence-correlations between them. Independence of the *judging activity*, of the *going-after truth*, of *cognition* itself, does not mean independence of the relevant judgments *as* truths (or as pretended, hypothetical truths), nor independence of the states of affairs. What remains decisively important, Husserl concludes in that note, is that I *can* judge purely phenomenologically and win phenomenologically insightful truths *without taking a stand on the ontological*; and, as regards the actuality of nature, that *even an eidetic judging does not presuppose the existence of the world*. Against (objectivist) empiricism — which recognizes only "positive," objective experiential sciences — it must be shown that there *is* pure consciousness, and that pure consciousness, however modified, *remains over as my ego cogito even if the world did not exist*: I cannot strike out my ego, but the realm of ontological possibilities and that of pure eidetic phenomenology alike are *independent of the existence of an objective world*. This later self-correction does not retract the independence the lecture asserts; it refines it into the precise claim that the *cognitive enterprise* of phenomenology is autonomous, resting on the indubitable residue of the *ego cogito*, even though the truths of the several regions remain woven together by essential correlation.

## **Third Chapter: Preliminary Discussion of Some Objections against the Aim of the Phenomenological Reduction**

### **§ 18\. The Objection of Solipsism**

Having delimited the reduction, Husserl turns to a series of objections that the new attitude naturally provokes — the first being the charge of *solipsism*. Is phenomenological research, then, solipsistic? Does it restrict research to the individual I, and more narrowly to the domain of its individual psychic phenomena? *Nothing less than that.* Husserl unfolds the meaning of *solus ipse*: it would mean "I alone am," or "I switch off all the rest of the world, only *not* myself and my psychic states and acts." But this is precisely the *reverse* of what the phenomenologist does. As phenomenologist I switch off *myself* just as much as all the world (Husserl later glossed "myself" with "(world)"), and no less my psychic states and acts, which, *as mine*, are precisely *nature* (later: belong to "this human person"). The solipsist exempts his own mental life from the bracketing; the phenomenologist brackets it too. Husserl then diagnoses the *source* of the solipsistic error: the countersensical epistemology of solipsism arises because one — ignorant of the *radical principle* of the phenomenological reduction, yet aiming likewise at the switching-off of transcendence — *confuses psychological (and psychologistic) immanence with the genuinely phenomenological*. (In a later revision Husserl tightened this to the confusing of psychological immanence with the phenomenological in a *psychologistic* manner, after the switching-off of all *worldly* transcendence.) Equivalently, a *misunderstanding of the proper sense of transcendence* and of its switching-off leads one to mistake *psychological* immanence — which is the solipsistic one, the immanence of *my* mental states within *my* worldly soul — for *phenomenological* immanence. Husserl sets the strictly epistemological treatment aside here. The essential point is that phenomenological immanence is *not* the private interior of an individual psyche but the field of pure consciousness that opens only after the bracketing of *all* worldly being, the proper ego included.

### **§ 19\. The Objection against the Phenomenological Switch-off-ability of the I**

A second objection presses on the bracketing of the *own I*. One may object: the phenomenological reduction, which wants to switch off the own I, is something *unthinkable*. We are to reduce to the mere *cogitatio in itself*, to "pure consciousness" — but *whose* cogitatio, *whose* pure consciousness? The relation to the I is essential to the *cogitatio*; and so the absolutely given is, in fact, as Descartes wanted, the *cogito* — the I-think, not a free-floating thinking. Husserl's answer carefully separates two senses of "I." The *possibility* of switching off all empirical (later: *worldly*) transcendence in the indicated sense — of bracketing the existence of all nature — is *indisputable*; and with it, therefore, the bracketing of the existence of the own *empirical* I, about which, within the frame of phenomenology, no judgment is passed and of which no use is made. So the objection can only mean that, *over against* the empirical I, a *pure I* must still be assumed as something *inseparable* from the *cogitationes*. On *that*, Husserl says, we need make *no decision* here. We need only say that phenomenological research can and must speak of *everything it finds* in its attitude. If it finds — while the natural world, with things and persons, world-space and world-time, stands bracketed and is thus not there *as existence* for it — that something like a *pure I*, or pure time, or whatever else, is given and to be posited (and, Husserl adds in a later note, this *is* indeed the case), well, *then it is something phenomenological*. The dispute over the pure ego is thus left open as an *internal* phenomenological question, to be settled by what beholding actually discloses, not by an a-priori objection that the reduction is "unthinkable."

### **§ 20\. Objections against the Absolute Character of the Phenomenologically Given and against the Possibility of a Phenomenological Science and the Phenomenological Grounding of Natural Science**

Husserl now states, in their full force, the *serious* doubts one can raise against phenomenological knowledge — doubts he will answer, but only after letting them stand sharply. The first targets the claimed *absoluteness* of phenomenological givenness. One will say: the experiential givennesses are switched off, and with them all experiential judgments, *because* experience, though a *giving* act, is in principle *not a finally-giving* one. (A footnote fixes the terminology: "experience" here means *objective-natural* experience; the empirical is *objective* experience.) Such givenness includes in principle the possibility that the given *be, not be, or be otherwise*. Phenomenological beholding is supposed to be *free* of this defect: what it gives is *not mere appearance but the being itself*. But — the objector asks — can this really be maintained? Is *absolute* givenness ever attainable? (Husserl later softened "absolute character" to "claim of absolute evidence.") Husserl flags that he will *himself* soon voice grounded doubts on this very point, so the objection is not lightly dismissed.

The second doubt is even more deflating: *even if* phenomenological givenness could vindicate its absolute character, *what would that help us much?* Even *Descartes* could make nothing further of the doubtless being of the *cogitatio*. It is not to be foreseen how a *science* is to be established here — let alone a *natural* science. (Husserl later expanded this to the question of how, *thereby, from sources of absolute evidence*, natural science is to be grounded.) For *nature* (later: *the universe*) is, after all, everywhere our interest. Does one hope, on a purely phenomenological ground — by some *fabulous modes of inference* — to *reach nature*, and so to win, over against the switched-off experiential knowledge of nature, a *higher, absolute* natural knowledge? This Husserl rejects as *countersense from the outset*. Nature is, by its very essence, knowable *only* by way of experience: *nature and empirical knowledge are correlatives*. Every inference that ends with an assertion about objectivities of nature requires, if it is rational, *premises that are ultimately grounded in experience*. One cannot deduce the world from pure consciousness; the dream of a presuppositionless rational physics is incoherent. These two doubts — about the attainability of absolute givenness, and about the very point of a phenomenological science cut off from nature — set the agenda that the fourth and fifth chapters will have to meet.

### **§ 21\. The Motivationlessness of the Phenomenological Reduction**

Before turning to the grounds of doubt against the *absolute character* of phenomenological beholding, Husserl answers the *hypothetical* worry just raised — the worry that phenomenology is pointless because nature is "our only interest." His answer is a clarification of the *motivelessness* of the reduction. One need *impute no motives whatever* to phenomenology for *why* it switches off the experiential positing. *As* phenomenology, it has no such motives; the individual phenomenologist may have them, but those are *private matters*. Phenomenology simply switches off the empirical positing and restricts itself to what then remains. The only legitimate question is then: *what is there to research*, and *whether room remains for a science* at all. (A footnote here is important for dating Husserl's conception: "beholding" stands for a *reduced perception*, and "phenomenology" is here thought as an *empirical* science on the ground of the phenomenological reduction — thus *not* as eidetic phenomenology, and *without* the question of "apodictic" justification. The course is pursuing the *experiencing* phenomenology announced in the synopsis, not yet the eidetic discipline of *Ideas I*.) Accordingly, Husserl says, one may *not* declare that "our only interest is nature." The natural scientist may say so, but even that is his *private* view. The phenomenologist's interest is precisely *not* nature as the existence posited in experience and in the universal experiential science. And whether, and how, phenomenological research can come to *mean* something for natural knowledge itself is, of course, *no question that precedes* the establishment of phenomenology. The reduction needs no external justification and no promissory note about its eventual usefulness for physics; it is entitled to constitute its own field and to ask, only afterward, what that field contains. With this, the merely hypothetical objection is set aside, and the way is clear to confront the *real* question — the absoluteness of phenomenological givenness — in the chapter to come.

### **§ 22\. Preliminary Reflection for the Discussion of the Objections against the Absoluteness of Phenomenological Cognition**

Before weighing the doubts about the *absoluteness* of phenomenological cognition (later: about the absoluteness of the *evidence* of phenomenological cognition), Husserl interposes a reflection that reframes the stakes and, in the process, opens a vista onto the internal differentiation of the phenomenological field. He begins from the natural scientist's situation. The natural scientist's heart hangs on natural knowledge; he sees that *experience has its doubtless right*, and that on the basis of experience doubtlessly valuable knowledge is to be won in infinite fullness. Yet — and this is the leveling observation — the doubtless right of experiential knowledge does *not* mean that it is *absolute* knowledge. The natural scientist himself does not claim this; he knows very well that each of his ever-so-methodically-exact theses can be *essentially modified* by future experience. Well then, Husserl reasons, perhaps phenomenological knowledge — the knowledge that switches off all (later: *objective*) empirical positing — is *likewise* knowledge in the genuine sense; perhaps it too has its doubtless right; and perhaps here too there is a sphere of *rich scientific insights*. If so, then for the *establishment* of phenomenology no further proof is needed. The argument neutralizes the demand for absoluteness by parity: natural science is valuable without being absolute, so phenomenology need not be absolute to be valuable.

Husserl presses the point with a striking concession. *Even if* absolute indubitability is an *Idea* not fully realizable in any actual science — not even in phenomenology; *even if* phenomenological establishments can deceive, or turn out to be hasty and in need of modification — *even then* phenomenology, like natural science, keeps its value, *provided only* it is evident that, *in principle*, phenomenological givenness is *real givenness* and phenomenological method *real method*. And perhaps, he adds, phenomenological givenness *can* in fact be vindicated as *absolute* givenness, while on the other hand its *scientific working-up* — like every theorizing, for instance in the form of linguistic fixing — carries its own sources of deception. In that case phenomenological research would stand *nearer to the Idea of absolute science* than any other science. And surely already for *this* reason: that the *right* of every methodical step in genuine science must be able to *show itself* — and can show itself *only in immediate givenness*, hence in the *phenomenological* sphere. (A later insertion specifies that what must show itself are "the experiencing and theoretical achievements and the achievement-formations constituting themselves in them.") Phenomenology is thus the place where the warrant of *all* science must finally be redeemed, whether or not it attains perfect apodicticity itself.

Husserl closes with an anticipation of the greatest structural importance for the rest of the course. Perhaps, he says, matters stand *somewhat otherwise* still. Perhaps *within* the phenomenological reduction itself one must *again distinguish* between different *modes of givenness* — among them some that are absolutely doubtless and some that are not. And perhaps the title "phenomenology" is more the title of a *method* than of a single *discipline*: perhaps there are *various* phenomenological disciplines, some related to *absolute* givennesses, others to "*incomplete*" givennesses. This is the hinge on which the next chapter turns. The reduction does not deliver a homogeneous field of nothing but apodictic data; it delivers a *graded* field, in which the absolute self-givenness of the present *cogitatio* is interwoven with non-absolute modes — recollection, expectation, the givenness of the enduring stream — whose warrant is of a different and weaker rank. To investigate this is to *go beyond* the sphere of the absolutely given while remaining strictly within the reduction.

## **Fourth Chapter: The Going-Beyond of Phenomenology beyond the Sphere of the Absolutely Given**

### **§ 23\. The Problem of the Absolute Character of Phenomenological Givenness**

Husserl now takes up the reservations against the absolute character of phenomenological givenness, and announces that pursuing them will be useful precisely because it lets us cast some glances into the *kinds* of such givenness. The result he previews is the program of the entire chapter. It will show, in fact, that the phenomenological reduction *first* leads us to the *absolute givenness* we provisionally called phenomenological beholding — to phenomenological *perception*, whose absolute and indubitable character can indeed be vindicated. The live, present *cogitatio*, grasped in reduced perception, is given without remainder and beyond doubt. *But* — and here is the going-beyond — interwoven with this perception, *other modes of givenness* soon present themselves (always *within* the phenomenological attitude) whose absolute character can *no longer* be vindicated in the same sense, namely *as indubitability*. Husserl therefore states the methodological consequence: we shall have to *extend* the concept of phenomenological beholding so that it runs *parallel to empirical experience*, becoming, as it were, *phenomenological experience* (*phänomenologische Erfahrung*) — encompassing both phenomenological *presentation* (*Gegenwärtigung*, the giving of what is present) and phenomenological *presentification* (*Vergegenwärtigung*, the giving of what is not present, as in recollection and expectation). Just as empirical experience reaches beyond the momentary perception to the remembered and the anticipated, so phenomenological experience must reach beyond the absolutely given present *cogitatio* to the non-present phases of conscious life — and the question of the *warrant* of that reach is the problem the chapter must solve.

### **§ 24\. The Absolute Givenness of the Phenomenologically Perceived. The Senselessness of a Switching-Off in Phenomenological Perception**

Husserl enacts the problem on the perception itself. I perceive, switch off the existence of the perceived thingly object, and hold fast, *as a This*, the perception itself and in itself. *But* the perception is an *enduring* being: it lasts, in that it *has just been* and *is now still*, and in that the now changes again into a just-past while a new now sets in. *How does it stand here with absolute givenness?* The *past* of the perception is no longer (now) given. If one says it *was* given, then it is questionable whether this "was" is given. In the now it is supposed to be given *as* a "was," as having-been-given — but perhaps that is deception; memory, after all, often deceives. Perhaps I (merely) mean that it was given, and it begins only in the now. And "in the now": as soon as I want to judge thus, constatingly, and to grasp what I *really* had given as now, it is already gone — the now has become a new now, and in it what I wanted to register stands there as *past*. The past *transcends* the now, and so, it seems, I must switch it off, analogously to the empirically transcendent. *But now this whole undertaking of switching-off loses its sense* (Husserl later qualified: *if the epistemological interest is the determining one*). For we wanted, for the judging investigation, to switch off the *non*\-given, in order to bring a given *in a stricter sense* into the judgment-sphere. But now we bring in *nothing at all*; the switching-off has become so radical that we find *nothing more to judge*. Carried to the limit, the demand for nothing-but-the-instantaneous-now annihilates its own object, since the very now cannot be caught and registered without already being past.

Husserl then halts the slide. *Let us not be misled\!* Directed at the perception, we grasp it as a wholly *immediate This\!* and as the *unity of a duration*; and if we add nothing further and purely accept the positing performed *with* this This\!, taking the perception purely *as this enduring thing there*, then *every doubt loses its sense*. He shows why by analyzing what doubt itself requires. When we doubt whether something only *seems* to be or *really* is, that plainly means: we doubt whether the relevant *seeming-to-be* — the appearing in the manner of perception, or memory, or indication, or judgment — is *valid*, whether in truth perhaps *nothing corresponds* to it. *But precisely thereby* this appearing, this perceiving, remembering, judging, is *presupposed as given* — as in fact it is given. (Husserl refines: one should distinguish the *appearing as such*, the perceived or remembered or thought as such — in a word, the "*mere meaning*" — from the *being* that would correspond to it in case of its validity.) At all events, *doubt presupposes givenness* — the doubtless givenness of the very meaning that is placed in doubt. Therefore, Husserl concludes, *this perception, this phenomenon of enduring empirical givenness, is given in its own-being and in its duration, and given absolutely.* The reduced perception, taken purely as the enduring lived-experience it is, is the bedrock of indubitable givenness — and the attempt to shrink it to a durationless instant is not a heightening but a *destruction* of evidence.

A rich editorial footnote marks the systematic significance of this moment: *here, for the first time, the idea of an apodictic critique of phenomenological experience* — according to its basic forms, perception, retention, recollection, and so on — *makes its appearance*. The fourth chapter shows, step by step, how the phenomenological reduction is exercised *on a perception* (according to its intentional structure) so that a phenomenologically *pure* perception is gained; and likewise in free retention, in recollection, in expectation. The order is essential: *first* pure phenomenological experience must be *gained*, and *only then* can apodictic critique be exercised on it. (The note cross-refers to the appendix in which Husserl seeks a "self-understanding" about the fourth and fifth chapters.) A further footnote sets out the temporal structure presupposed throughout: the perception has a *flowing point of originary self-presence* and, beyond it, a *horizon of "retentional" givenness* as just-past, and on the other side an *immediate future-horizon of protentional givenness*; once a perception has elapsed, *mere retention* takes its place, for a while still alive in the form of a progressive "*sinking*," until finally it is wholly sunk. This is the doctrine of internal time-consciousness, here brought to bear directly on the problem of the reduction's reach.

### **§ 25\. The Retention Implied in Phenomenological Perception as a "Transcendence" within the Phenomenological Attitude**

From the result of §24, Husserl says, one will find the *right position* regarding the *just-past perception* that is co-included in the givenness of the enduring perception. This "been" is itself a (later: *absolutely*) *given*, a *This* — but given *as* just-having-been, as the *given past-phase* of the given enduring thing. We must take it *exactly so and not otherwise*, and with *no other content* than that with which it gives itself. Its *description* and *analysis* — in particular the *comparing* analysis of this been and the now-phase — is, to be sure, a further question. But at all events the phenomenological beholding and grasping *is a judgment-ground*; it opens a ground on which thinking can establish itself. What such thinking can *seriously accomplish*, and whether it can deliver *science*, is not yet decided and will have to be thought through. Above all, Husserl says, we must still ask: *how far is the frame to be drawn?* — how far does the field of phenomenologically admissible givenness extend?

And here the decisive admission is made. Holding fast to the phenomenological reduction, we come upon *some things* that can *no longer* be claimed as "absolutely given" in the same sense as what was named before. We could *not* yield to the inclination to let *only the now* of perception (of phenomenological beholding) count. The now is the *eternally fleeing boundary-point* between past and future — and even *that* it is this could not be asserted if we wanted to switch off *retention*. *Therewith we have admitted a "transcendence" within the phenomenological attitude*: insofar as we admit not only the retention *itself* as phenomenological being, but *also that of which it is retention*. Every now of retention is retention of a *not-now*, of a *just-having-been*; and this been, we said, *is given*. This is the precise and paradoxical result: the reduction, far from confining us to a punctual self-presence, *must* admit a giving of the *non*\-present — the just-elapsed — and this giving is a *transcendence* (a reaching beyond the now) that nonetheless falls *within* the phenomenological sphere and carries genuine evidence. Husserl reinforces the point by an argument from consistency: to *mistrust* such givenness would mean throwing ourselves into the arms of *absolute skepticism*; for such *retentional evidence* is presupposed *also in empirical perception* — and so the natural scientist who builds on empirical perception, and with him every philosopher who trusts natural knowledge, may *not* suddenly play the hypercritic precisely when it is a question of phenomenology. One cannot demand of phenomenology a stricter standard of self-givenness than one already, tacitly, relies on everywhere else. Finally, Husserl notes, what holds of retention *within* the enduring perception will naturally also be granted of the so-called *free retention* that attaches immediately to a wholly elapsed perception — the freshly-past that lingers and sinks after the act itself is over.

### **§ 26\. The Phenomenological Recollection and Its Possibility of Deception. Transformation of Empirical Memory into Phenomenological Memory**

Husserl extends the analysis to *recollection* (*Wiedererinnerung*), first to the recollection still running *within* retention. (A footnote of methodological importance specifies that *eidetic* reduction has *not* been performed here: the investigation considers the phenomenologically reduced consciousness in its *individual flux* — phenomenology as the description of the singular stream of consciousness, not as a doctrine of essences.) The phenomenologically reduced content sinks back into phenomenological *been-ness*; and while it is still conscious *as* sinking, there appears the consciousness of *repetition*, of the *renewal* of the elapsed run of what had been conscious, in the form of recollection. The question is sharp: is the *recollected* — what runs off as repetition — really *the same* as what elapsed? And all the more so in the case of a recollection *not* unified with the consciousness of retention, a memory of something long past?

Husserl works it through with a concrete case. Suppose I saw a process yesterday and at once performed a reflection on *this perception*. Now I recollect this perception, and I perform the phenomenological reduction: I do *not* claim that yesterday, in natural reality, such a psychic process elapsed at such-and-such objectively determinable time. I claim *only*, first, that *this* is there — this consciousness of recollection — and second, that the perception remembered *in* it (the perception of such-and-such a process) *in fact was, and is now again conscious*. With this, Husserl says, we would have *new "transcendences" within the phenomenological immanence*: the reduction admits not only the present recollecting but also the *past perceiving* that the recollection presents as having-been. But are such recollections *justified*? Justified as *absolute indubitabilities*? Here Husserl does not flinch: as indubitability — *by no means\!* Memory deceives, empirical memory as well as phenomenological memory. And he demonstrates the possibility of *phenomenological* deception by way of the empirical. For one can still reflect *within* empirical memory and, so to speak, produce a phenomenological one in it. Empirical memory tells me two events were *simultaneous*. Afterward I have a *new* recollection — far superior in force through a richer memory-connection — and it tells me the two events were *separated* by such-and-such clearly recollected events. I now perform the phenomenological reduction: the existence of the (later: *objective*) events is bracketed, like all nature, and the existence of the *recollected* natural events along with that of the perceived. What results for the phenomenological data? Obviously, the reduction of the *first* recollection yields a phenomenological *simultaneity* for the two event-*perceivings*, the *second* a phenomenological *non*\-simultaneity for the same two event-perceivings. (The "simultaneity" must not be taken *punctually*, just as the perceivings are not punctual.) This already suffices, Husserl says, to make *visible* the possibility of deception even of *phenomenological* recollection: two reductions of two memories of *my own perceivings* contradict each other, so at least one phenomenological recollection misrepresents the past phase of my stream.

From this Husserl draws a general structural insight about the *transformation* of empirical into phenomenological memory. Every unified recollection in the empirical sense — insofar as it gathers a manifold of earlier experience into *one* empirical consciousness — yields, through phenomenological reduction, a unified *phenomenological* recollection that grasps a manifold of the *phenomenological* kind into a phenomenologically reduced *re-consciousness*. Memory says, first: such-and-such *has been*. But it also makes possible a *reflection* that says: such-and-such *has been perceived* — or, *perception* of such-and-such *has been*. To the supposed simultaneity of the *perceived* there corresponds a simultaneity of the *perceiving*; and it is the latter that becomes *re-conscious* and becomes *objective* in phenomenological reflection. Hence every empirical deception that shows itself in subjective intuition yields a *deception-consciousness* for the phenomenologically reduced recollection. (The same, Husserl notes, could be shown for the domain of psychological self-experience, which he forgoes.) The upshot is that phenomenological recollection inherits both the *reach* and the *fallibility* of empirical memory: it genuinely gives the past phases of the stream, but it gives them *non-absolutely*, with the standing possibility of correction by a stronger memory.

### **§ 27\. The Possibility of the Phenomenological, but Not Absolute, Appropriation of the Entire Domain of Empirical Experience. The Expectation**

Husserl now generalizes the result to the whole of experience. We become aware, quite generally, that we can *appropriate the entire domain of the empirical* — that is, the experiential *positing* of every kind — in the same way, through phenomenological reduction; though, he immediately adds, *with the same result*: namely, that what is thereby won is genuine phenomenological givenness but *not* absolute givenness. To every empirical positing there corresponds a phenomenological one, and to the *fallibility* of the empirical there corresponds the fallibility of the phenomenological.

The illustration is *expectation* (*Erwartung*), and Husserl gives it in a vivid, almost playful concrete scene. My gaze observes a charming pair of bullfinches. The male always follows the female, who flies from tree to tree. Now the female flies across into the neighboring garden, and *I expect that the male will follow*. If we perform the phenomenological reduction, what is *nature* in this gets its bracketing. And is it not clear, Husserl asks, that every such empirical expectation harbors, as it were in itself, a *phenomenological* expectation? Does not the expectation of a certain content-determined *seeing* — the seeing of the male's subsequent flight — *hang upon* the seeing of the female's flying up? *De facto*, to be sure, we are turned toward the *things*, toward nature. But is it not evident that, instead of being directed at the things we experience, we *could have been directed at the experiencing of the things*, and at the fact that now such-and-such experience "*must*" come? And we can, from the outset, so direct ourselves — and, *instead of* living in the experiencing and naïvely performing its positing (thus "taking the existent as existing"), rather *turn to the experiencing* and *bracket* its positing. Present experiencing then *motivates* future experiencing, and this motivational structure is itself a phenomenological datum. *But* — and here is the lesson — the *purity* of the phenomenological reduction does *nothing* to confer *absolute* givenness-value on this motivation. Husserl drives the point home with a comic deflation: while I observe the scene, a gnat flies into my nose, and I must sneeze — and *with the expected seeing it is now nothing more*. The expectation, phenomenologically reduced or not, can simply *fail to be fulfilled*. Phenomenological expectation, like phenomenological recollection, is a legitimate but *non-apodictic* mode of phenomenological experience: it reaches beyond the absolutely given present without sharing in its indubitability.

### **§ 28\. The Phenomenological Experience. Its "Transcendence in the Immanence" and the Possibility of Deception. Empathy and Self-Experience**

Husserl deepens the picture by showing how *vast* the field of non-absolute phenomenological givenness is. Considered more closely, the stock of phenomenological *motivations* (Husserl later glossed: the peculiar shape of experience — anticipation, expectation) is *infinitely richer* than the few and rather vague titles "perception, retention, recollection, expectation" suggest. One must take account also of the empirically *objective* itself, which is perceptually or otherwise experienced. The phenomenological reduction always yields a *surprising abundance* of intuitive connections — connections *not* beheld in the manner of phenomenological *perception*, but in various *other* manners of *phenomenological experience*. And even where they are not beheld — where no meaning-directedness and positing interweaves with them — it can be seen that such connections, though *unmeant* (later: *unthematic*), are yet in a certain way *conscious and intended*, and that the *possibility* of turning toward them, and of constituting actual phenomenological experience, is *guaranteed*. Throughout, there is "*transcendence in the phenomenological immanence*," and throughout, the *possibility of deception*.

The example is a homely one: the perception of an enduring or changing thing — a cigar box standing before us — as to its spatial shape, its coloring, its physical-causal properties, so far as these are actually perceivable. The thing stands there; we *see*, holding to the seen as such, keeping all thinking at bay; we see the spatial shape of the box. The gaze *wanders* — following now this line, now that, jumping from one wood-grain to another. All of this, Husserl says, we can think transformed, through phenomenological reduction and with the changing of gaze-direction, into something like phenomenological *expectation-series*; and where actual expectation is not established (later: as an act-intention performed *from the I*), there are *series of motivations* that, by their essence, are to be transformed into such actual expectation-series. These phenomenological motivations have their *determinate syntax*, their form and rule, however arbitrarily the wandering gaze may glide over the object. To each determinate space-form there corresponds a determinate special syntax; to each gaze-position belongs a system of complicated possibilities; to each objective change there correspond precisely such-and-so-formed change-series of the phenomena. All this is thought *in* phenomenological reduction: gaze-position and gaze-change then reduce to certain *sensation- and apprehension-phenomena*; eye, head, and everything else fall, in their *existence*, under the reduction. The same would hold for the manifolds of sensations and apprehensions belonging to the coloring of the box, and specifically to this or that side-surface. And even the peculiar form of experience called *empathy* into foreign soul-life — and, earlier, of course, the empirical *I-experience* — could be drawn in, and would again reduce to motivation-connections of reduced phenomena, and as to form and kind to *quite determinate* ones. *But* — and Husserl returns to the central distinction — the *knowing of these connections of motivation* is *no knowing of phenomenological perception*; it is not that *seeing of absolute self-givenness* to which the evidence of the *cogitatio* first leads. *And this holds everywhere.* The field opened by the reduction is thus stratified: a thin core of absolute, perceptual self-givenness, surrounded by an immense periphery of motivational, anticipatory, recollective, and empathic connections that are genuinely phenomenological yet given only in the non-absolute mode of "experience."

### **§ 29\. The Going-Beyond the Domain of Absolute Givenness as Necessary Condition of the Possibility of a Phenomenological Science**

Now the architectonic question of the chapter is joined. Whether a *science* is possible with respect to the givennesses ascertainable by the phenomenological method will depend, first of all, on how we *interpret the value* of these modes of givenness that confront us as "phenomenological experience," as *transcendent phenomenological reflection* of various kinds. Husserl restates the dilemma precisely. If one wished to restrict oneself to the givenness of the *cogitatio* as *absolutely* doubtless givenness — that is, to *perception*\-givenness as it grows from phenomenological reduction and reflection *during the performance of the enduring cogitatio* — then we could forever say only "*this*," and how a *scientific* cognition should ever come about would not be foreseeable. A science cannot be built out of nothing but isolated, instantaneous, indubitable "thises." But now it shows itself that with retention, recollection, and expectation — and especially with the phenomenological reduction of *all* inner and outer natural experience, drawing in its manifold content — an *infinite abundance* of phenomenological givennesses streams toward us. (Husserl spells out the mechanism on recollection: with a recollection, not only is *one* reflection-and-reduction possible — the one that makes the recollection *itself*, as a present experience, the object of an *absolutely* giving phenomenological perception — but *also a second* reflection-and-reduction, which runs, so to speak, *within* the recollection and brings a *recollected* experience to givenness *as phenomenological been-ness*, though no longer to *absolute* givenness; and so in all other cases.) All these objectivities we call phenomenological are thought as *singular, individual* objectivities — each phenomenon as the individual *this-here*, as an *absolute one-time-ness* (*Einmaligkeit*).

Husserl then stages, and answers, the *psychologist's* objection (in a paragraph he later lightly struck in pencil). The psychologist will say: all that is just *psychic phenomena* — present or past, my own, or, if assumed on the basis of empathy, the other's. And certainly, Husserl grants, all this field of objectivities of a peculiar kind the psychologist *rightly* claims — *provided* it is *not* considered purely in phenomenological reduction, but apprehended as *I-experience* (later: *in the world*), as the phenomenon of an *empirical* I; though the psychologist, he adds pointedly, has no inkling of the great difficulties that this *naturalistic* apprehension of experiences, the very concept of "psychological experience," brings with it. So far all is in order. *But we insist on switching off the empirical subject as well* — and *then*, of course, the phenomenologically-singular being is *no psychological experience* at all. If one still wishes to speak of the "psychic," one would have to speak of a *transcendental-psychic* over against the *empirical-psychic*. This is a terminologically momentous distinction: the reduced field is "psychic" only in a wholly new, transcendental sense, severed from the worldly soul that empirical psychology studies.

Finally Husserl answers the chapter's guiding question. May one *admit* those kinds of phenomenological experience that do *not* have absolute character (later: the character of *bodily* givenness, and of givenness *not through mere presentification*)? The answer, he says, the hearer will already expect. *No one* demands of the *natural scientist* that the givenness-modes he builds on be *absolute* givenness-modes — not only because to demand this would be foolish, indeed countersensical, but because for the establishment of a strict science (later: *in the ordinary sense*) such absoluteness is *not at all necessary*, as natural science itself shows. Therefore *nothing stands in the way* of attempting a *transcendental psychology* — a *science of the experiences in phenomenological reduction*. Phenomenological experience may in many respects be *no better*, but it is at any rate *no worse*, than empirical experience. Why, then, should there not be able to be, *over against* the naturalistic experiential science, a *phenomenological* one? So, Husserl says, one would *at first* at least think — taking it as self-evident that to every kind of *experience* there must be able to correspond an *experiential science*. The going-beyond the sphere of the absolutely given is thus vindicated as the *necessary condition* for the very possibility of a phenomenological science: only by admitting the non-absolute but genuine modes of phenomenological experience does the reduced field become rich enough to be a field for science at all. (That this first, "at-first" answer still needs refinement — through the clarification of immanence and transcendence — is the task Husserl reserves for the next section.)

### **§ 30\. Immanence and Transcendence. The Ambiguity of these Terms and the Sense of Immanence and Transcendence in the Field of Phenomenology**

This section resolves the apparent paradox that has accumulated through the chapter — that the reduction must admit a "transcendence within the immanence" — by disentangling the *several senses* of "immanence" and "transcendence." Husserl first characterizes the objects of the projected science. This science would deal with objects that are, to be sure, *occasionally* "*transcendent*" to experiencing — insofar as they are *remembered* or *expected* — but that are, in the other and *more important* (later: *originary*) sense, *immanent*: namely, that *by their own nature* they are to be given *themselves* and *absolutely* (later: *bodily*). In principle they are *absolutely perceivable*, and accordingly absolutely intuitable in recollection; they are *not* objectivities *through appearance*, through mere adumbrating presentation. This is the mark that distinguishes the phenomenological object from the natural one even when the former is given non-absolutely (as remembered): the past *cogitatio*, though not now self-present, is of *such a nature* that it *could* be given in absolute self-presence, whereas the thing *never* could.

Husserl then states the peculiarity of *nature* in contrast. It is the distinctive feature of nature, and of everything standing under that title, that such things not only *exceed* experiencing in the sense of not being absolutely given, but exceed it in the stronger sense that they *in principle cannot* be "absolutely" given — *because* they are *necessarily* given through presentations, through *adumbrations* (later: adumbrations *relative to the here and now*); and adumbrating presentation can in principle *never be a reduplication of the presented itself*. The thing is essentially perspectival, given always through one-sided profiles that no synthesis can ever convert into the thing's own self-presence; the lived-experience is not.

On this basis Husserl distinguishes three concepts of transcendence, and the distinction is the conceptual payoff of the chapter. *First*, transcendence in the wholly *general* sense that the cognition-object is *not itself present* in the cognition-act. But this belongs to the very essence of the *intentional* relation: consciousness — the respective *cogitatio* — is always consciousness *of something that it is not itself*, and this holds *even* in phenomenological beholding, since the beheld too is not, in the proper sense, *in* the act. In this respect, however, one does *not* speak of "transcendence," for then the opposite term "immanence" would lose all sense. (Husserl later marked these sentences critically, and a footnote insists that immanent data are "objects" only as *enduring unities* of manifold actual and possible reproductions — a refinement pointing toward the next chapter.) *Second*, and quite otherwise: when one sets on one side the *being bodily present* to consciousness in the *strictest* sense, and on the other its negation, the *being-meant without* such self-presence. This strictest sense is that consciousness is a *beholding* that now *itself has*, itself grasps, itself *touches* what it beholds — as when a beholding directed at a *cogitatio* that is *now living present* has it, as it were, *in itself*. Both then form a *present-unity*, as reflection teaches: the living-present beholding is *one with* the living-present beheld. On the side of *immanence*, in this second sense, stands *only the beheld* (and at most what is beholdable in this way and so united with the actually beheld that a turn of the reflecting gaze could pass from the one to the other); on the side of *transcendence* stands *all the rest* — above all, everything *non-present*, even though it be consciously meant as object. Thus *even* a phenomenologically reduced recollection or retention, when it reproduces a been-beheld, has *that remembered* as something *transcendent* to the consciousness "memory." It is in *this* second sense that the chapter's "transcendence in the immanence" was meant: the just-past and the recollected are *transcendent* (not bodily self-present) yet fall *within* the reduced sphere.

*Third*, and decisively for fixing the field of phenomenology: a concept of immanence and transcendence as a *division of objects* — individual objects. On this division, individual objects fall into those that are *beholdable*, to be given in *absolute self-presence*, and those that can *only appear* as self-present, that can be given *only through appearances and presentations*. In *this* sense, *every* phenomenological consciousness relates to *immanence*: the *immanent is the field of phenomenology*, insofar as we now understand phenomenology as the eventual *science of individual objects brought to immanence by every switching-off of nature*. On the side of *transcendence*, in this third sense, belongs precisely *nature* — for "nature" is just the title encompassing the *totality of objectivities that present themselves through appearances*. From this Husserl draws the conclusion that crowns the chapter: *phenomenology does not want to switch off transcendence in every sense*. From the very outset it was defined by the switching-off of *nature* — of transcendence in a *determinate* sense, transcendence in the sense of the *appearing* (the adumbrationally given). It was never the project of confining cognition to the punctual self-present *cogitatio*; it admits the non-self-present phases of conscious life (the second-sense "transcendences") precisely because they are, in the third and governing sense, *immanent* — objects that by their nature can be given in self-presence and are reached by a phenomenological experience parallel to empirical experience. With this clarification the "going-beyond the sphere of the absolutely given" is fully legitimated, and the way is open to the fifth chapter's task: to show how this immanent field coheres into the *one* unitary stream of consciousness.

## **Fifth Chapter: The Phenomenological Attainment of the Whole, Unitarily Connected Stream of Consciousness**

### **§ 31\. The Background of the Phenomenological Object and the Identity of the Phenomenological Object in Different Acts of Consciousness. The Phenomenological Time-Consciousness**

Husserl returns from the analyses of immanence and transcendence to the main line, now in order to show how the reduced field forms a *unitary* domain. He restates the guiding question: is a phenomenology of the intended sense — a science on the ground of "phenomenological experience" — *possible*, and is it already guaranteed by what has been carried out? His answer affirms the *parity* established in the previous chapter: just as empirical (or naturalistic) experience, though *no* absolutely doubtless giving act, nonetheless carries in *all* its modes its *right*, its *evidence*, in itself, so too does phenomenological experience with its *parallel* modes. From this side *nothing is lacking*. The field of knowledge is, on both sides, an *infinite* one: *there*, the totality of objects we call *nature*; *here*, the totality of objects we call *consciousness*, *cogitatio*, *phenomenological datum*. The two infinite fields stand over against one another, each with its own experience and its own evidence.

Looking more closely within the phenomenological sphere, Husserl observes that the objectivities are *individual singularities* that come to us through the reduction — through phenomenological *perception* as absolute self-givennesses, but also through phenomenological *retention, recollection, expectation, and empathy*. And the act-situations, he notes, are far more complicated than they appear. First, *every phenomenological object has its objective background* (*Hintergrund*) — for perception, a background of *co-conscious but not co-meant* present. This background can be brought to *meantness* by *subsequent reflection and memory*, as something that was present but was *not meant* in the earlier perception. And so for every experience: if I now have a recollection of an object, I can reflect and find a *background* bearing the character "*having-been-present*," exactly like the recollected object itself — a background that was *co-conscious* in the preceding recollection but *not meant* in it. The reduced field thus has the same horizonal structure as ordinary experience: a meant foreground always ringed by an unmeant but co-conscious background that later reflection can thematize.

The decisive point of the section concerns *identity across acts*. In the manifold of phenomenological experience, the *same* phenomenological datum may be conscious to us — first *expected*, then *perceived*, then *retained*, then *recollected* and *re-recollected*. *Empathy* has here no exceptional position: the empathically posited datum can be the meant or background datum of an *empathized phenomenological* perception or other experience, and insofar as empathy is *itself* experience, the datum is posited as the givenness of a perception or other experience. And now the crucial methodological claim: the *identity* of the phenomenological datum in different acts of consciousness (in different *cogitationes*) is *not* an extra-phenomenological fact, but is *itself a phenomenologically given* — a fact of *phenomenological experience*. The unity of the stream is not imposed from outside (e.g. by reference to a worldly soul that has all these acts); it is *itself* disclosed *within* the reduced sphere. With this the concept of phenomenological experience *broadens* in an obvious way: that a datum is *the same* is given in a *beholding identity-consciousness*, itself founded in a *memory-series*. Not only do I have, successively, expectation, perception, retention, recollection, and repeated recollection of the datum; these *act-series* themselves stand, *as series*, before consciousness in recollecting reflection, and I can say — purely expressing the given — that these acts *have been* as a *time-series*, and that in them, again and again, *the same* datum was first expected, then perceived, then retained, then recollected, and I say this on the basis of an *overarching identity-consciousness*. Husserl closes by noting the lawful *bindings* that hang together with the *phenomenological time-consciousness* (not to be confused with the *empirical* one): expectation *precedes* perception, perception *precedes* recollection, the first recollection *precedes* the second — and *necessarily* so for the same phenomenological content, with yet other bindings for different content. The stream of consciousness has, as its own immanent form, a time-order with essential laws of succession; and it is this immanent temporality, not worldly clock-time, that holds the reduced field together as *one* connected whole.

### **§ 32\. Repetition and New Presentation: The Phenomenological Reduction to the Pure Consciousness as Individual Being and the Problems of the Reach of the Reduced Consciousness-World and of the Possibility of Phenomenological Science**

Husserl interrupts the forward movement to *repeat* the main thoughts of the recent lectures, which had not yet come to complete execution. A footnote candidly gives the reason: *because of the difficulties his hearers had found*, he offers a *new presentation*. The recapitulation is valuable precisely because it states the reduction afresh and draws out a point about the apriori that the first presentation had left implicit.

He begins again from the natural starting point. Beginning our reflection, we find ourselves in the attitude of natural world-consideration, as human beings in a determinate environment, performing manifold determinate psychic acts — perceiving, fantasizing, judging. We then carry out an *all-embracing* phenomenological reduction: we switch off *all* transcendence in the sense of natural existence-positing. We will make *no judgment-use* of any existence that comes to us as *natural* givenness — *without*, however, doubting it or suspecting it in any way. For the present investigation we exclude, *in principle*, every empirically founded judgment as a premise or theoretical establishment.

Husserl then forestalls a natural misreading. Understood in the usual way, that last sentence would mean we wish to judge *a priori*. *But that was by no means the contrast intended.* For the *genuine* apriori — that which is to carry *necessity* and *unconditioned universal validity* in the judgment — has *nothing to do with individual facts*. Apriori judgments are *generally* valid; aposteriori judgments are *individually* valid, positing individual being even when they are general in form. But what remains to *us* through the all-embracing reduction is precisely a *world of individual being* — the world of *phenomenological data*, the world of *pure consciousness*. This is a clarification of the first importance for understanding the 1910/11 conception: the reduction here does *not* coincide with the turn to essences; it yields a domain of *individual* being (singular *cogitationes* in their actual flux), to be investigated by a phenomenological *experience*, not (yet) by eidetic intuition. The phenomenology of this course is thus a *descriptive science of the individual reduced stream*, deliberately distinguished from an apriori doctrine of essences.

In a paragraph he later struck, Husserl sharpens how the reduced consciousness relates to the natural world. Certainly, the natural world spans *all* individual being, insofar as everything is to be ordered into it, and is so ordered in the natural attitude. But *a part* of this world — called *consciousness* — has the peculiarity that it *lets itself be phenomenologically reduced*: if we put out of action all the natural existence-positings performed *in* it and interwoven *with* it, and switch them off for our judging, *it itself remains over as purely immanent being* — as something that, in this positing, is *not nature*, since it is neither self-appearing (merely self-presenting) being, nor has any part in such being through an *indirect co-positing* of nature. (Such co-positing is performed, for instance, as soon as consciousness is apprehended as something *causally interwoven* with the nature-thing posited as a body.) The reduction, then, takes a "part" of the world — consciousness — and discloses it as a being that, properly considered, was never *of* the world in the natural sense at all.

Husserl then sets the program of the chapter: to make clear *how far* this reduced consciousness-world *reaches*, which *givenness-modes* we possess of it in the reduction, what *kind of knowing* it makes possible, and how far a *science* can be established in it. Among the givenness-modes we have, first, *phenomenological perception* (also called phenomenological beholding). Each reduced phenomenon offers itself as an *enduring* being — indeed as *enduring self-presence*. The object of empirical thing-perception, too, offers itself as self-present existence, *but* it is given through *mere appearance*; the *phenomenological* present is *not* an appearing present but *self-presence in the absolute sense*. Thus, for example, the being of the *perception-appearance* itself — the being of the phenomenon of the *externally-self-presenting* — is an *absolutely given immanent being*. And this givenness-mode of immanent being *implies* much: the enduring being *is* in *duration*, and this duration is a *filled* duration, with a *flowing point Now* and a *continuity of flowing points of the past*. Correspondingly, to each phenomenological perception there belongs a *point of now-perception* and, belonging to the same now, a *continuity of retentional memory* — and this in *steady flux*. Phenomenological perception is thus the *absolute positing of a Now* and, *in* the now, of a *determinate not-now*, in steady gradation. Beyond perception, Husserl recalls the further givenness-modes already discussed: *free retention* and above all *recollection*, likewise *expectation*, and finally *empathy*. The inventory of phenomenological experience is thereby complete, and the next sections will use it to demonstrate the unity and reach of the whole stream.

### **§ 33\. The Extension of Phenomenological Experience over the Whole Unified Stream of Consciousness**

Husserl now shows how the several givenness-modes cooperate to give the *whole* stream. When we perform one phenomenological perception after another — directed at *cogitationes* in pure beholding — each is given as (later: *bodily and*) self-present *so long as it lasts*. When it has elapsed, a stretch of it remains in *living retention*, finally flowing away into the *dark background*. Retention may also take place in such a way that a *still-consciousness* of the elapsed, no-longer-intuitive *cogitatio* holds it fast and *connects* it with the new one — and then we have a *consciousness of the succession* of *cogitationes*. *Recollections*, too, can emerge, of single such *cogitationes* and of whole series: we live through the beholding of each *once more*; once more it begins, lasts, with its flowing now and its *tail of fading been-nesses* — *but only "as it were."* This "as-it-were-given-again" is the very character of recollection. And a *unitarily-grasping* consciousness can unite a series of such recollections into a *group* — a group-consciousness of the *been-succession* that may be established only *subsequently*. Husserl's example is acoustic: tone-appearances run off; we attend to one, the others do not interest us, and we form *no* delimiting group-consciousness of a pair or a series. But *in recollection* we attend to the *time-background* of the recollected tone and now form, within it, a *separate* group- and series-consciousness joining the remembered tone-appearances. What was *not attended* in the original perception becomes *attended* in memory; what was *not grouped* becomes *grouped*. And just as each *cogitatio* has an unmeant time-background of *succession*, so it has one of *simultaneity*, and this too can be attended to in recollection. Memory thus does not merely repeat the original; it can *thematize* what the original left unthematic.

Husserl reaffirms the epistemic status of these modes: retention and recollection, with the operations possible in them, constitute a *phenomenological experiential consciousness* — provided only that we use *no nature-positing*. While phenomenological *perception*, in fitting limitation, can claim *absolute indubitability*, this does *not* hold (as was seen earlier) for these new forms of phenomenological experience. *But experience is experience*; as such it has its *value*. The non-absolute modes are not thereby disqualified; they are valid in their own right, exactly as the non-absolute modes of empirical experience are.

From this Husserl draws the chapter's central result. Assuming the like can be carried out for *expectation*, it becomes *visible* that phenomenological experience does *not* hang on the isolated *cogitationes* that are the now-attended presents, but (as a later insertion puts it, *through continuous unfolding of the horizons under continuous performance of the reduction from each place*) *extends over the whole stream of consciousness as a single time-connection* — one that does not, of course, fall in its *whole* breadth and length into the light of intuition at any one time. He restates this in his preferred image: remaining within the reduction, there stands in it an *infinite unity of consciousness* — an *endlessly unified stream*. Again and again we can exercise phenomenological experience: make an earlier *cogitatio* the object in remembering re-consciousness; bring its time-background (earlier attended or unattended) into the intuiting and meaning gaze; enter the connections of simultaneity or pursue those of succession; and *see* how, in the *unity of time-consciousness*, the phenomena hang together, are *continuously one*, *one stream*. To be sure, retentions and recollections are often *unclear* and indeterminate — and all the more the recollected backgrounds. But where memory is unclear, *it can become clear*: a second, richer, clearer memory may attach to the first; separate, unconnected memories can be brought, by the *awakening* of continuously joined clear memories, into the *unity of a clear memory*, thereby raising the experiential *force* and *value* of all the single ones. And the experiences are followed by the *experiential judgments* that faithfully express and articulate them. Thus *the stream of consciousness becomes, in phenomenological purity, an own experiential field, a domain of cognition*. The unity of the stream is not a postulate but an achievement of phenomenological experience itself, progressively clarified through the awakening and concatenation of memories.

### **§ 34\. Lifting of an Artificial Restriction. The Attainment of the Phenomenological Stream of Consciousness Starting from the Natural Reflection on the Stream of Consciousness, and the Double Phenomenological Reduction**

Husserl now confesses, and removes, an *artificial restriction* under which the analysis has so far labored. What has been said acquires its *proper value* — indeed first its *validity* — only when we first take the stream of consciousness *as it offers itself in the first reflection, the natural one*, and *only then* exercise the phenomenological reduction. So far we proceeded *from* an already phenomenological beholding, or several such acts, and *then* exercised retention, recollection, and expectation upon them. But these are only *artificial exceptional cases* — as if the reduction were already in force and we were watching reduced phenomena succeed one another. The realistic situation is the reverse. We must take the stream *as it is*: that is, *in the natural attitude in which we in fact already are*, we direct a regard upon the I-experiences and exercise the phenomenological reduction *on them and in them* — on the perceptions, retentions, memories, expectations, on *all* the inner and outer experiences through which we bring to natural intuitive givenness, like outer nature, so also our own experiences, the *phenomena of psychic nature*. The reduction is thus to be applied to the *naturally* given mental life, not presupposed as already operative.

And here, Husserl says, showed itself the *highly remarkable* fact that *every experience permits a double phenomenological reduction*. The *first* is the one that brings the experience *itself* to *pure immanent beholding* — that reduces the act *as* a lived-experience. The *second* is the one exercised *on its intentional content and object*. There is, accordingly, a phenomenological reduction exercised on the *intentional content and object of a recollection*. Husserl explains by analogy with the background-analysis of §33. Just as, in recollection, we can "*subsequently*" attend to the *background* of the remembered object — a background that, in the original perception, was an *unattended* perceptual background — so we can, *in* the recollection, exercise a phenomenological reduction on *foreground and background* that was *not* performed in the original perception. This second reduction is therefore *not* itself the *recollection of an earlier reduction*; it is a *new* reductive act, performed now, on the *intentional correlate* held in memory. The two reductions thus operate on two registers of every experience: the act itself (the *noetic* side, in later terminology), and what is meant *in* it (the *noematic* side).

Husserl pauses to register the intrinsic importance of what he has uncovered. Considered in themselves, the phenomena of *reflections in memory*, and in *presentification of every kind*, are of the *very greatest interest*; their exact description and analysis is a *fundamental piece of all phenomenology* — and yet, he remarks, they have *so far not even been seen by anyone*. (The claim to priority is characteristic of the 1910/11 breakthrough.) Here, however, they come into consideration *for the sake of* a determinate and *highly wonderful achievement* that they make possible: namely, the *all-embracing turning of all natural experience* — not only according to what *in* it is *cogitatio*, but *also according to what lies in it intentionally*. The double reduction is the instrument by which the *entire* natural-experiential life, with all its intended objects, can be carried over into the phenomenological field; and it is this that prepares the final extension of the next section, where the *transcendent unities* meant in natural experience are themselves brought under phenomenological treatment — the threshold across which the multiplicity of monads will come into view.

### **§ 35\. The Transcendent Unities of Natural Experience as Indices of Actual and Possible Pure Connections of Consciousness. Turning of All Natural Experience and All Sciences into the Phenomenological**

This is the culminating section of the chapter, and one of the most consequential in the course, for it establishes the formula — *transcendent unity as index of consciousness-connections* — that governs Husserl's mature doctrine of constitution. He states the result of the double reduction thus: *wherever natural experience posits a transcendent unity* — an existing real thing, a real constellation, an existing process of change, whether in present, past, or future — *there this existence is indeed bracketed, but the positing serves as an* index *for determinate pure connections of consciousness*, connections demonstrable in those experience-positings *through* the reduction, and demonstrable *in the form of acts of phenomenological experience*. The bracketed thing is not lost; it is *converted* into a rule for the consciousness in which it is, and could be, experienced.

Husserl unfolds this concretely. We set out from the natural attitude and from nature as it stands before us in simple experience. We look around; we go back in memory to the earlier perceived; we move forward and backward in intuitive experience, having before the experiencing eye the intuitive connection of appearing nature with its manifold things, events, persons. If we perform the relevant reductions *on* and *in* all these experiences, then to each experience — insofar as it is, say, experience of *this table*, which presents itself in this factual experience just so, in this appearance, meant and posited just so as to front and back, form and material — there corresponds a *determinate manifold of experience-possibilities* that are *motivated, real possibilities* and may pass over into *actual* experiences, which then, as motivated, become and *must* become *expected* given the corresponding direction of meaning. (A footnote adds that, seeing the thing, we can at any time also *think or pre-judge* how it would look upon arbitrary turns of the head, on moving away or stepping closer.) The switching-off of nature means that we now make, *not the experienced thing*, the object of our establishing judgments, but rather the *experiences of the thing* — the actual and possible ones — taken in *pure immanence*; and the establishments referring to *them* now belong to our sphere.

And here, Husserl says, is a cognition of *enormous importance*: that *every natural experience, taken as immanent being, motivates a manifold of other natural experiences and a manifold of real possibilities of natural experience*, and that *we can unwind these motivation-connections — which are connections of pure consciousness — and direct our regard upon them*. This regard has the character of *phenomenological experience*. So if, for the present attitude, we switch off the existence of nature — judging in no way *about* nature in the present establishing-sphere — there *remains* to us the *enormous and in each case determinate field of actual and possible natural experience*; and *only thereby* do we gain the field of the *pure stream of consciousness*, which of course contains *no nature* but only *nature-experience*, together with all the other interwoven representing, feeling, desiring, willing acts. The reduction does not subtract a world and leave a residue of bare data; it discloses the full, structured life of consciousness in which a world is experienced.

Husserl pauses to locate the historical *germ* of this peculiar reduction. Its first seed lies with *Hume*, and more determinately in the extreme empiricist *Mill*, in his doctrine of the *permanent possibilities of sensation* to which the existence of the outer thing is to be reduced; and in essence the same is said by the *sensation-monism* of *Mach*, who likewise substitutes for the thing connected groups of sensations. But Husserl at once sets aside all such *metaphysical-epistemological* constructions: he is not reducing the thing's *being* to sensations as its true metaphysical substance, but exhibiting, phenomenologically, the consciousness-connections of which the thing is the index. Setting these constructions aside, we can exercise the reduction on every thing-perception first by making it *in itself* the object, and can make an object of everything we can find *under* the switching-off of the thing-existence it posits and of all other nature-existence.

The detailed phenomenological description that follows is the heart of the matter. We find *sensation-contents* offering themselves thus and so, hanging together thus and so. But not only that: the sensation-contents *enter into thing-appearances* as the *total presentation* of the thing, and to this belongs a sphere of *co-meaning* (*Mitmeinung*). What is established concerns not only the momentary now but the whole *elapsed* perception — more exactly, the *stretch of retention* — so that we obtain *been-sensation, been-appearance*, and so on; and exactly the same we can perform in *recollection* of the earlier perceived thing or process, finding *recollected* sensations, appearances, co-meaning, present-consciousness. Now to perception belongs the structural fact that, when the thing presents itself *just now* from *this* side, with *this* appearance-content and in *this* co-meaning, it *could* also present itself from *another* side, in another mode of appearance. And this is *not an empty but a real, i.e. motivated, possibility*. It means, for example: if I *turn my head* — if the determinate sensation-groups belonging to the title "head-turning" run off in voluntary or involuntary doing — then *such-and-such determinate continuous changes must proceed with the thing-appearance*; the sensation-stock and appearance-stock change in such-and-such a way. And so the first perception, as the one belonging to the original head- and body-posture, *motivates* a *manifold of possible perceptions* with corresponding other postures. The thing's identity is thus the correlate of a *rule-governed system of kinaesthetically-motivated appearance-manifolds*: "if I move thus, then the appearance changes so."

The switching-off of nature here yields, then, *quite determinate connections of phenomenological data* — of sensation-contents, of apprehensions, of co-apprehensions, voluntary acts, tendentially running *kinaesthetic* series — *actual* ones and ones *motivated* in respect of their possibility. The motivation, Husserl notes, is mostly such that it does *not* completely and determinately prescribe the appearance-possibilities, but then it harbors in itself an *index of indeterminacy* that signifies *determinability within a determinate sphere* — the horizon is open, but open in a rule-bounded way. These motivated possibilities pass over into *motivated positings of what is coming* — that is, into *expectations* — when, in the web of motivating and motivated, *factual* changes run off on the side of the motivating appearances that consciously *demand* the corresponding running-off of the motivated. If I really turn the head, then I *expect* the changes in the mode of presentation of the thing.

All this, Husserl adds, *transfers* when, in the memory-series of thingly past, we exercise the *inner reduction* (the second of the double reductions): we can direct our regard, instead of upon the past thing or past natural actuality, rather upon the *past perceptual appearance* of the thing, upon all that belongs to it — including the perceptual background and what *it* offers of sensation, appearance-content, co-meaning — and from there upon the *motivation-connections* that run out from these phenomenological data given in the *turned* recollection, upon how they interweave with other phenomenological data, and finally upon the *possible* motivations and the *functional regulations* that link appearance-changes with appearance-changes. *Thus we turn all natural experience into phenomenological*, making use of every natural positing *without* making it the substrate of any judgment *about* nature. It is, Husserl stresses, something *entirely different* to investigate nature — to describe things, causal changes, time-orderings of thingly objects — and, on the other hand, to *let this whole nature be* and instead describe and investigate the *thing-experiences in their immanence*: what lies in them, how they hang together, how they motivate one another, and in particular how they connect with judgings, feelings, desirings, and motivate these — *all under consistent switching-off of every judgment about the being of nature*. Now, he says, one understands what it means that *every experienced thing as such is an index for a certain regulation of consciousness as pure consciousness*.

Husserl adds a forward-pointing remark that ties the index-doctrine to the *warrant* of existence-claims. When we ascribe *validity* to the experience of a thing — when we are of the opinion that it is legitimate to say the thing *exists* — there belongs to this the *possibility of convincing oneself* of the thing's existence in *renewed and ever-renewed confirmations*, which *restrict* and practically *exclude* the possibility that the thing is not, that it turns out to be an illusion. Then the *true existence* of the thing is an *index* for quite determinate and determinately describable appearance-connections of the same thing — and possibly of associated thought-processes, judgments, and groundings — just as its *non-existence* is an index for *other* connections of consciousness, in which the existence-positing *evidently cancels itself* or the non-existence *comes to evidence*. Existence and illusion are thus themselves phenomenological indices, distinguished by the type of consciousness-connection (concordant confirmation versus self-cancellation) that the thing's positing indexes.

Finally Husserl universalizes the result to the *sciences*. Naturally, all the *content of the sciences* lets itself be turned into the phenomenological — more exactly, regarded as *index* for phenomenological connections. We do *not* posit the theories; we do *not* posit nature in the determinacy grounded by the theories; rather we go *back* to the *judgment- and grounding-connections* whose *meaning-content* and *validity-content* these theories are, and now perform the phenomenological turn and reflection *in the acts of such theorizing*, pursuing the *consciousness-interweavings* that belong to them purely phenomenologically. The entire edifice of natural knowledge — perception, thing, theory, science — is thereby reclaimed as a field of indices for the pure life of consciousness; and with this the reduced stream has been shown to reach not merely over the isolated *cogitationes* but over the whole constituted world *as experienced*. This sets the stage for the sixth chapter, where the same index-method, applied to the experience of *foreign* bodies and foreign life (empathy), will yield not merely other appearances but *other streams of consciousness* — the multiplicity of monads.

## **Sixth Chapter: The Attainment of the Phenomenological Multiplicity of Monads**

### **§ 36\. The Intersubjective Connection of Consciousness. Question whether Phenomenological Reduction Means Restriction to the Single Consciousness**

Husserl announces that the foregoing requires an *important supplement*, and with it the course reaches its proper destination — intersubjectivity. The point of departure is an observation about what the reduction of "all the sciences" in §35 quietly presupposed. The *theoretical content* of a science — understood as the *total validity-content* of the science — *and nature itself* are *intersubjective* unities. (A footnote refers to a parallel appendix.) But in the previous lecture, Husserl notes, we had not yet spoken of the *intersubjective* connection of consciousness — not yet of the *experience* running from *one* I-consciousness to *another* I-consciousness. The reduction so far seemed to deliver *my* stream; yet what it was used to reclaim (science, the one objective nature) is essentially *shared*. This tension forces the question that titles the section: does the phenomenological reduction mean *restriction* to the connections of pure consciousness which, in empirical-psychological apprehension, belong to a *single* empirical I — namely *mine*, the phenomenologist's? Before that can be answered, a prior question must be faced: *how does this pure consciousness, the pure I-consciousness, characterize itself?* What makes a manifold of *cogitationes* into the unity of *one* consciousness at all?

### **§ 37\. The Principle of the Construction of a Unified Stream of Consciousness**

Husserl approaches the intersubjective question by first solving the *intra*subjective one: what principle binds many *cogitationes* into *one* stream? The empirical I has a *body* and has, in a quite different sense, *consciousness*; to its consciousness belongs every singular consciousness in the sense of the *cogitatio* that it has, that it lives through. But does this give a *unity* under phenomenological reduction? Husserl answers by identifying two things that might have seemed distinct: the *unity of the phenomenological stream of consciousness*, and the *unity of the consciousness* that is exclusively the consciousness of a *single* I in the empirical apprehension (or that issues from this by reduction), are *one and the same*. The unity of the stream is therefore to be exhibited *purely phenomenologically*, without recourse to the empirical I or its body as the principle of unification.

The argument proceeds from the result of §31 (every *cogitatio* has its temporally ordered background). No *cogitatio* is isolated; each is, as it were, something *meant-out* of an environment of more or less intimately connected phenomenological *data* — or better, Husserl says, phenomenological "*data-bilia*" (*Dabilien*), since it first requires a *turn of the meaning gaze* (later: *and a penetrating into the dark horizons*) to make them into actual data, into meant-nesses and givennesses. This holds, lawfully, for every *cogitatio* that comes to phenomenological givenness, whether perceptual or otherwise. We reckon, self-evidently, to *one* phenomenological I everything that such a background harbors in itself, by way of present and simultaneity-in-the-present, or in the direction of past or future — just as, in empirical apprehension, all this belongs to *one* empirical I-consciousness. This background may be now clear, now dark, and may — insofar as memory becomes clear after having been dark — rise to clarity and determinacy; similarly with fore-memory and expectation, which may in general be quite indeterminate. But it is *absolutely certain* that such a *temporal halo* (*zeitlicher Hof*) is *always there and must be there*, and that, where it is indeterminate, it is *not arbitrarily and freely variable but determinable*. However vague a memory — empty, without any intuitively graspable and analyzable content — a *clear* memory is *possible*, and one that legitimately belongs to the unclear as its *clarifying* and determinately content-giving fulfillment. Here again, then, is a *wonderful motivation-connection and a rule of consciousness*: the unity of the stream is secured by the lawful determinability of the temporal halo surrounding every *cogitatio*.

Husserl now poses the sharp problem that the principle must solve. *What if* we have *two* memories, each with its own memory-halo, while *no intuitive memory-band* mediates the content of the one and the other? Could there not be *separated* memories? More distinctly: each memory posits (suitably reduced) a *past perceptual consciousness* with a halo of its temporal environment — hence a *stretch of earlier consciousness-flux*. Could not two consciousness-fluxes posited by two memories be *without connection*? And *must* they, with their time-backgrounds, order themselves into the unity of a stream of consciousness that is *not at all* (later: *in advance*) given? (Husserl later reformulated the problem as: what if we gained two memory-*continua*, each with its disclosable time-horizon, without our ever, in the progress of disclosure, passing over from the one into the other?) We cannot, after all, *wait* to see whether a chain of clear memory really brings the two memories to unity. To this question, Husserl says, a *law of consciousness* — and these are throughout *essence-analyses and essence-laws* — gives a *determinate and absolutely evident* answer: *any two memories that belong to the unity of a consciousness-present connecting them close together, in it, into the unity of one memory* — that is, of *one* time-consciousness (even if not intuitively filled) — in which the remembered of the one memory and the remembered of the other *close together into one remembered*, belong to *one* time, and are therefore, in the sense of this unity-consciousness, *necessarily* intuitable as simultaneous or successive. It may be that the *time-order* is indeterminately conscious — that it remains open, in the sense of this time-consciousness, which is the earlier and which the later, or whether they are not simultaneous; but then this is an *indeterminacy* that harbors a *determinability* in the sense of one of the three possible cases, provided only that the memory can hold up *as valid* (and, Husserl notes, every memory is either valid or invalid). Further, this implies that it must then be "*possible*" so to awaken and traverse a memory-series, clearly and completely, that it connects the one and the other memory in a way that *really establishes* the continuous time-connection of the stream of consciousness. This is, of course, a *motivated* possibility — one that does *not* mean we *actually* dispose over this memory-series.

Husserl generalizes to the governing law. Two experiences that join *at all* into the unity of a synthetic consciousness spanning them join therein into the unity of *one experience*; and to the unity of one experience there belongs, again, a *time-unity of the experienced*. This holds as belonging to the *essence of experience as such*, and in particular for *phenomenological* experience. *Consequently, therewith the principle — and the single decisive principle — that constructs the unity of the stream of consciousness has been found*: the principle that decides whether several *cogitationes* belong to the unity of *one* phenomenological I, and exhibits *by what it is to be recognized* that several *cogitationes*, however given in phenomenological experience, *must* belong to *one* stream of consciousness, and that grounds, on the other side, that *one* stream must exist that comprises them in itself — always presupposed that these *cogitationes* are at all, that the experiences giving them in fact hold. (Husserl later bracketed and marked for deletion the clause "that decides whether several cogitationes … must belong," a sign of his continuing dissatisfaction with the formulation, but the principle itself stands.) The unity of the ego, phenomenologically construed, is thus *nothing but* the lawful synthesizability of its *cogitationes* into one time — not a substantial pole behind them, but the form of their possible temporal interconnection.

Husserl states the immediate corollary. If I set out from any of my psychologically inner or outer experiences and perform on them the phenomenological reduction, then the resulting phenomenological data, with all their connections, belong *throughout to a single stream of consciousness, to a single phenomenological I* — and not only the experiences *in themselves*, but also whatever we may find *in* them, by reduction, of *motivation-connections*. The intra-subjective unity is thereby secured by a purely immanent, temporal principle. The decisive question of the chapter now becomes acute: if *this* is what makes consciousnesses *one*, then by the very same principle a consciousness that *cannot* be synthesized into my one time — that of the *other*, reached through empathy — will fall *outside* my stream, as a *second* stream; and the way to the *multiplicity of monads* lies open. That is the task of §§38–39.

### **§ 38\. Empathy. Setting-Off of Empathy from the Analogizing Image-Consciousness**

Husserl now asks the question on which the whole turn to intersubjectivity depends: do we *ever* come to *another* phenomenological I? Can phenomenological reduction reach the *idea of several* phenomenological I's at all? On the path so far, he answers, *naturally not* — for the unity-principle of §37 binds everything found into *my one* stream. But so far we have *not taken account of empathy* (*Einfühlung*), which is, after all, a *special form* of empirical experience. In empathy the empathizing I *experiences* the soul-life — more exactly, the *consciousness* — of the other I. It *experiences* it; but no one will say that it *lives through* it and perceives it in *inner perception*, in Lockean reflection, as it does its own consciousness. Still less will anyone say it *remembers* or *expects* it. The question of empathy's mode of givenness is thus posed precisely: it is an experience of foreign consciousness that is neither self-living-through, nor inner perception, nor memory, nor expectation.

Husserl considers, and rejects, the construal of empathy as an *image-consciousness* (*Bildbewusstsein*) — a consciousness that *analogizes* the foreign consciousness through a *simultaneous own and similar* consciousness. He credits *Lipps* with being, in a certain respect, on good paths — though he by no means accepts everything Lipps says about empathy — in that Lipps vigorously opposed the usual and (Husserl agrees) *wretched* psychology of empathy. But Husserl's own analysis turns on a precise point about image-consciousness. In *empirical* image-consciousness an *object-appearance* (real or merely imaginative), an *image-object*, functions as the *bearer* of the analogizing relation to the "*subject*" (the *Sujet*) of the image. In an *immanent* image-consciousness, then, a *self-present consciousness* would have to serve as *image-object* for *another* consciousness — so that an *own* experience, an own act, e.g. of *anger*, would have to function as the *analogon* for the foreign one. *But this is nonsense.* For when I empathize anger into the *Thou*, I am *not myself angry* — not in the least — any more than I am angry when I *fantasize* an anger or merely *remember* one (unless, in the latter case, I now fall anew into anger). Empathy is *as little* a consciousness of proper *imaging* as is after-memory, fore-memory, or any other kind of memory. Husserl therefore classifies it positively: he sees in it a consciousness *most closely akin to these acts*, an act of the *widest group of presentifications* (*Vergegenwärtigungen*). (A footnote: it is of course a *modification* of anger, akin to it as the recollecting reproduction of an impression is to the impression; and: every empty intention would then be a presentification.) Empathy belongs, with memory and expectation, to the family of acts that present *without* self-presence — it makes the other's lived-experience *co-present* without my living it.

Husserl then considers a *better* alternative to imaging-in-a-present-similar-act: the other kind of *analogizing* that occurs when we make something *illustratively* present in a so-called *fantasy-image* — as when, after a description, we form in fantasy an image of the described matter, well aware that it is a "*mere* fantasy-image." In this way, too, we often *picture to ourselves* how another feels. But to interpret *every* empathy thus, he says, *has its reservations*. For we *behold into* the other his experiencing, *quite immediately* and *without* consciousness of any impressional or imaginative picturing; and when we *merely make ourselves an image* of his experiencing, we feel *that* as something special, distinct from straightforward empathy. For this reason Husserl *cannot resolve* to claim even the much better second kind of analogizing for empathy. (Two footnotes qualify: we do not *always* "behold into" the other, and necessarily, it seems to him, an *empty appresentation* stands in front, which may pass over into a reproductive intuition.) Empathy is thus left as a *sui generis* presentation: an immediate, non-imaging, direct apprehension of foreign consciousness, founded — Husserl will increasingly say — in an *appresentation* that presents the other's life without presenting it in the flesh.

### **§ 39\. The Attainment of Other Phenomenological I's through Double Phenomenological Reduction. Nature as the Index of the Coordination of a Multiplicity of I-Monads**

This is the destination of the entire course: the phenomenological attainment of *other* I's, and with them the *multiplicity of monads*. The decisive move is to apply to *empathy* the very tools developed for self-experience. Empathy is, at all events, an *experience* that we can *phenomenologically reduce* like any other — and here too in the *double* manner. *First*, the empathy *in itself*, which we have given, beholding, in phenomenological *perception*: it has its time-background like every phenomenologically perceived act and orders itself into the *one* stream of consciousness to which, starting from a given *cogitatio*, all phenomenological perception and all recollective presentification belong. (This is the empathy as *my* act.) *Second*, however, empathy is *experience of an empathized consciousness*, in which we can *also* exercise phenomenological reduction. And the phenomenological datum *so* won *also has its time-background* and is thus the *datum of a phenomenological I*. The double reduction here yields, on its noetic side, *my* act of empathizing, and on its noematic side, a *reduced foreign cogitatio with its own temporal halo*.

Now Husserl invokes the *law* of §37, but with a momentous negative result: *in principle, an empathized datum and the empathizing experiencing of it cannot belong to the same stream of consciousness, hence to the same phenomenological I*. From the empathized stream *no channel leads into* the stream to which the empathizing itself belongs. *Never* can a datum of the one stream and a datum of the other stand in the relation that the one is the *environment* (*Umgebung*) of the other. But "environment" here means *time-environment*; and our law says precisely that what cannot be synthesized into *one* time-consciousness cannot belong to *one* I. Since the foreign *cogitatio*, given through empathy, can *never* be brought into the unified time of my stream — there is no memory-chain that could continuously join it to my present — it *necessarily* falls outside my I, constituting a *second*, separate phenomenological I. The same unity-principle that bound my own *cogitationes* into one stream thus *individuates* the other as a stream I can experience but never live.

Husserl faces the obvious objection. Against this it *seems to speak* that an *empathy-act* and the *empathized act* belong to the *same time*, and *for consciousness* belong to the same time: empathy posits the empathized *as now*, and posits it in the *same now* as itself. (A note records that Husserl probably replaced the following execution, in 1921, with the text of a separate appendix.) But here, Husserl says, the following is to be noted. There is also a *presentified now* (one that is *not* a *recollected* now) — that is, a presentification that *identifies* the presentified now, *although* it is only presentified, with the *actual* now. For example, when I presentify to myself the *Roons* (an editorial note explains: a restaurant near Göttingen — Husserl imagines what is happening there *now*, simultaneous with his actual now, though not perceived). Just so, the *empathized now* is a *presentified*, not a *self-beheld*, now; and *therefore the simultaneity of the empathy and the empathized is no self-beheld simultaneity*. Further, the one is not *environment* of the other, and conversely; nor does any *possible way of continuity* lead from the one to the other — as such a way *does* lead from the otherwise-presentified now (the imagined Roons) to the actual now. The time posited in empathizing is, when *empirical* empathy is in question, a now that is *empirically* posited as the *same objective time-point* as the now of one's own consciousness. This identification is *mediated* through the relation to the *objective time of the body and of the thing-world*: I identify even my own feeling, thinking, perceiving — and its now — with the now of the perceived thing-world, which receives its objective time-determination. And this objective time, naturally, *falls under the phenomenological reduction*. (A footnote offers an alternative formulation: phenomenological empathy is phenomenological experience of a phenomenological I that, in it and *in principle*, experiences an *other* such I than itself — *not* a tautology, unlike the corresponding statement for *empirical* empathy that in it one human gains experience of another's soul-life, which is at bottom the *definition* of empirical empathy.)

Husserl now draws the result of switching off this objective thing-time together with the things and bodies. What remains over? *All phenomenological being reduces then to one (to "my") phenomenological I* — distinguished as the perceiving, remembering, empathizing, and therein phenomenologically reducing I — *and to other I's, posited in empathy*, and posited as beholding, remembering, possibly empathizing I's. Further, for my I the empirically experienced natural objects reduce, through the switching-off of their existence, to *indices* for certain actual consciousness-connections and associated motivated consciousness-possibilities (the result of §35). *But* by virtue of natural empathy the empathized I's are posited as belonging to *their* bodies, as *midpoints of thingly environments* that widen out to the *all-nature* — *the same* all-nature that is also there for me, that I too perceive and otherwise experientially posit. In the phenomenological reduction, *every thing is, for the empathized I too, an index* of experience-connections and experience-possibilities belonging to *it* and empathically attributed to it by me — and so for *every* I.

From this Husserl states the culminating formula of the course. *Nature is an index for an all-embracing regulation* — embracing *all* the streams of consciousness that stand in experiential relation to one another *through empathy*. And specifically, *every objective time-point*, and every objectively grasped "*simultaneous*" that sets my present now and the now of every other I *as one* (and likewise every past now of my memory with every past now of another's memory), *is an index for a quite determinate, lawful coordination* — a coordination that, so to speak, *brings every I-monad into relation with every other*, and that with respect to *quite determinate, correlatively belonging consciousness-motivations*. Here the term *monad* (*Ichmonade*) makes its decisive appearance: each pure I is a closed stream that no channel joins to another, yet the whole plurality of these windowless streams is *coordinated* through the index "objective nature" and "objective time," so that the one common world is the correlate of a *community* of mutually empathizing monads. The phenomenological reduction, far from imprisoning the philosopher in solipsism (the objection of §18), has issued in a *monadology*: a multiplicity of pure I's, each absolutely separate as a temporal stream, yet bound into one intersubjective nexus by the lawful coordination of which objective nature is the index. With this the course has reached the goal announced in the opening synopsis — the doctrine of monads and their connection — and the concluding chapter can take the measure of what phenomenological cognition has thereby achieved.

## **Seventh Chapter: Concluding Considerations on the Scope of Phenomenological Cognition**

### **§ 40\. The Abstention from Every Judgment about the Being of Nature in the Phenomenological Reduction**

The concluding chapter takes the measure of what has been achieved, and Husserl opens it by guarding the results of the course against a fatal misinterpretation — the slide from phenomenology into *metaphysical idealism*. All that has been said, he reminds us, holds *when we perform what we called the phenomenological reduction* — when we do *not* judge about the *being of nature* but about the being of the *purely phenomenological connections*. (In a later revision Husserl sharpened this to: not judging about "the" nature simpliciter, but, *as phenomenologists abstaining from every co-performance of the belief*, judging only about the purely phenomenological connections.) *Well noted*, he insists: we have *not judged about the being of nature, in any way*. We have *not* said that nature "is in truth nothing other" than this regulation running from consciousness to consciousness. We have *not* said that consciousness is the *only* true being, and that nature is, as it were, only an *imaginary image* that consciousness projects within itself. All this *could not, with any sense, have been our opinion* — precisely because our whole investigation took place *within the phenomenological reduction*, and this reduction means, *ex definitione*, nothing other than the *refraining* from every establishment about nature. Theories like the ones just mentioned (that consciousness alone is real, that nature is a mere projection), on the other hand, make *expressly asserting* establishments *about* nature; consequently they *do not concern us here at all*. The point is one of methodological hygiene of the greatest importance: the index-doctrine of §§35 and 39 — that the thing is an index for consciousness-connections — must *not* be read as the idealist thesis that the thing *is nothing but* those connections. To say so would be to make a judgment *about nature*, and thus to abandon the very epoché that gave the analysis its meaning. (An editorial note records Husserl's own later misgiving, written into Landgrebe's 1924 transcription beside this paragraph: "*That is dubious and at any rate not clear*," with a reference to a later appendix — a sign that the precise relation between phenomenological neutrality and transcendental idealism continued to occupy him.)

### **§ 41\. The Problem of the Possibility of Phenomenological Science as Essence-Science and Fact-Science**

Having secured the monadological result and disarmed the idealist misreading, Husserl returns — with apparent paradox — to the question whether a phenomenological *science* is so much as possible. It *seems strange*, he admits, that after seeing how the field of phenomenological experience spans a *multiplicity of phenomenological I's* — of *closed monads coordinated with one another by unitary lawfulnesses* — and after recognizing that these coordinations, in which nature consciously expresses itself, must surely be *describable more closely*, one should *still* deliberate over the possibility of phenomenological science. The cognitions just won in passing are, after all, *scientific* and evidently very *illuminating*. Why doubt the possibility of what one has just been doing?

*Nonetheless*, Husserl says, *not everything here is clear* — and the unclarity he isolates is the one that has shadowed the course from the start (recall the synopsis's distinction between an *eidetic* and an *experiencing* phenomenology, and §32's insistence that the reduction yields *individual* being). Above all: we thought of phenomenology as a kind of *parallel to natural science*, both occupied with *individual* objectivities — the one with givennesses of the natural attitude, the other with those of the phenomenological attitude. But, Husserl observes, we have *not at all* considered *what role apriori cognition plays in the phenomenological sphere* — in how far, *on the ground of phenomenological experience*, *ideations* are to be performed and *ideal scientific cognitions* to be won. The whole course has proceeded as a description of the *individual* reduced stream and its connections; it has *not* asked whether its genuine yield is *eidetic*.

He clarifies the issue by the analogy with nature. As regards nature, we know there is such a thing as *pure* natural science — an apriori of nature and associated apriori disciplines such as geometry. But *besides* this there is *empirical* natural science, and this does *not* consist in *transferring* the pure apriori of nature onto occurring individual cases, onto givennesses of outer experience. That would be an *empty business*, without scientific value. The apriori cognition serves as a *methodical instrument* of the empirical, but the empirical delivers something *completely new* in the system of the empirical sciences. The question, then, is whether phenomenology has a corresponding two-storey structure — an eidetic phenomenology and a genuinely *empirical*, matter-of-fact phenomenology — or whether its real content is, at bottom, only eidetic.

And here Husserl voices a pointed self-doubt. Have we made sure whether what we have won as "interesting glances into the phenomenological sphere" does not, *au fond*, concern *pure essence-cognition* — and whether something like an *experiential* phenomenology is not still quite *questionable*, indeed perhaps (later: *as a rational-empirical science*) *impossible*? Indeed, he concedes, what we said about the phenomenology of time-consciousness, about the motivation-connections belonging to thing-consciousness, and much else, may well — at least for the greatest part — bear *from the outset the stamp of apriori cognition*. The deep, general structures the course has uncovered look suspiciously like *essential* laws, not contingent facts about a particular stream. *But* if we hold fast to experience really *as* experience — that is, take it as the *positing of individual being* — then, while we may be sure that the domain of such positing is *very wide*, we can be *not quite so sure* whether on the ground of such experience something like an *experiential science* (later: *as a system of rationality*) can be founded as a real *matter-of-fact science*. The course thus ends by reopening, with full rigor, the very question of its own scientific status: it has demonstrated a method and disclosed a field, but whether that field grounds a *factual* science of the individual stream, over and above an eidetic phenomenology, remains genuinely undecided.

### **§ 42\. The Equivalence of Natural Cognition and of Cognition of the Correlative Consciousness-Connections, and the Application of Apriori Consciousness-Cognition to the Phenomenological Connections of Empirical Natural Cognition. Psychophysics**

The final section presses the same difficulty to its sharpest form and breaks off — fittingly, given that the surviving manuscript records only the early weeks of the course — on an unresolved question. What strikes us in this respect, Husserl says, is this: all the *consciousness-connections* in which the being of nature, as it were, consciously expresses itself *come to our cognition* without our pronouncing any judgment *about* nature, and without our even *tacitly* using the existence of nature as a *premise* — and yet, on the other hand, the cognition of these consciousness-connections is *in a certain way equivalent* to the cognition of nature, and conversely. Understood at least thus: the *validity* of experience and of empirical experiential cognition has its *correlate* in certain actual and possible connections of experiential consciousness; and conversely, if these connections are assumed as obtaining, then experiential cognition *has validity*. The cognition of nature and the cognition of its correlative consciousness-connections are, in this correlational sense, *equivalent*: each maps onto the other without remainder.

From this equivalence Husserl draws the worry that gives the section its critical edge. In this domain of phenomenology, then, we have, after all, *nothing other than a kind of turning of natural cognition into the phenomenological*. And — here is the doubt — *should phenomenology be able to accomplish this cognition for itself, without preceding natural cognition?* Should it not rather be the case that *apriori* cognition, belonging to the *essence of consciousness* and winnable in *purely immanent* research, finds *application* to the *empirically developed natural cognition* with respect to its phenomenological connections, and *now* delivers a cognition of *existence-connections* (*Daseinszusammenhänge*) of phenomenological data that *could not have been won in another way* — namely, directly from the individual data? In other words: perhaps the rich "phenomenological" results really arise only by taking the *already accomplished* empirical knowledge of nature and *turning it* into its consciousness-correlates by means of *essential* laws of consciousness — so that phenomenology, far from being an autonomous matter-of-fact science of the stream, would be the eidetic-correlational *interpretation* of a natural cognition that must first be given to it. This would mean that the *factual* content of the "phenomenological experience" the course celebrated is parasitic on natural experience, and that the genuinely *self-standing* yield of phenomenology is, after all, *apriori*.

Husserl concedes the force of this doubt for one whole region and distinguishes a harder case. *Surely this doubt strikes the entire domain of physis* — of physical nature: there the equivalence with consciousness-connections, and the dependence on prior natural cognition, seem undeniable. *More difficult* is the matter for *psychophysical* cognition, which is properly natural cognition only through its *interweaving* with genuine, physical natural cognition (transcendent cognition). At bottom, psychophysical cognition is an *intermediate member* between natural cognition and purely phenomenological cognition. (An editorial footnote records that Husserl later *struck* this last sentence.) Here the printed lecture-text ends — on the threshold of a problem rather than at a resolution: psychophysics, standing between the cognition of physical nature and the pure phenomenology of consciousness, marks the precise point where the question of phenomenology's independence from natural cognition becomes most delicate, and where the 1910/11 course, in its surviving form, leaves the reader. The lecture thus closes exactly where Husserl's lifelong labor would continue: with the unfinished task of clarifying how a pure phenomenology of consciousness — and of the intersubjective, monadic community of consciousnesses it has just attained — stands to the knowledge of nature whose correlate, and yet whose ground, it claims to be.

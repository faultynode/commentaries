# Commentary on Husserl, Lectures on the Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness (Hua X)

## Part One: The Lectures on Internal Time-Consciousness from the Year 1905

### Introduction

Husserl opens by placing the analysis of time-consciousness among the oldest unsolved problems of descriptive psychology and epistemology, and names Augustine as the first to feel its difficulty to the point of despair. Book XI of the *Confessions*, chapters 14–28, remains required reading, because modern thought, for all its pride in knowledge, has not advanced beyond it. Augustine's dictum, that he knows what time is until asked to explain it, sets the tone. The choice of ancestor matters: Augustine located time in the "distension" of the soul, in memory, attention and expectation, and so placed the problem where Husserl will place it, in consciousness rather than in the motion of bodies. The text itself has a layered history that bears on everything that follows. The editor's note records that the version printed here is Edith Stein's 1917 redaction, published by Heidegger in 1928, which works in material from 1905 to 1917; the Introduction, §§1–6 and the first paragraph of §7 rest on the original 1905 lecture sheets. Readers must therefore not treat the work as a single synchronic doctrine: the early sections argue within the schema of apprehension and apprehended content, and later sections, drawn from manuscripts of 1908–1911, break with it.

Everyone knows what time is, Husserl continues; it is the most familiar thing of all. Difficulty begins with the attempt to account for time-consciousness: to set objective time and subjective time-consciousness in their proper relation, and to understand how temporal objectivity is constituted in subjective consciousness. Husserl glosses temporal objectivity as "individual objectivity in general." The equation is the programme in miniature. Whatever is individual is fixed by its position in time; so to explain how temporal objectivity is constituted is to explain how any individual object at all becomes given. The problem is therefore not a regional curiosity but the foundation of a theory of the individual. The final clause sharpens the difficulty further: even the bare attempt to analyse the phenomenological content of time-experiences, before any question of objectivity arises, entangles us in contradictions. The trouble lies in the subjective side itself.

Husserl takes Brentano's analysis of time as his point of departure. Brentano never published it and presented it only in lectures; Marty summarized it briefly in his study of the development of the colour sense, and Stumpf mentions it in the *Tonpsychologie*. Husserl's access is thus to an oral doctrine, which explains why Section One reconstructs it at some length before criticizing it. The choice also announces the method: Husserl will win his own position by isolating the phenomenological core in a psychological theory and freeing it from that theory's naturalistic framing.

#### §1. The Exclusion of Objective Time

The analysis is to be phenomenological, and that means excluding every assumption, stipulation and conviction about objective time, every "transcending" presupposition of existing things. Husserl grants that each experience, like every real being, may occupy a place in the one objective time, including the experience of perceiving or representing time. One may wish to date an experience objectively, or to compare how time posited as objective in some act of consciousness relates to actual objective time, whether estimated intervals match real ones. These are legitimate inquiries, but not phenomenology's. The real thing and the real world are not phenomenological data, and neither is world-time, the time of nature as natural science or psychology understands it. The exclusion is a bracketing, not a denial: Husserl does not claim that objective time is unreal, only that its existence cannot serve as a premise. This is the reduction in its 1905 form, two years before the *Idea of Phenomenology* lectures give it its name, and it already has the sense it will keep: suspension of transcendent positing in favour of what is given as such. Constitution here never means causal production of time by the mind.

An objection follows. When we speak of the temporal character of what we perceive, remember or expect, it may look as if we already assume an objective course of time and merely study the subjective conditions of intuiting and knowing it. Husserl answers that what we accept is not the existence of world-time or the duration of things, but appearing time and appearing duration as such. These are absolute data, and doubting them would be senseless. We do also accept a time that exists, but it is the immanent time of the flow of consciousness, not the time of the world of experience. That my consciousness of a melody I am now hearing exhibits succession is evident beyond any meaningful doubt or denial. Two levels are thereby fixed for the whole work: appearing objective time, accepted only as appearing, and immanent time, accepted as actually existing because it is given with an evidence that excludes doubt. The later sections will discover a third level beneath the second.

The parallel with space clarifies what excluding objective time means. Spatial consciousness, the experience in which perception and imagination of space take place, belongs to the phenomenologically given. When I open my eyes I see into objective space, but reflection shows that I have visual sensory contents which found the appearance of things located in space. If I strip away every transcending interpretation and reduce the perceptual appearance to its primary contents, what remains is the continuum of the visual field. That field is quasi-spatial but is not space or a surface in space; roughly, it is a two-dimensional continuous manifold with relations of beside, above and within, and with closed lines that bound regions. Yet these relations are not objective-spatial ones. It makes no sense to say a point in the visual field is a metre from the corner of this table, and the appearance of a house is not beside or above the house. The example exposes a category mistake that recurs throughout the debate Husserl inherits: assigning to sensory content, or to the appearance as a lived experience, the properties of the object it presents.

Time behaves the same way. The phenomenological data are the apprehensions of time, the experiences in which something temporal in the objective sense appears, together with the experiential moments that specifically found such apprehension, the temporal contents of apprehension. Husserl identifies these last with what moderate nativism calls the "originally temporal." None of this is objective time; phenomenological analysis cannot find the least scrap of it. The "original temporal field" is not a stretch of objective time, and the lived now is not a point of objective time. Objective space, objective time and the objective world of actual things and events are all transcendencies. Husserl blocks a misreading at once: transcendent here does not mean a mysterious thing in itself behind appearances. It means phenomenal space, phenomenal spatiotemporal reality, the appearing shape and the appearing temporal form. These are not experiences, and the orderings found within experiences as genuine immanent contents do not fit into the empirical, objective order. The transcendence in question is that of the appearing object over the experience in which it appears, not that of a Kantian noumenon over phenomena.

A full phenomenology of space would have to investigate the local data, which nativism posits in a psychological attitude, that make up the immanent order of the visual sensory field, and that field itself. These local data stand to appearing objective places as quality data stand to appearing objective qualities. If one speaks of "local signs," one should equally speak of "quality signs." The sensed red is a phenomenological datum which, animated by a function of apprehension, presents an objective quality; it is not itself a quality. A quality in the proper sense, a property of the appearing thing, is the perceived red, not the sensed red, and the sensed red is called red only equivocally. Where we speak of a "coincidence" between the two, we must remember that the sensed red acquires its value as presenting a thing's quality only through apprehension, and that the coincidence of what presents and what is presented is not the coincidence of an identity-consciousness whose correlate is "one and the same." Husserl here applies the *Logical Investigations* doctrine of sensation and apprehension with full rigour: sensation is not a faint copy of the quality, and the presentational relation is sui generis, distinct from identification.

If we call "sensed" a phenomenological datum that, through apprehension, makes something objective conscious as bodily given, and call that objective item "perceived," then we must distinguish a sensed temporal item from a perceived one. The perceived one is objective time. The sensed one is not objective time or a position in it, but the phenomenological datum through whose empirical apprehension the relation to objective time is constituted. Temporal data, or "temporal signs," are not times. The sensed temporal data are themselves laden with characters of apprehension, and these carry norms and entitlements: to measure the appearing times and temporal relations against one another, to order them objectively, to separate apparent from actual orders. What is constituted as objectively valid being in this way is finally the one infinite objective time, in which all things and events, bodies and their physical properties, souls and their states have determinate places measurable by clocks. A note added later bears directly on the sense of the distinction and revises it. "Sensed" would then name only a relational concept, indicating that something functions as presenting, without settling whether what is sensed is sensuous, or immanent in the manner of the sensuous; it would leave open whether what is sensed is itself already constituted, perhaps quite differently from sensuous content. Husserl adds that the whole distinction is best set aside, since not every constitution follows the schema of apprehension-content and apprehension. The note anticipates the decisive turn of §§34–40 and the discussion in Beilage VI: the temporal "contents" of 1905 are not contents waiting for an apprehension but are themselves constituted in the flow, and at that deepest level the schema fails.

Husserl does not decide whether objective temporal determinations ultimately rest on noting differences and relations among temporal data, or even on directly matching those data. Either way, a sensed simultaneity is not objective simultaneity, sensed equality of phenomenological intervals is not objective equality of time-spans, and the sensed absolute temporal datum is not simply the experience of objective time; this holds even for the absolute datum of the now. To grasp a content evidently, just as it is lived, is not to grasp an objectivity in the empirical sense, the kind of objective reality meant when we speak of objective things, events, positions in space and time, and spatial and temporal shapes. The claim guards against an inference that would ruin the analysis: the evidence attaching to immanent temporal data does not transfer to objective time. The lived now has absolute evidence as lived, but that evidence does not certify a point of world-time.

The chalk example makes the point concrete. I look at a piece of chalk, close my eyes and open them again. I have two perceptions, and I say I see the same chalk twice. The contents are separated in time, and I see a phenomenological temporal gap, but the object shows no gap; it stays the same. Duration belongs to the object, change to the phenomenon. Conversely, I can sense a succession where objectively there is coexistence. The lived content is "objectified": the object is constituted out of the material of lived contents by way of apprehension. But the object is not a sum or complex of these contents, which do not enter into it at all; it is more than content and in a certain sense something other. Objectivity belongs to "experience," to the unity of experience, the lawful connection of nature. Phenomenologically put, objectivity is not constituted in the primary contents but in the characters of apprehension and in the laws belonging to their essence. To see this through fully is the task of the phenomenology of knowledge. The example establishes the non-coincidence of immanent and objective temporal structure in both directions, which is why no analysis of sensory succession alone can yield objective duration. It also locates constitution in apprehension, and so in an act-theoretical framework that the later strata of the book will deepen and partly dissolve.

#### §2. The Question of the "Origin of Time"

These reflections separate the phenomenological, or epistemological, question of origin from the psychological question about any concept constitutive of experience, including the concept of time. The epistemological question of the possibility of experience is the question of the essence of experience, and clarifying its phenomenological possibility requires going back to the phenomenological data of which what is experienced phenomenologically consists. Since experience divides into improper and proper, and proper experience, intuitive and ultimately adequate, supplies the standards by which experience is assessed, a phenomenology of proper experience is especially needed. Husserl takes the Kantian question of the possibility of experience and converts it into a descriptive question of essence answered by return to data. The improper/proper distinction comes from the *Logical Investigations*' theory of signitive and intuitive acts; for time it will separate the intuited temporal field from the merely conceptual representation of infinite time.

Accordingly, the question about the essence of time leads back to the question of the "origin" of time. This origin question concerns the primitive forms of time-consciousness, in which the primitive differences of the temporal are constituted intuitively and properly as the original sources of all evidence concerning time. It must not be confused with the psychological question of origin, the dispute between empiricism and nativism, which asks from what original sensory material objective intuition of space and time arises in the human individual or even the species. Empirical genesis is a matter of indifference; experiences interest us for their objective sense and descriptive content. Psychological apperception takes experiences as states of empirical persons, psychophysical subjects, establishes psychic or psychophysical connections among them, and traces their formation and transformation according to natural laws. Phenomenological apperception is quite different. We place experiences within no reality and deal with reality only as meant, represented, intuited or conceptually thought. For time this means that time-experiences interest us; whether they are themselves objectively dated, whether they belong to the world of things and psychic subjects with a place, efficacy, empirical being and origin there, is no concern of ours, and we know nothing of it. What concerns us is that objective temporal data are meant in these experiences. Phenomenology describes that the relevant acts mean this or that objective item, and more exactly it exhibits the a priori truths belonging to the various constitutive moments of objectivity. We seek to clarify the a priori of time by exploring time-consciousness, bringing out its essential constitution and the contents of apprehension and act-characters specifically belonging to time, to which the a priori temporal laws essentially belong. Husserl means self-evident laws: that the fixed temporal order is a two-dimensional infinite series, that two different times can never be simultaneous, that their relation is asymmetrical, that it is transitive, that every time has an earlier and a later. "Origin" thus means epistemic source, not causal beginning; this is the standard confusion the whole Section One will diagnose in Brentano. The a priori laws listed here return in §33, where Husserl derives them from the structure of the flow. "Two-dimensional" can only mean unbounded in two directions, toward past and future; the substance of the list is linearity, asymmetry, transitivity and unboundedness.

### Section One: Brentano's Theory of the Origin of Time

#### §3. The Original Associations

Husserl approaches the problem through Brentano's doctrine of the origin of time. Brentano believes he has found the solution in "original associations," in the arising of immediate memory-representations that attach to each perceptual representation without mediation and by an exceptionless law. When we perceive something, what is perceived regularly remains present to us for a while, though not unmodified. Apart from changes in intensity and fullness, a peculiar modification always occurs: what persists in consciousness appears as more or less past, as if pushed back in time. When a melody sounds, a tone does not vanish entirely when the stimulus or the nerve process it excites ceases. If it did, we could not notice the relations between successive tones; we would have one tone at each moment, perhaps an empty pause between them, but never the representation of a melody. Yet mere persistence is not enough either. If the tone-representations remained unmodified, we would have a chord, or rather a dissonant jumble, as if all the tones sounded so far were struck together. Only because each tone-sensation, once its stimulus has gone, awakens from itself a similar representation endowed with a temporal determination, and because this determination keeps changing, can there be a representation of a melody in which each tone has its place and measure. Husserl reports the theory sympathetically and keeps its decisive insight: neither disappearance nor unmodified persistence can explain the perception of succession. A specific, continuous modification is required. Brentano's error will lie in how he characterizes that modification, not in his seeing that one is needed. The vocabulary of stimulus and nerve process already shows the naturalistic framing that §6 will bracket.

It is therefore a general law, on Brentano's account, that each given representation is naturally followed by a continuous series of representations, each reproducing the content of the preceding one but attaching to the new one the moment of pastness. Three features define the doctrine: the series is continuous, each member is a reproduction, and the time-character is an added moment. The second and third will be Husserl's targets.

Phantasy thereby proves productive in a peculiar way. This is the only case where it creates a genuinely new moment of representation, namely the temporal moment, so that the origin of temporal representations lies in the domain of phantasy. Psychologists before Brentano looked in vain for the source of this representation because they confused subjective and objective time and did not see the real problem. Many think the origin of the concept of time is no different from that of colour or tone: as we sense a colour, we sense its duration, and duration is an immanent moment of sensation alongside quality and intensity. The external stimulus produces quality through the form of physical processes, intensity through their living force, and felt duration through its persistence. This is a palpable error. That the stimulus lasts only means that the sensation lasts, not that it is sensed as lasting. The duration of a sensation and the sensation of duration are two different things, and so are a succession of sensations and a sensation of succession. This maxim is Brentano's lasting contribution and Husserl adopts it without reservation; it recurs, sharpened, throughout the work. The thesis that the past is given in phantasy is the part Husserl will reject, since it assimilates the just-past to the imagined.

The same objection applies to those who trace the representation of duration and succession to the fact that psychic acts themselves last and succeed one another. Brentano carries out the refutation for sensations specifically. Husserl notes the extension because it will matter later: acts too have their time, and their succession does not by itself make their succession conscious. The problem of the consciousness of acts' own temporality, deferred here, returns in §§34–40 as the problem of the absolute flow.

One can conceive sensations lasting or succeeding one another without our knowing anything of it, because our representations would carry no temporal determination. Suppose the sensations vanished with their stimuli: we would have a succession of sensations with no inkling of a temporal course, since each new sensation would leave no memory of the preceding one having been, and at each moment we would be conscious only of the sensation just produced. Nor would mere persistence of sensations help. If earlier tones persisted unchanged while new ones sounded, we would have a simultaneous sum of tones, indistinguishable from all of them sounding at once. Likewise, if a moving body were held unchanged in consciousness at each position, the traversed space would appear continuously filled, but there would be no representation of motion. Representation of succession arises only because the earlier sensation does not remain unchanged but is modified in a peculiar way from moment to moment. In passing into phantasy it acquires a steadily changing temporal character, so that its content appears ever further pushed back. This modification is not a matter of sensation and is not produced by the stimulus. The stimulus produces the present content; when it vanishes, the sensation vanishes. But the sensation now becomes creative: it produces a phantasy-representation of equal or nearly equal content, enriched by temporal character, and this in turn awakens another, and so on. Brentano calls this steady attachment of a temporally modified representation to a given one "original association." Consistently, he denies that succession and change are perceived. We think we hear a melody, and so still hear what has just passed, but this is an illusion arising from the vividness of original association. The consequence exposes the cost of the theory: if the just-past is given only in phantasy, then there is no perception of anything temporally extended, and the experience of hearing a melody is a mistake. Husserl's own account will reverse this verdict by extending perception, in a precise sense, to include retention (§§16–17).

#### §4. The Acquisition of the Future and Infinite Time

The intuition of time arising from original association is not yet an intuition of infinite time. It is developed further, with respect to the past and also by adding a wholly new branch, the future. Relying on the appearance of momentary memory, phantasy forms representations of the future in a process like the one by which we sometimes arrive at new kinds of colour or tone by following familiar relations and forms. In phantasy we can transpose a melody heard in one key to other registers, and may thereby reach tones we have never heard. In the same way phantasy forms the representation of the future out of the past, in expectation. The view that phantasy can offer nothing new, only repeat moments given in perception, is mistaken. The full representation of time, the representation of infinite time, is a product of conceptual representation, like the infinite number series or infinite space. Brentano thus derives the future from the past by extrapolation, and makes expectation a secondary construct. Husserl will give protention an original place beside retention (§24, §§39–40), though even in his account the analysis of the future remains much thinner than that of the past. The relegation of infinite time to concept matches the improper representation of §2.

#### §5. The Alteration of Representations by the Temporal Characters

Brentano notes a further peculiarity. The temporal species past and future do not determine the elements of the sensory representations they combine with, as other added modes do, but alter them. A louder tone c is still a tone c, and so is a softer one; but a past tone c is not a tone c, and a past red is not a red. Temporal determinations alter essentially, as "represented," "wished" and the like do: a represented or possible thaler is not a thaler. Only the determination "now" is an exception. A now-existing A is an actual A; the present neither alters nor determines. Adding "now" to the representation of a man gives him no new feature; perceiving something as now adds nothing to its quality, intensity or place. Brentano accordingly calls the modifying temporal predicates irreal and only the determination now real. Remarkably, the irreal determinations can belong to a continuous series with a single real determination, attaching to it by infinitesimal differences. The real now continually becomes irreal. Asked how the real could become irreal by the addition of modifying temporal determinations, one can only answer that every arising and passing in the present is necessarily followed by temporal determinations of every kind: whatever is will, evidently, have been, simply because it is, and is now a future having-been. The distinction between determining and modifying predicates is Brentano's, and Husserl will take it over in his own way, holding that "past" modifies the mode of givenness without altering the content, so that the past tone is identically the same tone given otherwise. Brentano's thesis that the present is real and the past irreal confuses the ontological status of the object with the mode of its givenness; §6 will make the objection. The closing observation, that what is will necessarily have been, anticipates Husserl's laws of temporal modification and the role of the "now" as perpetually turning into a "just past."

#### §6. Critique

What does the theory achieve, and what does it aim at? It does not stand on the ground required for a phenomenological analysis: it works with transcendent presuppositions, with existing temporal objects that exert "stimuli" and "cause" sensations in us. It presents itself as a theory of the psychological origin of the representation of time. Yet it also contains pieces of an epistemological reflection on the conditions of possibility of a consciousness of objective temporality, a consciousness that itself appears, and must be able to appear, as temporal. It further contains discussions of the peculiarity of temporal predicates, which must relate to psychological and phenomenological predicates, though these relations are not pursued. The critique begins by sorting rather than refuting: Brentano mixes three inquiries, and only one of them, the epistemological, belongs to phenomenology. The phrase "a consciousness that itself appears as temporal" plants the problem of the self-temporalization of consciousness that the late sections confront.

Brentano's law of original association, by which representations of momentary memory attach to each perception, is evidently a psychological law of the formation of new psychic experiences from given ones. These experiences are psychic and objectified, they have their own time, and their becoming and being produced are at issue. Such matters belong to psychology and do not concern us. But the account contains a phenomenological core, and the following analysis will hold to that alone. Duration, succession and change appear. What does this appearing involve? In a succession, a "now" appears, and in unity with it a "past." The unity of a consciousness that intentionally spans what is present and what is past is a phenomenological datum. The question is whether the past really appears in this consciousness in the manner of phantasy, as Brentano claims. Husserl isolates the fact any theory must respect, the single consciousness that holds now and just-past together, and reduces the dispute to one question about the mode in which the just-past is given. The rest of the book can be read as an answer: it is given neither as image nor as phantasy, but in a sui generis intentionality.

When Brentano speaks of acquiring the future, he distinguishes original intuition of time, the product of original association, from extended intuition of time, which also springs from phantasy but not from original association. We may also contrast intuition of time with improper representation of time, the representation of infinite time and of times and relations not intuitively realized. It is most striking that Brentano's theory of time-intuition ignores the difference between perception of time and phantasy of time, which he cannot have overlooked. Even if he rejects talk of perceiving anything temporal except the now-point, the difference underlying the distinction between perceiving a succession and remembering a succession once perceived, or merely imagining one, cannot be denied and must be clarified. If original time-intuition is already a creature of phantasy, what distinguishes this phantasy of the temporal from the phantasy in which something long past is conscious, something that does not belong to the sphere of original association and is not joined in one consciousness with the momentary perception but was once joined with a past perception? If the presentification of a succession experienced yesterday is a presentification of yesterday's originally experienced temporal field, and that field is already a continuum of originally associated phantasies, we would have phantasies of phantasies. These are unresolved difficulties that call Brentano's analysis of original time-consciousness into question. Two notes are meaning-bearing. One specifies that "phantasy" here covers all presentifying acts and is not opposed to positing acts, so the objection does not trade on the unreality of imagined objects. The other points forward to §19, where Husserl gives the positive account. The objection is decisive: by locating the just-past in phantasy, Brentano loses the difference between the just-past and the recollected past, and with it any way to tell an original temporal field from its reproduction. Husserl's distinction between primary and secondary memory answers exactly this gap.

Brentano's failure has a further source: he does not distinguish between act and content, or between act, content of apprehension and apprehended object. We must decide to which of these the temporal moment belongs. If original association attaches a continuous series of representations to each perception and thereby produces the temporal moment, is that moment a difference essential to the act-character, or does it belong to the contents of apprehension, the sensory contents, when we consider colours or tones in their temporal being? Brentano holds that representing as such admits no differentiation, that representations differ only in their primary contents. On that view, the only remaining option is that phantasms attach continuously to the primary contents of perception, qualitatively alike and diminishing perhaps in intensity and fullness, while phantasy adds a new moment, the temporal one. This is unsatisfactory in several respects. Temporal characters, succession and duration are found not only in primary contents but in apprehended objects and in apprehending acts. An analysis confined to one layer will not do; it must follow every layer of constitution. The irony is pointed: Brentano was the first to draw the radical distinction between primary contents and act-characters, yet his theory of time ignores act-character and places time among contents. Husserl's demand to follow "all layers of constitution" lays out the structure of the Second and Third Sections: temporal objects, the appearances of them, and finally the consciousness in which those appearances are themselves temporal.

Set aside transcending interpretations and take the view at its strongest for immanent contents: temporal modification would consist in adding a moment, the temporal moment, interwoven with the rest of the content, its quality, intensity and so on. Let a lived tone A have just sounded, be renewed by original association and held fast in its content. Then A, apart perhaps from weakened intensity, has not passed at all but has stayed present. The only difference would be that association is creative and adds a new moment, called "past," which is graded and varies continuously, so that A is more or less past. The past, insofar as it falls within original time-intuition, would then be present at the same time. The temporal moment "past" would be a present experiential moment in the same sense as the redness we actually experience, which is an evident absurdity. The argument is a reductio: a content plus a "past"-tag is still a present content, so the theory turns pastness into a present property and thereby abolishes the past it was supposed to explain.

One might reply that A itself is past and that what is in consciousness by original association is a new content, A with the character "past." But if a like content A is continuously in consciousness, even with a new moment, A has not passed but endures; it is present, continually present, together with its new moment "past," past and present in one. And how do we know that an A existed earlier, before this present one? Where do we get the idea of the past at all? The presence of an A in consciousness, with a new moment attached, even if we call it the moment of pastness, cannot explain the transcending consciousness that A is past. It cannot give the faintest idea that what I now have as A with its new character is identical with something not now in consciousness but that has been. What, then, are the now-lived moments of original association? If they are themselves times, we contradict ourselves: they are all here now, enclosed in one consciousness of an object, so simultaneous, while succession excludes simultaneity. If instead they are not temporal moments but "temporal signs," we have only a new word. Time-consciousness has not been analysed. It remains unclear how consciousness of a past is constituted on the basis of such signs: in what sense, in what way and through what apprehensions these lived moments function differently from quality moments, and so function that a consciousness that is supposed to be a now relates to a not-now. The sharpest point is that pastness requires a relation to something identical that is not now present, and no present content, however marked, supplies that relation. The "temporal signs" option also strikes at Husserl's own §1 vocabulary of temporal data: the 1905 lectures already sense that the apprehension-content schema cannot explain how a present datum gives a non-present phase.

Equally dubious is the attempt to present the past as something non-real, non-existent. An added psychic moment cannot create irreality or abolish present existence. The whole domain of original associations is a present, real experience, and to it belongs the whole series of original temporal moments produced by original association, along with the other moments belonging to the temporal object. Husserl here turns Brentano's real/irreal distinction of §5 against him. Brentano wanted the past to be irreal, yet his mechanism consists of really present phantasms; the irreality is asserted, not achieved. "Real" (reell) in this context means really inherent in the experience, not existent in the world.

An analysis of time-consciousness is therefore useless if it tries to make the intuited stretch of time intelligible through continuously graded new moments that are somehow attached to, or fused with, the content moments constituting the temporally located object. In short: temporal form is neither itself a temporal content nor a complex of new contents somehow joined to temporal content. Brentano did not fall into the sensualist error of reducing all experiences to primary contents; he was the first to recognize the radical separation of primary contents and act-characters. Yet his theory of time shows that he did not attend to the very act-characters decisive for it. How time-consciousness is possible and is to be understood remains unsolved. The formula "temporal form is neither temporal content nor a complex of contents" is the result Section One delivers. It closes off every attempt to explain time by adding a datum, and directs the search toward modes of consciousness. In 1905 Husserl still thinks of these modes as act-characters of apprehension; the later strata will replace that framework with the self-constituting flow, while preserving the negative result.

### Section Two: Analysis of Time-Consciousness

#### §7. Interpretation of the Grasping of Temporal Objects as Momentary Grasping and as Enduring Act

A thought from Herbart, taken up by Lotze and influential ever since, drives Brentano's theory: to grasp a succession of representations, a and b, these must be wholly simultaneous objects of one relating knowledge that gathers them indivisibly in a single act. Lotze argues that every representation of a path, a transition or a distance, every comparison of several elements, must be the product of a timelessly unifying knowing, and would be impossible if representing itself dissolved into temporal succession. On this view it is evident that intuiting a stretch of time happens in a now, at one point of time. Any consciousness of a whole made of distinguishable moments, any consciousness of relation or complex, must embrace its object in an indivisible instant, and a consciousness of a whole whose parts are successive can be intuitive only if the parts come together as representatives in a momentary intuition. William Stern objected to this "dogma of the momentariness of a whole of consciousness." Some apprehensions, he argued, arise only on the basis of a temporally extended content of consciousness and stretch over a span, the "presence-time." A discrete succession can be held together by one band of consciousness, one act of apprehension, despite the non-simultaneity of its members. That successive tones make a melody is possible only because successive psychic processes unite "of themselves" into a whole: they are successive in consciousness but fall within one total act. We do not have the tones all at once, nor hear the melody because earlier tones persist at the last, but the tones form a successive unity with a common effect, the form of apprehension, which is completed only with the last tone. There is thus perception of successive unities as of coexisting ones, and direct apprehension of identity, equality, similarity and difference; comparison does not require that a memory image of the first tone stand beside the second. Husserl sets out the two positions as the poles of the contemporary debate. The momentary view preserves the unity of the consciousness of succession at the cost of making that consciousness instantaneous; Stern's view preserves extension at the cost of giving no account of how the extended act holds its earlier phases. Husserl will reject both: the consciousness of a succession is neither a durationless act nor merely an act spread over time, but a now-phase that intends the just-past in retention.

What blocks clarification in Stern's account and the discussion around it is the lack of the distinctions already missing in Brentano. One must ask, first, how to understand the apprehension of transcendent temporal objects that extend over a duration and fill it either in steady sameness, like unchanging things, or with constant change, like processes, motion and alteration. Such objects are constituted in a manifold of immanent data and apprehensions that themselves run off in succession. Can these successive representing data be united in a now-moment? A wholly new question then arises: how is time itself, the duration and succession of objects, constituted alongside the immanent and transcendent temporal objects? These directions of description belong together and none can be solved without the others. Evidently, the perception of a temporal object is itself temporal: perception of duration presupposes duration of perceiving, and the perception of any temporal shape has its own temporal shape. Abstracting from all transcendence, perception keeps, in all its phenomenological constituents, a phenomenological temporality belonging to its inalienable essence. Since objective temporality is always constituted phenomenologically and appears to us as objectivity only through this constitution, a phenomenological analysis of time cannot clarify the constitution of time without regard to the constitution of temporal objects. By temporal objects in the special sense Husserl means objects that are not just unities in time but contain temporal extension in themselves. When a tone sounds, I can make the tone my object without making its duration, or the tone in its duration, my object; the latter is the temporal object. The same holds for a melody, for any change, and for any persistence taken as such. Take a melody. The matter seems simple: we hear the melody, and hearing is perceiving. Yet the first tone sounds, then the second, then the third. When the second sounds, do I not hear it and no longer the first? Then I do not really hear the melody, only the present tone; I owe the elapsed part to memory and my not taking the present tone as the end to expectation. This explanation will not do, because it transfers to the single tone. Each tone has temporal extension: when struck I hear it as now, while it continues it has an ever new now, and each preceding now turns into a past. So I hear only the current phase of the tone, and the objectivity of the whole lasting tone is constituted in a continuum of acts that is partly memory, in a smallest, punctual part perception, and in a further part expectation. This seems to lead back to Brentano, and a deeper analysis must begin. The editor notes that the paragraph draws partly on 1905 sheets numbered 52–53, so its formulations still belong to the lecture's original stratum. Two results carry weight. The first is the regress argument from the melody to the single tone: any theory that confines perception to the present and assigns the past to memory dissolves even the simplest duration into a point plus memories. The second is the thesis that perception of duration presupposes duration of perceiving, which raises the question of how the perceiving itself is conscious as enduring. The concept of the temporal object in the special sense, an object that contains its own extension, becomes the guiding example of the whole analysis.

#### §8. Immanent Temporal Objects and Their Modes of Appearance

Husserl now excludes all transcendent apprehension and positing and takes the tone purely as a hyletic datum, a sensory content considered in its immanence. It begins and ends, and the unity of the whole process recedes after the end into an ever more distant past. While it sinks back I still hold it in a "retention," and as long as retention lasts the tone keeps its own temporality; it is the same tone with the same duration. I can attend to the way it is given. The tone and its duration are conscious in a continuity of modes, a "constant flow," and one phase of this flow is the consciousness of the beginning tone, in which the first point of its duration is conscious as now. The tone is conscious as now as long as any of its phases is conscious as now. When some phase other than the first is the actual now, a continuity of phases is conscious as "before," and the stretch from the starting point to the now-point is conscious as elapsed, while the rest of the duration is not yet conscious. At the end-point, that point is conscious as now and the whole duration as elapsed. Throughout this flow one and the same tone is conscious as lasting, as lasting now. Before, unless expected, it is not conscious; afterward it is "still" conscious "for a while" in retention as having been, and can be held in a fixing regard. The whole stretch then stands there as something dead, no longer producing itself in a living way, a formation animated by no producing point of the now, but still continuously modified and sinking back into "emptiness." The modification of the whole stretch is then essentially identical with that which the elapsed part of the duration underwent during the period of actuality, as consciousness passed into ever new productions. The editor reports that §§8–10 rest on a manuscript of 10–13 November 1911. This places these sections in the latest stratum of the lectures: the vocabulary of "flow," of the "producing point," and of the dead as against living stretch belongs to the mature doctrine, not to 1905. The term "retention" makes its first appearance here, replacing Brentano's "original association" and the 1905 "primary memory." The decisive structural claim is the identity of the modification before and after the end of the tone: the passing of the elapsed portion while the tone still sounds, and its passing after it has ceased, are one and the same process.

What has been described is how the immanent temporal object "appears" in constant flux, how it is "given." Describing this is not describing the appearing duration itself. The same tone with its duration was presupposed by the description, not described. The same duration is now a duration actually building itself up, later an elapsed duration, still conscious or "as it were" produced anew in recollection. The tone sounding now is the one of which the later flow of consciousness says that it has been. The points of the duration recede for consciousness as the points of a stationary object in space recede when I move away from it. The object keeps its place and the tone keeps its time; each point of time stays fixed but flees into distances of consciousness, its distance from the producing now growing ever greater. The tone is the same, but the tone "in the way that" it appears is ever different. Husserl here draws the distinction on which all his analysis depends: the temporal object with its fixed time, and the same object in its changing mode of givenness. Temporal position is fixed; orientation toward the now changes. The spatial analogy should be taken precisely: just as perspectival foreshortening does not alter the thing's size, the receding of a tone into the past does not alter its temporal position or duration. The analogy also blocks a standard misreading of retention as a weakened copy: the receding tone is not a fainter tone but the same tone given at an increasing temporal distance.

#### §9. The Consciousness of the Appearances of Immanent Objects

More exactly, two directions of description can be distinguished. First, we can make evident statements about the immanent object in itself: that it endures now, that part of its duration has elapsed, that the point of duration grasped in the now continuously sinks into the past while a new point enters the now, that the elapsed duration moves away from the now-point into an ever more remote past. Second, we can speak of the way in which all these differences in the "appearing" of the tone and its duration are conscious. For the part of the duration that reaches into the actual now we speak of perception and say that the enduring tone is perceived, though at any moment only the point characterized as now is perceived in the full and proper sense. Of the elapsed stretch we say that it is conscious in retentions: the parts or phases nearest the now, which cannot be sharply bounded, are conscious with diminishing clarity, the more remote phases quite unclearly and emptily. After the whole duration has elapsed, what is still nearest to the now may keep a little clarity, while the whole vanishes into darkness, into an empty retentional consciousness, and finally disappears entirely, if one may assert this, once retention ceases. A note proposes paralleling these modes of givenness with the ways a spatial thing appears under changing orientation, and investigating the "temporal orientations" in which spatial things, also temporal objects, appear; Husserl defers this and stays within the immanent sphere. The distinction between the object in itself and the object in its mode of givenness here becomes the methodological division between two kinds of statement. The phrase "if one may assert this" registers a real difficulty: whether retention ever ends, or the retained past only becomes empty, is left open, and later manuscripts argue that it sinks into an undifferentiated background rather than vanishing.

In the clear sphere we find greater distinctness and separation, the more so the nearer to the actual now. The farther we move from the now, the greater the blurring and compression. Reflective immersion in the unity of an articulated process shows that a segment of it "contracts" as it sinks into the past, a kind of temporal perspective within original temporal appearance, analogous to spatial perspective. As the temporal object moves into the past it contracts and at the same time darkens. Temporal perspective is a feature of the mode of givenness, not of the object: the past segment does not become shorter, but its phases are given with diminishing differentiation, as distant objects appear smaller without being so.

The task is now to examine what we can find and describe as the phenomenon of time-constituting consciousness, the consciousness in which temporal objects with their temporal determinations are constituted. We distinguish the enduring immanent object from the object in its how, the object conscious as actually present or as past. Each temporal being "appears" in some continually changing mode of running-off, and the "object in its mode of running-off" is constantly another, although we say that the object, each point of its time and its time itself are one and the same. We cannot call this appearance, "object in its mode of running-off," consciousness, any more than we call the spatial phenomenon, the body as it appears from this or that side, near or far, consciousness. "Consciousness," the "experience," relates to its object by means of an appearance in which precisely the "object in its how" stands before us. We must therefore recognize that "intentionality" has a double sense, according as we consider the relation of the appearance to what appears, or the relation of consciousness to the "appearing in its how" on the one hand and to the appearing simply on the other. Husserl here distinguishes three items that ordinary talk collapses: the object simply, the object in its mode of running-off, and the consciousness of both. The middle term is not a mental image or a part of consciousness; it is the object as given, the forerunner of the noematic "object in its how" in *Ideas I*. The resulting ambiguity of intentionality anticipates the "double intentionality" of §39, though there the issue will be the flow's relation to the object and to itself, not the relation of consciousness to the object and to its appearance.

#### §10. The Continua of Running-Off Phenomena. The Diagram of Time

For the phenomena that constitute immanent temporal objects, Husserl prefers to avoid the word "appearances," since these phenomena are themselves immanent objects and "appearances" in an entirely different sense. He speaks instead of "running-off phenomena," or better of "modes of temporal orientation," and, for the immanent objects themselves, of their "running-off characters," such as now and past. The running-off phenomenon is a continuity of steady changes forming an inseparable unity: it cannot be split into stretches or divided into phases that could exist by themselves. The segments we abstractly isolate can exist only within the whole run-off, and the same holds for its phases and points. This continuity is, in a sense, unchangeable in form. It is unthinkable that the continuity of phases should contain the same phase-mode twice, or spread over a whole sub-stretch; just as each point of time is individually distinct from every other and none can occur twice, no mode of running-off can occur twice. The modes of running-off of an immanent temporal object have a beginning, a source-point, the mode in which the object begins to be, characterized as now. In the steady progression of these modes, each later phase is itself a continuity, a steadily widening continuity of pasts. To the continuity of running-off modes of the object's duration we oppose the continuity of running-off modes of each point of the duration, which is contained in the first: the running-off continuity of an enduring object is a continuum whose phases are the continua of running-off modes of the various time-points of the object's duration. Following the concrete continuity, we advance through steady modifications, and the running-off mode, the running-off continuity of the time-points in question, changes continuously. As a new now always arises, the now turns into a past, and the whole running-off continuity of pasts belonging to the preceding point moves "down" uniformly into the depth of the past. In the diagram, the horizontal line AE represents the series of now-points, the oblique line AA′ the sinking-down, and the vertical EA′ the continuum of phases, the now-point with its horizon of the past; the extension of E stands for the series of nows possibly filled with other objects. The ordinates represent the running-off modes of the enduring object: they grow from A, a point, to a determinate stretch with the last now as its end-point. Then begins the series of modes that contain no now of this duration; the duration is no longer actual but past and sinking ever deeper. The figure thus gives a complete picture of the double continuity of running-off modes. A later note adds that the diagram ignores the limitedness of the temporal field: it provides for no end of retention, and ideally a consciousness is perhaps possible in which everything is retained. The terminological correction at the start matters: the running-off phenomena are not appearances of the object in the sense in which an appearance is something that could itself be an object; Husserl is groping for a level that is not itself objectlike, which §36 will name the absolute flow. The phases are dependent moments in the sense of the Third Logical Investigation, abstracta that cannot exist alone; this is why the now cannot be an atom from which the flow is assembled. The double continuity is the structure that makes the flow two-dimensional: each actual phase is a continuum of retentional modifications, and the succession of such phases is itself continuous. The diagram's vertical is the cross-section of the flow at a now; its oblique lines track the sinking of a single point.

#### §11. Primal Impression and Retentional Modification

The "source-point" with which the "production" of the enduring object begins is a primal impression. This consciousness is in constant change: the bodily present tone-now turns continually, within consciousness, into a having-been, and a new tone-now continually replaces the one that has passed into modification. But when the consciousness of the tone-now, the primal impression, passes into retention, this retention is itself a now, something actually existing. While it is itself actual, though not an actual tone, it is retention of the tone that has been. A ray of attention can turn to the now, the retention, or to what is retentionally conscious, the past tone. Every actual now of consciousness is subject to the law of modification: it changes into retention of retention, continuously. There thus arises a continuum of retention in which each later point is retention of every earlier one, and each retention is already a continuum. The tone begins and "it" continues; the tone-now turns into tone-having-been, and impressional consciousness passes continuously into ever new retentional consciousness. Going along the flow, we have a continuous series of retentions belonging to the starting point. Moreover, each earlier point of this series, as a now, is again shaded off retentionally. To each of these retentions is attached a continuity of retentional modifications, and this continuity is itself a point of actuality that shades off retentionally. This leads to no simple infinite regress, because each retention is in itself continuous modification that carries the heritage of the past in the form of a series of shadings. Each later retention is not merely a continuous modification arising from the primal impression, but a continuous modification of all earlier continuous modifications of the same starting point. The editor dates this paragraph to a manuscript of 1908–1909. The new term "primal impression" names the source-point without making it a content: it is the consciousness of the now, not a sensation. The crucial move is the distinction between the retention as a present phase of consciousness and the retained as past; a retention is now, but what it intends is not. The nesting of retentions, retention of retention, explains how the whole elapsed stretch is given in each present phase without an infinite regress: the past is not re-represented at every step by a new act but carried forward as a continuous modification that includes all previous modifications. This structure later supports the "longitudinal intentionality" of §39.

So far Husserl has considered mainly perception, the original constitution of temporal objects, and tried to understand the time-consciousness given in it. But time-consciousness does not take only this form. When a temporal object has elapsed and its actual duration is over, consciousness of the now-past object does not die, though it no longer functions as perceptual or, better, impressional consciousness. Husserl notes in parenthesis that immanent objects are not, strictly speaking, constituted in a "perception." Primary memory, or retention, attaches continuously to the impression. This mode of consciousness was already analysed in the case of perception, since the continuity of phases attached to each now was nothing other than such retention, or a continuity of retentions. In perceiving a temporal object, whether immanent or transcendent, retention always terminates in an apprehension of the now, a perception in the sense of a positing-as-now. While a motion is perceived, a grasping-as-now takes place moment by moment, constituting the currently actual phase of the motion. This now-apprehension is the nucleus of a comet's tail of retentions referring to the earlier now-points of the motion. When perception ceases, when we no longer see the motion or the melody has ended in silence, the last phase is followed not by a new phase of perception but by a phase of fresh memory, then another, and so on. The same continuous complex is pushed back into the past and continuously modified until it disappears, since modification goes hand in hand with a weakening that ends in imperceptibility. The original temporal field is evidently limited, just as in perception. One may even venture that it always has the same extension; it shifts over the perceived and freshly remembered motion and its objective time much as the visual field shifts over objective space. This paragraph comes from the 1905 manuscript, and the change of register from the preceding 1908–1909 paragraph is visible: here Husserl still speaks of "apprehensions" and "positing-as-now," the language of the act-content schema. The "comet's tail" image presents retention as continuous with perception, not as a separate act. The comparison of the temporal field with the visual field returns to §1: the temporal field is a fixed-size window moving over objective time, not a stretch of that time.

#### §12. Retention as a Peculiar Intentionality

It remains to examine what kind of modification retentional modification is.

People speak of sensory contents fading or dying away when proper perception passes into retention. But what has been said already shows that retentional "contents" are not contents in the original sense. When a tone dies away, it is first sensed with particular fullness, or intensity, followed by a rapid decline. The tone is still there and still sensed, but only as an echo. This genuine tone-sensation must be distinguished from the tonal moment in retention. The retained tone is not present but "primarily remembered" in the now; it is not really, inherently present in retentional consciousness. Nor can the tonal moment belonging to retention be another tone really present, even a very weak one of the same quality as an echo. A present tone can recall a past one, represent it, picture it; but that already presupposes another representation of the past. Intuition of the past cannot itself be picturing. It is an original consciousness. Echoes exist, of course, but where we recognize them they belong to perception, not to retention. The echo of a violin tone is a weak present violin tone and is utterly different from the retention of the loud tone just past. Echoing, and after-images generally, have nothing to do with the essence of retention. The editor dates §§12–13 to a manuscript of 1908 or slightly later. Husserl separates three things that the psychology of his day ran together: a fading sensation, an after-image or echo, and retention. The first two are present contents; retention is a consciousness of the past. The argument that a present content can stand for a past one only if the past is already given otherwise is decisive against every image theory of memory, and it is the reason retention must be "original consciousness," a kind of perceiving of the just-past rather than a representation of it.

It does belong to the essence of time-intuition that at each point of its duration, which we can reflectively make into an object, it is consciousness of what has just been, and not merely consciousness of the now-point of what appears as enduring. In this consciousness what has just been is conscious in proper continuity, and in each phase in a definite "manner of appearance," with the differences of "content" and "apprehension." Attend to a steam whistle as it sounds: at each point there stands an extension, and in the extension the "appearance," which in each phase has its quality-moment and its apprehension-moment. Yet the quality-moment is not a real quality, not a tone that would be really present, that could be addressed as an immanent tone-content now existing. The real content of now-consciousness may contain sensed tones, which in objectifying apprehension must be designated as perceived and present, never as past. Retentional consciousness really contains consciousness of the past of the tone, primary tone-memory, and is not to be split into sensed tone and apprehension as memory. Just as a phantasy-tone is not a tone but a phantasy of a tone, so that tone-phantasy and tone-sensation are fundamentally different and not the same thing interpreted differently, so the primarily remembered tone is in principle something other than the perceived one, and retention of a tone is something other than sensation of a tone. This paragraph shows Husserl breaking the content-apprehension schema at a specific point. He still speaks of quality-moment and apprehension-moment, yet denies that retention consists of a present sensed content plus a memory apprehension. The parallel with phantasy is the key: since the *Logical Investigations*, Husserl had held that phantasy is not a sensation interpreted differently but a different kind of consciousness with its own content (phantasm). Retention gets the same autonomy. Here lies the root of the later claim, in §§39–40, that the flow is not composed of contents at all.

#### §13. Necessity of an Impression Preceding Every Retention. The Evidence of Retention

Is it a law that primary memory is possible only in continuous connection with a preceding sensation or perception, that each retentional phase is thinkable only as a phase and cannot be spread out into a stretch identical in all its phases? It will be declared evident. The empirical psychologist, used to treating everything psychic as bare fact, will deny it: why could there not be a consciousness that begins with a fresh memory without a prior perception? Factually, perception may be needed to produce fresh memory, but the opposite is thinkable. Against this Husserl teaches the a priori necessity that a corresponding perception or primal impression precede retention. A phase is thinkable only as a phase, without possible extension. The now-phase is thinkable only as the limit of a continuity of retentions, just as each retentional phase is thinkable only as a point of such a continuum, for every now of time-consciousness. A whole completed series of retentions is equally unthinkable without a preceding perception. This means that the series of retentions belonging to a now is itself a limit and necessarily changes; what is remembered "sinks ever further into the past," and more than that, it is necessarily something sunk, something that necessarily permits an evident recollection leading it back to a reproduced now. The conflict is between eidetic and factual necessity. Retention intends a "having been present"; its sense refers back to an impression, so a retention without a preceding impression is contradictory, not just unusual. The final clause adds an important principle: what has been retained is in principle open to recollection, which returns it to a now in reproduction. This ties retention to recollection as the possibility of its fulfilment, the theme of §15.

But can I not have a memory, even a primary one, of an A that never took place? Certainly; I can even have a perception of A when A does not in fact occur. So the evidence we claim is not that, if I have a retention of A, where A is a transcendent object, A must have preceded, but that A must have been perceived. Whether or not it was noticed, it stood there bodily, in a conscious if unnoticed or incidentally noticed way. For an immanent object, however, if a sequence, change or alteration of immanent data "appears," it is absolutely certain. Likewise, within a transcendent perception the immanent sequence essential to its construction is absolutely certain; a note refers to the discussion of inner and outer perception in §44. It is fundamentally wrong to argue: how can I know of a not-now in the now, since I cannot compare the not-now, which no longer is, with the now, namely the memory image present in the now? As if it belonged to the essence of memory that a present image be substituted for something else resembling it, which I could and must compare as in pictorial representation. Memory, or retention, is not image-consciousness but something entirely different. What is remembered is not now, otherwise it would be present and not past; and in retention it is not given as now, otherwise retention would not be memory but perception, or primal impression. Comparing what is no longer perceived but only retentionally conscious with something outside it makes no sense. As I see being-now in perception, and enduring being in extended perception as it is constituted, so in memory, insofar as it is primary, I see the past; it is given in it, and the givenness of the past is memory. Husserl separates two evidences. For transcendent objects, retention guarantees only that the object was perceived, not that it existed, since perception itself can deceive. For immanent objects, the retained succession is absolutely certain. The rejection of the comparison argument completes §12: retention does not compare a present image with an absent original, because it gives the past directly. "Givenness of the past is memory" states the thesis in its strongest form: retention is the original intuition of the past, as perception is of the present.

Returning to the question whether a retentional consciousness could exist that did not continue an impressional one, Husserl answers that it is impossible, because every retention refers back in itself to an impression. "Past" and "now" exclude each other. The identical thing can be now and past, but only by having endured between the now and the past. The final sentence states the condition under which an identical object can carry contradictory temporal characters: it carries them at different times, linked by duration. The point anticipates §31, where the identity of the temporal position across changing modes of givenness becomes the problem of objective time.

#### §14. Reproduction of Temporal Objects (Secondary Memory)

Primary memory, or retention, was described as a comet's tail attached to each perception. Secondary memory, recollection, must be sharply distinguished from it: after primary memory has gone, a new memory of the motion or melody can arise. It is tempting, with Brentano, to say that perception is constituted on the basis of sensations as presentation, primary memory on the basis of phantasies as re-presentation or presentification, and that presentifications can also occur independently of perception, which would be secondary memories. Serious objections stand against this, as the critique of Brentano showed. Consider recollecting a melody heard recently at a concert. The whole memory-phenomenon has, mutatis mutandis, exactly the same constitution as the perception of the melody. It has a privileged point: to the now-point of perception corresponds a now-point of memory. We run through the melody in phantasy, "as it were" hearing first the first tone, then the second; at each moment one tone or tone-phase stands in the now-point. The preceding ones are not erased: with the apprehension of the tone now appearing, the tone as it were heard now, fuse the primary memory of the tones just as it were heard and the expectation, protention, of those to come. The now-point again has a temporal halo, realized in a continuity of memorial apprehensions, and the whole memory of the melody consists in a continuum of such halo-continua. When the presentified melody has ended, a retention attaches to this as-it-were hearing: what was as it were heard still echoes for a while, as a continuity of apprehension no longer heard. Everything is thus like perception and primary memory, yet is not itself perception and primary memory. We do not really hear, and have not really heard, when we let a melody play tone by tone in memory or phantasy. In the earlier case we really hear, the temporal object itself is perceived, and the times, temporal determinations and relations are themselves given. After the melody has ended we no longer perceive it as present, but still have it in consciousness as just past, and its having just passed is not merely meant but a given fact, itself given and thus "perceived." In recollection, by contrast, the temporal present is a remembered, presentified present, and the past is a remembered, presentified past, not actually present, perceived, or primarily given and intuited. Recollection is the whole structure of time-consciousness, primal impression, retention and protention, reproduced with an index of "as it were." That is why Brentano's model fails: if retention were already phantasy, recollection would have to be a phantasy of a structure that already contains phantasy, and the difference between the just-past given and the long-past reproduced would vanish. Protention appears here for the first time, within reproduction; the word will be used for original consciousness only later. The claim that the just-past is "perceived" prepares §§16–17.

Recollection is, in turn, itself present: it is originally constituted recollection and afterwards just-past recollection. It builds itself up in a continuum of primal data and retentions, and in one with this constitutes, or rather reconstitutes, an immanent or transcendent enduring objectivity according to its direction. Retention, by contrast, produces no enduring objectivities, either originally or reproductively; it only holds what has been produced in consciousness and stamps it with the character "just past." A note refers ahead to §19. The editor ascribes this paragraph and §15 to a lost manuscript on the a priori of memory dated 1917, the last stratum of the redaction. Two levels are made explicit. As an act, recollection is itself constituted in inner time-consciousness like any other experience; as an intentional achievement, it reconstitutes an object. Retention is not an act in this sense: it constitutes nothing new but modifies what has been constituted. This distinction anticipates §§34–39, where retention becomes a structural moment of the flow and not an act among acts.

#### §15. The Modes of Accomplishing Reproduction

Recollection can be carried out in different forms. In simple grasping, a memory "surfaces" and we look at the remembered in a single ray of regard; the remembered is vague, perhaps presenting a privileged momentary phase intuitively, but this is not repeating memory. Or we carry out genuinely re-productive, repeating memory, in which the temporal object is fully rebuilt in a continuum of presentifications and we as it were perceive it again, but only as it were. The whole process is a presentifying modification of the perceptual process, with all its phases and stages down to the retentions, but everything bears the index of reproductive modification. Simple grasping and repeating reproduction differ as glance and enactment. Only the second has the full temporal structure of the original; the first gives the past object in a single ray, as a whole, without unfolding it.

The simple looking-at is also found directly on the basis of retention, as when a melody has ended within the unity of one retention and we turn back to a part of it, reflecting, without producing it again. This act is possible for anything that has come about in successive steps, including steps of spontaneity such as thinking, since objects of thought too are constituted successively. It seems that objectivities built up originally in temporal processes, member by member or phase by phase, as correlates of continuously and variously connected unified acts, can be grasped in a backward look as if they were objects completed in one point of time. But this givenness refers back to another, "original" one. The paragraph explains how objects that take time to constitute can afterward be held in a single grasp, the basis of what Husserl later calls polythetic and monothetic acts in *Ideas I* §119 and of the "retentional grip" on judgments in *Experience and Judgment*. The derivative givenness refers to the original stepwise one, which it presupposes.

Looking or looking back at what is retentionally given, and retention itself, are fulfilled in genuine re-presentation: what was given as just past proves identical with what is recollected. Retention's relation to recollection is that of an intention to its fulfilment. The empty, darkening retention is confirmed by the reproduction that renders the same past again, which gives retention a place in Husserl's general theory of intention and fulfilment.

Further differences between primary and secondary memory will appear when they are related to perception. The sentence announces §§16–17 and marks the boundary of the 1917 material inserted into the 1905 text.

#### §16. Perception as Presentation in Contrast to Retention and Recollection

The talk of "perception" needs further explanation. In the "perception of a melody," we separate the tone given now, which we call perceived, from the tones that have passed, which we call not perceived. Yet we also call the whole melody perceived, though only the now-point is. We do so because the extension of the melody is not merely given point by point in an extension of perceiving: the unity of retentional consciousness still "holds" the elapsed tones themselves in consciousness and continuously establishes the unity of the consciousness related to the unified temporal object, the melody. An objectivity like a melody cannot be "perceived," originally self-given, in any other form. The act built from now-consciousness and retentional consciousness is adequate perception of the temporal object, which includes temporal differences, and temporal differences are constituted precisely in such acts, in primal consciousness, retention and protention. A note points to §37, where acts appear as unities constituted in original time-consciousness. If the meaning intention is directed to the melody, the whole object, we have nothing but perception. If directed to a single tone or bar, we have perception as long as it is perceived and mere retention once it is past; objectively, the bar then appears no longer as "present" but as "past." The whole melody appears as present while it still sounds, while tones belonging to it, meant in one apprehension, still sound; it is past only when the last tone has gone. Husserl solves the puzzle of §7 by tying the concept of perception to the object meant: the melody is perceived because its extension is constituted in a now-consciousness together with retention, and an object with temporal extension can be originally given in no other way. "Adequate perception" is a strong claim: retention belongs to the perception of the temporal object not as a supplement but as part of what makes it self-giving. The term "primal consciousness" here names the impressional now-consciousness, not yet the self-awareness of the flow it will mean in Beilage IX.

This relativization carries over to the single tones. Each is constituted in a continuity of tone-data, of which only a punctual phase is present as now at any moment, while the others attach as a retentional tail. We can say that a temporal object is perceived, or impressionally conscious, as long as it is still being produced in continually new primal impressions. Perception is relative to the unit meant, and the criterion "still being produced in primal impressions" applies at every scale. There is no privileged minimal unit that would be perceived simply.

We have also called the past itself perceived. Do we not perceive the passing, are we not directly conscious of having-just-been, of the "just past" in its self-givenness? The sense of "perception" here evidently differs from the earlier one, and further distinctions are needed. If we distinguish perceiving and remembering, retentional, consciousness in grasping a temporal object, the contrast between perception and primary memory corresponds on the object side to that between "now present" and "past." Temporal objects essentially spread their matter over a stretch of time and can be constituted only in acts that constitute temporal differences. Time-constituting acts are essentially acts that constitute present and past; they have the type of the "temporal-object perceptions" described above in their peculiar apprehensional structure. Temporal objects must be constituted in this way: an act claiming to give a temporal object itself must contain "now-apprehensions," "past-apprehensions" and so on, in an originally constituting manner. Two concepts of perception now diverge: perception as consciousness of the now, contrasted with retention, and perception as originally giving consciousness, which includes retention. The paragraph also states the eidetic law that grounds the first: a self-giving of a temporal object must contain originally constituting consciousness of both present and past.

If we relate "perception" to the differences of givenness with which temporal objects appear, its opposite is primary memory and primary expectation, retention and protention, and perception and non-perception pass continuously into each other. In directly intuiting a melody, the bar, tone or part of a tone heard now is perceived, and what is momentarily intuited as past is not. The apprehensions pass continuously into one another and terminate in an apprehension that constitutes the now, which is only an ideal limit. It is a continuum of increase toward an ideal limit, as the continuum of red species converges toward an ideal pure red. But we do not have single apprehensions corresponding to single shades of red, which can be given separately; we have only, and by essence can only have, continuities of apprehensions, or rather one single continuum that continually modifies itself. If we divide this continuum into two adjoining parts, the part that includes or can constitute the now is privileged and constitutes the "rough" now, which at once falls apart into a finer now and a past if we divide further. The now is a limit, not a part: like pure red, it is approached but never isolated. The comparison differs in one decisive respect, which Husserl underlines: the shades of red can exist separately, the phases of time-consciousness cannot. The "rough now" explains our everyday talk of a present of some breadth.

Perception here is thus an act-character that gathers a continuity of act-characters and is distinguished by possessing that ideal limit. A like continuity without this limit is mere memory. In the ideal sense, perception, or impression, would be the phase of consciousness that constitutes the pure now, and memory every other phase of the continuity. But this is only an ideal limit, something abstract that can be nothing by itself. Moreover, even this ideal now is not toto coelo different from the not-now but is continuously mediated with it, to which corresponds the continuous transition from perception into primary memory. The punctual primal impression is an abstraction; the concrete is the continuum of which it is the limit. This guards against two misreadings at once: the now is not an atomic instant from which the flow is built, and the boundary between perception and retention is not a sharp break.

#### §17. Perception as Self-Giving Act in Contrast to Reproduction

A different opposition now confronts perception, the self-giving of the present that has its correlate in the given past: the opposition of perception to recollection, secondary memory. In recollection a now "appears," but in a quite different sense than in perception. This now is not perceived, not itself given, but presentified; it represents a now that is not given. Likewise, the course of the melody in recollection represents a "just past" but does not give it. In mere phantasy too, every individual is temporally extended, with its now, before and after, but these are merely imagined, like the whole object. A quite different concept of perception is at issue here: perception is the act that places something before our eyes as itself, the act that originally constitutes the object. Its opposite is presentification, re-presentation, the act that does not place an object before our eyes itself but presentifies it, as it were in an image, though not in the manner of genuine image-consciousness. Here there is no continuous mediation between perception and its opposite. Before, consciousness of the past, primary consciousness, was not perception, because perception was taken as the act constituting the now originally; consciousness of the past constitutes not a now but a "just been," something that intuitively preceded the now. But if we call perception the act in which all "origin" lies, which constitutes originally, then primary memory is perception. Only in primary memory do we see the past, only in it is the past constituted, presentatively and not representatively. The just-been, the before in contrast to the now, can be seen directly only in primary memory; its essence is to bring this new and peculiar thing to primary, direct intuition, just as the essence of now-perception is to bring the now to direct intuition. Recollection, like phantasy, offers mere presentification; it is "as it were" the same consciousness as the time-creating now-act and past-act, as it were the same but modified. The phantasied now represents a now without giving one, and the phantasied before and after only represent a before and after. A note refers to Beilage II. This is the central result of the 1905 lectures. Under the concept of perception as original giving, retention belongs with perception against recollection; under the concept of perception as consciousness of the now, it belongs with recollection against perception. The two oppositions differ in kind: perception and retention pass continuously into each other, while perception and presentification are separated discretely. Husserl's answer to Brentano is complete. The past is originally given, not imagined, and the line between original and reproductive consciousness runs between retention and recollection, not between the now and everything else.

#### §18. The Significance of Recollection for the Constitution of the Consciousness of Duration and Succession

The constitutive role of primary and secondary memory looks somewhat different when we consider the givenness of duration and succession themselves rather than of enduring objects. Suppose A arises as primal impression and lasts a while, and B arises together with a retention of A at some stage and constitutes itself as an enduring B. Throughout this process consciousness is consciousness of the same A "receding into the past," the same in the flow of its modes of givenness and the same in its form of being, "duration," at all points of that duration. The same holds of B and of the interval between the two durations or their points. But something new enters: B follows A. A succession of two enduring data is given with a determinate temporal form, a stretch spanning the succession. Consciousness of succession is originally giving consciousness, "perception" of this succession. Now consider its reproductive modification, recollection. I "repeat" the consciousness of the succession and presentify it in memory. I "can" do so, and "as often as I like." A priori, presentification of an experience lies within my "freedom," and this "I can" is a practical "I can," not a mere representation. What does presentifying a succession of experiences look like? One might say: first I presentify A, then B; if I originally had A–B, I now have A′–B′, where the prime marks memory. That will not do, since it would mean that I now have a memory A′ and "afterward" a memory B′, in the consciousness of a succession of memories. I would then have a perception of the succession of memories, not a memory of the succession. I must write (A–B)′. This consciousness indeed includes A′ and B′, and also a modified "–", a modified succession. The succession is not a third piece, as though the order of signs on paper designated it, but the law can be written: (A–B)′ = A′ –′ B′, meaning that there is a consciousness of the memory of A and of B, and also a modified consciousness of "B follows A." The editor again ascribes the section to the 1917 manuscript on the a priori of memory. The formal point is that reproduction modifies the whole structure, including the relation, and not merely its terms. A succession of memories is not a memory of a succession. The same error that Brentano made for perception, mistaking a succession of sensations for the sensation of a succession, recurs here for memory, and Husserl corrects it with the same maxim. The "I can" of reproduction introduces a practical, egological dimension absent from retention, which is passive.

Asking about the originally giving consciousness for a succession of enduring objects, and already for duration itself, we find that retention and recollection necessarily belong to it. Retention constitutes the living horizon of the now; in it I have consciousness of the "just past," but what is originally constituted, as in holding on to the tone just heard, is only the pushing back of the now-phase, or of the finished duration that is no longer being constituted or perceived. In "coincidence" with this receding "result," however, I can carry out a reproduction. Then the past of the duration is given to me, given precisely as "re-givenness" of the duration simply. Note that only past durations can I intuit "originally" in repeated acts, really intuit, identify and have as an identical object of many acts. I can live through the present again, but it cannot be re-given. When, as I always can, I return to one and the same succession and identify it as the same temporal object, I carry out a succession of recollecting experiences in the unity of an encompassing consciousness of succession: (A–B) – (A–B)′ – (A–B)″ and so on. How does this identifying look? The series is first a series of experiences: the original constitution of A–B, then the memory of it, then another, and so on. The whole series is originally given as presence. Of this series I can again have a memory, and of such a recollection another memory, in infinitum. By essential law every memory is iterable, not only in that arbitrarily high levels are possible, but in that this is a sphere of the "I can"; in principle each level is an activity of freedom, though hindrances are not excluded. Husserl makes an unexpected claim: retention gives the past, but only recollection gives the past as an identifiable object, the same in repeated acts. Objectivity in the full sense, identity across a plurality of acts, requires the possibility of repetition. This is the point of contact with the *Logical Investigations*' account of ideal objects as identical across acts, transposed to individual temporal objects, and it becomes the basis of §32's account of objective time.

How does the first recollection of that succession look? It is [(A–B) – (A–B)′]′. By the earlier law it contains (A–B)′ and [(A–B)′]′, a second-level memory, in succession, and also memory of the succession (–′). If I repeat again, I have still higher memory-modifications and at the same time the consciousness that I have carried out a repeating presentification several times in succession. This happens quite commonly. I knock twice on the table, presentify the succession, then notice that I first had the succession perceptually and then remembered it; then I notice that I have done this noticing, as third member of a series I can repeat, and so on. All this is very common in phenomenological method. The iteration is not an idle regress but the ordinary structure of phenomenological reflection itself, which consists in repeated reproductive modifications of experiences held in a series.

In a succession of like objects, objects identical in content but given only in succession and not as coexisting, we have a peculiar coincidence in the unity of one consciousness: a successive coincidence. This is spoken improperly, since they are set apart, conscious as a succession, separated by a stretch of time. The phrase "successive coincidence" names the passive synthesis by which like contents in succession overlap in consciousness despite their temporal separation, an early instance of what Husserl will later call association in the phenomenological sense.

And yet, if unlike objects with like salient moments are given in succession, "lines of equality" run as it were from one to the other, and with similarity, lines of similarity. We have a relatedness that is not constituted in relating contemplation but lies before all "comparison" and all "thinking," as a presupposition of the intuition of equality and difference. Strictly, only what is similar is "comparable," and "difference" presupposes "coincidence," the genuine unification of what is alike and connected in transition or coexistence. Husserl answers Stern's claim of §7, that equality and difference across succession are directly apprehended, but grounds it differently: not in an extended act of apprehension, but in a pre-predicative coincidence that precedes any comparing act. This is the germ of the theory of passive synthesis developed in the 1920s.

#### §19. The Difference between Retention and Reproduction (Primary and Secondary Memory or Phantasy)

Husserl's position on Brentano's thesis that the origin of temporal apprehension lies in phantasy is now finally settled. Phantasy is consciousness characterized as presentification, reproduction. There is presentified time, but it necessarily refers back to originally given, presented, not phantasied time. Presentification is the opposite of an originally giving act, and no representation can "spring" from it. Phantasy cannot present any objectivity, or any essential and possible feature of one, as itself given; not giving itself is its very essence. Even the concept of phantasy does not spring from phantasy. To have originally given what phantasy is, we must form phantasies, but that alone does not amount to givenness; we must consider phantasizing, perceive it. Perception of phantasy is the originally giving consciousness for forming the concept of phantasy; in it we see what phantasy is, grasping it in the consciousness of self-givenness. This paragraph comes from the 1905 manuscript, where Husserl's argument began. The core is a transcendental principle about origin: every concept must be drawn from a consciousness that gives its object itself. Phantasy cannot be the source of the representation of time, because it gives nothing itself. Even the concept of phantasy is drawn from the inner perception of phantasizing, which anticipates the later doctrine of inner consciousness in §§39–40 and Beilage XII.

An attentive comparison shows a vast phenomenological difference between re-presenting memory and the primary memory that extends now-consciousness. Hearing two or three tones, we have, during the temporal extension of the act, a consciousness of the tone just heard. This consciousness is essentially the same whether a member of the tonal figure is still actually perceived as now or whether the figure is only retentionally conscious. Now suppose that while the continuous intention toward the tone or tonal course just heard is still alive, that same course is reproduced once more. I presentify to myself the bar I have just heard and to which my attention is still directed, by inwardly going through it again. The difference is striking. In presentification we have the tone or tonal figure once more with its whole temporal extension. The presentifying act is temporally extended exactly like the earlier perceptual act; it reproduces it, letting tone-phase after tone-phase and interval after interval run off, and reproduces also the phase of primary memory selected for comparison. It is not a mere repetition, and the difference does not lie merely in having simple reproduction in one case and reproduction of reproduction in the other. We find radical differences in content. They appear when we ask what distinguishes the tone's sounding in presentification from the after-consciousness of it that we also retain in phantasy. The reproduced tone during its "sounding" is reproduction of the sounding. The after-consciousness following the reproduced sounding is no longer reproduction of the sounding but of the sounding just past, still just heard, which presents itself quite differently from the sounding itself. The phantasms that present the tones do not remain standing in consciousness, as if each tone were continued as an identically persisting datum in presentification; otherwise no intuitive representation of time, of a temporal object, could arise in presentification. The reproduced tone passes; its phantasm does not stand identically and receive continued apprehension, but is modified in a peculiar way and grounds the presentifying consciousness of duration, change, succession and so on. The experiment isolates the difference by running the two side by side: retention holding a bar, reproduction re-running the same bar. Reproduction takes as long as the original and re-runs its phases; retention takes no time of its own and does not re-run anything. And reproduction contains its own retentions, so the retentional modification is not peculiar to perception but recurs within reproduction. Brentano's error was to identify retentional modification with reproductive modification; the comparison shows they lie on different axes.

The modification that turns an original now into a reproduced one is quite different from the one that turns an original or reproduced now into a past. The latter is a continuous shading; as the now shades off continuously into the past and further past, so intuitive time-consciousness shades off continuously. But there is no continuous transition from perception into phantasy, from impression into reproduction; that difference is discrete. What we call original consciousness, impression or perception is therefore a continuously shading act. Every concrete perception implies a whole continuum of such shadings. Reproduction, phantasy-consciousness, requires exactly the same shadings, only reproductively modified. On both sides it belongs to the essence of the experiences that they be extended in this way and that a punctual phase can never exist by itself. Two orthogonal modifications are now distinguished: the continuous temporal modification, now to past, and the discrete reproductive modification, impression to reproduction. Every experience, original or reproductive, has the full temporal structure. Retention is located on the first axis, and so cannot be a case of reproduction.

This shading of what is given originally or reproductively concerns, as we saw, the contents of apprehension already. Perception is built on sensation. The sensation that functions presentatively for the object forms a continuous continuum, and likewise the phantasm forms a continuum for the representation of a phantasy-object. Whoever assumes an essential difference between sensations and phantasms must not call the contents of apprehension for the just-past phases phantasms, since they pass continuously into the contents of apprehension of the now-moment. The argument turns Brentano's own commitments against him: if sensation and phantasm differ in kind, as Brentano held, then retentional contents, which pass continuously into sensation, cannot be phantasms. The paragraph also shows the 1905 Husserl still thinking of retention as having its own "contents of apprehension," a framework §12 and the later sections abandon.

#### §20. The "Freedom" of Reproduction

Remarkable differences arise between the original and the reproduced course of "sinking back." The original appearing and flowing-off of the modes of running-off is something fixed, conscious through "affection," at which we can only look, if we perform the spontaneity of looking at all. Presentifying, by contrast, is free: a free running-through that we can perform faster or slower, more distinctly and explicitly or more confusedly, in a flash or in articulated steps. Presentification is itself an event of inner consciousness and as such has its actual now, its modes of running-off and so on. Within the same immanent stretch of time in which it actually takes place, we can "freely" fit larger or smaller parts of the presentified process with its modes of running-off, and so run through it faster or slower. The relative modes of running-off of the presentified points of the stretch remain unchanged, provided identifying coincidence continues. I keep presentifying the same, the same continuity of running-off modes of the stretch, always it itself in its how. But as I return again and again to the same starting point and the same series of points, the starting point itself sinks steadily further back. The editor dates §20 to the November 1911 manuscript. The contrast is between passivity and freedom: the original flow is given by affection and cannot be altered, only observed; reproduction is an activity whose tempo and articulation are at the ego's disposal. The decisive structural observation is that reproduction has two times: the time of the reproducing act, which is immanent and flows like everything else, and the reproduced time, whose internal relations are preserved under any change of tempo. The reproduced past keeps its relative order, but its absolute position keeps sinking, since the original to which it refers recedes with every repetition.

#### §21. Levels of Clarity of Reproduction

What is presentified floats before us more or less clearly, and the various modes of unclarity concern the whole that is presentified and its modes of consciousness. In the original givenness of a temporal object, too, we found that it first appears vividly and clearly and then passes with diminishing clarity into emptiness. These modifications belong to the flow. But while the same modifications occur within the presentification of the flow, other "unclarities" also appear there: even what is "clear" in the first sense stands there unclear, as if seen through a veil, more or less so. The two kinds of unclarity must not be confused. The specific modes of vividness and lack of vividness, clarity and unclarity of presentification do not belong to what is presentified, or belong to it only in virtue of the how of presentification; they belong to the actual experience of presentifying. The 1917 manuscript is again the source. Husserl distinguishes the retentional fading built into every temporal structure, original or reproduced, from the veil that belongs to reproduction as such. A clearly reproduced moment is still unclear in comparison with perception, because the reproductive modification affects the whole. The distinction blocks a naturalistic conflation of memory with weakened perception, which Hume's account of ideas as fainter impressions exemplifies.

#### §22. The Evidence of Reproduction

Primary and secondary memory also differ in evidence. What I am retentionally conscious of is absolutely certain. What of the more distant past? When I remember something experienced yesterday, I reproduce the process, perhaps step by step, and am conscious of a succession in doing so. Apart from this succession, which evidently belongs to the reproduction as a present course of experience, the reproduction presents a past temporal course. The single steps of the remembered process may differ from those of the past one, and even the actual order may differ from the one the memory means. Errors are possible here that spring from reproduction as such and must not be confused with the errors to which perception of transcendent temporal objects is subject. If I am originally conscious of a temporal succession, it is beyond doubt that a temporal succession has taken place and is taking place, but not that an objective event took place in the sense in which I apprehend it; single apprehensions can be false. If the objective intention of what is apprehended is preserved as it is pushed back in time, the error pervades the whole temporal apprehension of the appearing process. But restricted to the sequence of presenting "contents" or "appearances," an indubitable truth remains: a process has come to givenness, and this succession of appearances took place, even if the succession of events that appeared to me did not. The editor dates the core of §22 to a sheet probably written before 1901, which shows that the distinction between immanent and transcendent evidence in time-consciousness predates the 1905 lectures. Husserl distinguishes two sources of error: the apprehension of transcendent objects, which can fail in perception and retention alike, and reproduction itself, which can misrender even the immanent course. Retention is fallible only in the first way; recollection in both.

Can the evidence of time-consciousness be preserved in reproduction? Only through a coincidence of a reproductive course with a retentional one. If I have a succession of two tones, c and d, I can repeat it while fresh memory still holds, and in a certain respect repeat it adequately: I repeat c, d inwardly with the consciousness that c occurred first and then d. While this is still alive I can repeat again. In this way I can extend the original domain of evidence, and we see how recollections are fulfilled: the reproductive representation of the succession c, d is fulfilled in the earlier succession still alive in retention. A note adds the converse: reproduction can also make intuitive a succession that is only retentionally conscious. The evidence of recollection is borrowed from retention. A chain of reproductions, each overlapping with the retention of the previous, can carry evidence beyond the original temporal field. The mutual relation is exact: reproduction is fulfilled by retention, and retention, which darkens, is made intuitive by reproduction.

#### §23. Coincidence of the Reproduced Now with a Past. Distinction between Phantasy and Recollection

Having distinguished reproductive consciousness of the past from original consciousness, a further problem arises. When I reproduce a melody I have heard, the phenomenal now of recollection presentifies a past. In phantasy, in recollection, a tone sounds now; it reproduces, say, the first tone of the melody, which is a past melody. The consciousness of the past given with the second tone represents the "just past" that was formerly given originally, so a past "just past." How does the reproduced now come to represent a past? Directly, a reproduced now represents simply a now. How does the reference to a past enter, which can be given originally only in the form of "just past"? The editor notes that the first half of §23 belongs to the 1905 manuscript and the second to a text of 1907–1909. The problem is acute: reproduction re-runs a now, but what is remembered is past. The reference to the past cannot come from the reproduced content, which presents itself as now.

To answer, a distinction only touched on so far is needed: between mere phantasy of a temporally extended object and recollection. In mere phantasy there is no positing of the reproduced now and no coincidence of it with a past. Recollection posits what is reproduced and in this positing gives it a position relative to the actual now and to the sphere of the original temporal field to which recollection itself belongs; a note refers to Beilage III. The relation between a reproduced now and a past can be established only in original time-consciousness. The flow of presentification is a flow of experiential phases built exactly like every time-constituting flow, and so itself time-constituting. All the shadings and modifications that constitute temporal form are found here, and just as the immanent tone is constituted in the flow of tone-phases, the unity of the tone-presentification is constituted in the flow of tone-presentification phases. Quite generally, phenomenological reflection leads us back from everything that appears, is represented or thought, in the widest sense, to a flow of constituting phases that undergo an immanent objectivation, into perceptual appearances, memories, expectations, wishes and so on, as unities of inner consciousness. So presentifications of every kind, as courses of experience of the universal time-constituting form, constitute an immanent object: "an enduring process of presentification, running off in such and such a way." Positing distinguishes recollection from phantasy, and positing means placing the reproduced in a temporal context that reaches to the actual now. The second, larger thesis is the universal law that every experience whatever, perception, memory, wish, is constituted as an immanent temporal unity in a flow of phases. This is the first appearance, in the text's order, of the doctrine that acts are constituted unities, the theme of §37.

Presentifications also have something peculiar: in themselves and in all their phases they are presentifications of something in another sense, with a second, different intentionality that belongs to them alone and not to all experiences. This new intentionality is in form a counterpart of time-constituting intentionality. As it reproduces in each element a moment of a flow of presentation, and in the whole a whole flow of presentation, it produces a reproductive consciousness of a presentified immanent object. It thus constitutes something twofold. Through its form as a flow of experience it constitutes the presentification as an immanent unity. And because the moments of this flow are reproductive modifications of moments of a parallel flow, usually consisting of non-reproductive moments, and because these modifications mean an intentionality, the flow closes into a constituting whole in which an intentional unity is conscious: the unity of what is remembered. Every presentification has two intentionalities. As an experience it is constituted in inner time like any act; as a presentification it reproduces another flow and thereby intends the object of that flow. Recollection is a flow that mirrors another flow phase by phase, and this mirroring is its intentionality. The doubling here anticipates the double intentionality of retention in §39, but the two must not be confused: here the second intentionality is peculiar to presentifications, there it belongs to the flow as such.

#### §24. Protentions in Recollection

To understand how the constituted unity "memory" fits into the unified stream of experience, one must take into account that every memory contains intentions of expectation whose fulfilment leads to the present. Every originally constituting process is animated by protentions that emptily constitute what is coming and catch it, bring it to fulfilment. But the recollecting process does not merely renew these protentions in memory. They were not only there catching; they have caught, they have been fulfilled, and we are aware of this in recollection. Fulfilment in recollecting consciousness is re-fulfilment, in the modification of memorial positing. If the original protention of the event's perception was indeterminate and left open otherness or non-being, recollection has a pre-directed expectation that leaves none of this open, except in the form of "imperfect" recollection, whose structure differs from that of the indeterminate original protention. Yet this protention is also contained in recollection. Difficulties of intentional analysis therefore arise already for a single event, and in a new way for the expectations concerning the succession of events up to the present. Recollection is not expectation, but it has a horizon directed toward the future, the future of the recollected, and this is a posited horizon. As the recollecting process advances, this horizon is continually opened anew and becomes livelier and richer, and it is fulfilled with ever new recollected events. What was only foreshadowed before is now quasi-present, in the mode of quasi-actualizing presence. The section was written in 1917 expressly to supplement Stein's redaction, and protention here receives its first explicit analysis as a structure of original consciousness. Recollection contains reproduced protentions that differ from the original ones: the original protention was open, the recollected one knows how things turned out. Husserl takes this double character as a difficulty rather than resolving it. Its importance is that the recollected past carries a forward horizon reaching to the actual present, which is how recollection inserts its object into the one time leading to now.

#### §25. The Double Intentionality of Recollection

If we distinguish in a temporal object its content and its duration, which could occupy different positions in "the" time, from its temporal position, then in reproducing an enduring being we have, besides the reproduction of the filled duration, intentions concerning its position, and necessarily so. A duration cannot be represented, or better posited, without being posited in a temporal context, without intentions of that context. These intentions must have the form of past-intentions or future-intentions. To the doubling of intentions, toward the filled duration and toward its temporal position, corresponds a double fulfilment. The total complex of intentions making up the appearance of the past enduring object has its possible fulfilment in the system of appearances belonging to the same enduring thing. The intentions of the temporal context are fulfilled by establishing the filled connections up to the actual present. In every presentification, then, we must distinguish the reproduction of the consciousness in which the past enduring object was given, perceived or originally constituted, from what attaches to this reproduction as constitutive for the consciousness "past," "present" (simultaneous with the actual now) or "future." The editor dates §§25–26 and the first paragraph of §27 to the manuscript of 1907–1909. The distinction between content-with-duration and temporal position is the decisive step toward objective time: the same content could have occurred at another time, so position is a separate intentional moment with its own fulfilment, by filling in the chain of events up to now. The structure parallels the one Husserl later calls the "inner horizon" and "outer horizon" of the thing.

Is the latter also reproduction? The question is misleading. The whole is reproduced, not only the conscious present of that time with its flow but, "implicitly," the whole stream of consciousness up to the living present. As a basic piece of a priori phenomenological genesis, this means: memory is in constant flux because conscious life is in constant flux, and does not merely add link to link. Every new element acts back on the old: its forward-going intention is fulfilled and determined, and this gives the reproduction a definite colouring. There is an a priori necessary retroaction. The new points again to the new, which on arrival is determined and modifies the reproductive possibilities for the old. The retroactive force runs back along the chain, since the reproduced past bears the character "past" and an indeterminate intention toward a certain position relative to the now. We do not have a mere chain of "associated" intentions, each recalling the next, but one intention that is in itself an intention toward the series of possible fulfilments. Husserl introduces "genesis" here in a phenomenological, a priori sense: the past is not fixed in its sense once and for all but is redetermined by what follows. The rejection of a chain of associations recalls the critique of Brentano: unity across time is not a sequence of links but one intention directed toward a series. The notion of retroaction anticipates the later theory of retroactive sense-constitution that Husserl develops in the *Analyses Concerning Passive Synthesis*.

This intention is non-intuitive, "empty," and its object is the objective temporal series of events, the dark surroundings of what is actually recollected. Does this not characterize "surroundings" in general: a unified intention related to a multiplicity of connected objectivities and fulfilled in their separate, manifold, gradual givenness? So it is with the spatial background. Every thing in perception has its back side as background, not a background of attention but of apprehension. The component "improper perception," which essentially belongs to every transcendent perception, is a "complex" intention fulfillable in connections of givenness of a definite kind. Foreground is nothing without background; the appearing side is nothing without the non-appearing ones. So too in the unity of time-consciousness: the reproduced duration is the foreground, and the intentions of placement make a temporal background conscious. In a way this continues into the constitution of the temporality of the enduring thing itself, with its now, before and after. We have analogies: for the spatial thing, placement in encompassing space and the spatial world, and the thing itself with its foreground and background; for the temporal thing, placement in the form of time and the temporal world, and the thing itself in its changing orientation toward the living now. Temporal context is a horizon in exactly the sense in which a thing's unseen sides are: an empty intention that belongs to the givenness of the foreground and is fulfillable in ordered further givenness. The phrase "background of apprehension, not of attention" is crucial: the horizon is not what lies outside the focus of attention but what is co-meant without being given. This is the horizon-intentionality that *Ideas I* will make central, and here it is applied to the past as a "temporal world."

#### §26. Differences between Memory and Expectation

Are memory and expectation on a par? Intuitive memory gives a living reproduction of the elapsing duration of an event, and only the intentions pointing back to the before and forward to the living now remain non-intuitive. The question is set against Brentano's derivation of the future from the past in §4.

In intuitively representing a future event, I now have intuitively the reproductive "image" of a process running off reproductively. Indeterminate future-intentions and past-intentions attach to it, intentions concerning the temporal surroundings from the beginning of the process to the living now. Expectation-intuition is thus memory-intuition turned inside out: in memory the now-intentions do not "precede" the process but follow it, lying as empty surrounding intentions "in the opposite direction." What of the mode of givenness of the process itself? Is it essential that in memory the content is more determinate? Memory too can be intuitive without being very determinate, insofar as some intuitive components lack genuine memorial character. In "perfect" memory everything would be clear in detail and characterized as memory, but ideally this is possible in expectation too. Expectation generally leaves much open, and openness is a character of the components in question. In principle, a prophetic consciousness, one that takes itself to be prophetic, is conceivable, before which every character of the expectation of what will be stands: as when we have a precisely determined plan and, intuitively representing what is planned, accept it as future reality with skin and hair. Even there the intuitive anticipation contains some irrelevant fillers that complete the concrete image but could be otherwise; they are characterized from the outset as open. Structurally, expectation mirrors memory, its context-intentions running from the anticipated event back to the now rather than forward from the remembered event to the now. Determinacy does not distinguish them in principle: perfect memory and perfect prophecy are both ideally possible, though expectation characteristically contains marked openness.

The principal differences lie in the mode of fulfilment. Past-intentions are necessarily fulfilled by establishing the connections of intuitive reproductions. Reproduction of a past event admits, as to its validity in inner consciousness, only confirmation of its indeterminacies and completion by transformation into a reproduction in which every component is characterized as reproductive. The questions here are: did I really see, perceive it, did I really have this appearance with exactly this content? All this must fit into a connection of similar intuitions up to the now. Whether what appeared was real is another question. Expectation, by contrast, is fulfilled in a perception. It belongs to the essence of the expected that it is something that will be perceived. When the expected arrives and has become present, the state of expectation has itself passed: if the future has become present, the present has become relatively past. The same holds of the surrounding intentions: they too are fulfilled by the actuality of impressional experience. The sentence on the expected arriving is noted by the editor as taken from a manuscript before 1901. The asymmetry is decisive: memory is fulfilled by further reproduction, expectation by perception. Memory never meets its object again in person, while expectation is aimed precisely at original givenness. Husserl also separates two questions for memory: whether I really had this experience, which reproduction can confirm, and whether what appeared was real, which concerns transcendent validity.

Despite these differences, expectation-intuition is just as original and peculiar as intuition of the past. Against Brentano's derivation of the future from the past by extrapolation, Husserl affirms the originality of the future dimension. The claim is programmatic in 1905; the analysis of protention, as opposed to reproductive expectation, remains thin until the Bernau manuscripts of 1917–1918.

#### §27. Memory as Consciousness of Having-Been-Perceived

For the positing reproductions analysed, it is of the greatest importance that their essence includes not only reproductive positing of temporal being but a certain relation to inner consciousness. It belongs primarily to the essence of memory that it is consciousness of having-been-perceived. When I intuitively remember an external process, I have a reproductive intuition of it, and a positing one. But this external reproduction is necessarily conscious through an internal reproduction; a note refers to Beilage XII. An external appearing must be reproduced, since the external process is given in a definite manner of appearance. External appearing, as an experience, is a unity of inner consciousness, and inner reproduction corresponds to inner consciousness. Two possibilities arise for the reproduction of a process. The inner reproduction can be positing, so that the appearance of the process is posited in the unity of immanent time. Or the external reproduction can be positing, placing the process in objective time but not positing the appearance itself as a process of inner time, nor therefore the time-constituting stream in the unity of the whole stream of life. Remembering an event is always also, implicitly, reproducing my experience of it. The two positings can come apart: I may posit the remembered event in world-time without positing my past experience of it in my inner history, as when I recall a fact without the episode of learning it. The appeal to "inner consciousness" makes the stream of experience itself the ultimate field into which memory inserts what it remembers.

Memory, therefore, is not simply memory of an earlier perception. But since memory of an earlier process includes reproduction of the appearances in which it was given, it is always possible to have a memory of the earlier perception, a reflection within memory that brings the earlier perception to givenness. The earlier whole of consciousness is reproduced, and what is reproduced has the character of reproduction and of pastness. The editor dates this and the following paragraph, with the first of §28, to a text from around 1901. The distinction is between what memory reproduces and what it means: it reproduces the earlier perception but means the earlier object. The earlier perception becomes an object only in a reflection within memory, which is always possible.

An example: I remember the lit theatre. This cannot mean that I remember having perceived the theatre, otherwise it would mean that I remember having perceived that I perceived the theatre, and so on. It means that "in my inner life" I see the lit theatre as having been; in the now I see the not-now. Perception constitutes presence. For a now to stand before me as such, I must perceive. To represent a now intuitively, I must carry out a perception "in an image," representatively modified, but not so that I represent the perception; I represent what is perceived, what appears as present in it. Memory thus really implies a reproduction of the earlier perception, but it is not in the proper sense a representation of it: the perception is not meant and posited in the memory, but its object and its now, which is moreover related to the actual now. Remembering yesterday's lit theatre, I reproduce the perception of the theatre, so that the theatre hovers before me as present; this is what I mean, but I take this present as lying back relative to the actual present of current perceptions. Now it is evident that the perception of the theatre was, that I perceived the theatre. What is remembered appears as having been present, directly and intuitively, because a present appears intuitively at a distance from the present of the actual now. The actual present is constituted in actual perception; the intuitively appearing present, the intuitive representation of the not-now, is constituted in a counterpart of perception, a "presentification of the earlier perception" in which the theatre is given "as it were now." This presentification of the perception does not mean that, living in it, I mean the perceiving; I mean the being-present of the perceived object. The regress argument shows that memory cannot be a memory of a perception as its object, since then the memory of that perception would require a further memory, and so on. Memory is a modification of perceiving, not a perception of perceiving. The double structure, reproducing perception while meaning its object, matches the ordinary structure of perception, which is lived through while its object is meant; recollection reproduces the lived-through and the meant together, with the meant remaining in front.

#### §28. Memory and Image-Consciousness. Memory as Positing Reproduction

What kind of presentification is at issue? Not representation by a similar object, as in conscious picturing, a painting or bust. In contrast to such image-consciousness, reproductions have the character of self-presentification. They divide again into non-positing ones, "mere" phantasies, and positing ones. Temporal characters are added to these: memory is self-presentification in the sense of the past. Present remembering is a phenomenon wholly analogous to perception. It shares with the corresponding perception the appearance of the object, but the appearance has a modified character, by which the object stands there not as present but as having been present. "Self-presentification" names the decisive point: memory presents the past object itself, not a likeness of it, though in a modified way. This finally excludes the image theory that §13 attacked and aligns memory with phantasy as a reproductive modification, the difference being positing.

What is essential to the kind of reproductions called memory and expectation lies in placing the reproduced appearance in the context of being of inner time, the flowing series of my experiences. Positing normally extends to the object of the external appearance too, but this positing can be cancelled or contradicted, and memory or expectation still remains: we still call it memory or expectation even if we describe the earlier or future perception as merely "supposed." In the reproduction of immanent rather than transcendent objects, the layered structure of reproductive intuitions falls away, and positing what is reproduced coincides with placing it in the series of experiences, in immanent time. The positing proper to memory is primarily positing in inner time: I remember having had an experience even when I disown its object as illusory. The world-positing can fail while the inner positing stands. For immanent objects the two levels collapse, since the object and its place in the stream are the same.

#### §29. Memory of the Present

For intuition of external time and objectivity, another type of immediate reproductive intuition of temporal objects must be considered. All the analyses so far have been restricted to immediate intuition of temporal objects, leaving aside mediate or non-intuitive expectations and memories. The restriction matters because the new type is still intuitive and immediate, though not memory of the past.

I can also represent something present as now existing without having it bodily before me now, whether on the basis of earlier perceptions or from a description. In the first case I have a memory, but I give what is remembered a duration up to the actual now, and for this duration I have no inwardly remembered "appearances." The "memory image" serves me, but I do not posit what is remembered, the object of inner memory, in its own duration. The enduring thing is posited as presenting itself in this appearance, and we posit the appearing now and the ever new now, but not as "past." Thinking of my distant house as standing there now uses a memory image, but posits its object as present, not past. The reproduction is thus detached from the positing of a past experience in which the house appeared; it is a presentification of something present.

The "past" in memory does not mean that in present remembering we make an image of the earlier, or any such constructions. We simply posit what appears, what is intuited, which can be intuited in its temporality only in temporal modes, and we give what so appears, in the manner of memory, a position relative to the actual now through the appearance's surrounding intention. So for the presentification of something absent but present we must ask about the surrounding intentions of the intuition, and these are of a quite different kind: they have no relation to the actual now through a continuous series of inner appearances all of which would be posited. Yet the reproductive appearance is not without context. It is supposed to be something enduring that appears, that has been, is now and will be. I "can" go by some route and see, find the thing still there, then go back and produce the intuition in repeated "possible" series of appearances. Had I set out earlier and gone there, which is a prefigured possibility with corresponding possible series of appearances, I would now have this intuition as a perceptual intuition. The appearance hovering before me reproductively is not characterized as having been inwardly impressional, and what appears is not characterized as perceived in its duration; but a relation to the here and now exists here too, and the appearance carries a certain positing character: it belongs in a definite context of appearances, fully positing ones, and in relation to these it has a motivating character. The surrounding intention yields a halo of intentions for the "possible" appearances themselves. The same holds for intuiting an enduring being that I now perceive and posit as having been before without having perceived it then, and posit as going to be. The present absent object is placed not through actual past experiences but through possible ones: the counterfactual "had I gone there, I would now be perceiving it." This is the first appearance in the text of the modal structure of motivated possibilities, the "I can" that grounds the horizon of the perceptual world in *Ideas II* and *Thing and Space*. The final example extends it to the unperceived past and future of a thing now perceived: its duration beyond my perception is posited through possible, not actual, appearances.

#### §30. Preservation of the Objective Intention in Retentional Modification

It often happens that while the retention of what has just passed is still alive, a reproductive image of it emerges, an image of it as it was given in the now-point. We recapitulate what we have just experienced. This inner renewal relates the reproductive now to what is still living in fresh memory, and here the consciousness of identity arises that brings out the identity of the two. The phenomenon also shows that primary memory includes, besides the intuitive part, an empty part reaching much further: while we still have something in fresh, though empty, memory, an "image" of it can emerge. It is a general and fundamental fact that each now, as it sinks into the past, keeps its strict identity. Phenomenologically, the now-consciousness constituted on the basis of matter A changes continuously into consciousness of the past while new now-consciousness is built up, and in this transformation the modifying consciousness preserves its objective intention, which belongs to the essence of time-consciousness. The editor dates §30 and part of §31 to the 1905 manuscript. The observation that retention has a long empty tail beyond its intuitive part corrects the impression, from §11, that retention ends quickly in imperceptibility; it fades from intuition but continues emptily. The central thesis, preservation of objective intention, says that the past phase is meant as the same phase that was now, not as a new object.

The continuous modification that each original temporal field contains with respect to its constituting act-characters must not be understood as if, in the series of apprehensions belonging to one object-phase, from its first appearance as positing-as-now down to the last reachable phenomenal past, the objective intention were continuously modified. On the contrary, it remains absolutely the same and identical. Yet there is a phenomenal shading, not only of the contents of apprehension, which die away from the full height of sensation in the now to imperceptibility, but above all in that the now-moment is characterized as the new. The now just sinking down is no longer the new but what the new has pushed aside. This pushing aside is a change. But while it loses its character as now, it remains absolutely unchanged in its objective intention, as an intuiting intention directed to an individual objectivity. What does "preservation of the objective intention" mean? The total apprehension of the object contains two components: one constitutes the object according to its non-temporal determinations, the other creates the temporal position, being-now, having-been and so on. The object as temporal matter, as what has temporal position and extension, what endures or changes, what is now and then has been, springs purely from the objectivation of the contents of apprehension, in the case of sensory objects from sensory contents. We should not lose sight of the fact that these contents are themselves temporal objects, generated in succession as a continuum of primal impressions and retentions, and that these temporal shadings of sensory data matter for the temporal determinations of the objects constituted through them. But as representatives of thingly qualities in their pure what, their temporal character plays no role. The non-temporally taken apprehension-data constitute the object in its specific content, and where this is preserved we can already speak of identity. Preservation of the objective relation meant, however, that the object is preserved not only in its specific content but as individual, temporally determined, sinking back in time with its temporal determination. This sinking back is a peculiar phenomenological modification of consciousness by which, relative to the ever newly constituted actual now, an ever growing distance forms through the continuous series of changes leading there. Two things change as a phase sinks: the fullness of its content and its character as new. What does not change is its intention toward this individual. Husserl splits the apprehension into a component for the "what" and a component for temporal position, which sets up §31's problem: the "what" can be the same at different times, so the identity of an individual must rest on temporal position, and temporal position on something other than content. "Newness," here a character of the now, becomes in §31 the source of individuation.

#### §31. Primal Impression and the Objective Individual Point of Time

An antinomy seems to arise: in sinking back the object constantly changes its temporal position, yet it is supposed to keep its temporal position. In truth the object of the steadily receding primary memory does not change its temporal position at all, only its distance from the actual now, because the actual now counts as an ever new objective point of time while the past point remains what it is. The question is how, against the constant change of time-consciousness, the consciousness of objective time arises, and first of identical temporal positions. Closely tied to this is the constitution of the objectivity of individual temporal objects and events: all objectivation takes place in time-consciousness, and without clarifying the identity of temporal position no identity of an object in time can be clarified. The antinomy dissolves once position and distance are separated: the sinking past changes its relation to the now, not its place. The second claim is the strongest thesis of the 1905 lectures: objectivity as such, the identity of an individual, depends on the identity of its time-point, so the theory of time-consciousness is the foundation of the theory of objectivity, as the Introduction announced.

More exactly: the now-phases of perception undergo continuous modification; they do not simply persist as they are but flow, and in this the sinking back into time is constituted. The tone sounds now and at once sinks into the past, the same tone, in each of its phases and so as a whole. The sinking seems somewhat intelligible by now. But how is it that we nonetheless say the tone has a fixed position in time, that points and durations of time can be identified in repeated acts, as the analysis of reproduction showed? The tone and each point in the unity of the enduring tone have an absolutely fixed position in "objective," even if immanent, time. Time is rigid, and yet time flows. In the flow of time, in the steady sinking into the past, a non-flowing, absolutely fixed, identical, objective time is constituted. That is the problem. The editor marks the rest of §31 as resting on 1905 sheets 58–61. "Objective" here includes immanent time: the problem arises already at the level of hyletic data, before any transcendent object. The formula "time is rigid, and yet time flows" states the puzzle every later theory of the living present inherits, from Heidegger's critique of the "now-time" in *Being and Time* §81 to Derrida's reading in *Speech and Phenomena*.

Why do we speak of the same tone sinking? The tone is built up in the flow of time through its phases. Each phase, say an actual now, is subject to the law of continuous modification and must yet appear as objectively the same, the same tone-point, because there is a continuity of apprehension governed by identity of sense and in continuous coincidence. The coincidence concerns the non-temporal matter, which keeps the identity of objective sense throughout the flow. This holds for each now-phase. But each new now is new and characterized phenomenologically as such. Even if the tone continues wholly unchanged, each new now having exactly the same content of apprehension in quality and intensity and bearing exactly the same apprehension, there is an original difference belonging to a new dimension, and it is continuous. Phenomenologically, only the now-point is characterized as actual now, and as new; the preceding now-point has undergone its modification, the one before that a further modification, and so on. This continuum of modifications of the contents of apprehension and of the apprehensions built on them creates the consciousness of the tone's extension, with the constant sinking of what is already extended into the past. Identity of sense explains why a phase is the same tone-point, but not why two qualitatively identical phases of an unchanging tone are two. Their difference lies in "a new dimension," which is neither quality nor intensity but temporal novelty. Duration of an unchanging content is therefore not constituted by content at all.

How then, against the continuous change of time-consciousness, does consciousness of objective time arise, and first of identical temporal position and extension? Because against the flow of temporal pushing back, the flow of modifications of consciousness, the object that appears pushed back is apperceptively preserved in absolute identity, together with the positing as "this" experienced in the now-point. The continuous modification of apprehension does not affect the "as what" of the apprehension, its sense; it intends no new object or object-phase and yields no new time-points, but always the same object with its same time-points. Each actual now creates a new time-point because it creates a new object, or rather a new object-point, which is held fast in the flow of modification as one and the same individual object-point. The continuity in which a new now is always constituted shows that what is at issue is not "newness" in general but a continuous moment of individuation in which temporal position has its origin. It belongs to the essence of the modifying flow that this temporal position stands as identical and as necessarily identical. The now as actual now is the givenness of the temporal position as present. When the phenomenon moves into the past, the now takes on the character of a past now, but remains the same now, standing as past only relative to the current, temporally new now. The answer locates individuation in the now's novelty: each primal impression creates a new point, which retention then holds identical. The now is the "presence-givenness" of a time-position, not the position itself, which persists when its givenness changes. This lays down the distinction between temporal position and temporal mode that later analytic philosophy would call B-series and A-series, and gives it a constitutive ground.

The objectivation of the temporal object thus rests on these moments. The sensory content belonging to the different actual now-points can remain qualitatively unchanged, yet even with full identity of content it does not have true identity; the same sensation now and at another now differs phenomenologically, and this difference corresponds to absolute temporal position. It is the primal source of the individuality of the "this" and so of absolute temporal position. Each phase of the modification has "in essence" the same qualitative content and the same temporal moment, though modified, and has them in such a way that later apprehension of identity is made possible. That is on the side of sensation, the basis of apprehension. The different moments bear different sides of the apprehension, of objectivation proper. One side rests purely on the qualitative content of the sensory material and yields temporal matter, for example the tone, held identical in the flow of past-modification. A second side springs from the apprehension of the representatives of temporal position, likewise held fast in the flow. Husserl still works with the apprehension-content schema, now applied to temporal position itself: there are "representatives of temporal position" in the sensory material, apprehended as positions. This is exactly what the later footnote in §1 and the analyses of §§36–40 will call into question, since it treats temporal position as a sensed content awaiting apprehension.

In sum, the tone-point in its absolute individuality is held fast in matter and temporal position, and the latter first constitutes individuality. Then there is the apprehension that belongs essentially to the modification and that, while holding fast the extended objectivity with its immanent absolute time, makes the continuous pushing back into the past appear. In the tone example each now-point of the continually new sounding and dying away has its sensory material and objectifying apprehension; the tone stands there as the tone of a bowed violin string. Setting aside the objectifying apprehension and looking only at the sensory material, it is perhaps always the tone c, with unchanged quality and timbre, intensity fluctuating perhaps. This content, purely as sensory content underlying objectifying apperception, is extended: each now has its sensory content, each other now an individually different one, even if materially exactly the same. Absolutely the same c now and later is the same as sensation but individually other. Three apprehensional components are now named: matter, temporal position, and the modification that makes the receding appear. The violin example shows that even below the transcendent apprehension, at the level of sensation, extension and individual difference are present; the hyletic layer is already temporal.

What "individual" means here is the original temporal form of sensation, or the temporal form of original sensation, here the sensation of the respective now-point and only of it. Strictly, the now-point itself is to be defined through original sensation, so the statement serves only to point to what is meant. Impression is distinguished from phantasm by the character of originality; a note refers to Beilage II. Within impression we must single out primal impression, over against which stands the continuum of modifications in primary memory. Primal impression is the absolutely unmodified, the primal source of all further consciousness and being. Its content is what the word "now" says, taken in the strictest sense. Each new now is the content of a new primal impression. A new impression continually flashes up, with ever new matter, now the same, now changing. What distinguishes one primal impression from another is the individuating moment of the original impression of temporal position, which is fundamentally different from quality and the other material moments of sensory content. The moment of original temporal position is nothing by itself; individuation is nothing beside what has individuation. The whole now-point, the whole original impression, undergoes past-modification, and only through it is the concept of the now exhausted, since it is relative and points to a "past" as "past" points to "now." This modification too first affects sensation without cancelling its general impressional character. It modifies the whole content of the primal impression in matter and in temporal position, but in exactly the way a phantasy-modification does: modifying through and through yet not changing the intentional essence, the total content. The matter is the same matter and the temporal position the same position; only the mode of givenness has changed to givenness as past. On this sensory material the objectifying apperception is built. Even when we look purely at sensory contents, setting aside any transcendent apperceptions built on them, we perform an apperception: the "flow of time," duration, stands before us as a kind of objectivity. Objectivity presupposes consciousness of unity and identity. We apprehend the content of each primal sensation as a self. It yields a tone-point individual, which remains identically the same in the flow of past-modification: the apperception related to this point stays in continuous coincidence through the past-modification, and identity of the individual is eo ipso identity of temporal position. The continuous welling up of new primal impressions yields, as they are apprehended as individual points, ever new and distinct temporal positions, and their continuity yields a continuity of positions. In the flow of past-modification there stands a continuous, tonally filled piece of time, of which only one point is given through primal impression, while from there the positions appear continuously in modified gradation, receding into the past. Husserl's admission that the now must be defined through primal impression rather than the reverse is significant: the now is not a point on a pre-given line, but the correlate of the originality of a consciousness. Primal impression is called "the primal source of all further consciousness and being," which gives it a foundational role that Michel Henry will press against Husserl's own hyletic reading. The comparison of past-modification with phantasy-modification is notable: both modify everything without changing the intentional essence, though §19 showed they lie on different axes. Finally, even the reflection on bare sensory content is an apperception; the immanent flow appears as a "kind of objectivity" only because each primal sensation is grasped as an identical self. Immanent time is thus itself constituted, which prepares the question of §34 about what constitutes it.

Every perceived time is perceived as a past terminating in a present, and the present is a limit-point. Every apprehension, however transcendent, is bound by this law. Perceiving a bird's flight or a cavalry squadron at a gallop, we find in the sensory basis the differences described: ever new primal sensations carrying their character of temporal position, which yields their individuation, and the same modes in the apprehension. The objective itself, the bird's flight, therefore appears as primally given in the now-point, but as fully given only in a past-continuum terminating in the now, continually in ever new nows, while what preceded recedes ever further into the past-continuum. The appearing process keeps its identical absolute temporal values. Pushed further into the past, it is pushed back with its absolute positions and its whole temporal stretch; the same process with the same absolute extension appears continually, as long as it appears at all, as identically the same, only in a different form of givenness. At the same time new primal being wells up in the living source-point of being, the now, relative to which the distance of the process's time-points from the current now grows continually, and so the appearance of sinking back, of moving away, arises. The law is universal: transcendent processes are given only as a past ending in a present limit, because their sensory basis is so given. "Primal being" and "the living source-point of being" give the now an ontological rank. The phrase "living present," central in the late manuscripts, has its root here.

#### §32. The Part Played by Reproduction in the Constitution of the One Objective Time

Preserving the individuality of time-points as they sink back does not yet give consciousness of a unified, homogeneous, objective time. For this consciousness to arise, reproductive memory, intuitive or in the form of empty intentions, plays an important role. Each receding time-point can, by reproductive memory, be made the zero-point of a temporal intuition, and repeatedly. The earlier temporal field in which what now recedes was a now is reproduced, and the reproduced now is identified with the time-point still alive in fresh memory: the individual intention is the same; a note refers to Beilage IV. The reproduced field reaches further than the actually present one. Taking a past point in it, reproduction, by overlapping with the field in which that point was now, yields a further regress into the past, and so on. This process is evidently thinkable as continuable without limit, though actual memory soon fails in practice. Every time-point evidently has its before and after, and the points and stretches before cannot condense as in approach to a mathematical limit, such as the limit of intensity. A limit-point would correspond to a now preceded by nothing, which is evidently impossible; a note refers back to §16. A now is always and essentially the edge-point of a stretch of time, and this whole stretch must sink back while keeping its size and individuality. Phantasy and reproduction do not extend temporal intuition in the sense of enlarging the range of really given temporal gradations in simultaneous consciousness. How then, with these successive concatenations of temporal fields, do we reach the one objective time with one fixed order? Through the continued overlapping of temporal fields, which is not a mere temporal concatenation. The overlapping parts are individually identified in the intuitive, continuous regress into the past. When we go back into the past from any actually experienced time-point given originally in the perceptual field, or from one reproducing a distant past, along a fixed chain of connected and repeatedly identified objectivities, how is the linear order grounded by which any stretch of time, even one reproduced out of continuity with the actual field, must be part of one chain running to the actual now? Even an arbitrarily phantasied time is subject to the requirement that, to be thinkable as actual time, the time of some temporal object, it must exist as a stretch within the one and only objective time. The editor dates the section to the 1905 sheets 46–47. Objective time is constituted by chaining temporal fields through recollection, each reproduced field overlapping and identified with the next. Recollection thus does for objective time what §18 said it does for the identity of objects: it makes the past repeatable and so objective. The impossibility of a first now is an eidetic claim about the form of time-consciousness, not a cosmological one, and recurs in §33 and in the later doctrine that the transcendental ego has no beginning. The closing question is left as a demand: every stretch, even an isolated recollection, must belong to one linear time, and §33 grounds this in a priori laws.

#### §33. Some A Priori Temporal Laws

This a priori requirement is evidently grounded in the validity of the fundamental temporal evidences, immediately graspable, which become evident on the basis of intuitions of given temporal positions. §33 begins the 1905 sheets 61–62, and the opening claim answers §32's question: the unity of time is not inferred but grounded in evidences about temporal positions.

Compare two primal sensations, or correlatively two primal givens, both actually appearing in one consciousness as primal givens, as now. They differ by their matter but are simultaneous: they have identically the same absolute temporal position, both are now, and in the same now they necessarily have the same temporal value. They have the same form of individuation and are both constituted in impressions belonging to the same stage of impression. In this identity they are modified, keeping it through past-modification. A primal given and a modified given, of different or the same content, necessarily have different positions; two modified givens have the same or different positions, the same if they arise from the same now-point, different if from different ones. The actual now is one now and constitutes one temporal position, however many objectivities are separately constituted in it; they all have the same temporal present and keep their simultaneity as they flow off. A note refers to §38 and Beilage VII on the constitution of simultaneity. That temporal positions have distances, that these are magnitudes and the like, can be evidently seen here; also truths such as the law of transitivity or the law that if a is earlier than b, b is later than a. It belongs to the a priori essence of time to be a continuity of temporal positions with objectivities, now identical, now changing, that fill them, and that the homogeneity of absolute time is constituted inalienably in the flow of past-modifications and the continuous welling up of a now, the creative time-point, the source-point of temporal positions in general. The laws listed in §2 are now derived from the structure of primal impression and modification. Simultaneity is defined by sameness of impressional stage, succession by difference of origin in different nows. The homogeneity of time, every position like every other in form, follows from the uniform repetition of the same structure. The phrase "creative time-point" makes primal impression the generative source of positions, a point Beilage I will develop as "primal generation."

It also belongs to the a priori essence of the situation that sensation, apprehension and position-taking all participate in the same flow of time, and that the objectified absolute time is necessarily identical with the time belonging to sensation and apprehension. The pre-objectified time belonging to sensation necessarily founds the only possible objectivation of temporal position, corresponding to the modification of sensation and its degree. To the objectified time-point at which a bell begins to ring corresponds the time-point of the corresponding sensation. It has the same time in its initial phase: made an object afterward, it necessarily receives the position coinciding with the bell's. Likewise the time of perception and the time of what is perceived are identically the same; a note refers to Beilage V. The act of perception sinks back into time as the perceived does in appearance, and in reflection each phase of perception must be given identically the same temporal position as what is perceived. Husserl asserts a strict isochrony between the subjective and objective sides at the level of constitution, against the physiological truism that the sensation follows the stimulus. Objective time is constituted from immanent time, so the two cannot diverge at the origin. The thesis that perception and perceived are simultaneous leads directly to the problem of the next section: if acts are in the same time as their objects, what constitutes the time of acts?

### Section Three: The Levels of Constitution of Time and Temporal Objects

#### §34. Distinction of the Levels of Constitution

Having studied time-consciousness from the most obvious phenomena in several main directions and layers, it is well to establish the levels of constitution in their essential structure and go through them systematically. We found three: first, the things of experience in objective time, where further levels of empirical being would have to be separated, the experiential thing of the single subject, the intersubjectively identical thing, the thing of physics; second, the constituting manifolds of appearance of various levels, the immanent unities in pre-empirical time; third, the absolute time-constituting flow of consciousness. The editor ties §34 to a text of 1907–1909 and refers to Beilage VI. The list states the result of the whole book. The decisive novelty is the third level: below immanent unities, which include acts and sensations, lies a flow that is not itself a unity in time. The second level now contains appearances and acts, not only sense-data, which means acts too are constituted. The intersubjective and physical levels of the thing, mentioned in parenthesis, are the theme of *Ideas II* and *Thing and Space*.

#### §35. Differences between the Constituted Unities and the Constituting Flow

This absolute consciousness, which lies before all constitution, is to be examined more closely; its peculiarity appears clearly in contrast to the constituted unities of every level. First, every individual object, every unity constituted in the stream, immanent or transcendent, endures and necessarily endures: it is continuously in time and is identical in this continuous being, which can also be seen as a process. Conversely, what is in time is continuously in time and is the unity of a process that carries with it inseparably the unity of what endures in it. In the tone-process lies the unity of the tone that lasts throughout, and the unity of the tone is unity in the filled duration, in the process. If anything is determined as being at a time-point, it is thinkable only as a phase of a process in which the duration of an individual being has its point. The editor dates §§35–39 to a manuscript of late 1911 or later. The first contrast-term is the law of temporal objects: whatever is in time endures, and endurance and process are two aspects of one unity. No temporal object is punctual.

Second, individual or concrete being is in principle either unchange or change; the process is a process of change or rest, and the enduring object is either changing or at rest. Every change has its speed or acceleration, figuratively speaking, relative to the same duration. In principle every phase of change can be spread out into rest, and every phase of rest can be led over into change. Temporal objects admit variation of rate and the alternation of rest and change. These are the predicates the flow will be denied.

Comparing the constituting phenomena, we find a flow, each phase of which is a continuity of shadings. But no phase of this flow can in principle be spread out into a continuous succession, the flow transformed so that the phase extended itself in identity with itself. On the contrary, we find of necessity a flow of continual "change," and this change has the absurdity of running exactly as it runs, unable to run "faster" or "slower." There is no object here that changes, and insofar as in every process "something" proceeds, this is no process. Nothing is there that changes, and so there is no sense in speaking of something that endures. It is senseless to look here for something that, in a duration, does not change. The predicates of temporal objects fail for the flow. It cannot endure, since no phase can be prolonged; it cannot change in the ordinary sense, since nothing undergoes the change; it has no rate, since a rate presupposes a time in which it is measured. The flow is not a fast or slow process in a deeper time; to think it so is to generate the regress of times that Husserl is avoiding. The quotation marks around "change" mark the predicament: we must describe the flow in words borrowed from what it constitutes.

#### §36. The Time-Constituting Flow as Absolute Subjectivity

The time-constituting phenomena are therefore evidently objectivities of a fundamentally different kind from those constituted in time. They are not individual objects or individual processes, and the predicates of such cannot meaningfully be ascribed to them. It cannot make sense to say of them, in the same meaning, that they are in the now and were before, that they succeed one another or are simultaneous. But one can and must say that a certain continuity of appearance, namely one that is a phase of the time-constituting flow, belongs to a now, the one it constitutes, and belongs to a before, as the one constitutive (we cannot say: was) for the before. But is the flow not a succession, does it not have a now, an actual phase, and a continuity of pasts now conscious in retentions? We can only say: this flow is something we name by what is constituted, but it is nothing temporally "objective." It is absolute subjectivity and has the absolute properties of something to be designated in the image as "flow," as welling up in a point of actuality, a primal source-point, "now," and so on. In the actuality-experience we have the primal source-point and a continuity of moments of echo. For all this we lack names. Two misreadings must be guarded against. The flow is not a second temporal process underneath the first; it is not in time at all, and the temporal words that describe it are borrowed "from what is constituted." Nor is it an unknowable substrate: it is called absolute subjectivity, and its properties, though only figuratively named, are described. The relation "belongs to a now" replaces "is now": a phase of the flow is not present but constitutive of a present. "We lack names" is the classic statement of the limit of phenomenological language at the level of the absolute, the passage Fink, Henry and Derrida all return to. The section is the first in which Husserl speaks of absolute subjectivity, and it is the turning point from a theory of time-consciousness to a theory of the self-constitution of consciousness.

#### §37. Appearances of Transcendent Objects as Constituted Unities

When we speak of the "act of perception" and call it the point of perception proper, to which a continuous series of "retentions" is attached, we have described not temporal immanent unities but moments of the flow. The appearance, say of a house, is a temporal being, enduring and changing, just like the immanent tone, which is not an appearance. But the house-appearance is not the perceptual consciousness and retentional consciousness; these can be understood only as time-constituting, as moments of the flow. Likewise the memory-appearance, the remembered immanent or perhaps the remembered primary content, must be distinguished from the memory-consciousness with its memory-retentions. We must distinguish everywhere: consciousness, which is flow; appearance, which is immanent object; and transcendent object, unless a primary content is the immanent object. Not all consciousness relates to objectively, that is transcendently, temporal things, to objective individuality, as external perception does. In every consciousness we find an "immanent content," and for contents called "appearances" this is either the appearance of something individual, an external temporal thing, or appearance of something non-temporal. In judging, for example, I have the appearance "judgment" as an immanent temporal unity, and in it the judgment in the logical sense "appears"; a note marks "appearance" as used here in an extended sense. Judging always has the character of the flow. What the *Logical Investigations* called an "act" or "intentional experience" is everywhere a flow in which an immanent temporal unity is constituted, the judgment, the wish and so on, which has its immanent duration and may proceed more or less quickly. These unities, constituted in the absolute stream, are in the one immanent time, in which there is simultaneity and equal duration, or the same duration for two immanent objects lasting simultaneously, and a certain determinability by before and after. Husserl here revises his own earlier doctrine: the "act" of the *Logical Investigations* turns out to be a constituted immanent unity, not the ultimate subjective term. The talk of perception as a now-apprehension with attached retentions, used throughout Section Two, described moments of the flow, not acts. The threefold distinction, flow, immanent unity, transcendent object, must be kept apart from the triad of act, content and object: acts now belong to the middle level along with sensations. Only immanent unities can be faster or slower; the flow, as §35 showed, cannot. The example of judgment shows that ideal objects too are given through temporally constituted experiences, which is where *Formal and Transcendental Logic* will locate the genesis of logical sense.

#### §38. Unity of the Flow of Consciousness and Constitution of Simultaneity and Succession

The constitution of immanent objects from ever new primal sensations and modifications has been treated earlier; a note refers to §11, another to Beilage VII. In reflection we find a single flow that breaks up into many flows, but this plurality has a unity that permits and demands talk of one flow. We find many flows insofar as many series of primal sensations begin and end. But there is a connecting form, since the law of transformation of now into no-longer and of not-yet into now holds not only for each separately; there is something like a common form of the now, an equality in the mode of flowing. Several primal sensations are "at once," and when each flows, the plurality flows "together," in exactly the same mode, with exactly the same gradations and tempo, except that one generally stops while another still has its not-yet, its new primal sensations, before it. Better: the many primal sensations flow and have the same modes of running-off from the outset; only the series constitutive of enduring immanent objects continue for different lengths according to those objects' durations, and not all use the formal possibilities equally. Immanent time is constituted as one time for all immanent objects and processes. Correlatively, consciousness of the immanent is an all-unity. All-embracing is the "together," the "at once," of the actual primal sensations; all-embracing is the "before" of all the just-preceding primal sensations, and the continual transformation of each together into such a before. This before is a continuity, each point of which is an identical running-off form for the whole together. The whole together of primal sensations is subject to the law that it changes into a continuum of modes of consciousness, modes of elapsedness, and that in the same continuity an ever new together of primal sensations springs up originally, to pass again into elapsedness. What is together as a together of primal sensations remains together in the mode of elapsedness. Primal sensations have their continuous "succession" in the sense of a continuous running-off, and their together, their "at once." Those that are at once are actual primal sensations; in succession, one sensation or group is actual primal sensation and the others have elapsed. What does this mean? One can say nothing further than "look": a primal sensation or group of primal sensations that is conscious of an immanent now, a tone-now and in the same now a colour, changes continually into modes of before-consciousness in which the immanent object is conscious as past, and "at the same time" a new primal sensation always arises, a new now is established, and a new tone-now, shape-now and so on is conscious. In a group of primal sensations, one differs from another by content; only the now is the same. Consciousness, in its form as primal-sensation-consciousness, is identical. The many flows of particular contents share one form, the form of the flow itself. This explains why there is only one immanent time: not because objects are placed into a pre-given time, but because all primal sensations share the identical form of now-consciousness and the identical law of modification. "One can say nothing further than: look" marks the limit of description again, as "we lack names" did in §36.

"Together" with primal-sensation consciousness are continuous series of modes of running-off of "earlier" primal sensations, of earlier now-consciousness. This together is a together of modes of consciousness continuously modified in form, while the together of primal sensations is a together of modes identical in form. Taking a point in the continuity of running-off modes, we find there too a together of modes alike in form, or rather one identical running-off mode. These two togethers must be essentially distinguished. One is the foundation for constituting simultaneity, the other for constituting temporal succession, although simultaneity is nothing without succession and succession nothing without simultaneity, so that they must be constituted correlatively and inseparably. Terminologically, we can distinguish the fluxional pre-simultaneity from the impressional simultaneity of fluxions. Neither may be called simultaneity proper. There can be no talk of a time of the ultimate constituting consciousness. With the primal sensations that initiate the retentional process, the simultaneity of a colour and a tone, their being in one actual now, is originally constituted; but the primal sensations themselves are not simultaneous, and still less do we call the phases of the fluxional pre-simultaneity simultaneous phases of consciousness, any more than we can call the succession of consciousness a temporal succession. Husserl coins terms to keep the flow out of time: "pre-simultaneity" (Vor-Zugleich) names the cross-section of the flow at an actual phase, a primal sensation together with its retentional continuum, which is a together of differently modified modes. It is not simultaneity, since simultaneity is a relation of constituted objects in immanent time. The consistency is strict: if the flow is not in time, its phases cannot be simultaneous or successive, and even primal sensations, as moments of the flow, are not simultaneous though they constitute simultaneity.

What this pre-simultaneity is we know from earlier analyses: the continuum of phases attached to a primal sensation, each of which is retentional consciousness of the earlier now, its "original memory." When the primal sensation recedes and is continually modified, we have not only an experience that is a modification of the earlier one; we can turn our regard into it so as to "see," as it were, the earlier unmodified one in the modified. When a not-too-fast series of tones runs off, after the first tone has passed we can not only "look at" it as "still present," though no longer sensed, but attend to the fact that the mode of consciousness this tone now has is a "memory" of the mode of consciousness of the primal sensation in which it was given as now. Then we must sharply distinguish the consciousness of the past, retentional or re-presentifying, in which an immanent temporal object is conscious as before, from the retention, or the recollecting reproduction, of the earlier primal sensation, according as it is the original flow of sensory modification or its re-presentation; and likewise for every other fluxion. Here is the hinge of §39. A retention intends the past tone, but it can also be regarded as intending the earlier phase of consciousness, the primal sensation, in which the tone was given. Two objects of retention are thus distinguished: the immanent object as past, and the past phase of the flow. The second is what makes the flow conscious of itself.

If some phase of an immanent object's duration is a now-phase, conscious in primal sensation, then united with this primal sensation in pre-simultaneity are continuously connected retentions characterized in themselves as modifications of the primal sensations belonging to all the other elapsed points of the constituted duration. Each retention has a definite mode corresponding to its temporal distance from the now-point. Each is consciousness of the past of the corresponding earlier now-point and gives it in the mode of before that corresponds to its place in the elapsed duration. The cross-section is a graded continuum: the depth of retentional modification maps the temporal distance of each point. This is the vertical line of the diagram in §10, now redescribed in terms of the flow rather than of apprehensions.

#### §39. The Double Intentionality of Retention and the Constitution of the Flow of Consciousness

The doubleness in the intentionality of retention gives a hint for solving the difficulty of how we can know of a unity of the ultimate constituting flow. There is a difficulty: when a closed flow belonging to an enduring process or object has elapsed, I can look back on it; it seems to form a unity in memory. So the flow of consciousness evidently also constitutes itself in consciousness as a unity. In it the unity of a tone-duration is constituted, but it is itself again constituted as the unity of tone-duration consciousness. Must we not say that this unity is constituted in an analogous way and is just as much a constituted temporal series, so that we must speak of a temporal now, before and after after all? A note refers to Beilage VIII. The threat is the regress: if the flow is known as a unity, it seems to be a constituted temporal object, requiring a further flow to constitute it, and so on.

Husserl answers that it is the one, single flow of consciousness in which the immanent temporal unity of the tone is constituted and, at the same time, the unity of the flow itself. Offensive and initially even absurd as it seems that the flow constitutes its own unity, it is so, and it can be understood from its essential constitution. The regard can be directed through the phases that "coincide" in the continual progress of the flow, as intentionalities toward the tone. But it can also be directed onto the flow, onto a stretch of it, the transition of the flowing consciousness from the tone's onset to its end. Every shading of consciousness of the type "retention" has a double intentionality. One serves the constitution of the immanent object, the tone; this is what we call primary memory of the just-sensed tone, or more clearly, retention of the tone. The other is constitutive of the unity of this primary memory in the flow: the retention, in being still-consciousness, holding consciousness, is retention of the elapsed tone-retention; in its continual shading off in the flow it is continual retention of the continually preceding phases. Take any phase of the flow, in which a tone-now and a stretch of tone-duration appear in the mode of just having elapsed. It comprises a continuity of retentions unified in pre-simultaneity, which is retention of the whole momentary continuity of the continually preceding phases of the flow: in the initial member it is new primal sensation, in the immediately following first phase of shading it is immediate retention of the preceding primal sensation, in the next momentary phase retention of the retention of the primal sensation before that, and so on. If the flow continues, we have the flow-continuum in its running-off, which makes the continuity just described change retentionally, and each new continuity of momentarily simultaneous phases is retention relative to the whole continuity of the simultaneous in the preceding phase. Through the flow thus runs a longitudinal intentionality which, in the course of the flow, is in continuous unity of coincidence with itself. In absolute transition, flowing, the first primal sensation turns into retention of it, this into retention of this retention, and so on. But together with the first retention a new "now," a new primal sensation, is there and joined continuously-momentarily with it, so that the second phase is primal sensation of the new now and retention of the earlier, the third again new primal sensation with retention of the second primal sensation and retention of the retention of the first, and so on. A retention of a retention has intentionality not only toward the immediately retained but also toward what is retained in the retaining at the second level, and finally toward the primal datum, which is objectified throughout. Similarly, a presentification of a thing-appearance intends not only the appearance but also the appearing thing, or better, a memory of A makes conscious not only the memory but also A as what the memory remembers. This is the decisive solution. Each retention intends two things at once: the past phase of the object, the tone just past (transverse intentionality), and the past phase of the flow itself, the retention just past (longitudinal intentionality). Because retentions retain retentions, the flow is conscious of its own elapsed phases in the very process by which it constitutes the object. No second flow is required, since self-consciousness is a dimension of the same retentional structure. The comparison with memory makes the transitivity explicit: as the memory of A gives both memory and A, so the retention of a retention gives both the earlier retention and what it retained. The editor reports no separate source for this passage beyond the late-1911 manuscript underlying §§35–39, which places the solution at the end of Husserl's development in this volume.

Accordingly, in the flow of consciousness, by virtue of the continuity of retentional modifications and their being continually retentions of the continually preceding ones, the unity of the flow itself is constituted as a one-dimensional quasi-temporal order. If I turn toward the tone, living attentively in the "transverse intentionality" (in primal sensation as sensation of the current tone-now, in the retentional modifications as primary memories of the series of elapsed tone-points, and in the flow of retentional modifications experiencing unity throughout), the enduring tone stands there, continually extending its duration. If I adjust to the "longitudinal intentionality" and what is constituted in it, I turn the reflecting regard from the tone, which has lasted so long, to what is new in pre-simultaneity at one point, the primal sensation, and to what is retained "at the same time" in a continuous series. What is retained is past consciousness in its series of phases, first its preceding phase; and in the continual flowing on of consciousness I grasp the retained series of elapsed consciousness with the limit-point of actual primal sensation, and the continual pushing back of this series as new retentions and primal sensations are set. "Quasi-temporal" is precise: the flow has an order, one-dimensional and linear, but it is not time, since time is constituted by it. The two intentionalities correspond to two directions of regard: transverse, toward the object through the phases; longitudinal, toward the flow along the phases. Reflection on consciousness is thus grounded in a structure already present in non-reflective consciousness, which Beilage IX develops as "primal consciousness."

Can I find and grasp in one look the whole retentional consciousness of the past course of consciousness enclosed in one pre-simultaneity? The necessary process is that I first grasp the pre-simultaneity itself, which is continually modified, since it is only what it is in the flow. The flow, insofar as it modifies this pre-simultaneity, is intentionally in coincidence with itself, constitutes unity in the flow, and the one identical receives a continual mode of being pushed back, as the new continually attaches in front, only to flow away again in its momentary context. During this process the regard can stay fixed on the momentary pre-simultaneity as it sinks; but the constitution of retentional unity reaches beyond it and keeps adding the new. The regard can turn to this in the process, and it is always consciousness in the flow as constituted unity. Reflection on the flow can only fix a cross-section as it sinks, never freeze the flow; what reflection grasps is itself already the flow constituted as a unity, never the flowing in its actuality. This point, here stated calmly, becomes for Fink and Derrida the irreducible delay of reflection behind the living present.

In the one, single flow of consciousness, therefore, two inseparably unified intentionalities are interwoven, requiring each other like two sides of one thing. By one, immanent time is constituted, an objective time, a genuine one, in which there is duration and change of what endures; by the other, the quasi-temporal arrangement of the flow's phases, which always and necessarily has the flowing "now"-point, the phase of actuality, and the series of pre-actual and post-actual, not yet actual, phases. This pre-phenomenal, pre-immanent temporality is constituted intentionally as the form of time-constituting consciousness and in it. The flow of immanent time-constituting consciousness not only is, but is so remarkably and yet intelligibly constituted that a self-appearance of the flow necessarily exists in it, and so the flow itself must be graspable in its flowing. The self-appearance of the flow does not require a second flow; as phenomenon it constitutes itself in itself. A note refers to Beilage IX. The constituting and the constituted coincide, and yet they cannot coincide in every respect. The phases of the flow in which phases of the same flow are phenomenally constituted cannot be identical with these constituted phases, and are not. What is brought to appearance in the momentary actuality of the flow are, in the series of its retentional moments, past phases of the flow. The section ends with the most cited formula of the book: the flow's self-appearance requires no second flow. The qualification that follows is just as important: constituting and constituted coincide in the flow but not in the same phase. The actual phase makes past phases appear; it never appears to itself as actual in the same way. Husserl thus avoids both an infinite regress and an absolute self-transparency of the present. Whether this residual non-coincidence reintroduces a gap at the heart of self-presence is the question Derrida presses in *Speech and Phenomena* and Henry presses from the opposite side in the *Phenomenology of Material Life*, denying that the flow's self-givenness can be retentional at all.

#### §40. The Constituted Immanent Contents

The editor notes that no manuscript source for §40 could be found. Passing to the layer of immanent "contents," whose constitution is the achievement of the absolute flow, Husserl lists them: they are experiences in the ordinary sense, sensory data, even unnoticed ones, such as a red or a blue; appearances, of a house or the surroundings, whether or not they and their objects are attended to; "acts" of asserting, wishing, willing and the like, with their reproductive modifications, phantasies and memories. All these are contents of consciousness, contents of the primal consciousness constituting temporal objects, which is not itself content or object in phenomenological time in this sense. The inventory places sensations, appearances and acts at one level, the level of immanent temporal unities. "Primal consciousness" (Urbewusstsein) here names the flow as the consciousness for which these contents are contents, without being itself a content. This is the solution to the problem of how acts are conscious without an infinite regress of inner perceptions: they are conscious through the flow, not through further acts.

Immanent contents are what they are only insofar as during their "actual" duration they point forward to something future and back to something past. In this pointing different things must be distinguished. In each primal phase that originally constitutes the immanent content we have retentions of the preceding and protentions of the coming phases of just this content, and the protentions are fulfilled as long as the content lasts. These "determinate" retentions and protentions have a dark horizon; they pass fluidly into indeterminate ones related to the past and future course of the stream, through which the actual content is fitted into the unity of the stream. Retentions and protentions must also be distinguished from recollections and expectations, which do not concern the constituting phases of the immanent content but presentify past or future immanent contents. Contents endure and have their time; they are individual objectivities, unities of change or unchange. Protention now receives its place in the structure of original consciousness, symmetrical with retention, and is fulfilled continuously as the content proceeds. The distinction between determinate retention/protention and an indeterminate horizon prepares the later doctrine of the stream as a whole given in horizon-consciousness. The distinction from recollection and expectation repeats the axis of §19, now applied to both temporal directions.

#### §41. Evidence of Immanent Contents. Change and Unchange

When one speaks of the evident givenness of an immanent content, evidence cannot mean indubitable certainty about the punctual temporal existence of the tone; Husserl calls such an evidence, as Brentano still assumed it, a fiction. If it belongs to the essence of a content given in perception to be temporally extended, the indubitability of perception can only mean indubitability about temporally extended existence; a note refers to §44. Every question about individual existence can be answered only by returning to perception, which gives individual existence in the strictest sense. Insofar as something not perception is mixed into perception, questionability remains. For immanent contents, as opposed to empirical things, duration and change, coexistence and succession can be fully realized in perception and often are, in perceptions that are purely intuiting and constitute enduring or changing contents in the most proper sense, containing no possible questionability. To them we are led back in all questions of origin, while they exclude any further question of origin. The much-discussed evidence of inner perception, the evidence of the cogitatio, would lose all meaning if temporal extension were excluded from the sphere of evidence and true givenness. The editor dates §41 to the 1905 sheets 47–49. Husserl turns the analysis of retention against the Cartesian and Brentanian restriction of evidence to the present instant. A punctual evidence is a fiction, since nothing punctual is ever given. If retention were not evident, the cogito itself, which takes time, would have no evidence. This makes the evidence of retention, established in §13, the condition of all phenomenological evidence.

Analysing this consciousness of the evidence of duration: if the tone c, not just its quality but its whole content, held completely unchanged, is perceived as enduring, then c is spread over a stretch of the immediate temporal field; at each now not another tone but continuously the same appears. That the same continually appears, this continuity of identity, is an inner character of consciousness. The time-points are not separated by distinct acts; the unity of perception is unbroken, without any marked inner differences. Yet there are differences, since each time-point is individually different from every other: different but not separated. The indistinguishable sameness of temporal matter and the continuity of modification of time-positing consciousness essentially found the fusion into the unbroken extension of c, and only so does a concrete unity arise. Only as temporally extended is the tone c a concrete individual. The concrete alone is ever given, and intellectual processes of analysis make descriptions like these possible. The unbroken unity of c, which is what is first given, proves to be divisible, a fusion of moments ideally distinguishable within it and perhaps findable, for example by means of a simultaneous succession that makes sections distinguishable in the parallel duration, which can then be compared and identified. Husserl's formula "different but not separated" states the continuity of duration: individual points are distinct but never given apart. Analysis into phases is an abstraction from the concrete continuum, consistent with §10's thesis that phases are dependent moments. A concurrent change, a parallel succession, is what lets us mark off parts of an unchanging duration, which explains how we measure an unchanging duration against a clock.

These descriptions already involve idealizing fictions. It is a fiction that the tone endures absolutely unchanged; some fluctuation will always occur in some moments, so that continuous unity in one moment will be combined with a difference in another that indirectly divides it. A break in qualitative identity, a leap from one quality to another within the same genus at a time-point, yields a new experience, the experience of change, and evidently a discontinuity is not possible at every point of a stretch. Discontinuity presupposes continuity, whether as changeless duration or continuous change. In continuous change the phases of the consciousness of change pass into one another without break, in the manner of a consciousness of unity and identity, as with changeless duration. But the unity proves not to be undifferentiated. What first passes over without difference shows, as the continuous synthesis proceeds, deviation and growing deviation, so that sameness and difference mix and a continuity of increasing difference with increasing extension is given. The original now-intention, maintaining itself individually, appears in each new simultaneous consciousness joined with intentions that, the further removed they are in time, make an increasing difference, a distance, stand out. What first coincided and then almost coincided moves increasingly apart; old and new no longer appear essentially the same but ever more other and alien, despite generic community. So arises the consciousness of "gradually changed," of growing distance in the flow of continuous identification. Change is constituted as a growing non-coincidence within a continuing coincidence. The synthesis that identifies the enduring also registers drift, and the consciousness of change arises from comparing the retained with the new along the continuum, without any separate act of comparison. This is the passive synthesis of §18 applied to the continuum, and it explains why discontinuity can only appear against a background of continuity.

In changeless duration there is continuous consciousness of unity that remains homogeneous throughout; coincidence continues through the whole series of advancing intentions and allows no consciousness of "otherwise," of moving away, of distance. In the consciousness of change there is also coincidence running in a certain way through the whole extension, but in the coincidence according to the general there emerges, at the same time and increasingly, deviation according to the difference. The way the matter of change is distributed over the stretch determines the consciousness of fast or slow change, of its speed and acceleration. In every case, and not only in continuous change, consciousness of otherness and difference presupposes a unity. In alternation something enduring must be there, and likewise in change, something constituting the identity of what changes or alternates. This refers back to essential forms of the consciousness of something individual. If the tone's quality stays the same while its intensity or timbre changes, we say the same tone changes its timbre or intensity. If nothing in the whole phenomenon stays the same, if it changes "in all its determinations," there is still enough to establish unity: the absence of difference with which adjacent phases pass into one another, thereby establishing consciousness of unity. The kind and form of the whole remains generically the same. Like passes into like within a manifold of similarity; conversely, the similar is what can belong to a unity of continuous transition, or whatever has a distance, just as the same is what can ground the unity of a changeless duration, rest, or has no distance. So it is wherever change and alternation are spoken of: a consciousness of unity must underlie them. The definitions at the end are striking: similarity and equality are defined through the possibility of continuous transition and of changeless duration. Basic categories of comparison are thus grounded in the structure of temporal synthesis, not in a separate act of comparing. Even total change preserves identity through the seamless passage of adjacent phases, which makes continuity itself the minimal principle of individual identity.

#### §42. Impression and Reproduction

The editor notes that no manuscript source survives for §§42–45 and suggests they date from after 1911. When we follow the constitution not of impressional but of, say, remembered contents in their duration, we cannot speak of primal impressions corresponding to their now-point. At the head stand primal memories, as absolute phases: not something set in "from outside," "alien to consciousness," primally generated, but something that has surfaced, resurfaced, at least in memory. Though not an impression, this moment, like impression, is not a product of spontaneity but in a certain way receptive. One might speak of passive reception and distinguish passive receiving that brings in something new, alien and original, from passive receiving that merely brings back, presentifies. Reproductions too have a source-point, but a different one: not the alien newness of impression, but the receptive surfacing of the already had. Both are passive. The vocabulary of "alien to consciousness" and "primal generation" links to Beilage I and gives primal impression the character of a radical receptivity, the point at which, for Husserl, consciousness is affected by what it does not produce.

Every constituted experience is either impression or reproduction; as reproduction it is either a presentifying or not. In either case it is itself immanently present. To every present and presenting consciousness corresponds the ideal possibility of an exactly corresponding presentification of this consciousness. To impressional perceiving corresponds the possibility of a presentification of it, to impressional wishing a presentification of it, and so on. This presentifying also concerns every sensory content: to the sensed red corresponds a phantasm red, a presentifying consciousness of the impressional red, so to sensing, the perceiving of hyletic data, corresponds a presentification of sensing. Every presentifying is itself present through an impressional consciousness. In a certain sense all experiences are conscious through impressions, are impressed. Among them some occur as reproductions, presentifying modifications of impressions, and to every consciousness such a modification corresponds; presentifying is not here understood as attentive meaning. A perceiving is consciousness of an object. As consciousness it is also an impression, something immanently present. To this immanently present perceiving of A corresponds the reproductive modification: presentification of the perceiving, perceiving in phantasy or memory. Such "perception in phantasy" is at the same time phantasy of the perceived object. In perception an object, say a thing or process, stands there as present; perception is not only itself present, but a presenting, in which something present stands there. Likewise a presentifying modification of perception is a presentification of the perceived object: the thing is phantasied, remembered, expected. In original consciousness all impressions are constituted, primary contents as well as experiences that are "consciousness of." Experiences divide into two fundamental classes: acts, consciousness of something, experiences with a "relation to something," and those without. The sensed colour has no relation to anything, nor do phantasy-contents such as a phantasm red hovering before us, even unattended. But the phantasy-consciousness of red does, as do all primitive presentifications. We thus find impressions that are presentifications of impressional consciousness: as impressional consciousness is consciousness of the immanent, so impressional presentification is presentification of the immanent. A note bears on the meaning of the whole: insofar as one may call primal consciousness, the flow constituting immanent time and its experiences, an act, or divide it into units and acts, one could and perhaps must say that a primal act or nexus of primal acts constitutes unities that are themselves acts or not; but that raises difficulties. Husserl distinguishes two senses of "impression": every experience is an impression in being immanently present, and some impressions, perceptions, are also presentings of an object. Reproduction, too, is immanently present as an experience while presentifying its object. Every experience can be reproduced, including sensation. The division between acts and non-acts, intentional experiences and hyletic data, is the *Logical Investigations*' distinction and the hyle–morphe schema of *Ideas I*. The note shows Husserl hesitating to call the flow an act: it is "consciousness," but not an intentional act among others, since acts are constituted unities.

Impression, in the narrower sense opposed to presentification, must be taken as primary consciousness that has no further consciousness behind it in which it would be conscious. Presentification, even the most primitive immanent one, is secondary consciousness; it presupposes primary consciousness in which it is impressionally conscious. The regress of consciousness stops at impression: primary consciousness is self-conscious without a further consciousness of it. Presentification is always secondary, since it is itself conscious through an impression. This anticipates Beilage IX's "primal consciousness" and closes the door to the reflection-model of self-consciousness that §39 left partly open.

#### §43. Constitution of Thing-Appearances and Things. Constituted Apprehensions and Primal Apprehensions

Take such primary consciousness, say the perception of this copper ashtray, which stands there as an enduring thing. Reflection distinguishes the perception itself, the perceptual apprehension taken concretely with its data of apprehension, the perceptual appearance in the mode of certainty, from the perceived, which is to be described in evident judgments founded on perception and is also what is meant; meaning "lives" in perceiving. The perceptual apprehension in its mode is, as reflection teaches, itself something constituted in immanent time, standing in the unity of presence, though not meant. It is constituted by the manifold of now-phases and retentions. Both the contents of apprehension and the apprehending intentions, including the mode of certainty, are constituted this way. Sensory contents are constituted as unities in sensory impressions, apprehensions in other act-impressions interwoven with them. Perception, as a constituted phenomenon, is in turn perception of the thing. The ashtray example lays out three levels again: the flow, the perception as an immanent unity (both sensory content and apprehension), and the thing perceived. The new term "act-impressions" names the primal phases in which apprehensions, not only sensations, are constituted, so the apprehension itself has its primal impressions and retentions.

In primary time-consciousness the thing-appearance, the thing-apprehension, is constituted as an enduring unchanged or changing phenomenon. In the unity of this change another unity is "conscious": the unity of the thing, unchanged or changing in its time, its duration. In the same impressional consciousness in which perception is constituted, what is perceived is constituted, and precisely thereby. It belongs to the essence of consciousness so built to be at once consciousness of unity of the immanent kind and of the transcendent kind, and a meaning regard can be turned now to sensation, now to appearance, now to object. Mutatis mutandis this holds for all "acts": it belongs to their essence to have transcendent intentionality and to be able to have it only through something immanently constituted, through "apprehensions." This grounds the possibility of relating the immanent, the apprehension with its immanent content, to the transcendent, and this relating yields again an "act," of a higher level. The constitution of perception and of the perceived happen in one process: by constituting the immanent appearance, consciousness constitutes the transcendent thing. Transcendent intentionality is always mediated by immanent constitution, and the relation between the two can become the object of a higher act, which is how the phenomenological attitude itself becomes possible.

In perception a complex of sensory contents, themselves unities constituted in the original temporal flow, receives unity of apprehension, and the unified apprehension is again a constituted unity in the first sense. Immanent unities are not conscious in their constitution in the same way as what appears in transcendent appearance, or what is perceived in transcendent perception. Yet they must share an essential community. For immanent impression is presenting, as perceiving is presenting: in one case immanent presenting, in the other transcendent presenting "through" appearances. So while transcendent appearances are unities constituted in inner consciousness, other unities are supposed to be constituted "in" these unities: the appearing objects. Husserl names the parallel and the difference. Both levels are presentings; but the immanent unity is given directly in the flow, while the transcendent object is given through appearances. The question is how one kind of constitution can be nested in another.

Immanent unities, we saw, are constituted in the flow of the manifolds of temporal shading. Following the flow in the longitudinal direction, we have for each temporal point of the immanent content the manifold modified primal contents, characterized as retentional modifications of the primal content with the now-character. These primal contents bear primal apprehensions, which in their flowing connection constitute the temporal unity of the immanent content as it moves back into the past. The "contents" in the case of perceptual appearance are just these whole appearances as temporal unities. So perceptual apprehension too is constituted in such a manifold of shadings, unified by the unity of temporal apprehension. We must therefore understand apprehension in two senses: the immanently constituted one, and the one belonging to immanent constitution itself, to the phases of the original flow, the primal apprehension, which is no longer constituted. In the immanent course of appearances, the continuous succession of apprehensions in phenomenological time that we call perceptions, a temporal unity is constituted insofar as the continuity of apprehensions yields not only unity of changing appearances, such as the series of aspects in turning a thing, which appear as aspects of the same thing, but unity of appearances of an enduring or changing thing. The distinction between constituted apprehension and primal apprehension is the last refinement of the apprehension schema in the volume. Primal apprehension belongs to the flow and is not constituted, which reuses the language of apprehension for what §1's later note said does not fit that schema. Husserl keeps the word but changes its level: at the bottom, "apprehension" names the intentional character of the flow's phases, not an act applied to a datum.

Immanent time is objectified into a time of the objects constituted in immanent appearances because, in the manifold of shadings of sensory contents as unities of phenomenological time, or in the phenomenological-temporal manifold of apprehensions of these contents, an identical thing appears that continually presents itself in all phases in manifolds of shadings; a note refers to Beilage X. The thing is constituted in the course of its appearances, which are themselves constituted as immanent unities in the flow of original impressions, and the one is necessarily constituted with the other. The appearing thing is constituted because sensory unities and unified apprehensions are constituted in the original flow, hence continually consciousness of something, presentation, more precisely presenting of something, and in continuous succession presentation of the same. The presenting fluents flow and connect so that what appears in them spreads out into manifolds of presentational shadings formed just as a sensory content spreads out into sensory shadings. That is why the manifold of apprehension is characterized as presenting, exactly as immanent impressions are. If the primally present sensory data, beyond their primal presentations and the primal retentions and protentions essentially belonging to them, continually carry characters of apprehension of spatial-thing constitution, then phenomenological time, to which sensory data and thing-apprehensions belong, and the spatial time of things must coincide point for point. With each filled point of phenomenological time a point of filled objective time presents itself through the sensory contents and their apprehensions. This is the explanation of §33's isochrony. Objective time coincides point for point with phenomenological time because thing-apprehensions ride on sensory data that are themselves temporally constituted; objective time is immanent time objectified through the apprehension of things. The parallel between temporal shadings and spatial shadings ("Abschattungen") makes the thing's spatial presentation and the content's temporal presentation two cases of one structure.

In the vertical series of the diagram we have not only the continuous vertical coincidence belonging to phenomenological time-constitution, where in one moment the primal datum is united with its retentional modifications, but also the retentional shadings of the thing-apprehensions belonging to each vertical series stand, as thing-apprehensions, in continuous coincidence. These are two coincidences. The series of thing-apprehensions coincides not only insofar as it co-constitutes a continuous sequence, but insofar as it constitutes the same thing. The first is a coincidence of connecting essential sameness, the second a coincidence of identity, since in the continuous identification of the sequence an enduring identical thing is conscious. This also involves the continuous successive identification from vertical series to vertical series, with fulfilment of protentions, which now have an objective-spatial sense as well. Two syntheses run together: the temporal synthesis that binds phases as phases, and the identity synthesis that binds them as presentations of one thing. The first holds for any content; the second exists only where a transcendent object is apprehended. Protention acquires a spatial sense in thing-perception: it anticipates the next aspect of the thing, which is the temporal form of the horizon structure of perception.

The analogy between the constitution of immanent and transcendent unities has been noted. As "sensory shadings," the primal data of presentation for sensory unities in phenomenological time, have their law, their essential character in the primal succession, and constitute the unity of sensation through the modification shown in the diagram, so it is with the shadings of things, the "appearances," which now function as primal data of the primal succession. The primal succession of moments of appearance constitutes, through the time-founding retentions and so on, the changing or unchanging appearance as a phenomenological-temporal unity. Moreover, appearances from the manifold belonging to the same unchanged thing have an ontic essence, the essence of what appears, that is entirely the same, as the momentary data belonging to an unchanged red are of entirely the same essence. The series of a thing's change, like those of a red's change, are governed by a fixed law. Thus two things are constituted intentionally at once: the appearance and what appears, and, in different appearances, what appears unchanged or changed. Thing-constitution repeats temporal constitution one level up: appearances function as the primal data from which the thing is constituted, as sensory phases are the data from which a sensation is constituted. The "ontic essence" of appearances, the sameness of what they present, plays the role that qualitative sameness played for the unchanged tone.

The question now is what properties thing-appearances have that are appearances of the same thing. That is the question of the constitution of the spatial thing, which presupposes the constitution of time. The section ends by placing the theory of time beneath the theory of space, which Husserl had begun in the 1907 *Thing and Space* lectures; time-constitution is the foundation on which spatial constitution builds.

#### §44. Inner and Outer Perception

We speak of an enduring perception in thing-perception and in immanent perception alike; a note refers to Beilagen XI and XII. In thing-perception one counts as perception also the continuous perceptual appearance, the continuity of the thing's now-appearances, apart from retentional and protentional interweavings. The thing-appearance, the "thing in its orientation," in a definite presentation, is something enduring, just like the thing that appears. Even the merely appearing side-surface endures and changes in its duration. Strictly, I should not say "the thing in its orientation" but the process of thing-appearance, which persists while the orientation stays the same and is otherwise a continuous course of changing appearances, but within one duration. The appearance is itself an enduring immanent process, the middle level of §34. Husserl corrects his own phrase so as not to confuse the object-in-its-how with an object.

In perceiving an immanent object we can also take together the immanent of the now in its continuity, but then it is the duration of the object itself; the object does not appear in the sense it does in external perception. So while "perception" in the case of consciousness of an external object can designate the external appearance as an immanent object, perception and perceived being obviously different, when we speak of inner perception and want perception and perceived to remain distinct, perception cannot mean the immanent, which is the object itself. Inner perception can mean only one of two things: first, the inner consciousness of the unified immanent object, which exists even without turning toward it, as the consciousness constituting the temporal; or second, inner consciousness with turning toward. It is easy to see that turning toward, grasping, is an immanent process with its own immanent duration, which coincides with the duration of the immanent tone during the turning toward it. There is no appearance between inner perception and its object, since the immanent object does not present itself in profiles. Hence inner perception is either the time-constituting consciousness itself, which is always there, or the act of attending, which is itself a constituted immanent process. This resolves the old dispute about whether inner perception is a separate act: in its primary sense it is not an act but the flow; only attentive inner perception is an act, and it is simultaneous with its object, as §33 required.

In the case of the external object we have: the external appearance; the constituting consciousness in which the external appearance is constituted as immanent; and the turning toward, which can be directed to the appearance and its components as well as to what appears. Only the latter is in question when we speak of external perception. The threefold scheme fixes the place of attention. Attention is not constitution: it selects among the levels already constituted, and only its direction to the transcendent object counts as external perception in the ordinary sense.

The analogous reflection holds for memory, except that memory has its own intentionality, that of presentification. Memory has its unity as a process in inner consciousness and its place and duration in immanent time, whether it is memory of the immanent or of the transcendent. And every memory, apart from turning toward, is at the same time memory of something immanent. So while the consciousness of the immanent tone, as original inner consciousness, can have no immanent temporality, the presentifying consciousness of the immanent tone, which in a correspondingly altered sense is presentifying consciousness of the inner consciousness of the tone, is an immanent object belonging to immanent temporality. The asymmetry is the decisive point: original inner consciousness is not in immanent time, while a presentification of it is. Presentification is always a constituted act; original consciousness never is. Memory of a tone is, implicitly, memory of the inner consciousness of the tone, the reproductive counterpart of the longitudinal intentionality of §39.

#### §45. Constitution of Non-Temporal Transcendencies

Every consciousness in the unified sense, as a constituted immanent unity, is necessarily also consciousness of the objectivity to which it "relates." But not every consciousness is itself time-consciousness, consciousness of something temporal, constituting an intentional time. A judging consciousness of a mathematical state of affairs is an impression, but the mathematical state of affairs, standing there in its unity, is nothing temporal, and judging is not a presenting or presentifying; a note refers to Beilage XIII. Accordingly, one can say that a thing, an event, a temporal being is represented in phantasy, that it appears in phantasy, memory, expectation or retention, just as one can say it appears as present or perceived. But one cannot say that a mathematical state of affairs appears as present or presentified. Judging can last longer or shorter, has its extension in subjective time, and can be present or presentified. What is judged is not long or short, more or less enduring, nor is what is quasi-judged in presentifying a judgment. What is presentified is the judgment, not what is judged. Saying that one "merely thinks" a state of affairs does not mean that it is presentified, but that it stands in the character of the neutrality modification instead of belief. The belief-modalities do not coincide with those of present and non-present but cross them. With an individual state of affairs one can still speak, improperly, of temporal characters, insofar as the thing that is logically articulated and synthetically grasped in it can be perceptually present or presentified in phantasy. But for a non-temporal state of affairs, one that says nothing about anything temporal, this makes no sense. Phantasying oneself into a mathematical judgment does not mean bringing the mathematical state of affairs to a phantasy representation, as if it could be something presented or presentified. Husserl separates the temporality of acts from the temporality of their objects. Every act is in immanent time, but not every object is temporal. Ideal objects are not given in temporal modes; the distinction between present and presentified does not apply to them. This preserves the *Logical Investigations*' anti-psychologism within the theory of time-constitution: the judging is temporal, what is judged is not. The two axes, belief versus neutrality and presentation versus presentification, cross: imagining a judgment neutralizes belief but does not make its content past or imagined.

Appearance in the pregnant sense of presentation belongs only to the sphere of presenting and its modifications, and the constitution of what appears, or better the genuine givenness of individual being, requires that it be given in the form of a continuity of appearances as presentations. That states of affairs too can "merely appear" and demand verification in a genuine givenness is obvious. Nor does it change anything that states of affairs founded on individual appearances, "facts of nature," come to givenness on the basis of the underlying appearances, and thus similarly in infinities of "presentations." Still, the "presentation" of a state of affairs is not presentation in the proper sense, only in a derived sense. The state of affairs is not properly something temporal: it obtains for a certain time but is not itself in time like a thing or process. Time-consciousness and presenting belong not to the state of affairs as such but to its subject matter. Even empirical states of affairs, "the leaf is green," are not temporal objects: they hold for a time, but their temporality is borrowed from the individual things they concern. Givenness in profiles, presentation proper, is reserved for individuals. Here the theory of time sets the boundary between individual and categorial objects that *Ideas I* will draw as the boundary between individual and eidetic intuition.

The same holds for all other founded acts and their correlates. A value has no position in time. A temporal object may be beautiful, pleasing or useful, and may be so at a certain time, but beauty, pleasingness and the like have no place in nature or in time. They are not something appearing in presentings or presentifications. The final claim extends the result to the axiological sphere: values and other founded qualities are not temporal individuals, though the objects that bear them are. The lectures close by delimiting their own domain. Time-consciousness constitutes individuals; it is the necessary form of every act and the form of every individual object, but it is not the form of every objectivity. The ideal and axiological objects that consciousness also intends exceed time, which is why the theory of time-consciousness, foundational as it is, is not the whole of transcendental phenomenology.

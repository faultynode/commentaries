# ***Logical Investigations*****, Investigation VI: *Elements of a Phenomenological Elucidation of Knowledge***

*A commentary on the text of Husserliana XIX/2 (the running B² text of 1921), covering the Sixth Investigation in its entirety. Significant A/B divergences are noted only where they bear on the philosophical content.*

## **Introduction**

Husserl opens by looking back on the Fifth Investigation, which had seemed at first to lose itself in remote questions of descriptive psychology but in fact advanced the interests of epistemic clarification considerably. The governing thesis is stated at once: all thinking, and above all all theoretical thinking and knowing, is carried out in certain "acts" (*Akte*) which appear in the context of expressive discourse. In these acts lies the source of all the unities of validity (*Geltungseinheiten*) that confront the thinker as objects of thought and knowledge, or as their explanatory grounds and laws, their theories and sciences. In the same acts, therefore, lies the source of the corresponding general and pure ideas whose ideal-lawful interconnections pure logic sets forth and whose clarification the critique of knowledge means to accomplish. Husserl insists that simply by establishing the phenomenological peculiarity of acts as such — that much-disputed and much-misunderstood class of experiences (*Erlebnisse*) — a great deal has already been gained for the clarifying work. By ordering the logical experiences into this class, a first important step has been taken toward delimiting and analytically rendering intelligible the logical sphere and the fundamental concepts of knowledge. The progress of the inquiry also led to the separation of various concepts of "content" (*Inhalt*) which tend to run confusingly into one another wherever acts and the ideal unities belonging to them are in question.

Continuing the retrospect, Husserl recalls that these confusions — distinctions that had already struck us in the narrower circle of meanings and meaning-conferring acts in the First Investigation — returned in the wider field and in their most general form. Even the content-concept newly won in the last (Fifth) Investigation and especially remarkable, namely that of the *intentional essence* (*intentionales Wesen*), did not lack this relation to the logical domain; for the same series of identities that had earlier served to illustrate the unity of meaning yielded, suitably generalized, a certain identity referable to arbitrary acts — the identity of the "intentional essence." Through this linkage, or subordination, of the phenomenological characters and ideal unities of the logical domain under the wholly general characters and unities that have their domain in the field of acts überhaupt, the former gained a considerable measure of phenomenological and critical understanding. The inquiries carried out in the last chapters, attaching to the distinction between *act-quality* and *act-matter* within the unified intentional essence, led once again deep into the sphere of logical interest. The pressing question of the relation of this intentional matter to the presentational substratum (*Vorstellungsgrundlage*) essential to every act forced us to separate several important and perennially confused concepts of *Vorstellung* (presentation/representation), whereby at the same time a fundamental piece of the "theory of judgment" was worked out.

Husserl concedes, with deliberate modesty, that the specifically logical concepts of *Vorstellung* and the concept of judgment were left without conclusive clarification: here and everywhere a great stretch of road still lies ahead, and "we still stand at the beginnings." Even the nearer goal of laying bare the origin of the idea *meaning* (*Bedeutung*) has not yet been reached. He grants a valuable insight already secured: unmistakably, the meaning of expressions lies in the intentional essence of the acts in question. But the question of *what kinds of acts* are capable of the meaning-function at all — or whether instead acts of every kind are on a par in this respect — has not yet been weighed. As soon as we want to take up this question (as the next paragraphs will show), we strike upon the *relation between meaning-intention and meaning-fulfilment* (*Bedeutungsintention und Bedeutungserfüllung*), or, in traditional but equivocal terms, the relation between "concept" or "thought" (here understood as intuitively unfulfilled meaning) and "corresponding intuition" (*korrespondierende Anschauung*). This pair names the master-theme of the whole Investigation: the contrast between an empty meaning-directedness and its intuitive fulfilment.

The most exact investigation of this distinction — already indicated in the First Investigation — is, Husserl says, of exceptional importance. In carrying out the relevant analyses, attached first to the very simplest nominal intentions, we soon notice that the whole consideration demands a *natural extension and delimitation* (*naturgemäße Erweiterung und Umgrenzung*). The widest class of acts in which we find differences of intention and fulfilment (or disappointment, *Enttäuschung*, of the intention) reaches far beyond the logical domain. The logical domain itself is delimited by the peculiarity of a fulfilment-relation. A class of acts — the *objectifying* acts (*die objektivierenden Akte*) — is distinguished from all others in that the fulfilment-syntheses belonging to their sphere have the character of *knowledge* (*Erkenntnis*), of *identification* (*Identifizierung*), of the "setting-into-one" of what agrees, while correlatively the disappointment-syntheses have the correlate character of "separation" of what conflicts (*Widerstreitendes*). Within this widest sphere of objectifying acts we shall study all relations pertinent to the unity of knowledge — and not only insofar as a fulfilment of those special intentions which attach to expressions as meaning-intentions is at stake. Analogous intentions occur independently of grammatical linkage. Moreover, intuitions too, and indeed as a rule, have the character of intentions which demand and often receive further fulfilment.

Husserl now sketches the program. We shall characterize the wholly general concepts of *signification* (*Signifikation*) and *intuition* (*Intuition*) phenomenologically, and indeed by recourse to the fulfilment-phenomena, and shall investigate the analysis — fundamental for the clarification of knowledge — of the *various kinds of intuition*, first of sensuous intuition. We shall then enter into the *phenomenology of the degrees of knowledge* (*Phänomenologie der Erkenntnisstufen*) and confer clarity and firm determinacy on a series of basic concepts of knowledge bearing upon it. New content-concepts, only touched on in passing in the preceding analyses, will here emerge: the concept of *intuitive content* (*intuitiver Inhalt*) and the concept of *representing* (apprehended) content (*repräsentierender Inhalt*). To the previous concept of the intentional essence there will be added the *cognitive essence* (*erkenntnismäßiges Wesen*); and within the latter we shall distinguish the intentional quality, the intentional matter *as the apprehension-sense* (*Auffassungssinn*), the *apprehension-form* (*Auffassungsform*), and the apprehended (apperceived, representing) content. The concept of *apprehension* or *representation* (*Auffassung oder Repräsentation*) will be determined as the unity of matter and representing content through the apprehension-form. This dense paragraph fixes the conceptual machinery — sense, form, representing content — that the body of the Investigation will deploy.

Turning to the graded series of intention and fulfilment, Husserl announces that we shall come to know the differences of greater or lesser *mediacy* (*Mittelbarkeit*) within the intention itself — a mediacy that excludes a simple fulfilment and demands instead a graded sequence of fulfilments — and thereby learn to understand the most important, hitherto unclarified, sense of talk about *indirect presentations* (*indirekte Vorstellungen*). We shall then pursue the differences of greater or lesser *adequacy* (*Angemessenheit*) of the intention to the intuitive experience that fuses onto it as fulfilment in knowledge, and determine the case of *objectively complete adequation* (*objektiv vollständige Anmessung*). In connection with this we strive for a final phenomenological clarification of the concepts of *possibility* and *impossibility* (unity, compatibility — conflict, incompatibility) and of the ideal axioms bearing on them. Taking into account the act-qualities hitherto left out of play, we then consider the distinction — relating to *positing* acts (*setzende Akte*) — of *preliminary* and *ultimate* fulfilment (*vorläufige und letzte Erfüllung*). Ultimate fulfilment represents an ideal of perfection; it lies always in a corresponding "perception" (presupposing a necessary extension of the perception-concept beyond the bounds of sensibility). The fulfilment-synthesis of this case is *evidence* (*Evidenz*) or knowledge in the pregnant sense; here being in the sense of *truth*, the rightly understood "agreement," the *adaequatio rei ac intellectus*, is realized — here it is itself given, directly to be beheld and grasped. The various concepts of *truth* constituted on the basis of one and the same phenomenological state of affairs find here their complete clarification, and the analogous holds for the correlate ideal of imperfection, the case of *absurdity* (*Absurdität*), in respect of "conflict" and of the non-being experienced therein, *untruth*.

Husserl previews the Second Section. The natural course of the inquiry — originally interested only in meaning-intentions — brings it about that all these considerations first take the simplest meanings as their point of departure and thus abstract from the *form-differences* of meanings. The supplementary inquiry of the Second Section, which takes these differences into account, leads us at once to a wholly new *concept of matter*, namely to the fundamental opposition of *sensuous stuff* (*sinnlicher Stoff*) and *categorial form* (*kategoriale Form*) — or, exchanging the objective for the phenomenological standpoint, between *sensuous* and *categorial acts*. Closely connected is the important distinction between sensuous (real) and categorial objects, determinations, connections; whereby it proves characteristic of the categorial that, in the manner of "perception," they can be "given" only in acts which are *founded* (*fundiert*) in other acts, ultimately in acts of sensibility. The intuitive — hence also the imaginative — fulfilment of categorial acts is in general founded in sensuous acts. Never, however, can mere sensibility offer fulfilment to categorially-formed intentions; rather, the fulfilment lies always in a sensibility formed by categorial acts. With this hangs together an indispensable *extension* of the originally sensuous concepts *intuition* and *perception*, permitting us to speak of *categorial* and especially of *universal* intuition (*allgemeine Anschauung*). The distinction between sensuous and purely categorial abstraction then conditions the distinction of general concepts into sensuous concepts and categories. The old epistemological opposition of *sensibility* and *understanding* finds, through the distinction between simple (sensuous) and founded (categorial) intuition, all desired clarity; likewise the opposition of *thinking* and *intuiting*, which in philosophical usage confuses the relations of signification and fulfilling intuition with the relations of sensuous and categorial acts.

Husserl concludes the sketch of the body of the Investigation. We close the main stock of the inquiry with a consideration of the *limits* that hem in the freedom of the actual categorial forming of a stuff. We become aware of the *analytic laws of genuine* (*eigentliches*) *thinking*, which, grounded in the pure categories, are independent of all particularity of the stuffs. Parallel limits delimit *improper* (*uneigentliches*) *thinking*, i.e., mere signification, insofar as it is to be capable of expression in the proper sense — *a priori* and independently of the stuffs to be expressed. From this requirement springs the function of the genuine laws of thought as norms of mere signification. (In a footnote Husserl records that, according to the "Additions and Improvements" to the A-edition, the text is to be supplemented to include also the adequacy of the mixed — partly purely intuitive, partly signitive — presenting of the various categorial degrees, and the norms of signitively clouded presenting in general; this marks the maturing of his doctrine of the strata of clear and obscure thinking.)

Husserl previews the Third Section. The question raised at the outset — the natural delimitation of the sense-giving and sense-fulfilling acts — has been settled by their ordering into the class of objectifying acts and by the division of objectifying acts into *significative* and *intuitive*. Only the clarification, accomplished in the whole of the inquiry, of the phenomenological relations concerning fulfilment puts us in a position to weigh critically the arguments that speak for and against the *Aristotelian* conception of wish- and command-sentences and the like as *predications*. The full elucidation of this controversy is the task to which the closing section of the present Investigation is devoted. This signposts the polemical engagement with Brentano and the Aristotelian tradition that occupies Chapter Nine.

In a closing reflection set in smaller print, Husserl frames the limits of his ambition. The goals just described are not the last and highest of a phenomenological clarification of knowledge überhaupt. The so exceedingly fruitful field of *mediate* thinking and knowing the analyses leave, comprehensive as they are, almost wholly unworked; the essence of mediate evidence and its ideal correlates remains without sufficient clarification. Nonetheless he believes he has not aimed at too little: he hopes to have laid bare the lowest and, by their nature, first foundations of the critique of knowledge. In the critique of knowledge, too, one must practice that self-limitation (*Selbstbescheidung*) which lies in the essence of all strictly scientific research. If its aim is directed at the actual and definitive settling of the matters themselves — if it no longer deceives itself that the great problems of knowledge can be solved by mere critique of handed-down philosophemes and by probable reasoning, and if it is finally conscious that the matters can be moved and shaped only in hands-on work — then it must also resign itself to grasping the problems of knowledge first not in their higher and highest forms, in which they most interest us, but in their relatively simplest forms, at the lowest of the levels of formation accessible to it. That a research thus self-limiting still has an over-rich measure of difficulties to overcome — indeed has almost everything still to accomplish — the analyses now following will prove.

# **First Section: The Objectifying Intentions and Their Fulfilments. Knowledge as a Synthesis of Fulfilment, and Its Degrees**

## **Chapter One: Meaning-Intention and Meaning-Fulfilment**

### **§ 1\. Whether all or only certain kinds of acts can function as bearers of meaning**

Husserl ties back to the question raised in the Introduction: whether meaning (*Bedeuten*) is carried out only in acts of certain restricted genera. At first it might seem entirely self-evident that no such restrictions exist and that every act whatever can function as sense-giving. After all, we can bring acts *of every kind* — presentations, judgments, surmises, questions, wishes, and so on — *to expression* (*zum Ausdruck bringen*); and in doing so they furnish us with the meanings of the corresponding speech-forms: of names, of statements, of question- and wish-sentences. This is the prima facie egalitarian thesis: any mental act, simply by being expressible, would seem already to be a possible meaning-bearer.

Husserl then shows that the *opposite* conception can equally claim self-evidence — specifically the view that all meanings are restricted to a narrowly bounded class of acts. Granted, every act is expressible; but it finds its respective expression in a speech-form specially adapted to it (in a sufficiently developed language): among sentences we have the differences of statement-sentences, question-sentences, command-sentences, and among the first again the categorical, hypothetical, disjunctive sentences. In any case the act, in coming to expression in this or that speech-form, must be recognized in its kind-determinacy — the question as question, the wish as wish, the judgment as judgment — and this extends to the building-up part-acts insofar as the expression measures itself to them. The acts could not find the forms suited to them without being apperceived and recognized according to form and content. Hence the expressing of speech lies not in mere words but in *expressing acts* (*ausdrückende Akte*); these stamp the correlative acts to be expressed in a *new stuff* (*neuer Stoff*), they create of them a *conceptual expression* (*gedanklicher Ausdruck*) whose general essence makes up the meaning of the discourse in question. On this view, then, expressing is itself a higher act laid over the act expressed.

A telling confirmation of this conception seems to lie in the possibility of the purely *symbolic* function of expressions. The spiritual expression — that conceptual counter-image (*gedankliches Gegenbild*) of the act to be expressed — adheres to the linguistic expression and can come alive with it even when that act itself is not performed by the one who understands. We understand the expression of a perception without ourselves perceiving, the expression of a question without ourselves questioning, and so on. We have not the mere words but also the conceptual forms or expressions. In the contrary case, where the intended acts are actually present, the expression comes to *coincidence* (*Deckung*) with what is to be expressed: the meaning adhering to the words fits itself to what it means, its conceptual intention finds therein the fulfilling intuition. Husserl here already deploys the central pair of empty meaning-intention and intuitive coincidence that the whole Investigation will analyze.

In manifestly intimate connection with these opposed conceptions stands the old dispute whether the peculiar forms of question-, wish-, command-sentences and the like may count as *statements* (*Aussagen*) and their meanings accordingly as *judgments* (*Urteile*) or not. According to the *Aristotelian* doctrine, the meaning of all independently closed sentences lies in psychic experiences of various kinds — experiences of judging, wishing, commanding, and so forth. According to the other doctrine, increasingly widespread in recent times, meaning is carried out exclusively in judgments or their presentational modifications. On this view a question is indeed in a certain sense expressed in the question-sentence, but only by the question being apprehended as a question, set up in this conceptual grasp as the speaker's experience, and thus judged as his experience. So everywhere: every meaning is, on this doctrine, either nominal or propositional meaning — or, as we may better say, each is either the meaning of a whole statement-sentence or a possible part of such a meaning. Statement-sentences here are predicative sentences; for on this side judgment is generally understood as *predicating act* (*prädizierender Akt*), though, as we shall hear, the dispute keeps its sense even when judgment is understood as *positing act in general* (*setzender Akt überhaupt*).

To find the right position on the questions raised will require more exact considerations than those undertaken in the foregoing, nearest-lying argumentations. It will be shown that what is set up on the one and the other side as self-evident proves, on closer examination, to be unclear and even erroneous. Husserl thus refuses both the egalitarian and the judgment-monopoly views as premature, signaling that the truth lies in a more finely articulated theory of objectifying acts.

### **§ 2\. The expressibility of all acts is not decisive. Two meanings of talk about the expressing of an act**

All acts, we were just told, are *expressible*. This is of course beyond doubt, but it does not contain what one would like to slip in under it, namely that all acts can therefore also stand in the function of *bearers of meaning*. Talk of expressing is, as discussed earlier, manifold, and remains so when we refer it to the *acts* to be expressed. As "expressed" one may designate the meaning-conferring acts, the acts "intimated" (*kundgegeben*) in the narrower sense. But still other acts can, in another sense, be called expressed. Husserl means the very ordinary cases where we *name* acts *we are just experiencing* and, by means of the naming, *state* that we are experiencing them. In this sense I give expression to a wish in the form *I wish that...*, to a question in the form *I ask whether...*, to a judgment in the form *I judge that...*, and so forth. This second sense already separates expressing-as-meaning from expressing-as-reporting.

Obviously we can judge just as well about our own inner experiences as about external things; and when we do, the meanings of the relevant sentences lie in the *judgments* about these experiences and not in the experiences themselves — the wishes, questions, and the like. Exactly so, the meanings of statements about external things lie not in those things (the horses, houses, etc.) but in the judgments we inwardly pass about them, or in the presentations that help build up these judgments. That the judged objects are in one case transcendent to consciousness (or claim to be) and in the other immanent makes here no essential difference. To be sure, the wish that fills me is, in my pronouncing it, concretely one with the judgment-act; but it does not properly contribute to the judgment. The wish is apprehended in an act of reflective perception, subsumed under the concept *wish*, and named by means of this concept and the determining presentation of the wish-content; and so it is directly the conceptual *presentation of the wish* that contributes to the judgment about the wish, and the corresponding *wish-name* its contribution to the wish-statement — just as the presentation of man contributes to the judgment about man. If in the sentence *I wish that...* we substitute the relevant proper name for the subject-word *I*, the sense of the sentence in its unmodified parts certainly does not suffer; yet it is unmistakable that the wish-statement can now be understood and judgingly re-experienced *in identical sense* by a hearer who does not share the wish at all. One sees from this that the wish, even where it happens to be one with the judgment-act directed upon it, does not really belong to the *judgment-meaning*. A truly sense-giving experience can never drop away if the living sense of the expression is to remain unchanged.

Accordingly it is also clear that the expressibility of all acts is *irrelevant* to the question whether they can all also function in the manner of sense-givers — provided, that is, that by this expressibility nothing more is understood than the possibility of making certain statements *about* the acts. Precisely then the acts do not function as bearers of meaning at all. The reporting sense of "expression" is thus disqualified from settling the dispute.

### **§ 3\. A third sense of talk about the expression of an act. Formulation of our theme**

We have just distinguished a twofold concept of talk about expressed acts. Either acts are meant in which the sense, the meaning, of the relevant expression *constitutes itself*; or, on the other hand, acts which the speaker wants to set up predicatively as just experienced by him. This latter concept we may think of as suitably extended. Plainly the state of affairs it grasps is, in what essentially comes into consideration here, the same when the expressed act is referred predicatively not to the experiencing I but to other objects; and it is again the same for all expression-forms that *name* this act as experienced without doing so precisely in the manner that stamps it as subject- or object-member of a predication. The main thing is that the act, in being named or otherwise "expressed," appears as the actually present *object of discourse* — or of the objectifying positing underlying it — whereas in the case of the sense-giving acts this is *not* the case.

In a *third* sense of the same talk, it is a matter, as in the second, of a judging or other objectifying belonging to the acts in question; but not of a judging *about* these acts — hence not of an objectification of them by means of presentations and namings *referred to them* — but of a judging *on the ground of* these acts, which does not require their objectification. For example, that I give expression to my *perception* can mean that I predicate of my perception that it has this or that content. But it can also mean that I draw my judgment *from* the perception, that I do not merely assert the relevant fact but perceive it and assert it as I perceive it. Not about the perception, but about the *perceived* (*das Wahrgenommene*), the judgment is here passed. Where one speaks offhand of *perception-judgments* (*Wahrnehmungsurteile*), judgments of this just-characterized class are as a rule meant. This third sense — judging on the basis of intuition — is the one that opens the real problem.

In a similar way we can give expression to other intuitive acts: imaginations, memories, expectations. Husserl extends the third sense across the whole range of intuitive presentations, not only perception.

In statements on the ground of *imagination* it is admittedly doubtful whether a real judgment is present there — or rather it is certain that none is then present. Husserl thinks here of the cases where, given over to a pull of fantasy, we name what thereby appears to us in regular statements as though it were perceived; or of the form of reporting narration in which the writer of fairy tales, the novelist, and so on, "gives expression" not to actual events but to the formations of his artistic fantasy. According to the discussions of the last (Fifth) Investigation, these are *conformally modified acts* (*konform modifizierte Akte*) which correspond to the actual judgments expressible in the same words as *counterparts* (*Gegenstücke*), in a way similar to that in which the intuitive imaginations correspond to perceptions (and possibly to memories and expectations). For the time being Husserl wants to leave such distinctions out of account.

Attaching to the designated class of cases and to the new sense of talk about expressed acts delimited by it, Husserl wants to bring to clarity the *relation between meaning and expressed intuition*. We are to consider whether this intuition is itself the act constituting the meaning, and, if not, how the relation of the two is otherwise to be understood and ordered generically. In so doing we steer above all toward the more general question whether the acts that *give* expression and the acts that can *undergo* expression move in the spheres of essentially different yet firmly determinate kinds of acts, and whether in all this an overarching *generic unity* (*Gattungseinheit*) is decisive — one that would comprise and close off the totality of acts capable of a *meaning-function in the wider sense* (whether of the meaning-function itself or of "meaning-fulfilment"), so that acts of all other genera would *eo ipso* and lawfully remain excluded from such functions. With this our next goal is designated. As the considerations advance, the self-evident extension of the sphere of consideration will make evident the significance of these questions for an understanding of knowledge überhaupt, and new and higher goals will soon enter our field of vision.

### **§ 4\. The expression of a perception ("perception-judgment"). Its meaning cannot lie in the perception but must lie in proper expressing acts**

Husserl examines an example. He looks out into the garden and gives expression to his perception with the words: *a blackbird flies up*. *Which is here the act in which the meaning lies?* In agreement with the First Investigation, he holds that it is *not* the perception, at least not the perception alone. The situation cannot be described as though, besides the wording, nothing further were given and decisive for the significance of the expression than the perception to which it attaches. For on the ground of *this same perception* the statement could read quite otherwise and unfold a quite *different sense*: he could have said *this is black, is a black bird; this black animal flies up, soars up*, and the like. And conversely, the wording and its *sense* could remain *the same* while the perception varies manifoldly. Every chance change in the perceiver's relative position alters the perception itself, and different persons perceiving the same thing at once never have exactly the same perception. For the meaning of the perception-statement, differences of this kind are irrelevant; they can of course occasionally be aimed at, but then the statement would have to read quite otherwise.

One might now say that the objection only proves the meaning to be *insensible* to such differences of the *individual* perceptions; it lies precisely in something *common* that the manifold perception-acts belonging to one object all carry within themselves. Husserl states this rejoinder fairly before refuting it.

Against this Husserl notes that the perception can not only vary but also drop away *entirely* without the expression ceasing to remain significant. The hearer understands my words and the whole sentence without looking into the garden; trusting my truthfulness, he produces the same judgment without the perception. Perhaps a certain imaging through fantasy serves him, perhaps even this is lacking — or it is so lacunary, so inadequate, that it cannot even count as a counter-image of the perceptual appearance according to the features "expressed" in the statement.

If, on the dropping-away of the perception, a sense — and even *the same* sense as before — remains over for the statement, then we cannot assume that the perception is the act in which the sense of the perception-statement, its expressing meaning, is carried out. The acts united with the wording — according as the latter is significant purely symbolically or intuitively, on the ground of mere fantasy or of realizing perception — are phenomenologically too different for us to believe that the meaning plays itself out now in those, now in these acts. We shall have to prefer a conception that assigns this function of meaning to an act *everywhere of like kind* — one free from the limits of perception (so often denied us) and even of fantasy, and which, where the expression "expresses" in the proper sense, only unites itself with the expressed act. (Husserl appends a footnote setting aside, here in this section, the categorial forms, which are deliberately ignored.)

Despite all this it is undeniable that in the "perception-judgments" the perception stands in an *inner relation* to the sense of the statement. Not for nothing does one say: the statement expresses the *perception*, or expresses what is "given" in the perception. The same perception may underlie different statements, but however the sense of these statements may vary, it nonetheless "directs" itself according to the appearance-content of the perception; it is now these, now those partial perceptions (perhaps non-self-sufficient parts of the unified and full perceptions) that offer the judgment its special substratum, without their being for that reason the proper bearers of meaning — as precisely the possibility of the dropping-away of all perception taught.

One will therefore have to say: this "expressing" of a perception (or, objectively turned, of a perceived as such) is *not a matter of the wording* but a matter of *certain expressing acts*. "Expression" here means the expression enlivened by its whole sense, which is set in a certain relation to the perception — the perception being, for the sake of just this relation, called "expressed." At the same time there lies therein that *between* perception and wording *an act* (or act-formation) is *inserted*. Husserl says "an act," for the expression-experience has, whether accompanied by perception or not, an intentional relation to something objective. This mediating act must be the one that properly serves as sense-giver; it belongs to the meaningfully functioning expression as its essential component, and it conditions that the sense is identically the same whether or not a corroborating perception may join it.

The feasibility of this conception, Husserl says, the following inquiry will continually confirm.

### **§ 5\. Continuation. Perception as the act determining but not as the act containing meaning**

We may not proceed without weighing a near-lying doubt. Our presentation seems to require a certain restriction; it seems to contain more than we can fully justify. Even if perception never makes up the *full* meaning of a statement carried out on the ground of perception, it nonetheless contributes *something* to the meaning — precisely in cases of the class just discussed. This emerges more clearly if we first modify the example and speak not quite indeterminately of *a* blackbird but of *this* one. *This* is an essentially *occasional* expression which becomes fully significant only by regard to the circumstances of the utterance, and here to the perception carried out. The perceived object, just as it is given in the perception, is meant by the *this*. Moreover, the present tense in the grammatical form of the verb also expresses a relation to the actual present, hence again to the perception. Obviously the same holds of the unmodified example; for whoever says "*a* blackbird flies up" means not that a blackbird in general, but that a blackbird now and here, flies up.

Admittedly the intended meaning does not hang on the wording; it does not belong to the meanings generally and firmly bound by it. But since we cannot abandon the point that the sense of the unified statement lies in the *whole act of meaning* underlying it — whether or not it stamps itself fully in the words by virtue of their general meanings — we shall, it seems, have to concede that perception, where it brings to intuition the state of affairs which the statement judgingly expresses, contributes to the meaning-content of this judgment. It is, however, a contribution that can possibly be made by *other* acts in essentially agreeing fashion. The hearer does not perceive the garden, but perhaps knows it, presents it intuitively, transposes the presented blackbird and the stated event into it, and so produces, following the speaker's intention, by means of mere fantasy-imagery, an understanding of like sense.

But the situation admits also of a *second* interpretation. In a certain sense it can be said that intuition contributes to the meaning of the perception-statement: in the sense, namely, that the meaning could not unfold its *determinate* reference to the meant objectivity without the succour of intuition. On the other hand, it is not thereby said that the act of intuition *itself* is bearer of meaning, or that it furnishes *contributions* to the meaning in the proper sense — contributions that could then be *found* as components in the finished meaning. The essentially occasional expressions have indeed a meaning that varies from case to case; but through all variation a common element remains over, distinguishing such multivocity from that of chance equivocation. The accession of intuition has the effect that this common but, in its abstractness, indeterminate element of the meaning becomes *determined*. Intuition gives it the determinacy of the *objective direction* and thereby its last difference. This achievement does not require that a part of the meaning itself must lie *in* the intuition.

Husserl illustrates: *I say "this" and mean just now the paper lying before me.* The reference to *this* object the little word owes to the perception. But the meaning does not lie in the perception itself. When I say this, I do not merely perceive; rather, *on the ground of* the perception there builds itself up a *new act, directing itself according to it, dependent in its difference upon it* — the *act of this-meaning* (*Dies-Meinen*). *In this pointing meaning lies, and lies wholly alone, the meaning.* Without the perception — or a correspondingly functioning act — the pointing would be empty, without determinate differentiation, in concreto not possible at all. For of course the indeterminate thought that the speaker is pointing at "something" (which may set in for the hearer while he has not yet recognized which object we wanted to indicate with the *this*) is by no means the thought we ourselves carried out in the actual pointing — as though for us only the determinate presentation of the indicated were to join it. One must not confuse the *general character of actual pointing as such* with the *indeterminate presentation of a certain pointing*.

Perception thus *realizes the possibility* for the unfolding of the this-meaning with its determinate relation to the object, e.g., to this paper before my eyes; but it does not, it seems to us, itself *constitute* the meaning, not even in part.

As the act-character of the pointing directs itself according to the intuition, it takes on a determinacy of intention which fulfils itself in the intuition according to a *general* content, to be characterized as the *intentional essence*. For the pointing meaning is the same, whichever perception out of the manifold of mutually belonging perceptions may underlie it — perceptions in which the same, and *recognizably* the same, object appears. The meaning of the *this* is once more the same when, for the perception, any act from the manifold of imaginative presentations enters which presents in recognizably identical fashion the same object in image. It changes, however, when intuitions from other perception- or image-circles are supposed. We mean again *this*, but the common character of the meaning here prevailing — namely the directly (without any attributive mediation) aiming at the object — is differently differentiated; there now adheres to it an intention upon another object, similarly as physical pointing differentiates itself spatially with the change of spatial direction.

A confirmation of this conception — which lets perception count as the act *determining* but not as the act *containing* meaning — is offered by the circumstance that even essentially occasional expressions of the kind of *this* are widely used and understood without an adequate intuitive substratum. The intention upon the object, once conceived on the ground of fitting intuition, can be repeated or re-produced in like tenor without any adequate perception or imagination mediating.

The essentially occasional expressions would accordingly be closely akin to *proper names*, insofar as the latter function in their proper meaning. For the proper name too names the object "directly." It means it not in attributive fashion as bearer of this or that mark, but without such "conceptual" mediation, as the one it "itself" is, just as perception would set it before our eyes. The meaning of the proper name lies, then, in a directly-meaning-this-object — a meaning that *fulfils* itself merely through perception and, in "preliminary" (illustrating) fashion through imagination, but is not identical with these intuitive acts. Just so, perception *gives* the *this* (where it is directed upon objects of possible perception) the object; the this-meaning fulfils itself in the perception and is not it itself. And of course on both sides the meaning of these directly naming expressions originally grows out of the intuition by which the nominal intentions orient their direction upon the individual object. On other points there is a difference: to the *this* there adheres the thought of a *pointing*, which brings in a certain mediacy and complication, hence a certain form, absent in the proper name; while the proper name belongs as a fixed denomination to its object, a constant belonging that is attested by the fact of *naming-recognition* — I recognize Hans as Hans, Berlin as Berlin. This account, Husserl adds, disregards proper names standing in *derived* meaning-function: once any proper names have been formed in direct linkage to given objects (on the ground of giving intuitions), the concept of *being-called* (formed in reflection on proper-naming) can serve to assign proper names to objects that are not given and directly known to us but only indirectly characterized as bearers of certain marks — e.g., the capital of Spain is called (has the proper name) *Madrid*. Whoever does not know the city Madrid "itself" gains thereby knowledge of its name and the possibility of using it appropriately, and yet not the proper meaning of the word *Madrid*; in place of the direct meaning, which only the intuition of this city could excite, the indirect indication of such meaning serves him — mediated by characteristic mark-presentations and the concept of being-so-called.

If we may grant trust to these considerations, then not only is perception in general to be distinguished from the meaning of the perception-statement, but also *no part* of this meaning lies in the perception itself. *The* perception, which gives the object, and the *statement, which thinks and expresses* it by means of the judgment (or of the "thought-acts" interwoven into the unity of the judgment), *are to be completely separated*, although in the present case of the perception-judgment they stand in the most intimate mutual relation, in the relation of *coincidence* (*Deckung*), of fulfilment-unity.

It scarcely needs elaboration, Husserl says, that the same result will also hold for all other *intuition-judgments* — hence for statements which, in a sense analogous to that of the perception-judgments, "express" the intuitive content of an imagination, a memory, an expectation, and so on.

**Zusatz (Addition),** Husserl appends an addition correlating this analysis with the First Investigation. In the presentation of § 26 of Investigation I we distinguished — starting from the hearer's understanding — the "indicating" (*anzeigende*) and "indicated" (*angezeigte*) meaning of the essentially occasional expression, and especially of the *this*. In the hearer, into whose momentary field of vision the thing to be indicated may not fall at all, at first only the indeterminately general thought is awakened that something is being pointed at; only with the supplementing presentation (an intuitive one, if it is an intuitively-to-be-indicated thing) does the determinacy of the pointing, and so the full and proper meaning of the demonstrative, constitute itself for him. For the *speaker* the succession does not exist; he does not need the indeterminately pointing presentation that functioned in the hearer as "indication." Not the presentation of the pointing, but the pointing itself, is given in him, and it is *eo ipso* the factually determinately directed one; from the outset the speaker has the "indicated" meaning, and has it in the immediate presentation-intention orienting itself according to the intuition.

**Zusatz,** If the thing is not an intuitively findable one — as in the back-reference to a theorem in a mathematical demonstration — then the relevant conceptual thought takes over the function of intuition: the pointing intention would find its fulfilment on the ground of the actual re-establishment of that elapsed thought. In every case we ascertain a certain *doubleness* in the pointing intention: the character of pointing weds itself, in the first case, with the direct objective intention, in such a way that thereby the pointing upon the determinate, here-and-now intuited object grows. So too in the other case: even if the earlier conceptual thought is not just now actually carried out, there nonetheless remains in memory a corresponding intention, and this combines with the act-character of the pointing, lending it the determinacy of direction.

**Zusatz,** When, therefore, one speaks of *indicating* and *indicated* meaning, two things may be meant. (1) The two mutually relieving thoughts that characterize the hearer's successive understanding: *first* the indeterminate presentation of a certain thing meant with the *this*, *then* the modification forming itself through the supplementing presentation, the act of determinately directed pointing. In the latter act would lie the indicated, in the former the indicating meaning. (2) Holding to the finished, determinately directed pointing, given in the speaker from the outset, a duality can again be distinguished within it: the general character of the pointing and what determines it, what restricts it to the pointing upon *this here*. The former can again be designated as indicating meaning, or better as *the indicating element* in the inseparably unitary meaning, insofar as it is what the hearer, by virtue of its expressible generality, can immediately grasp and what can serve him as the indication of the meant. If I say *this*, the hearer at least knows that something is being pointed at. (Likewise with other essentially occasional expressions: if I say *here*, it is a matter of "something" in my nearer or farther spatial surroundings, etc.) On the other hand, the proper aim of the discourse lies not in this general element but in the direct intention upon the object in question; it is aimed at it and its content-fullness, and to their determination those empty generalities contribute nothing or as good as nothing. In this sense the direct intention is the primary and indicated meaning.

**Zusatz,** The second distinction was laid at the basis of the definition in the earlier presentation (of Investigation I); the distinction and closer elaboration here carried out should, Husserl says, have contributed to a further clarification of the difficult state of affairs.

### **§ 6\. The static unity between expressing thought and expressed intuition. Knowledge (*Erkennen*)**

Husserl now deepens the inquiry into the relations prevailing between intuitive acts on the one side and expressing acts on the other. For the time being — and in this section überhaupt — he restricts himself to a circle of cases as simple as possible: naturally, to expressions or meaning-intentions drawn from the *nominal* domain. He raises no claim thereby to encompass the whole domain. It is a matter of nominal expressions which relate in the most transparent possible way to "corresponding" perception and other intuition.

Within this circle Husserl first takes into view the *resting unity-relation* (*das ruhende Einheitsverhältnis*): the meaning-conferring thought is *grounded on intuition and thereby referred to its object*. For example, I speak of *my inkpot*, and at the same time the inkpot itself stands before me, I see it. The name names the object of perception, and names it by means of the meaning act, which according to its kind and form stamps itself in the form of the name. The relation between name and named shows, in this state of unity, a certain *descriptive character* of which we became aware earlier: the name *my inkpot* "lays itself," as it were, "onto" the perceived object, belongs, so to speak, *palpably* to it.

But this belonging is of a peculiar kind. The words do not belong to the objective context — here the physical-thingly one — that they express; in it they have no place, they are not meant as something in or on the things they name. Going back to the experiences, we find on the one side, as already described, the acts of word-appearance, on the other side the similar acts of thing-appearance. In the latter respect, in perception the inkpot stands over against us. According with our repeated assertion of the descriptive essence of perception, this means phenomenologically nothing other than that we have a certain run of experiences from the class *sensation*, sensibly unified in their so-and-so-determinate sequence and ensouled (*durchgeistigt*) by a certain act-character of "apprehension" (*Auffassung*) conferring objective sense on them. This act-character makes it that an *object* — precisely this inkpot — appears to us in the manner of perception. And in similar fashion the appearing word constitutes itself in an act of perception or fantasy-presentation.

Hence not word and inkpot, but the described act-experiences in which they appear (while they are nothing at all "in" them), enter into relation. But how is this so? What brings the acts to unity? The answer seems clear. This relation, *as naming*, is mediated by acts not merely of meaning but of *knowing/recognizing* (*Erkennen*) — and here, indeed, acts of *classification* (*Klassifikation*). The perceived object is *recognized* as inkpot, and insofar as the meaning expression is in a particularly intimate way one with the classificatory act, and this again, as recognition of the perceived object, is one with the perception-act, the expression appears as though laid upon the thing and as if its garment.

Normally we speak of knowledge and classification of the *perception-object*, as though the act *operated on objects*. But in the experience itself, we said, there is no object, only the perception, the so-and-so-determinate state of mind; hence the *knowledge-act in the experience is grounded on the perception-act*. One must not, in misunderstanding, object that we set things up as if the perception were classified instead of its object. We do nothing of the kind. Such a thing would presuppose acts of quite other and more complicated constitution, which would stamp themselves in expressions of corresponding complexity, such as *the perception of the inkpot*. Hence a knowing that fuses, in determinate and simple fashion, the expression-experience on the one side with the relevant perception on the other constitutes the experience: the recognizing of this thing as my inkpot.

Exactly so it stands in the cases where, instead of perception, an *image-presentation* (*Bildvorstellung*) serves. The pictorially appearing object — e.g., the same inkpot in fantasy or memory — is the palpable bearer of the nominal expression. Phenomenologically speaking, this means that an act of knowing, united with the expression-experience, is referred to the act of imaging in the manner that we objectively designate as recognition of the pictorially presented, e.g., as recognition of our inkpot. The pictorial object too is, in the presentation, simply nothing; the experience is rather a certain assemblage of phantasms (fantasy-sensations) ensouled by a certain apprehending act-character. To live this act and to have a fantasy-presentation of the object is one and the same. If we then say, expressingly, *I have a fantasy-image, and indeed that of an inkpot*, we have obviously, with the expressing, at the same time carried out *new* acts — and specifically also an act of knowing intimately unified with the act of imaging.

### **§ 7\. Knowledge (*Erkennen*) as act-character and the "universality of the word"**

That we are really justified in assuming, in all cases of the naming of an intuitively given thing, the *knowing* as a mediating act-character between the appearance of the wording (or of the whole sense-enlivened word) and the thing-intuition — of this, Husserl says, the following more exact consideration seems wholly to assure us. One often hears talk of the *universality of word-meanings* and means in this multivocal phrase mostly the fact that the word is not bound to the individuated intuition but belongs to an infinite manifold of possible intuitions.

What, then, lies in this belonging? (A question stated in its own short paragraph, marking a deliberate analytic pause.)

Husserl considers an example as simple as possible, say the name *Red* (*Rot*). In naming an appearing object as red, it belongs to this object by virtue of the red-moment appearing on it. And every object that carries a like moment in itself warrants the same naming; to each *belongs* this same name, and it belongs to it by virtue of the *identical sense*.

What, again, lies in this naming by virtue of an *identical sense*? (Another short question-paragraph, isolating the crux.)

We remark first: the word does not hang externally, merely on the ground of hidden psychic mechanisms, on the like individual features of the intuitions. Above all we do not get by with the mere fact that, wherever such an individual feature occurs in the intuition, the word as mere sound-formation now *joins* it. The mere togetherness, the merely external with-one-another or alongside-one-another of these two appearances creates between them no inner relation and certainly no intentional one. And such a one is manifestly present as a *phenomenologically thoroughly peculiar* relation. The word *names* the red *as* red. The appearing red is what is *meant* with the name, and indeed what is meant *as* red. In this manner of naming meaning the name appears as *belonging* to the named and one with it.

On the other hand, the word has its sense also *outside* the linkage with this intuition — indeed without linkage with any "corresponding" intuition at all. Since the sense is everywhere the same, it is clear that for the naming relation we must lay at the basis, in place of the *mere wording*, the proper and full word, namely the one endowed with the everywhere-alike character of *sense*. But even then we may not content ourselves with describing the unity of the meaningful word and the corresponding intuition as a mere *togetherness*. If we think of the *word* — as it is conscious, say, outside all actual naming, as merely symbolically understood — and now *the corresponding intuition*: then it may be that the two appearances close together, from genetic grounds, at once into the phenomenological unity of naming; but *in itself* the togetherness is not yet this unity, which first *grows* as something manifestly *new*. It would be conceivable *a priori* that it did not grow; then the coexisting appearances would be phenomenologically relationless: the appearing would not stand there as the *meant*, hence named, in the meaningful word, and the word not as the one belonging to it, naming it, in the manner of the name.

Since we now find phenomenologically, in place of the mere sum, the most intimate unity — and indeed an *intentional* unity — we may rightly say: the two acts, of which one constitutes for us the full word and the other the thing, close together intentionally into an *act-unity* (*Akteinheit*). Naturally we describe what lies before us just as well with the words *the name Red names the red object red* as with the words *the red object is recognized as red and named red by means of this recognition*. *Red-naming* — in the *actual* sense of naming, which presupposes the underlying intuition of the named — and *as-red-recognizing* are at bottom *meaning-identical* expressions; only that the latter brings more clearly to stamp that here no mere duality but a unity established by an act-character is given. Given the intimacy of the fusion, the implicated moments of this unity — the physical word-appearance with the animating moment of meaning, the moment of recognition, and the intuition of the named — do not, as we must admit, fall apart with distinct relief; but according to what has been set forth we shall have to assume them all. Supplementary considerations will be devoted to this point.

Manifestly, the act-character of knowing, to which the word owes its sense-fitting relation to the objective element of the intuition, is nothing essentially belonging to the *wording*; rather it belongs to the *word* according to its meaningful essence. With the most various wordings — think of "the same" word in different languages — the knowledge-relation can be identically the same; the object is essentially recognized as the same, though with the aid of different wordings. To be sure, the full *as-red-recognizing*, insofar as it is equivalent with the actual name, also includes the wording. Members of different language-communities experience the belongings of different wordings and comprise these latter too into the unity of the recognition. Nevertheless the meaning belonging to the wording, and the recognition-act in which it actually unites itself with the meant, are preserved everywhere unchanged, so that the differences must count as self-evidently *extra-essential* (*außerwesentlich*).

The *universality of the word* accordingly says that one and the same word, through its unitary sense, so spans an ideally fixed manifold of possible intuitions (and, where it is absurd, "pretends" to span it) that each of these intuitions can function as the basis of a like-sensed nominal knowledge-act. To the word *Red*, for example, belongs the possibility of recognizing and naming, precisely as red, all red objects given in possible intuitions. To this is joined, further, the *a priori* guaranteed possibility of becoming conscious, through identifying synthesis of such recognitions, that the one and the other are meaning-wise the same — that this A red and that A are the same, namely also red; the two singulars of the intuition belong under the same "concept."

A doubt-question forces itself here. The word, we said above, could be understood without actually naming anything. But must we not grant it at least the *possibility* of standing in the function of actual naming — hence of gaining an actual knowledge-relation to corresponding intuition? Must we not say: without this possibility it would be no word at all? Naturally the answer runs: this possibility hangs on the possibility of the relevant recognitions.

But not every intended knowledge is possible, not every nominal meaning is to be *realized*. "Imaginary" names are also names, but they can stand in no *actual* naming; they have, properly speaking, no extension, they have *no universality in the sense of possibility and truth*. Their universality is *empty pretension* (*leere Prätention*). How these phrases are in turn to be clarified — what lies phenomenologically behind them — the course of the further inquiry will yet set forth. (Husserl here flags the problem of objectless, absurd meanings that the later treatment of possibility and impossibility will address.)

What we have set forth holds *everywhere* and not merely with expressions that have a general meaning in the manner of *general concepts*. It holds also with expressions of *individual* meaning, as the proper names are. The fact one is wont to designate as the "universality of word-meaning" by no means means the universality one attributes to generic concepts in opposition to individual concepts; on the contrary, it comprehends the one and the other in like manner. Accordingly the "knowing" of which we speak in the relation of a meaningfully functioning expression to corresponding intuition is also not to be conceived precisely as an actual *classifying* — one that is carried out in the ordering of an intuitively presented object into a class (hence necessarily on the ground of general concepts and linguistically by means of general names). Even the proper names have their "universality," although with them, where they stand in the function of actual naming, there is *eo ipso* no talk of classification. The proper names too, like all other names, can name nothing without recognizing in naming.

The respective name belongs, manifestly, neither to a *determinate* perception nor to a *determinate* imagining or other imaging. In countless possible intuitions the same person comes to appearance, and all these appearances have not merely *intuitive* but also *knowledge-wise* unity. Each single appearance out of such an intuitive manifold can with equal right underlie the like-sensed naming by the proper name. Whichever is given, the namer means the one and the same person or thing. And he means it not in the mere manner of intuitive turned-toward-ness, as in the contemplation of an object individually foreign to him, but he *recognizes* it as this determinate person or thing; in naming he recognizes Hans as Hans, Berlin as Berlin. The *as-this-person, as-this-city recognizing* is again an act not bound to the determinate sensible content of the respective word-appearance. It is identically the same act with different (and possibly infinitely many) wordings — as, e.g., when several persons use different proper names for the same individual thing.

Naturally this universality of the proper name and of the proper meaning corresponding to it is of *quite other character* than that of the class-name. (A short transitional paragraph distinguishing the two.)

The former consists in this: that to *one* individual object there belongs a synthesis of possible intuitions which are one through a common *intentional character* — namely through the character that, notwithstanding the other phenomenal differences between the single intuitions, lends to each one a relation to the same object. This unitary element is then the *foundation* for the *knowledge-unity* (*Erkenntniseinheit*) that belongs to the "universality of word-meaning," to the extent of its ideally possible realization. Thus the naming word has a knowledge-relation to an unbounded manifold of intuitions whose one and the same object it recognizes and thereby names.

*Quite otherwise* with the class-names. Their universality spans an *extension of objects* (*Umfang von Gegenständen*), to each of which, considered *in and for itself*, there belongs a possible synthesis of perceptions, a possible proper meaning, a possible proper name. The general name "spans" this extension in the manner of the possibility of naming each member of this extension *generally* — i.e., naming it not in the manner of proper names through proper-recognizing, but in the manner of common names through *classification*. What is either directly intuited, or already recognized in its individual peculiarity, or even already recognized through marks, is now recognized and named *as an A*.

### **§ 8\. The dynamic unity between expression and expressed intuition. The fulfilment- and identity-consciousness**

In place of the resting, as it were *static* coincidence between meaning and intuition, Husserl now takes the *dynamic* one: to the expression, at first functioning merely symbolically, the (more or less) corresponding intuition joins itself *afterward*. When this event occurs, we experience a descriptively peculiar *fulfilment-consciousness* (*Erfüllungsbewußtsein*): the act of pure meaning finds, in the manner of an *aiming intention*, a fulfilment in the intuiting act. In this transition-experience the *belonging-together* of the two acts — the meaning-intention and the intuition more or less perfectly corresponding to it — emerges distinctly according to its phenomenological grounding. We experience how, in the intuition, the *same* objective element is intuitively made present which in the symbolic act was "merely thought," and that it becomes intuitive precisely as the so-and-so-determinate which it was at first merely thought (merely meant). It is only another expression for this when we say that the *intentional essence* of the intuiting act fits itself (more or less perfectly) to the *meaningful essence* of the meaning act.

In the static relation first considered, we spoke of *knowing/recognition* (*Erkennen*). This sets up, we said, the sense-fitting relation of the name to what is given in the intuition as named. But the *meaning* is not therein itself the *recognizing*. In purely symbolic word-understanding a meaning is carried out (the word *means* something to us), but nothing is *recognized*. The difference lies, according to what was discussed in the previous section, not in the mere co-givenness of the intuition of the named, but in the phenomenologically peculiar *unity-form*.

The characteristic of this *knowledge-unity* (*Erkenntniseinheit*) now makes the dynamic relation clear to us. First the meaning-intention is given, and given *for itself*; then corresponding intuition joins. At the same time the phenomenological unity establishes itself, which now announces itself as *fulfilment-consciousness*. The talk of knowledge of the object and of fulfilment of the meaning-intention thus express, merely from different standpoints, *the same* state of affairs. The former takes the standpoint of the meant object, while the latter takes only the bilateral acts as relation-points. Phenomenologically, at any rate, the *acts* exist, not always the objects. Hence the talk of *fulfilment* gives the *phenomenological essence* of the knowledge-relation the better-characterizing expression. It is a primitive phenomenological fact that acts of signification and intuition can enter into this peculiar relation. And where they do — where, as the case may be, an act of meaning-intention fulfils itself in an intuition — there we also say that "the object of the intuition is recognized through its concept" or that "the relevant name finds its application to the appearing object." (Husserl adds a terminological footnote: he uses *Signifikation* as the mere translation of *Bedeutung*, will often speak of *significative* or *signitive* acts, and notes that *signitiv* gives a fitting terminological opposite to *intuitiv*; *symbolisch* is a synonym for *signitiv*, given the recent misuse — already censured by Kant — of *symbol* as equivalent to *sign*.)

Husserl does justice to the undoubted phenomenological difference between *static* and *dynamic* fulfilment or recognition. In the dynamic relation the relation-members and the knowledge-act relating them are *temporally drawn apart*; they unfold in a *time-shape* (*Zeitgestalt*). In the static relation, which stands there as the abiding result of this temporal process, they are in *temporal and factual coincidence*. There, in the first step, we have "mere thinking" (= the mere "concept" \= mere signification) as the meaning-intention simply unsatisfied, which in the second step appropriates a more or less adequate fulfilment; the thoughts rest, as it were, *satisfied* in the intuition of the thought — which, precisely by virtue of this unity-consciousness, announces itself as the *thought of this thought*, as what is meant in it, as the more or less perfectly attained *thought-goal* (*Denkziel*).

In the static relation, on the other hand, we have this *unity-consciousness alone*, possibly without a noticeably bounded stage of unfulfilled intention having preceded. The fulfilment of the intention is here not a *process of self-fulfilling* but a resting *being-fulfilled*; not a *coinciding* but a *being-in-coincidence* (*in Deckung Sein*).

In objective respect Husserl speaks here also of *identity-unity* (*Identitätseinheit*). Comparing the two components of a fulfilment-unity überhaupt — whether we consider them in dynamic passing-over into one another, or, analyzing the static unity, hold the components apart in order soon to see them flow into one another — we ascertain *objective identity*. We said, and could say with evidence, that the object of the intuition is *the same* as the object of the thought fulfilling itself in it, and, in the case of exact fitting, even that the object is intuited *exactly as the same* as that which it was thought (or, what here always says the same: meant). It is clear that the identity is *not* first brought in through the comparing and conceptually mediated reflection, but that it is there from the outset, that it is *experience*, unexplicit, unconceptualized experience. In other words: what we phenomenologically characterize, with reference to the acts, as *fulfilment* is, with reference to the bilateral objects — the intuited object on the one side, the thought object on the other — to be expressed as *identity-experience, identity-consciousness, act of identification*; the more or less perfect *identity* is the objective element corresponding to the act of fulfilment, what is therein "appearing." Just for this reason we may designate not merely signification and intuition, but also the *adequation*, i.e., the fulfilment-unity, as an *act*, because it has an intentional correlate peculiar to it, something objective upon which it is "directed."

Yet another turn of the same state of affairs is, according to what was said above, expressed in the talk of *knowing/recognition*. The circumstance that the meaning-intention unites with the intuition in the manner of fulfilment gives the object appearing in the latter — where we are primarily turned to it — the character of the *known* (*das Erkannte*). For the more exact designation of the "as what" of the being-known, the objective reflection points, in place of the act of meaning, to the *meaning itself* (the identical "concept"), and the talk of knowing thus expresses the apprehension of the same unity-situation from the standpoint of the intuition-object (or the object of the fulfilling act) and in relation to the meaning-content of the signitive act. In the reverse relation one says, occasionally — though mostly in a narrower sphere — that the thought "comprehends" the thing, that it is its "concept." Self-evidently, after this exposition, one can designate knowing (which is only another word) just as well as the fulfilment as an *identifying act*.

**Zusatz (Addition),** Husserl does not suppress a *doubt* directed against the otherwise so plausible conception of the identity- or knowledge-unity arising here as an *act of identification or knowing* — a doubt he suppresses all the less because it will prove, in the later course of the inquiry, a serious one and will stimulate fruitful considerations. On closer analysis it strikes us that, in the present cases (where a name relates in actual naming to an object of intuition), we do indeed mean the intuited and thereby named *object*, but by no means mean the *identity* of this object as the simultaneously intuited and named one. Are we to say that it is the preference of *attention* that decides here? Or are we not rather to concede that the act of identification is properly not yet fully and wholly constituted? The main piece of this act — the moment of the connecting unification of meaning-intention and corresponding intuition — is indeed *really* present; but this unity-moment does not function as "representant" of an objectifying "apprehension"; the lived coincidence-unity grounds no act of *relating* identification, no intentional consciousness of identity in which the identity would first become *objective* to us as a meant unity. In reflection on the fulfilment-unity we naturally — indeed necessarily — carry out, with the articulation and juxtaposition of the connected acts, also that *relating* apprehension which the form of their unity *a priori* permits.

**Zusatz,** In its most general form, referred to the categorial act-characters überhaupt, this question will occupy us in the Second Section. Provisionally we continue to treat the designated unity-character as a full act, or not to separate it expressly from the full act. The essential point of our considerations is not thereby touched, insofar as the passage from the unity-experience to the *relating* identification stands open *at any time*, since its *a priori* possibility is guaranteed — so that we may rightly say: identifying coincidence is *experienced*, even if the *conscious intention upon identity*, the *relating* identifying, has remained undone.

### **§ 9\. The different character of the intention in and outside the fulfilment-unity**

The drawing-in of the *dynamic* fulfilment — which plays itself out in the form of an articulated process — for the purpose of interpreting the static knowledge-act also removes a difficulty that threatens to confuse the clear grasp of the relation between the meaning-intention and the full knowledge-act. May we really claim that in the unity of knowledge *four things* let themselves be distinguished — the verbal expression, the act of meaning, the act of intuiting, and finally the overarching unity-character of knowing (or fulfilment)? One could object that what analysis really finds is, on the one side, the *linguistic expression* (specifically the name), on the other the *intuition*, both unified by the character of *recognizing naming*; but that with the linguistic expression an *act of meaning* is also linked — as something distinguishable from the knowledge-character and identifiable, with the same expression, *outside* its knowledge-function, in its understanding-character — this might be *denied*, or is at least a *superfluous* assumption.

This doubt is directed, then, against the *leading conception* that offered itself to us in § 4, even before the analysis of the knowledge-unity, as the most intelligible. What we have to envisage in weighing it is the following.

*In the first place*, the comparison of the expression standing in the knowledge-function with the same expression standing outside it shows that the meaning is on both sides really *the same*. Whether I understand the word *tree* merely symbolically or use it on the ground of the intuition of a tree, both times I evidently mean something with the word, and both times the *same*.

*In the second place*, it is evident that in the process of fulfilment it is the *meaning-intention of the expression* that "fulfils itself" and thereby comes to "coincidence" with the intuition, and that thus the knowledge — as the result of the coincidence-process — is this *coincidence-unity itself*. But it already lies in the concept of a coincidence-unity that we are dealing here not with a duality that steps apart, but with an in-itself undivided unity which articulates itself only through displacement in time. Hence we shall have to say: the same act of meaning-intention that made up the empty symbolic presenting also *dwells in* the complex knowledge-act; but the meaning-intention, which earlier was a "free" one, is in the stage of coincidence "bound," brought to "indifference." It is so peculiarly woven into or fused with this complexion that its meaningful essence does not thereby *suffer*, but its *character* nonetheless undergoes a certain modification.

The like holds quite generally, wherever we consider contents — once for themselves and another time in connection with others, as woven-in parts of wholes. The connection would not connect if the connected contents did not *undergo* something through it. There result necessarily certain changes, and these are precisely the ones which, as *connection-determinacies* (*Verknüpfungsbestimmtheiten*), make up the phenomenological correlates of the *relative* objective properties. Think of a line-segment for itself, say on an empty white background, and then the same segment as a component of a figure. In the latter case it strikes together with other lines, is cut by them, touched by them, and so on. These are — if we abstract from the *mathematical* ideals and hold to the segments of empirical intuition — phenomenological characters that co-determine the impression of the segment-appearance. The same segment (the same, namely, according to its inner content) appears to us ever again differently, according as it enters into this or that phenomenal context; and if we fit it into a line or surface qualitatively identical with it, it even "goes under" indistinguishably into this background — it loses its phenomenal separation and proper validity. This analogy illuminates how the meaning-intention, while remaining identical in essence, is phenomenally modified by being bound in the fulfilment-unity.

### **§ 10\. The more comprehensive class of fulfilment-experiences. Intuitions as intentions in need of fulfilment**

For the further characterization of the fulfilment-consciousness, Husserl points out that it concerns an experience-character which plays a great role elsewhere in our soul-life too. We need only recall the oppositions of *wish-intention* and *wish-fulfilment*, *will-intention* and *will-fulfilment*, or the fulfilment of hopes or fears, the solution of doubts, the confirmation of conjectures, and the like, to see at once that within various classes of intentional experiences there comes to light in essence the *same* opposition which here confronted us specifically as the opposition between meaning-intention and meaning-fulfilment. We touched on this point earlier and, under the more pregnant title *Intentions*, delimited a class of intentional experiences characterized by the peculiarity of being able to *found* fulfilment-relations. Into this class order themselves all acts belonging to the narrower or wider sphere of the logical — among them also the acts called in knowledge to the fulfilment of *other* intentions: the *intuitions* (*Anschauungen*).

When, for example, the beginning of a familiar melody sounds, it arouses determinate intentions which find their fulfilment in the stepwise unfolding of the melody. The like takes place even when the melody is foreign to us. The lawfulnesses prevailing in the melodic condition intentions which, though they lack the full objective determinacy, nonetheless find or can find fulfilments. Naturally these intentions are themselves, as concrete experiences, fully determinate; the "indeterminacy" with respect to *what* they intend is manifestly a descriptive character of the intention itself — a peculiarity of its content — and not a mere lack to be supplied from without. The intuitions, then, are not self-sufficient final terms but themselves intentions awaiting and capable of further fulfilment.

Husserl refines the point: the "indeterminacy" (the peculiarity of demanding not a fully determinate completion but only one from a lawfully circumscribed sphere) is, paradoxically yet correctly, a *determinacy* of this intention. To it corresponds not only a certain *latitude* of possible fulfilment, but, for every actual fulfilment from this latitude, a *common element* in the fulfilment-character. It is phenomenologically something different whether acts with determinate or indeterminate intention fulfil themselves, and in the latter respect again whether intentions fulfil themselves whose indeterminacy points to this or that direction of possible fulfilment. — In the present example we have at the same time to do with a relation of *expectation* and *fulfilment of expectation*. But it would be incorrect to interpret, conversely, every relation of an intention to its fulfilment as an expectation-relation. *Intention is not expectation*; it is not essential to it to be directed upon a future occurrence. If I see an incomplete pattern, e.g., that of this carpet partly covered by furniture, then the seen piece is, as it were, beset with intentions pointing to completions (we feel, so to speak, that the lines and colour-shapes go on in the "sense" of the seen); but we *expect* nothing. We would be able to expect if movement promised us further seeing. But possible expectations, or occasions of possible expectations, are not themselves expectations.

An infinity of pertinent examples is supplied above all by *external perceptions*. The determinacies falling in each case into the perception point to the complementary ones, themselves entering into appearance in new possible perceptions, and this — according to the measure of our "experiential acquaintance" with the object — now in determinate, now in gradually indeterminate fashion. More exact analysis shows that every perception and every perception-context builds itself up from components understandable under these two viewpoints of *intention* and (actual or possible) *fulfilment*; a state of affairs that transfers without further ado to parallel acts of fantasy and of imaging überhaupt. Normally the intentions here do not have the character of expectations; they do not have it in every case of *resting* perception or imaging; they gain it only where perception comes into *flux* and spreads into a continuous series of perceptions from the *perception-manifold* belonging to one and the same object. Objectively speaking: the object shows itself from different sides; what, seen from the one side, was only pictorial indication comes, from the other, to confirming and fully sufficient perception; or what on the former was only indirectly co-meant by bordering, only pre-indicated, comes on the latter at least to pictorial indication — it appears perspectivally foreshortened and adumbrated, only to appear from a new side "just as it is." According to our conception, *every perception and imagination is a web of partial intentions, fused into the unity of a total intention*. The correlate of this latter is the *thing*, while the correlates of those partial intentions are *thingly parts and moments*. Only thus is it to be understood how consciousness can reach *beyond* the truly experienced. It can, so to speak, mean-beyond, and the meaning can fulfil itself.

### **§ 11\. Disappointment and conflict. Synthesis of distinction**

In the wider sphere of acts which admit at all of differences of intention and fulfilment, there ranges alongside fulfilment, as its *excluding opposite*, *disappointment* (*Enttäuschung*). The mostly negative expression usually serving here — e.g., *non-fulfilment* — means no mere privation of fulfilment, but a new descriptive fact, a form of synthesis as peculiar as fulfilment. This holds everywhere, hence also in the narrower sphere of meaning-intentions in their relation to intuitive intentions. The synthesis of knowledge was consciousness of a certain "agreement." But to agreement there corresponds as a correlate possibility "non-agreement," "*conflict*" (*Widerstreit*). The intuition does not "agree" with the meaning-intention, it "conflicts" with it. Conflict "separates," but the *experience* of conflict sets into relation and unity; it is a form of *synthesis*. Where the earlier synthesis was of the kind of *identification*, the present one is of the kind of *distinction* (*Unterscheidung*) — for which, unfortunately, we have here no other positive name. This "distinction" must not be confused with the one to which *comparison* stands opposed; the oppositions "identification and distinction" and "comparison and distinction" are not one and the same, though a close phenomenological kinship manifestly explains the use of the same expressions. In the "distinction" here in question, the *object of the disappointing act* appears as "*not-the-same*," as "*other*" than the object of the intending act. These expressions, however, point to more general spheres of cases than we have hitherto preferred. Not only the significative but also the intuitive intentions fulfil themselves in the manner of identification and disappoint themselves in the manner of conflict. The question of the natural delimitation of the total class of acts to which *the same* and *the other* (we may equally say: *is* and *is not*) belong we shall soon subject to a more exact consideration.

The two syntheses are, however, not entirely coordinate. Every conflict presupposes something that gives the intention überhaupt the direction upon the object of the conflicting act, and this direction can ultimately be given it only by a *fulfilment-synthesis*. Conflict presupposes, as it were, a certain *ground of agreement*. If I mean A is red, while it turns out "in truth" to be green, then in this turning-out — i.e., in the fitting to the intuition — the red-intention conflicts with the green-intuition. But it is unmistakable that such a thing is possible only on the ground of the *identification of the A* in the acts of signification and intuition. Only thus can the intention get at this intuition at all. The total intention goes upon a red-being A, and the intuition shows a green-being A. As meaning and intuition coincide in respect of their direction upon the same A, only then do the intentional moments unitarily co-given on both sides enter into *conflict*: the meant red (which is meant as red of the A) does not agree with the beheld green. Through the identity-relation the moments not come to coincidence first *correspond* to one another; instead of "connecting" themselves through fulfilment, they rather "separate" themselves through conflict — the intention is referred to what is now coordinated to it in the intuition, but is *rejected* by this.

What we have here set forth in special relation to the meaning-intentions and the disappointments befalling them holds manifestly for the whole class of objectifying intentions indicated above. Generally we may accordingly say: *an intention disappoints itself in the manner of conflict only by being a part of a more comprehensive intention whose complementary part fulfils itself.* With simple or isolated acts, therefore, there can be no talk of conflict.

### **§ 12\. Total and partial identification and distinction as the common phenomenological foundations of the predicative and determinative expression-form**

The relation between intention (specifically meaning-intention) and fulfilment hitherto considered was that of *total agreement* (*totale Übereinstimmung*). Therein lies a restriction that followed of itself from the fact that, in order to attain the greatest possible simplicity, we abstracted from all *form* — and especially from the form announcing itself in the little word *is* — and, in the relation of the expression to outer or inner intuition, took into account those expression-parts alone which fitted themselves to the intuited *as if a garment*. Now, by drawing in the cases opposed to the case of total agreement, the analysis broadens to encompass the articulated, *partial* coincidence on which predication and attribution rest.

By drawing in the possibility opposed to the case of total agreement, namely *conflict* (which we could accordingly, though not quite unambiguously, designate as *total conflict*), we become aware at the same time of new possibilities — namely the important cases of *partial agreement* and *non-agreement* between the intention and the act fulfilling or disappointing it.

Their closer consideration Husserl keeps from the outset so general that the validity of all essential findings for the intentions of the wider circle indicated above — hence not merely for meaning-intentions — is evident of itself.

All conflict led back to the fact that the given, self-disappointing intention was a part of a *comprehensive* intention which fulfilled itself partially (i.e., according to the complementary parts) and at the same time became estranged according to that first part. In every conflict, then, there lies in a certain way also *partial agreement* and *partial conflict*. To these possibilities the regard to the *objective relations* would also have had to lead us; for where there is talk of coincidence, there offer themselves of themselves, as correlate possibilities, those of *exclusion, inclusion, and crossing* (*Exklusion, Inklusion und Kreuzung*).

Remaining first with the case of conflict, it gives rise to a supplementary consideration.

Husserl formalizes: if a partial intention disappoints itself in an η by being interwoven with other intentions which fulfil themselves, these latter need not be so unified with η that the whole has the distinction of a self-erected *total act*, an act "in which we live," on whose unitary object we "attend." In the web of intentional experiences of our consciousness there are many possibilities of the *pointed* singling-out of acts and act-complexions, but they remain in general unrealized; and only such *pointed* unities come into consideration where we speak of single acts and their syntheses. The case of *total and pure disappointment* now consists in this, that the bare η (and not the whole) comes forth — or at least comes forth primarily — and that a pointed conflict-consciousness establishes unity exclusively between the disappointed and the disappointing; the interest is specially directed upon the relation of the corresponding objects. So when a Green-intention disappoints itself in an intuited Red, and only the Green and Red are attended to. If the conflicting intuition of Red comes somehow to expression (through a word-intention fulfilling itself in it), and likewise the disappointment as such comes to expression, we would have, say: *this \[this red\] is not green*. (This sentence does not mean the same as the thought-sentence "The word-intention Green disappoints itself in the intuition of Red"; for the new expression makes the relation of the acts *objective* and fits itself to it with its new meaning-intentions in total fulfilment.)

On the other hand it can also be that a whole Φ enters the synthesis *as a whole*, and indeed so that it thereby enters into special relation either with a correlate whole or with the bare, isolated part η out of it. In the first case there is, according to the interwoven elements, partly coincidence (in respect of the complementary parts) and partly total conflict (η against η). The whole synthesis here has the character of a *total conflict*, but not of a *pure* but of a *mixed* (*vermischt*) conflict. In the other case the bare η lifts itself out as correlate act (possibly by the unity of the whole dissolving in the mixed conflict); the special synthesis of conflict now connects as members the whole Φ and η; with a fitting expression, say: *this \[the whole object, the red tile roof\] is not green*. This important relation we can call that of *exclusion* (*Ausscheidung*). Manifestly its principal character remains where the members would themselves be complex, so that we could differentiate between *pure* and *mixed* exclusion. Roughly, the latter may be illustrated by the example *this \[the red tile roof\] is no green tile roof*.

Husserl now considers the case of *inclusion*. An intention can fulfil itself in an act that contains *more* than is needed for its fulfilment, insofar as it presents an object that *co-contains* its object — be it as a part in the common sense, be it as a moment belonging to it, explicitly or implicitly co-meant. Self-evidently we again disregard the acts in which a more comprehensive objectivity constitutes itself in the manner of the *objective background* — acts that do not delimit themselves unitarily and are not preferred as bearers of attention; otherwise we should come back to the synthesis of total coincidence. Let there be given, e.g., the presentation of a red tile roof, and in it let the meaning-intention of the word *Red* fulfil itself. The word-meaning fulfils itself here in coinciding fashion with the intuited red; but for that reason the *total intuition* of the red tile roof, in its unity sharply lifting itself off the background through the function of attention, enters into a synthetic unity of peculiar kind with the *meaning-intention Red*: *\[this\] is red*. We speak here of the relation of "*ordering-in / subordination*" (*Einordnung*), which has its opposite in the above exclusion. The ordering-in can manifestly only be a *pure* one.

The act of the ordering-in synthesis — and indeed as the total act setting the intending and fulfilling act into one — has its objective correlate in the relation of *partial identity of the corresponding objects*. To this the talk of *ordering-in* also points, which expresses the grasp of the relation under the image of activity: the part is ordered into the whole. Manifestly the same objective relation is also designated, according to the standpoint of the apprehension (which naturally points to unconsidered phenomenological differences co-attested in the expression-form), by the expressions: *Φ has η*, or *η accrues to Φ*. The index *g* may make one attentive that it is the *intentional objects* of the indicated acts which enter into these relations — we stress the *intentional* objects, namely the objects just as they are *meant* in these acts.

The transfer of what has just been set forth to the case of exclusion and to the expressions *has not*, *does not accrue to* follows of itself.

To the bare *is* belongs everywhere *objective identity* überhaupt, to the *is not* *non-identity* (conflict). That it is more specially a relation of *ordering-in* or *exclusion* requires other expressive means — such as the *adjectival form*, which characterizes the *had*, the *accruing* as such, just as the *substantival form* stamps the correlative, the *having* as such (i.e., in the function of forming the "subject" of an identification). In the attributive, or more generally determinative, expression-form (even full identity can determine), the being is contained in the adjectival inflection, insofar as it is not expressed explicitly and separately in the relative clause, or, on the contrary, not entirely suppressed (*this philosopher Socrates*). Whether the always-mediate expression of non-identity — in predication and attribution as well as in the substantival forms (non-identity, non-agreement) — expresses a necessary relation of the actual "negation" to a (even if not actual, yet modified) affirmation, leads to discussions we do not yet wish to enter.

In the normal statement, then, identity or non-identity is *stated*, and, in the case of relation to "corresponding intuition," *expressed* — i.e., the intention upon identity or non-identity fulfils itself in the carried-out identification or separation. *The tile roof*, it runs in the above example (if the *bare* intention preceded), *is really red*. The predicate-intention *fits* the subject (presented and intuited, e.g., in the manner "*this* tile roof"). In the opposite case it would run: *"in reality" it is not red*; the predicate does not accrue to the subject.

But when the meaning of the *is* now finds its fulfilment on the ground of an actual identification (which itself often has the character of a fulfilment), it is at the same time clear that we are thereby led *beyond* the sphere which, without becoming rightly clear about its boundary, we had hitherto always had in view — namely the sphere of expressions that can really fulfil themselves through *corresponding intuition*. Or rather, we become aware that *intuition* in the ordinary sense, laid at the basis by us as self-evident, of outer or inner "*sensibility*," is not the only function that may raise a claim to the title *intuition*, to the capacity for genuine fulfilment-achievement. We save the closer investigation of the difference here coming to light for the Second Section of this Investigation.

Finally, Husserl expressly remarks that with what has been set forth no *complete analysis of judgment*, but only a *fragment* of such an analysis, has been carried out. No account at all has been taken of the *quality* of the synthetic act, of the differences between attribution and predication, and the like.

## **Chapter Two: Indirect Characterization of the Objectifying Intentions and Their Essential Varieties Through the Differences of the Fulfilment-Syntheses**

### **§ 13\. The synthesis of knowledge as the form of fulfilment characteristic of the objectifying acts. Subsumption of the meaning-acts under the class of objectifying acts**

We have above ordered the meaning-intentions into the wider circle of "*Intentions*" in the pregnant word-sense. To all intentions there correspond, possibility-wise, fulfilments (or their negative counterparts, disappointments) — peculiar transition-experiences which are themselves characterized as acts and which let the respective intending act reach, as it were, its goal in a *correlative* act. The latter, insofar as it fulfils the intention, is called the *fulfilling act*, but it is so called only by virtue of the *synthetic act of fulfilment*, in the sense of the self-fulfilling. *This transition-experience does not everywhere have the same character.* With the significative and, no less manifestly, with the intuitive intentions it has the character of *knowledge-unity*, which in respect of the objects is unity of *identification*. But this does not hold in the wider circle of intentions überhaupt. To be sure, we can everywhere speak of a coincidence, and everywhere we shall even find an identification. But this often springs only by virtue of *interwoven acts* out of that group which admit an identification-unity and, in these contexts, also found one.

An example will at once make the situation clear. The self-fulfilling of a *wish* is carried out in an act that includes an identification — and indeed as a necessary component. For there obtains the lawfulness that the wish-quality is *founded* in a presentation, i.e., in an objectifying act, and more closely in a "*mere*" presentation; and to this is joined the complementary lawfulness that wish-*fulfilment* too is founded, namely in an act that identifyingly inserts the founding presentation: the wish-intention can find its fulfilling satisfaction only by the bare presentation of the wished, underlying it, transforming itself into the conform *for-perception* (*Fürwahrnehmung*). But what lies before us is not the bare transformation — the mere fact that the imagining is relieved by the for-perception — rather both are *one* in the character of identifying coincidence. In this synthetic character there constitutes itself the *it is really and truly so* (just as we had previously merely presented and wished it); which of course does not exclude that this being-real is only a supposed one, especially since in most cases it is something inadequately presented. If the wish is founded in a purely signitive presentation, the identification can naturally also have the character of that more special coincidence which fulfils the signification through a conform intuition. The like would manifestly be set forth for any intentions whatever that have their basis in presentations (as objectifying acts); and at the same time what holds of fulfilment is to be transferred, *mutatis mutandis*, to the case of disappointment.

This premised, it is now clear that, even though wish-fulfilment is founded in an identification (and possibly in an act of intuitive knowing), this act does not *exhaust* the wish-fulfilment but precisely only *founds* it. The self-satisfying of the specific *wish-quality* is a proper and other-kinded act-character. It is only a *simile* when we like to speak of satisfaction — indeed even of fulfilment — outside the sphere of the *Gemüt*\-intentions (intentions of feeling).

With the special character of the intention, then, hangs together the special character of the fulfilling coincidence. Not only does *to each shading of the intention there correspond a like shading of the correlate fulfilment* and at the same time of the self-fulfilling in the sense of the synthetic act; but *there correspond also, to the essentially different classes of intentions, pervasive class-differences of the fulfilment* in the mentioned twofold sense. Manifestly, in *these parallel rows* the belonging members always belong to *one* act-class. The fulfilment-syntheses with the wish- and will-intentions are surely closely related, and sharply distinguished, e.g., from those occurring in the meaning-intentions. Surely, on the other hand, the fulfilments of meaning-intentions and of intuitive acts are of the *same* character, and so überhaupt for all the acts we comprise under the title of the *objectifying*. For this class — alone interesting us here — we can say *that their fulfilment-unity has the character of identification-unity*, and possibly the narrower one of *knowledge-unity*, hence of an act to which *objective identity* corresponds as intentional correlate.

Husserl asks for attention to the following: it was shown above that every fulfilment of a *signitive* intention by an *intuitive* one has the character of a synthesis of identification. But not conversely does the fulfilment of precisely a *meaning-intention*, and the fulfilment precisely through a *corresponding intuition*, carry itself out in every synthesis of identification. And still more: we shall scarcely be inclined to speak, at every identification, already of the *fulfilment* of an intention and accordingly of a *recognizing*. In the widest sense, to be sure, every actual identifying is in ordinary talk called a knowing. But in the narrower sense it is a matter — we feel this clearly — of an *approach* to a *knowledge-goal*; in the narrowest sense of the critique of knowledge, even of the *attainment* of this knowledge-goal itself. To transform the bare feeling into distinct insight, and to delimit exactly the sense of this approach or attainment, will yet be our task. Provisionally we hold fast only that the *unity of identification* — and with it at the same time all *knowledge-unity* in the narrower and narrowest sense — has its *origin-site in the sphere of the objectifying acts*.

The *peculiarity of the fulfilment* can serve to characterize the unitary class of acts to which it essentially belongs. Accordingly we could outright *define* the objectifying acts as those whose fulfilment-synthesis has the character of *identification* (whose disappointment-synthesis, then, the character of *distinction*); or as those acts which can function phenomenologically as members of a possible synthesis of identification or distinction; or finally — anticipating a lawfulness yet to be formulated — as those acts which, be it as intending or as fulfilling (or disappointing) acts, can stand in a possible *knowledge-function*. To this class belong the *acts of identification and distinction themselves*; for they are themselves either a *mere meaning*, to grasp identity or non-identity, or the corresponding *actual grasping* of the one or the other. That meaning can "*confirm*" or "*refute*" itself in a knowledge (in the pregnant sense); and in the first case identity (or non-identity) is *actually grasped*, i.e., "adequately perceived."

The analyses just more indicated than carried out lead, then, to the result *that the acts of meaning-intention, just as well as those of meaning-fulfilment — the acts of "thinking" just as well as those of intuiting — belong to a single class of acts, to the objectifying*. With this it is established that *other-kinded acts never function in the manner of sense-givers* and can "come to expression" only in that the *signitive intentions adhering to the words find their fulfilment* by means of perceptions or imaginings directed upon the acts to be expressed *as objects*. While, then, in the cases where acts stand in meaning-function and in this sense find expression, the signitive or intuitive relation to any objects whatever constitutes itself precisely *in* these acts, in the other cases *the acts are mere objects* — and this naturally in respect of other acts here functioning as the proper bearers of meaning.

But before we enter upon the closer discussion of this state of affairs — especially upon the refutation of the in-themselves quite plausible counter-arguments (a task reserved for the closing section) — we must investigate somewhat more carefully the remarkable *facts of fulfilment*, and indeed in the sphere of the objectifying acts.

### **§ 14\. Phenomenological characterization of the distinction between signitive and intuitive intentions through the peculiarities of fulfilment**

#### **a) Sign, image, and self-presentation**

Within the last considerations the remark forced itself upon us that with the *generic character of the intentions* the generic character of the *fulfilment-synthesis* intimately hangs together, and this so much so that the class of objectifying acts lets itself be defined outright through the generic character of the fulfilment-synthesis (presupposed as known) as an *identifying* one. In carrying this thought further, there stirs the question whether the essential *kind-distinctions within* this class of objectivations are not also determinable through the corresponding differences of the *modes of fulfilment*. Through a fundamental division the objectifying intentions fall apart into the *significative* and the *intuitive*. Let us try to give an account of the difference of the two kinds of acts.

The *signitive intentions* we grasped, by virtue of our starting-point from the explicit acts, as *significations*, as *meanings of expressions*. Setting aside provisionally the question whether the same acts which function as sense-giving can also occur outside the meaning-function, these signitive intentions have in each case an intuitive *Anhalt* (a foothold) — namely in the sensuous of the expression — but for that reason they have not an intuitive *content*; they are one with intuitive acts only in a certain way, but are different from them in kind.

The easily graspable difference of the explicit as against the *purely intuitive* intentions emerges when we compare *signs* (*Zeichen*) and *images* (*Bilder*) with one another.

The *sign* has, content-wise, mostly *nothing in common* with the *signified*; it can signify the heterogeneous just as well as the homogeneous. The *image*, by contrast, relates to the thing *through similarity* (*durch Ähnlichkeit*), and where this is lacking there is no longer talk of an image. The sign as object constitutes itself for us in the act of appearing; this act is not yet a signifying one — it needs, in the sense of our earlier analyses, the linkage of a new intention, a new mode of apprehension, through which, instead of the intuitively appearing, a new thing, the *signified* object, is meant. Likewise the image — say the bust out of marble — is a *thing* like any other; only the new mode of apprehension makes it an *image*; there appears now not merely the thing of marble, but at the same time, and on the ground of this appearance, a person is pictorially meant.

The intentions adhering on both sides are *not externally attached* to the appearance-content but *essentially founded* in it, in such a way that the character of the intention is *determined* by it. It would be a descriptively incorrect conception of the situation to think that the whole difference consists in this: that the same intention, attached once to the appearance of an object *similar* to the meant object, is attached another time to the appearance of one *dissimilar* to it. For the *sign* too can be similar to the signified — indeed perfectly similar — yet the sign-presentation does not thereby become an *image-presentation*. The photograph of the sign A we grasp without further ado as *image* of this sign; but if we use it as a sign, the apprehension is of another kind, even where the similarity is complete.

Hence the objective fact of *similarity* between appearing and meant determines *no* difference. Nonetheless it is not *irrelevant* for the case of image-presentation. This shows itself in the *possible fulfilment* — and it is only the recollection of this possibility that let us draw in the "objective" similarity here. The image-presentation manifestly has the peculiarity that, wherever fulfilment is granted it, its object appearing "as image" identifies itself with the object *given* in the fulfilling act *through similarity*. In designating this as the peculiarity of image-presentation, we have already said that here *the fulfilment of the similar through the similar inwardly determines the character of the fulfilment-synthesis as an imaginative one*. If, on the other side, owing to a chance similarity between sign and signified, a knowledge of their mutual similarity sets in, this knowledge does not belong to the fulfilment of the signitive intention — apart from the fact that this knowledge is by no means of the kind of that peculiar identification-consciousness which brings the similar and the similar to *relating* coincidence in the manner of image and thing. Rather, it belongs to the peculiar essence of a *significative* intention that with it the appearing object of the intending act and that of the fulfilling act (e.g., name and named in the realized unity of both) "have *nothing* to do with one another." Hence it becomes clear that, in truth, the descriptively different manner of fulfilment, as it is grounded in the different descriptive character of the intention, can also conversely make the difference of this character noticeable and define it.

We have hitherto considered only the difference of the signitive and *imaginative* intentions. Passing over the here less significant differences *within* the wider sphere of imaginative acts (we preferred above the presentations through physical images, instead of going into the fantasy-presentations as well), there remain still the *perceptions*.

As against *imagination*, *perception* is characterized — as we are wont to express it — by the fact that in it the object appears "*itself*" (*selbst*) and not merely "in image." In this we recognize at once the characteristic differences of the *fulfilment-syntheses*. Imagination fulfils itself through the peculiar synthesis of *image-similarity*; perception through the *synthesis of factual identity* — the thing confirms itself through "*itself*," in that it shows itself from different sides and is therein ever the one and the same.

#### **b) The perceptive and imaginative adumbration of the object**

Here Husserl introduces a crucial qualification. Perception, in pretending to give the object "*itself*," thereby pretends properly to be *no mere intention* at all, but rather an act that may *offer* fulfilment to others while itself needing no further fulfilment. Mostly — e.g., in all cases of "*external*" perception — it remains at the *pretension*. The object is not really given, namely not fully and wholly given as the one it itself is. It appears only "from the front side," only "perspectivally foreshortened and adumbrated," and the like. While some of its determinacies are at least imaged in the core-content of the perception in the manner those latter expressions exemplify, others do not even in this pictorial form fall into the perception; the components of the unseen back side, of the interior, and so on, are indeed in more or less determinate fashion *co-meant*, symbolically indicated through the primarily appearing, but they themselves do not fall into the intuitive (perceptive or imaginative) content of the perception at all. With this hangs together the possibility of *unboundedly many*, content-wise different perceptions of *one and the same* object. Were perception everywhere what it pretends — actual and genuine self-presentation of the object — then, since its peculiar essence would exhaust itself in this self-presenting, there would be for each object only a *single* perception.

On the other hand it is to be noted that the object, *as it is in itself* (in the here alone questionable and intelligible sense which the fulfilment of the perception-intention would realize), is *not a totally other* than the one which perception, even if imperfectly, realizes. This lies, so to speak, in the very sense of perception, to be *self-appearance* of the object. Hence, going back to the phenomenological, even though common perception is built up from manifold — partly purely perceptual, partly merely imaginative, and even signitive — intentions, *as a total act* it grasps the object *itself*, be it only in the manner of *adumbration* (*Abschattung*). Thinking the respective perception set in fulfilment-relation to the *adequate* one — that perception which would give us the object, in the ideally strict and most proper sense, *itself* — we can say: perception intends the object so that the ideal fulfilment-synthesis would have the character of a *partial coincidence* of the purely perceptive content of the intending act with the purely perceptive of the fulfilling act, and at the same time the character of a *full coincidence* of the bilateral full perception-intentions. The "purely perceptive" content in "external" perception is what we keep over after abstraction from all merely imaginative and symbolic components; it is, then, the "*sensed*" content in the immediately belonging purely perceptive apprehension, which values all its parts and moments as *self-adumbrations* of corresponding parts and moments of the perception-object, and so confers on the whole content the character of the "*perception-image*," the *perceptive adumbration* of the object. In the ideal limiting-case of *adequate* perception this sensed or self-presenting content coincides with the perceived object.

This common relation, belonging to the sense of all perception, to the object *in itself* — hence to the *ideal of adequation* — announces itself also in the phenomenological belonging-together of the manifold perceptions belonging to *one* object. In the one perception the object appears from this side, in the other from that, once near, the other time far, and so on. In each, through all this, the one and the same object is "*there*"; in each it is intended according to the total run of what it is known to us as and is present in this perception. To this corresponds phenomenologically the *continuous flux of fulfilment or identification*, in the steady stringing-together of the perceptions "belonging to the same object." Each single one is therein a *mixture* of fulfilled and unfulfilled intentions. To the former corresponds in the object what of it is given in *this* single perception as more or less perfect adumbration, to the latter what of it is not yet given — hence what would come to actual and fulfilling presence in new perceptions. And all such fulfilment-syntheses are distinguished by a *common character*, precisely as *identifications of self-appearances of an object with self-appearances of the same object*.

It is without further ado clear that parallel differences hold also for the imaginative presentation. It too images the same object now from this, now from that side; to the synthesis of manifold *perceptions* in which ever the same object comes to *self-presentation* corresponds the parallel synthesis of manifold *imaginations* in which this same object comes to *pictorial* presentation. To the changing perceptive adumbrations of the object correspond here the parallel imaginative adumbrations, and in the ideal of complete imaging the adumbration would coincide with the complete image. When imaginative acts fulfil themselves now in the imaginative context, now through corresponding perceptions, the difference in the character of the fulfilment-synthesis is unmistakable: the transition from image to image is characterized otherwise than that from image to the thing itself.

These analyses — useful also for our further inquiry and to be carried on in the next chapter — instruct us about the *belonging-together* of perceptions and imaginations and about their common *opposition* to the signitive intentions. Everywhere we distinguish from the *meant* object — the signified, imaged, perceived — a content actually given in the appearance but *not meant*: the *sign-content* on the one side, the imaginative and the perceptive *adumbration* of the object on the other. But while *sign* and *signified* "have nothing to do with one another," there obtain between the adumbrations (whether imaginative or perceptive) and the *thing itself* inner *belongings-together* enclosed in the sense of these words. And these relations document themselves phenomenologically in differences of the *constituting intentions* and no less in differences of the *fulfilment-syntheses*.

Self-evidently, this presentation does not disturb our interpretation of *every* fulfilment as an *identification*. The intention comes *everywhere* to coincidence with the act offering it *fullness* (*Fülle*), i.e., the object meant in it is the same as that meant in the fulfilling act. But our comparison referred not to these *meant objects* but to *sign* and *adumbration* in their relations to the meant objects, or to what phenomenologically corresponds to these relations.

**Paragraph 7 (small print).** Husserl notes that the interest of the present section belonged primarily to the *peculiarities of the fulfilment-syntheses*; through these the differences of the intuitive and signitive acts underwent a merely *indirect* characterization. Only in the further course of the inquiry — in § 26 — will we, on the ground of the analysis of the intentions considered for themselves and without regard to the possible fulfilments, be able to furnish a *direct* characterization.

### **§ 15\. Signitive intentions outside the meaning-function**

In the last considerations we claimed certain components of intuitive acts as *signitive intentions*. But in the whole series of inquiries hitherto, the signitive acts counted for us as acts of *meaning*, as sense-giving factors with expressions; the words *signification* and *signitive intention* counted as meaning-identical. It is therefore time to weigh the question: can the same — or essentially like-kinded — acts which we otherwise find in the meaning-function also occur *outside* this function, detached from all expressions? That this question is to be answered affirmatively is shown by certain cases of *wordless knowing* which have throughout the character of verbal knowing, while the words, according to their sensuous-signitive content, are not actualized at all. We recognize, for example, an object as an antique Roman milestone, its furrows as weathered inscriptions, without words at once — or at all — setting in; we recognize a tool as a drill-bore, but the word simply will not occur to us; and the like. Genetically speaking, through the present intuition an association is dispositionally aroused which is directed upon the meaning-bearing expression; but only the *meaning-component* of it is actualized, which now, in the reverse direction, radiates back into the arousing intuition and flows over into it with the character of *fulfilled* intention. These cases of wordless knowing are thus nothing other than *fulfilments of meaning-intentions* — only of such as have phenomenologically *detached* themselves from the signitive contents otherwise belonging to them. Pertinent examples are also supplied by reflection on the ordinary contexts of scientific thinking, where one notices that the forward-storming thought-series bind themselves to a very considerable extent *not* to the words belonging to them, but are aroused through the flux of intuitive images or through their own associative interweavings.

With this also hangs together that *expressive speaking* goes far beyond what would have to be intuitively given for the purpose of actual adequacy of the cognizing expression. That this has in part an opposite ground — in the special ease with which the word-images let themselves be reproduced through the given intuitions, in order then for their part to draw in the symbolic *thoughts*, but not the intuitions corresponding to these — no one will doubt. But it is also to be observed conversely how the reproduction of the word-images often lags quite far behind the thought-series reproductively aroused through the respective intuition. In the one and the other way come about the *countless inadequate expressions* which do not fit themselves in simple fashion to the actually present primary intuitions and to the synthetic formations really built upon them, but go far beyond what is thus given. There arise remarkable *mixtures* of acts. Properly recognized are the objects only as the ones given in the actual intuitional basis; but since the unity of the intention reaches further, the objects appear as recognized also as those which are intended in the *total intention*. *The knowledge-character spreads itself out, as it were.* So we recognize, for example, a person as the emperor's adjutant, a handwriting as Goethe's, a mathematical expression as Cardano's formula, and the like. Here the recognizing can naturally *not* fit itself to what is given in perception; at best there is the possibility of adaptation to *courses of intuition* which need not themselves be actualized at all. In this way, on the ground of *partial* intuition, even knowings and knowledge-series are possible which, on the ground of full actual intuition, would not be possible at all and *a priori*, because they set together what is in itself incompatible. There are, and in only too great a measure, *false and even absurd "knowings."* But "properly" they are no *knowledge* — namely not logically valuable, perfect knowledge, not knowledge in the pregnant sense. With this, however, we anticipate future considerations; for the graded series of knowledge here touched on, and the ideals bounding them, are not yet laid clear.

We have hitherto had to do with signitive intentions which, identical as they are, occur now within, now outside the meaning-function. But *countless* signitive intentions lack any — whether fixed or transient — relation to expressions, while yet belonging according to their essential character with the meaning-intentions to one class. Husserl recalls the perceptive or imaginative course of a *melody* or of some other event known to us in kind, and the (determinate or indeterminate) intentions or fulfilments occurring therein; likewise the *empirical order and connection* of things in their phenomenal coexistence — with reference to what gives the appearing things in this order (and first the parts in each single thingly unity) the character of a unity *belonging-together in just this arrangement and form*. Representation and recognition through *analogy* can bring only image and thing (analogon and analogized) to unity and so let them appear as belonging together; but not what is given in *contiguity* (*Kontiguität*) not merely together but *as belonging together*. Even where, in the realization of contiguity-representations, images at first set in which imagine the signitively represented in advance and then confirm themselves at fulfilment in their things, the unity between the contiguity-representant and what is thereby represented cannot be given through the *image-relation* (since this is not effective between *these two*), but only through the simply peculiar relation of the signitive representation *as one through contiguity*.

Accordingly we shall have to see, quite rightly, in the inadequate perceptions and imaginings, *complexions of primitive intentions*, among which, alongside perceptive and imaginative elements, there are found also such of the kind of *signitive intentions*. Überhaupt we may judge that all phenomenological differences of the objectifying acts let themselves be led back to the *elementary intentions and fulfilments* building them up — the one and the other unified through fulfilment-syntheses. On the side of the *intentions* there then remain as the only ultimate differences those between *signitive intentions as intentions through contiguity* and *imaginative intentions as such through analogy*, each simple and pure in its kind. On the side of *fulfilment* there function as components partly again intentions of the one and other kind; but under circumstances (as in the case of perception) also such as are no longer to be addressed *as intentions*: components which only fulfil, yet no longer reach for fulfilment — *self-presentations* of the object meant by them in the strictest word-sense. Through the character of the *elementary acts* the characters of the fulfilment-syntheses determining the homogeneous unity of the complex act are then determined; and at the same time, with the aid of the preferring power of attention, the character of these or those elementary acts transfers itself to the unity of the total act: the whole act is *imagination* or *signification* or *perception* (perception simply). And where two such unitary acts enter into relation, there arise relations of *agreement* and *conflict* whose character is determined through the founding total acts, but ultimately through their elements.

In the next chapter these relations shall be pursued further, within the limits in which they are phenomenologically to be secured and therefore epistemologically to be exploited. We will thereby hold ourselves purely to the phenomenologically given unities, to the *sense* they carry in themselves and announce in fulfilment. So we avoid the temptation to tread the path of *hypothetical construction*, with whose doubts the clarification of knowledge by no means needs to be burdened.

## **Chapter Three: On the Phenomenology of the Degrees of Knowledge**

### **§ 16\. Mere identification and fulfilment**

When, starting from the linguistic expression of a perception, we described the relation of meaning-intention and fulfilling intuition, we said that the *intentional essence* of the intuitive act *fits* or *belongs* to the *meaningful essence* of the significative act. The same holds visibly in every case of a *total identification* which brings to synthesis qualitatively *equal* acts — hence positing and positing, or non-positing and non-positing acts; whereas with the *difference* of the qualities the identification grounds itself exclusively in the *matters* (*Materien*) of the bilateral acts. This transfers, with fitting changes, to the cases of *partial* identification, so that we may pronounce that the *matter* is the moment *essentially* coming into consideration for identification (and then self-evidently also for distinction) in the act-character of the acts coming in each case to synthesis.

For the case of identification the matters are the *special bearers* of the synthesis, but not themselves identified. For the talk of identification refers, according to its sense, to the *objects presented through the matter*. On the other hand, the matters do come to *coincidence* in the act of identification itself. That thereby — even if equality of qualities is presupposed — no complete equality of the bilateral acts is attained, every example shows; and this lies in the fact that the *intentional essence does not exhaust the act*. What remains over will prove, in the careful investigation of the phenomenology of the degrees of knowledge (our next task), to be exceedingly significant. From the outset the following is evident here: if knowing admits *degrees of perfection*, and this *at equal matter*, then the matter cannot account for the differences of perfection — hence cannot determine the peculiar essence of knowledge as against any arbitrary identification. We attach the further inquiry to the consideration of just this difference, already noticed by us earlier, between *mere identification* and *fulfilment*.

We had equated fulfilment with recognition (in the narrower sense) and indicated that thereby only certain forms of identification are designated — those, namely, which bring us *nearer to the knowledge-goal*. What this is to mean we can try to make clear somewhat thus: in every fulfilment there takes place a more or less perfect *intuitivization* (*Veranschaulichung*). What the intention indeed means, but makes presentational in more or less improper or inadequate fashion, the fulfilment — i.e., the act fitting itself in the fulfilment-synthesis, offering the intention its "*fullness*" — sets *directly* before us, or at least relatively more directly than the intention. In fulfilment we experience, as it were, a *that is it itself* (*das ist es selbst*). This "itself" is admittedly not to be taken in the strict sense, as though a perception must be given that would bring us the object itself to actual phenomenal presence. It may be that in the progress of knowledge — in the stepwise ascent from acts of lesser to those of richer knowledge-fullness — we must finally always arrive at fulfilling perceptions; but for that reason not every step (i.e., every single identification already characterized for itself as fulfilment) need itself be a perception.

The relative talk of "more or less direct" and of the "itself" indicates the main point well enough: that the fulfilment-synthesis shows an *inequality of value* (*Ungleichwertigkeit*) of the connected members, such that the fulfilling act brings in an *advantage* (*Vorzug*) which the bare intention lacks — namely that it *confers on it the fullness of the "itself," brings it at least more directly to the thing itself*. And the relativity of this "direct" and "itself" again indicates that the fulfilment-relation has in itself something of the character of a *relation of increase* (*Steigerungsrelation*). A *concatenation* of such relations appears accordingly as possible, in which the advantage increases step by step; whereby, however, every such increase-series points to an *ideal limit* (*ideale Grenze*), or already realizes it in its end-member, which sets to all increase an unsurpassable goal: the goal of *absolute knowledge*, of the *adequate self-presentation* of the object of knowledge.

With this the *characteristic distinction of the fulfilments* within the wider class of identifications is formulated, at least in provisional indication. For not in every identification does such an *approach to a knowledge-goal* carry itself out, and accordingly aimless identifications running into the infinite are quite possible. For example, there are infinitely many arithmetical expressions that have the identical numerical value 2, and so we can string identification to identification *in infinitum*. Likewise there may be infinitely many images of one and the same thing, and thereby is again determined the possibility of infinite identification-chains striving toward no knowledge-goal. Likewise for the infinite manifold of possible perceptions of one and the same thing.

Attending, in these intuitive examples, to the constituting elementary intentions, we find indeed that into the whole of the identification moments of *genuine fulfilment* are mostly also woven. So when we set into one image-presentations not exactly of fully equal intuitive content, so that the new image brings some things to clearer presentation and perhaps sets before our eyes "just as it is" what the earlier merely adumbrated or even symbolically indicated. Thinking an object in fantasy as turning and rotating on all sides, the image-sequence is throughout connected by fulfilment-syntheses in respect of the partial intentions; but the respective new image-presentation is *as a whole* no fulfilment of the preceding, and the total presentation-series is without progressive approach to a goal. Likewise with the manifold of perceptions belonging to the same external thing: gain and loss hold the balance at every step; the new act is richer in fullness in respect of some determinacies, but for that had to forfeit fullness in respect of others. Against this we can say that the *total synthesis* of the sequence of imaginations (or of perceptions) represents, in comparison with the isolated act out of such a sequence, an *increase in knowledge-fullness* — the imperfection of the one-sided presentation is *relatively overcome* in the all-sided presentation. We said only "relatively overcome": for the all-sided presentation carries itself out, in such a synthetic manifold, not — as the ideal of adequation demands — *in one stroke*, as pure self-presentation and without addition of analogizing and symbolizing, but piecewise and continually clouded by such additions. Another example of an intuitive fulfilment-series is offered by the transition from a rough outline-drawing to a more exactly executed pencil-sketch, from this to a finished picture, up to the life-fully executed painting — and indeed for the same and visibly the same object.

Such examples from the sphere of mere imagination show us at the same time that the character of fulfilment does *not* presuppose what is reckoned to the *logical* concept of knowledge — namely the *positing-quality* (*Setzungsqualität*), both in the intending and in the fulfilling acts. We speak of *knowledge* preferentially where a meaning in the normal sense of *belief* (*Glaube*) corroborates or confirms itself.

### **§ 17\. The question of the relation between fulfilment and intuitive illustration**

The question will now arise what role the various genera of objectifying acts — the signitive and intuitive acts, and, under the latter title, the perceptive and imaginative — play in the knowledge-function. Here the intuitive acts appear visibly privileged, and this so much so that one will at first be inclined to designate all fulfilment (as happened above in passing) as *illustration* (*Veranschaulichung*), or to characterize its achievement, where intuitive intentions are at stake from the outset, as a mere increase in *intuition-fullness*. Surely the relation between intention and fulfilment forms the basis for the formation of the concept-pair *thought* (more narrowly: *concept*) and *corresponding intuition*. But it must not escape us that a concept of intuition oriented merely by this relation would by no means coincide with that of the *intuitive act*, although, by virtue of the tendency toward intuition lying, so to speak, in the sense of all fulfilment, it would closely hang together with it — indeed presuppose it. To "make a thought clear," as one also says here, means first to procure for the *content* of the thought knowledge-*fullness*. But this can in a certain way also be achieved by a *signitive* presentation. To be sure, when we set the demand for evidence-making clarity — such that "the thing itself" is made clear to us and thereby its possibility and truth recognizable — we are referred to *intuition* in the sense of our intuitive acts. Just for this reason the talk of *clarity* has, in epistemological contexts, without further ado this narrower sense: it means the return to the fulfilling intuition, to the "*origin*" of the concepts and propositions out of the intuition of their things themselves.

Careful example-analyses are now needed to verify and carry further what has just been indicated. They will help us to clear up the relation between fulfilment and illustration and to delimit exactly the role intuition plays in *every* fulfilment. The differences of *proper* and *improper* illustration (or fulfilment) will distinctly separate themselves, and at the same time the difference between *mere identification* and *fulfilment* will undergo its last clarification. As the achievement of intuition will thereby be determined by its really bringing in to the intending act, in *proper* fulfilment, under the title "*fullness*," something *new*, we shall become attentive to a side hitherto not pointed up, fundamental for knowledge, in the phenomenological content of acts: the "*fullness*" will prove a moment of the intuitive acts that is *new* as against quality and matter, belonging specially to the *matter* in the manner of a *supplement*.

### **§ 18\. The graded series of mediate fulfilments. Mediate presentations**

Every mathematical concept-formation unfolding in a *definition-chain* shows us the possibility of *fulfilment-chains* that build themselves up *link for link out of signitive intentions*. We make the concept (5³)⁴ clear to ourselves by return to the definitory presentation: "number that arises when one forms the product 5³ · 5³ · 5³ · 5³." Wanting to make this latter presentation clear in turn, we must go back to the sense of 5³, hence to the formation 5 · 5 · 5\. Going back still further, we would have to resolve each such product into its definitory sense, and so the clarification advances through a graded series of purely signitive presentations, none of which need be redeemed in intuition proper.

Going back still further, we would explain 5 through the definition-chain 5 \= 4+1, 4 \= 3+1, 3 \= 2+1, 2 \= 1+1. After each step we would have to carry out the *substitution* into the last-formed complex expression (or thought); and were this thought always re-establishable (in itself it certainly is, though just as certainly not for us), we would finally arrive at the fully explicated *sum of ones*, of which it would be said: that is the number (5³)⁴ "*itself*." Manifestly not only the end-result, but already every single *step* which led from one expression of this number to the next, clarifying it and content-wise enriching it, would correspond to a real *act of fulfilment*. In this way, moreover, every simple decadic number is an *instruction* to a possible fulfilment-chain whose number of members is determined by the number of its units diminished by 1, so that such chains of unboundedly many members are *a priori* possible.

One usually speaks as though, in the mathematical sphere, the simple word-meaning were *identical* with the content of the complex definitory expression. Then there would indeed be no talk of fulfilment-chains; we would move in nothing but identities of the kind of tautologies. But whoever looks at the *complication* of the thought-formations growing through substitution — whoever compares it, even only in the very simplest cases where it is really carryable-through, with the originally experienced meaning-intention — will scarcely seriously assume that in this latter all the complication was contained from the outset. It is quite unmistakable that here real *differences of intention* obtain which, however one may more closely characterize them, are connected with one another through *totally identifying* fulfilment-relations.

A remarkable peculiarity of the examples just discussed — of the class of signitive presentations they illustrate — lies in this: that in them the *content* of the presentations (more distinctly, the *matter*) prescribes *a priori* a *determinate graded course of fulfilment*. The fulfilment that here ensues *mediately* can never at the same time ensue *immediately*. To each signitive intention of this class there belongs a *determinate* fulfilment (or a determinate group of fulfilments) as the *next*, to this again a determinate one as the next, and so on. This peculiarity we find also with certain *intuitive* intentions — so when we make a thing presentational through the *image of an image*. The matter of the presentation here too prescribes a *first* fulfilment, which would set the primary image "*itself*" before our eyes; but to this image there belongs a *new* intention whose fulfilment leads us to the thing itself.

Manifestly, the common feature of all these *mediate* presentations, whether signitive or intuitive, is characterized by their being presentations which make their objects presentational *not in simple fashion* but through presentations built one over the other of lower and higher level; or, to put it more sharply, they are presentations that present their objects *as objects of other presentations*, or *as standing in relation to objects so presented*. Just as objects can be presented in relation to any other objects, so they can be presented in relation to *presentations*; and these presentations are then, in the relation-presentation itself, *presented presentations* — they belong to its *intentional objects*, not to its *components*. With regard to this class of cases we speak of *mediate* (or built-over-one-another) intentions or fulfilments, hence also of *mediate presentations*. There holds, then, the proposition that *every mediate intention demands a mediate fulfilment*, which self-evidently ends, after a finite number of steps, in an *immediate intuition*.

### **§ 19\. Distinction between mediate presentations and presentations of presentations**

Well to be distinguished from these mediate presentations are the *presentations of presentations* (*Vorstellungen von Vorstellungen*) — those presentations which simply relate to other presentations *as to their objects*. Although the presented presentations are, generally speaking, themselves intentions and hence admit of fulfilment, the nature of the given, the *fronting* presentation here by no means demands a *mediate* fulfilment through the fulfilment of the presented presentations. The intention of the presentation-presentation V₁(V₂) goes upon V₂. This intention is therefore fulfilled, and simply fulfilled, when precisely V₂ "*itself*" occurs; it is not, say, *enriched* when, for its part, the intention of V₂ fulfils itself — when *its* object appears in image, or in the relatively richer image, or even in perception. For V₁ does not mean *this object* but simply its presentation V₂. Self-evidently nothing is changed by more complicated nesting, say after the measure of the symbol V₁(V₂(V₃…)).

For example, the thought *signitive presentation* finds fulfilment in the intuition *of* a signitive presentation — e.g., of the presentation *Integral* (if we wish, also of the presentation *signitive presentation* itself). One must not misunderstand these cases, as though here the signitive presentation *Integral* itself claimed the character of intuition — as though, then, here the concepts *intuition* and *signitive act* (meaning-intention) ran into one another. Not the signitive presentation *Integral*, but the *inner perception* of this presentation, is the fulfilling intuition for the thought *signitive presentation*; instead of functioning *as* fulfilling intuition, this presentation functions as *object* of the fulfilling intuition. Just as the thinking of a colour finds its fulfilment in the act of intuition of this colour, so the thinking of a thinking finds it in an act of intuition of this thinking — hence the ultimately-fulfilling intuition in an *adequate perception* of it. And of course here, as elsewhere, the mere *being* of an experience is not yet an *intuition* and especially a *perception* of it. It is to be noted that überhaupt, in our opposition between *thought* (or intention) and *fulfilling intuition*, by *intuition* the mere outer intuition — the perception or imagination of outer, physical objectivities — is by no means to be understood. The "inner" perception or imagery too can, as is to be seen from the example just discussed (and as is self-evident from the essence of presenting), function as fulfilling intuition.

### **§ 20\. Genuine illustrations in every fulfilment. Proper and improper illustration**

Having sufficiently stressed and clarified the difference of the mediate presentations and the presentation-presentations, it will be good to look on the other hand also at their *common* element. Every mediate presentation includes, according to the above analysis, presentation-presentations — namely by meaning its object as object of certain presentations presented *in* it. So, e.g., when we present 1000 as 10³, i.e., as the number characterized as *object of that presentation* which would, for its part, grow in the carrying-out of the indicated raising-to-a-power. From this it emerges that *genuine illustrations* play the *essential role in every fulfilment of mediate intentions, and at every step* of this fulfilment. The characterization of an object as object of a presented presentation (or as one standing in a certain relation to so-defined objects) presupposes, in the fulfilment, the *fulfilment of the presentation-presentations*; and these woven-in *intuitive* fulfilments first give the total identification the character of a *fulfilment*. The increase in "fullness" consists step by step in nothing other than that, little by little, all presentation-presentations — whether the ones woven in from the outset or those newly occurring in the fulfilment — are fulfilled through realizing "*construction*" of the respectively *presented* presentations and through *intuition* of these realized ones, so that finally the ruling total intention, with its over- and into-one-another of intentions, appears identified with an *immediate* intention. Thereby this identification has *as a whole* too the character of fulfilment.

This kind of fulfilment we shall, however, have to count among the *improper* illustrations (*uneigentliche Veranschaulichungen*); for as a *proper* illustration we shall with good ground designate one that does not bring in fullness in arbitrary fashion, but exclusively so that it confers the increase of fullness on the object *presented by the total presentation*, i.e., makes *it* presentational with greater fullness. At bottom this says nothing other than that a *mere signitive intention has no fullness at all*; rather all fullness lies in the actual presentification of determinacies that accrue to the object *itself*.

We shall soon pursue this latter thought further. Here we continue that the named difference between proper and improper illustration can also be designated as one between *proper* and *improper fulfilment*, insofar as the intention aims at its object, reaches as if craving for it, and *fulfilment in the pregnant sense* can now count as the expression for the intention's having brought to it at least something of the *fullness of the object*. Yet we must hold fast that the improper and proper fulfilments within the identification-syntheses are distinguished by a *common* phenomenological character (of fulfilment in the wider sense), and that it is a *special proposition* which teaches that all improper fulfilment *implies* proper fulfilments — hence "owes" its fulfilment-character to these.

To delimit the difference between proper and improper illustrations somewhat more exactly, and at the same time to dispose of an example-class in which improper illustrations occur quite with the look of genuine ones, Husserl sets forth the following.

Not always, when the fulfilment of a signitive intention carries itself out on the ground of an intuition, are the *matters* of the bilateral acts, as was presupposed above, *in the relation of coincidence*, so that the intuitively appearing object itself stands there as the one meant in the meaning. Only where this holds is there talk, in the true sense, of *illustration*; only then is the thought realized in the manner of perception, illustrated in the manner of imagination. It is otherwise when the fulfilling intuition lets an object appear that has the character of an *indirect representant* — e.g., when at the naming of a geographical name the fantasy-presentation of a *map* turns up and fuses with the meaning-intention of this name; or when an assertion concerning certain road-connections, river-courses, mountain-ranges is confirmed through the markings of a map lying before us. Here the intuition is, in the true sense, not to be designated as *fulfilling* at all; its own matter does not come into action; the real *fulfilment-foundation* lies not in it but in a *signitive* intention woven with it. That the appearing object here functions as *indirect representant* for the meant and named object says phenomenologically that the intuition constituting it is bearer of a *new intention* which points *beyond* it, the appearing object, and just thereby characterizes it as a *sign*. The analogy of the appearing and the meant, possibly present, here determines not a simple image-presentation but a *sign-presentation built upon the image-presentation*. The outline of England, as the map paints it, may depict the form of the land itself; but the fantasy-presentation of the map turning up at the talk of England does *not* mean England itself in pictorial fashion — not even mediately, in the manner of what is *depicted* through this map — but means England in the manner of the mere *sign*, thanks to the external relations of association which has linked all our knowledge about land and people to the map-image. Hence, as the nominal intention fulfils itself on the ground of this fantasy-presentation, not the object imagined in the latter (the map), but the object first *represented* by it, counts as *the same* as what is meant by the name.

### **§ 21\. The "fullness" of the presentation**

It will now be necessary to take the *achievement of the intuitive intentions* more closely into view. Since the fulfilment of *mediate* intentions leads back to the fulfilment — and indeed the *intuitive* fulfilment — of *immediate* intentions, and since it has turned out that the last result of the whole mediate process is an immediate intention, the question now interesting us is that of the *intuitive fulfilment of immediate intentions* and of the fulfilment-relations and \-laws prevailing therein. In the following inquiries, Husserl notes at once, in respect of the *intentional essence*, the *matter alone* is to be decisive for the relations to be established; the qualities (positing and "mere" presentation) can therefore be assumed arbitrarily.

Husserl begins with the following proposition: to every intuitive intention there belongs — spoken in the sense of ideal possibility — a signitive intention fitting it *exactly in matter*. This unity of identification necessarily possesses the character of a *fulfilment-unity* in which the *intuitive*, not the signitive, member has the character of the fulfilling and then also of the *fullness-giving* in the most proper sense.

The sense of this latter we only express otherwise when we say that the signitive intentions are in themselves "*empty*" and "*in need of fullness*." In the transition from a signitive intention to the corresponding intuition we experience not merely a *bare increase*, as in the transition from a faded image or mere sketch to a fully-living painting. Rather, the signitive presentation lacks for itself *any* fullness; only the intuitive presentation brings it *to* it and, through the identification, *into* it. The signitive intention merely *points to* the object; the intuitive makes it presentational in the pregnant sense — it brings something of the fullness of the object itself. However far the image may, in the case of imagination, fall behind the object, it has many determinacies in common with it; and more than that, it "*resembles*" it, it *depicts* it, and so it is "really presentational." But the signitive presentation does *not* present through analogy; it is "properly" not "presentation" at all — nothing of the object becomes living in it. The *complete fullness* as ideal is, then, the *fullness of the object itself*, as the totality of the determinacies constituting it. The *fullness of the presentation*, however, is the totality of those determinacies belonging to *itself*, by means of which it analogizingly presentifies its object or grasps it as self-given. This *fullness* is therefore, beside *quality* and *matter*, a *characteristic moment* of the presentations — a positive component, to be sure, only with the intuitive presentations, a *lack* with the signitive. The "*clearer*" the presentation, the greater its "*liveliness*," the higher the level of *imagery* it climbs, the *richer* it is in fullness. The ideal of fullness would accordingly be reached in a presentation that enclosed its object, the full and whole one, in its phenomenological content. This, if we reckon to the fullness of the object also the *individualizing* determinacies, no imagination can achieve, but only *perception*; disregarding these determinacies, however, an ideal is thereby determinately designated for imagination too.

We would therefore have to go back to the *marks* of the presented object: the more of these marks participate in the *analogical representation* — and for each single one, the greater the increase of *similarity* with which the presentation represents this mark in its own content — the greater the *fullness* of the presentation. In a certain way, to be sure, in the pictorial presentation as in every presentation, *every* mark of its object is *co-meant*; but not every is *analogically represented*; *not* to every there belongs, in the phenomenological content of the presentation, a proper, as it were *analogizing* (imaging) moment. The *totality* of these intimately mutually-fused moments — thought as the *foundations* of the purely intuitive (here purely imaginative) apprehensions which first give them the character of *representants* of the corresponding objective moments — makes up the *fullness* of the imaginative presentation. Likewise with the *perception-presentation*: here, besides the imaginative representations, perceptive *presentations*, self-graspings, *self-presentations* of objective moments come into consideration. Taking the totality of the moments of the perception-presentation functioning whether imaginatively or perceptively, we have thereby delimited its fullness.

### **§ 22\. Fullness and "intuitive content"**

Exactly seen, the concept of fullness is still beset with a *doubleness*. One can take the above-designated moments into view according to their own contentual stock, abstracting from the *functions* of pure imagination and perception which first give them the value of imagery or self-adumbration and thus their value for the fulfilment-function. One can, on the other hand, consider these moments *in* their apprehension — hence not these moments alone but the *full images* or *self-adumbrations*; thus, under mere exclusion of the intentional qualities, the whole purely intuitive *acts* which, in interpreting these moments objectively, at the same time include them in themselves. These "*purely intuitive*" acts we understand as mere components of the given intuitions — namely as that in the intuitions which lends to the moments designated above the *relation to the objective determinacies corresponding to them and presented through them*; we thereby exclude (apart from the qualities) the *signitive* relations possibly knotted in besides, to further parts or sides of the object *not coming to proper presentation*.

Manifestly it is these *purely intuitive components* that lend to the acts as wholes the character of perceptions and image-presentations — in short, the *intuitive character* — and that function, in the context of the fulfilment-series, as fullness-giving and as increasing or enriching present fullness. To meet the doubleness of the talk of fullness, Husserl introduces distinguishing *termini*.

By *presenting* or *intuitively representing contents* (*darstellende oder intuitiv repräsentierende Inhalte*) we understand those contents of intuitive acts which, by virtue of the pure imaginative or perceptive apprehensions whose bearers they are, point determinately to contents of the object exactly corresponding to them, presenting them in the manner of imaginative or perceptive *adumbrations*. The *act-moments* characterizing them in this way we, however, *exclude*. Since the character of imagination lies in analogizing depiction, in "*re-presentation*" in a narrower sense, while the character of perception can also be designated as *presentation*, there offer themselves as distinguishing names for the presenting contents in the one and the other case: *analogizing* or *depicting* and *presenting* or *self-presenting*; also the expressions *imaginatively* and *perceptively adumbrating* contents are quite apt. The presenting contents of external perception define the concept of *sensation* (*Empfindung*) in the ordinary, narrow sense; the presenting contents of external fantasy are the *sensuous phantasms* (*sinnliche Phantasmen*).

The presenting or intuitively representing contents *in and with* their belonging apprehension we call the *intuitive content of the act* (*intuitiver Gehalt des Aktes*) — still abstracting throughout from the quality of the act (whether positing or not) as indifferent for all the distinctions here in question. From the intuitive content there are, according to the above, further excluded all *signitive* components of the act.

### **§ 23\. The weight-relations between intuitive and signitive content of one and the same act. Pure intuition and pure signification. Perception-content and image-content, pure perception and pure imagination. The gradations of fullness**

For the full clarification of the just-delimited concepts, and for the easier delimitation of a series of new concepts rooting in the same soil, Husserl makes the following consideration. In an intuitive presentation an object is meant in the manner of imagination or perception; it "*comes*" in it more or less perfectly "*to appearance*." Necessarily, to each part — überhaupt to each determinacy of the object, and indeed as the *hic et nunc* meant one — there must correspond certain moments or pieces of the act. To what no meaning refers is not present for the presentation. Now we find in general the possibility given for the following phenomenological distinction.

Husserl distinguishes: (1) the *purely intuitive content* of the act, as that which in the act corresponds to the totality of the determinacies of the object "*falling into appearance*"; and (2) the *signitive content* of the act, likewise corresponding to the totality of the remaining determinacies — co-meant, to be sure, but not themselves falling into appearance.

Thus we all make, purely phenomenologically, in the intuition of a thing-perception or of an image, the distinction between what of the object really comes to appearance — the mere "*side*" from which it shows itself to us — and what lacks presentation, what is covered by other phenomenal objects, and the like. Manifestly it lies in the sense of this talk (which phenomenological analysis securely confirms within certain limits) that even *non-presented* is *co-meant* in the intuitive presentation, and that thus a content of *signitive components* must be ascribed to it. From this we must first *abstract* if we want to obtain the *intuitive* content *pure*. This latter gives the presenting content its *direct* relation to corresponding objective moments, and only through *contiguity* do the new — so far mediate — intentions of signitive kind attach themselves to it.

Defining now as the *weight* (*Gewicht*) of the intuitive (or signitive) content the *totality of the objective moments presented intuitively* (or signitively), the two weights complement each other in every presentation to the unity of the *total weight*, i.e., to the total totality of the objective determinacies. There holds, then, at all times the symbolic equation *i \+ s \= 1*. The weights *i* and *s* can manifestly vary in manifold ways: the same — intentionally the same — object can become intuitive with different, now with fewer, now with more determinacies; correspondingly the signitive content also changes, it grows or diminishes.

Ideally there results the possibility of *two limiting cases*: *i \= 0, s \= 1*; and *i \= 1, s \= 0*. In the first case the presentation would have *only* a signitive content; of its intentional object no determinacy would remain over which it brought to presentation in its content. The purely signitive presentations, well known to us specially as *pure meaning-intentions*, appear here, then, as *limiting cases of the intuitive*. In the second case the presentation contains *no* signitive content at all. Everything in it is fullness; no part, no side, no determinacy of its object that is not intuitively presented, none that is merely *indirectly co-meant*. Not only is everything presented meant (which is an analytic proposition), but everything meant is also presented. These presentations, new to us, we define as *pure intuitions* (*reine Anschauungen*). Husserl uses this expression in a harmless double sense: now so that the *full act* is comprised, now under abstraction from the quality. Distinguishingly, we can speak of *qualified* and *non-qualified* pure intuitions, and likewise with all kindred acts.

Now in every presentation we can abstract from the signitive components by restricting ourselves to what really comes to representation in its representative content. We can thus form a *reduced presentation*, with an object so reduced that, in relation to it, it is pure intuition. Accordingly we can also say that the *intuitive content* of a presentation comprises *that in it which is pure intuition*, as we may then also speak, in respect of the object, of *its* purely intuitive content — namely the content coming, in this presentation, to pure intuition. This transfers to the signitive content of the presentation: we can designate it as that in it which is *pure signification*.

The respective total act of intuition possesses either the character of *perception* or that of *image-presentation*. The intuitive content is then called specially *perceptive* or *perception-content* (*Wahrnehmungsgehalt*), or *imaginative* or *image-content* (*Bildinhalt*). It is not to be confused with the *perceptively or imaginatively presenting content* in the defined sense (of § 22). The perception-content comprises — though as a rule not exclusively — *presenting* contents; the image-content only *analogizing* contents. That these latter contents at times still admit *another* apprehension, in which they function, as in the case of physical images, as *presenting*, makes no difference to the matter.

By virtue of the mixture between perceptive and imaginative components which the intuitive content of a perception admits and as a rule exhibits, we can again think a separation carried out, according to which the perception-content is divided into the *pure perception-content* and a *supplementary image-content*. Likewise in every pure intuition. If *wᵣ* and *bᵣ* are the weights of its *purely* perceptive and imaginative components, we can set up the symbolic equation *wᵣ \+ bᵣ \= i*, where *i* symbolizes the weight of the total intuitive content of the pure intuition, hence the total content of its object. If now *bᵣ \= 0* — i.e., the pure intuition is free of all image-content — it is called *pure perception* (better, *pure perzeption*, since the qualitative character that the sense of the term *perception* tends to co-comprise as positing is here to be left out of consideration). If, conversely, *wᵣ \= 0*, the intuition is called *pure image-presentation* (*pure imagination*). The "*purity*" of pure perception thus relates not only to *signitive* but also to *imaginative* additions. The restriction of an *impure* perception through *exclusion of the symbolic components* yields the *pure intuition* dwelling in it, and only a further step of reduction — the exclusion of everything pictorial — yields the *content of pure perception*.

Is not, in pure perception, the presenting content *identical* with the object itself? The essence of pure *presentation* consists, after all, in being *pure self-presentation* of the object — hence in meaning the presenting content *directly* (in the manner of the "*itself*") *as* its object. But that would be a *fallacy*. Perception, as presentation, grasps the presenting content so that *with and in it* the object appears as *self-given*. The presentation is *pure* when *every* part of the object is *really presented* in the content and none merely imagined or symbolized. Just as in the object there is nothing that is not presented, so in the content nothing that is not presenting. Despite this exact correspondence, the self-presentation can have the character of a mere — even if all-sided — *adumbration* (of a complete "*perception-image*"); it need not reach up to the *ideal of adequation*, where the presenting content is at the same time the *presented*. The pure image-presentation, which by virtue of its purity from all signitive additions completely images its object, possesses in its presenting content a *complete analogon* of the object; this analogon can approach the object more or less, up to the limit of full equality. Exactly the same can hold of pure perception. The difference consists only in this: that imagination apprehends the content *as* analogon, *as* image, while perception apprehends it *as* self-appearance of the object. Not only pure imagination, then, but also pure perception, while holding fast its intentional object, still admits differences of *fullness*.

In respect of the *gradations of fullness* in intuitive content (with which the gradations of fullness in representing content run *eo ipso* parallel), Husserl distinguishes three dimensions.

These are: (1) the *extent* or *richness of fullness* (*Umfang*), varying according as the content of the object comes to presentation with greater or lesser *completeness*; (2) the *liveliness of fullness* (*Lebendigkeit*), as the degree of approach of the primitive similarities of the presentation to the corresponding content-moments of the object; and (3) the *reality-content of fullness* (*Realitätsgehalt*), its more or less of presenting contents. In all these relations the *adequate perception* presents the ideal: it has the maximum of extent, of liveliness, and of reality, precisely as *self-grasping* of the full and whole object.

### **§ 24\. Series of increase of the fulfilment**

The talk of "*fullness*" we formed with reference to the relations of "*fulfilment*," that peculiar form of the synthesis of identification. In the last findings we have detached the concept of fullness somewhat from these relations and considered the fullness as a moment belonging to the single intuitive presentation taken for itself. We now bring both back into connection.

It is evident that to these relations there correspond *possible increase-series, built out of fulfilment-syntheses* (*Steigerungsreihen, gebaut aus Erfüllungssynthesen*).

Fulfilment sets in, on the ground of a *first conferral of a fullness* überhaupt, in the identifying *adaptation* of "corresponding" intuition to a signitive intention. The intuitive act "*gives*" the signitive its fullness in the coincidence-context. The *increase-consciousness* here grounds itself in the *partial coincidence* of the fullness with the correlate part of the signitive intention, while to the identifying coincidence of the *empty places* of the bilateral intentions corresponding to one another no share in the increase-consciousness can be ascribed.

*Continuous increase of the fulfilment* then carries itself out further in the *continuity of intuitive acts* (or fulfilment-series) which present the object with ever more extended and heightened imagery. That B₂ is a "more perfect" image than B₁ says that in the *synthetic* context of the belonging image-presentations fulfilment, and on the side of B₂ increase, takes place. To increases there belong, as everywhere, so here, *distances*, and in the concatenation of the relations *transitivity*. If, then, at the same time B₂ \> B₁ and B₃ \> B₂, then B₃ \> B₁, and this latter distance is greater than the distances mediating it. So at least when we take the three above-distinguished moments of fullness — extent, liveliness, and reality — separately into account.

To these increases and increase-series there correspond, as analysis teaches, *similarities* and *similarity-series* in respect of the *presenting* contents of the fullnesses. The similarity of the representants is, to be sure, not without further ado to be claimed as an *increase*, nor the similarity-concatenation as an increase-series — namely not when *these* "fullnesses" are considered according to their own contentual stock and under abstraction from their representing function in the belonging acts. Only by virtue of *this function* — by virtue of the fact that, in the order of the fulfilment-series and the increases prevailing between its acts, every later act appears as still richer in fullness — do the representing contents of the acts also gain an *ascending order*; step by step they themselves appear not merely as fullness-giving in general, but as giving ever richer fullness. The designation of *these* components as fullnesses is precisely a *relative, functional* one; it expresses a characteristic that accrues to the content through the act and through the role of this act in possible fulfilment-syntheses. It is here similar to the designation "*object*": to be an object is no positive mark, no positive kind of a content; it designates the content only as the intentional correlate of a presentation. For the rest, the fulfilment- and increase-relations manifestly ground in the phenomenological content of the acts *purely according to its specific stock*. It is throughout a matter of *ideal* relations, determined by the species in question.

In the synthesis of intuitive acts, however, increase of fullness does not always take place; for *partial fulfilment* and *partial emptying* can go hand in hand, as we have already spoken of above. Ultimately, we can accordingly say, the *distinction between mere identification and fulfilment* leads back to this: that with the former either *no* fulfilment in the true sense takes place at all — because it is a matter of *identity-syntheses* of acts which are all *without fullness*; or fulfilment (enrichment of fullness) does take place, but under simultaneous emptying, under surrender of already present fullness, so that no pronounced and pure increase-consciousness comes about. The *primitive* relations, bearing on the elementary intentions, are at any rate: *fulfilment* of an empty (i.e., purely signitive) intention, and, as it were, *filling-up* of an already somewhat filled (i.e., imaginative) intention — hence increase and realization of an imaginative intention.

### **§ 25\. Fullness and intentional matter**

Husserl now weighs the relation of the new concept of presentation-content comprised under the title *fullness* to the content in the sense of *matter*, which has played so great a role in the inquiry hitherto. The matter counted for us as that moment of the objectifying act which makes it that the act presents *just this* object and just *in this way* — i.e., just in these articulations and forms, with particular reference just to these determinacies or relations. Presentations of *agreeing matter* present not merely the same object überhaupt, but *mean it entirely and wholly as the same*, namely as *completely alike-determined*. The one allots to it in its intention nothing that the other does not also allot; to every objective articulation and form on the one side corresponds an articulation and form on the other, such that the agreeing presentation-elements *objectively mean the same*. In this sense we said in the Fifth Investigation, in the elucidations of the concept of matter and of the meaningful essence: "Two judgments are essentially the same judgment \[namely judgments of the same matter\] where all that would hold of the judged state of affairs by the one judgment would also have to hold of it by the other, and nothing else. Their truth-value is the same." They *mean* the same in respect of the object, however else they may also be quite considerably different — e.g., the one carried out merely significatively, the other illumined by more or less intuition.

What originally gave Husserl the direction for this concept-formation was the *identical* in the stating and understanding of one and the same expression, whereby the one can "*believe*" the statement-content and the other "*leave it undecided*," without disturbing this identity; whereby it further does not depend on whether the expressing carries itself out in fitting to corresponding intuitions, and überhaupt can so carry itself out, or not. Hence one could even be inclined (and Husserl himself long wavered on this point) to define meaning outright as this "*matter*"; which would, however, have the disadvantage that, e.g., in the predicating statement the moment of actual *asserting* would be excluded from the meaning. (At any rate one could at first so restrict the concept of meaning and then distinguish between *qualified* and *unqualified* meanings.) The comparison of meaning-intentions and their correlate intuitions in the static and dynamic unity of the identifying coincidence then yielded that this *same*, delimited as the *matter of the meaning*, recurs in the corresponding intuition and *mediates the identification*; and that thus the *freedom* in the addition or omission of intuitive elements — and even of the whole corresponding intuitions, where only the identical significance of the respective expression is at stake — rests on the fact that the total act attached to the wording has *on the intuition-side the same matters as on the meaning-side*, namely according to all the meaning-parts that come to illustration at all.

Accordingly it is clear that the concept of *matter* is defined through the unity of the *total identification* — and indeed *as that in the acts which serves in them as the foundation of the identification* — and that consequently the differences of *fullness*, going beyond mere identification and manifoldly determining the peculiarities of fulfilment and fulfilment-increase, are *not considered* in this concept-formation. However the fullness of a presentation may vary within its possible fulfilment-series, its *intentional object* — which and *as which* it is intended — remains the same; in other words, its *matter* remains the same. On the other hand, matter and fullness are not *relationless*; and where we set beside a purely signitive act an act of intuition conferring fullness on it, this latter is distinguished from the former *not* simply by the fullness having been adjoined, as a third moment separate from these two, to the common quality and matter.

At least not so, if by *fullness* we understand the *intuitive content* of the intuition. For the intuitive content itself already comprises a whole *matter*, namely in respect of the act *reduced* to a pure intuition. If the given intuition-act is from the outset an act of pure intuition, its matter is at the same time a *component of its intuitive content*.

Most fittingly we shall be able to grasp the relations here prevailing by *paralleling* the signitive and intuitive acts in the following way.

The *purely signitive act* would subsist as a mere complexion of *quality* and *matter*, if it could be *for itself* at all — i.e., form for itself a concrete experience-unity. This it cannot; we always find it as an *appendage* of a founding intuition. This intuition of the *sign* has, to be sure, "nothing to do" with the object of the significative act (it enters into no fulfilment-relation with this act); but it *realizes its possibility in concreto* as that of a simply unfulfilled act. The following proposition seems thus to hold: a *signification* is possible only in that an *intuition* is burdened with a new intentional essence, through which the intuitive object points *beyond itself* in the manner of a *sign* (whether of a fixed one or one only momentarily offering itself). More exactly weighed, this proposition does not seem to express the necessity-connection prevailing here with the requisite analytic clarity, and perhaps says more than lets itself be justified. We may, it seems, say that *it is not the founding intuition as a whole, but only its representing content*, that essentially lends the signitive act its support. For what goes beyond this content and determines the sign as a *natural object* can *vary arbitrarily* without disturbing the signitive function. Whether, e.g., the letters of a word-sign are of wood, iron, printer's ink, and so on — whether they objectively appear as such — is indifferent. Only the everywhere re-recognizable *shape* (*Gestalt*) comes into consideration, but also not as the *objective* shape of the thing of wood, etc., but as the shape really *present in the presenting sensuous content* of the intuition.

If the connection holds only between the *signitive act* and the *presenting content* of the intuition — if, then, quality and matter of this intuition are *meaningless* for the signitive function — then we shall also not be able to say that every signitive act needs a *founding intuition*, but rather a *founding content*. As such, it seems, any content whatever can function, just as any can function as the presenting content of an intuition.

Considering now the parallel case, that of the *purely intuitive* act, its quality and matter (its intentional essence) too are not detachable *for themselves*; here too a *necessary supplement* is needed. This is provided by the *representing content* — i.e., the (in the case of sensuous intuition, sensuous) content which, in the present weaving with an intentional essence, has taken on the character of an *intuitive representant*. Noting that the same (e.g., sensuous) content can serve now as bearer of a signification, now as bearer of an intuition (pointing — depicting), it is natural to *extend* the concept of representing content and to distinguish between *signitively* and *intuitively representing contents* (or, in short, signitive and intuitive *representants*).

This division is, however, *incomplete*. We have hitherto considered only the purely intuitive and purely signitive acts. Drawing in now also the *mixed* acts, generally comprised under the title *intuition*, their peculiarity is thereby designated: that they have a representing content which functions, in respect of *one* part of the presented objectivity, as depicting or self-presenting representant, and in respect of the *complementary* part as mere *pointing*. We must therefore coordinate to the purely signitive and purely intuitive representants the *mixed* ones, which represent *at once signitively and intuitively*, and indeed *in reference to the same intentional essence*.

We can now say: *every concretely complete objectifying act has the three components: quality, matter, and representing content*. According as this content functions as pure signitive or pure intuitive representant, or as both at once, the act is a purely signitive, a purely intuitive, or a mixed act.

### **§ 26\. Continuation. Representation or apprehension. The matter as the apprehension-sense, the apprehension-form and the apprehended content. Differentiating characterization of the intuitive and signitive apprehension**

It now becomes a question *how this functioning is to be understood*, since *a priori* the possibility exists that the same content, in combination with the same quality and matter, functions in this threefold way. It is clear that only the *phenomenological peculiarity of the unity-form* can give the difference, as a phenomenologically findable one, its content. This form specially connects the *matter* and the *representant*. The representative function does not suffer through the change of *quality*: whether, e.g., the fantasy-appearance counts for us as the presentification of a real object or as mere imagining changes nothing in its being *image-presentation*, in its content bearing the function of *image-content*. We therefore call the *phenomenological unity between matter and representant*, insofar as it lends the latter the character *as* representant, the *form of representation*, and the whole produced through it the *representation* simply (*Repräsentation schlechthin*). This designation stamps the relation between representing and represented content (the object or object-part which is represented) according to its phenomenological ground. Leaving the phenomenologically not-given object out of play — only to express that, where the content functions as representant (and more closely as representant of this or that kind and for this or that objective element), we are again and again otherwise "*disposed*" with it — we speak of the change of *apprehension* (*Auffassung*). We can thus also designate the form of representation as *apprehension-form* (*Auffassungsform*). Since the matter, so to speak, gives the *sense* by which the representing content is apprehended, we can also speak of *apprehension-sense* (*Auffassungssinn*); wanting to hold fast the memory of the old terminus and at the same time indicate the opposition to form, we also speak of *apprehension-matter* (*Auffassungsmaterie*). Accordingly we would have to distinguish phenomenologically, in every apprehension: *apprehension-matter* or *apprehension-sense*, *apprehension-form*, and *apprehended content* — which last is to be distinguished from the *object of the apprehension*. (The expression *apperception*, though historically given, does not fit, through its false terminological opposition to *perception*; usable, by contrast, would be *apprehension*.)

The next question concerns the *differentiating characterization* of the various modes of representation or apprehension, which, according to what was said above, can be different even at *identity* of the apprehension-matter (the "*as what*" of the apprehension). In the previous chapter we characterized the differences of the representations through the differences of the *fulfilment-forms*; in the present connection it is aimed at an *inner* characterization, restricting itself to the proper *descriptive content* of the intentions. Using the beginnings of an analytic clarification that offered themselves in the earlier treatment, and at the same time the advances we have meanwhile made in the general understanding of the representations, the following series of ideas results.

We take our point of departure from the remark that the *signitive* representation establishes between the matter and the representant a *contingent, external* relation, the *intuitive* representation, however, an *essential, inner* one. The contingency consists, in the first case, in this: that *identically the same signification* is to be thought of as attached to *any content whatever*. The *significative matter* needs only *some* supporting content überhaupt; but between its specific peculiarity and the content's own specific stock we find *no bond* of necessity — the matter "*chooses*" no content, as the intuitive matter does, but is compatible with any.

Quite otherwise in the case of the purely *intuitive* representation. Here there obtains an *inner, necessary connection between the matter and the representant*, determined through the specific content of the two. As intuitive representant of an object only a content can serve which is *similar or equal* to it. Phenomenologically expressed: *as what* we apprehend a content (in which apprehension-sense) does not stand quite free to us; and not merely from *empirical* grounds — for empirically *every* apprehension, even the significative, is necessary — but because the content-to-be-apprehended sets us *limits* through a certain sphere of *similarity and equality*, hence through its specific content. This inwardness of the relation binds *not only the apprehension-matter as a whole and the whole content*, but *their bilateral parts piece for piece*. So in the presupposed case of *pure* intuition. In the case of *impure* intuition the specific unity is a *partial* one: *one part* of the matter — the matter of the reduced and then naturally pure intuition — gives the intuitive sense in which the content is apprehended; the *remaining* part of the matter undergoes no representation through equality or similarity, but through mere *contiguity*. That is, in the mixed intuition the representing content functions, according to one part of the matter, as intuitive, according to the complementary part as signitive representant.

If one finally asks what makes it that the same content, in the sense of the same matter, can be apprehended once in the manner of an intuitive, the other time in that of a signitive representant — or in what the different peculiarity of the *apprehension-form* consists — Husserl is unable to give a further-leading answer. It is, he says, presumably a matter of a *phenomenologically irreducible difference*.

In these considerations we have regarded the *representation* as unity of matter and representing content *for itself*. Going back again to the *full acts*, they present themselves as *connections* between the *act-quality* and the (whether intuitive or signitive) *representation*. The whole acts one calls *intuitive* or *signitive* — a difference, then, determined through the woven-in representations. The study of the fulfilment-relations had led us above to the concept of the *intuitive content* or the *fullness* of an act. Comparing this concept-formation with the present one, it delimits the *purely intuitive representation* belonging to an act of *impure* intuition (= the *pure intuition*). The "*fullness*" was a concept coined specially for the comparative consideration of the acts in their *fulfilling* function. The opposite limiting case to pure intuition — *pure signification* — is naturally the same as *purely signitive representation*.

### **§ 27\. Representations as necessary presentational substrata in all acts. Last clarification of the talk of the different ways of the relation of consciousness to an object**

*Every objectifying act includes a representation in itself.* Every act überhaupt is, according to the expositions of the Fifth Investigation, either itself an objectifying one or has such an act as its foundation. Hence the *ultimate foundation of all acts* are "*presentations*" in the sense of *representations* (*Repräsentationen*).

The talk of the *different way of the relation* of an act to its object has, according to the considerations hitherto, the following essential multivocities. It concerns:

(1) The *quality* of the acts — the modes of belief, mere leaving-undecided, wishing, doubting, and so on. (2) The underlying *representation*, and indeed: (a) the *apprehension-form* — whether the object is presentational merely signitively, or intuitively, or in mixed fashion; here belong also the differences between *perception-presentation*, *fantasy-presentation*, and so forth; (b) the *apprehension-matter* — whether the object is presented in this or that "*sense*," e.g., significatively through different meanings fronting this same object but determining it differently; (c) the *apprehended contents* — whether the object is presented by means of this or that *sign*, or by means of this or that *presenting content*. (Exactly seen, it is a matter, in this second case, by virtue of the lawful relation between intuitive representants, matter, and form, at the same time of differences which, even at equal matter, concern the *form*.)

### **§ 28\. Intentional essence and fulfilling sense. Cognitive essence. Intuitions *in specie***

In the First Investigation we opposed to the *meaning* the *fulfilling sense* (*erfüllender Sinn*) — or, to the intending meaning, the fulfilling one — by pointing out that in fulfilment the object is intuitively "*given*" in the same way in which the mere meaning *means* it. What thereby coincides with the meaning, ideally conceived, we took as the *fulfilling sense*, and said that through this coincidence the mere meaning-intention (or the expression) gains *relation to the intuitive object* — that the expression expresses it and just it.

Therein lies, if we use the later-introduced concept-formations, that the *fulfilling sense*, as the *intentional essence* of the fulfilling intuitive act, coincides with the meaningful essence (the intentional essence of the meaning-intention). The fulfilling sense, taken as a *species* (the ideal-identical to be grasped through ideating abstraction in the manifold of mutually corresponding intuitions), is just the *same* as the meaning-essence raised to the species — namely insofar as the matters coincide, and only the *fullness*, the differentiating moment of the intuitions, falls outside this coincidence.

This concept-formation is throughout correct and sufficient for the purpose of designating the wholly general feature of the situation where a signitive intention gains relation to its intuitively presented object — hence of bringing to expression the important insight that the *meaningful essence* of the signitive (expressing) act *recurs identically* in the corresponding intuitive act, despite the phenomenological difference of the bilateral acts, and that the living identification-unity realizes the coincidence itself and at the same time the relation of the expression to the expressed. On the other hand it is clear that, precisely by virtue of this identity, the fulfilling sense *implies nothing of the fullness* — that it therefore does *not comprise the entire content of the intuitive act insofar as this comes into epistemological consideration*. One might take offence that we have taken the intentional essence so narrowly, whereby so important a component of the act — indeed one decisive for knowledge — remains excluded. What guided us was the thought that there must count as the *essence* of an objectifying intention that which no intention of this kind can lack, or which is not freely variable in any such intention without its being affected, according to ideal necessity, in respect of its relation to the objective.

But the purely signitive acts are "*empty*" intentions; they lack the moment of fullness, and thus for objectifying acts überhaupt only the unity of *quality and matter* can count as *essence*. Now one might object that signitive intentions are not possible without a *sensuous foothold*, that in their way they too have *intuitive fullness*. But this — in the sense both of our expositions about signitive representants and of the earlier ones about improper and proper illustration — is in truth *no* fullness at all. Or rather, it is indeed fullness, but not that of the *signitive* act, but of the act *founding* it, in which the sign constitutes itself as an intuitive object. This fullness can, we saw, vary *limitlessly* without touching the signitive intention and all that concerns *its* object. With regard to this state of affairs, and at the same time in heed of the circumstance that even with the *intuitive* acts the fullness can vary (though limited) while it continues to mean the same object, with the same determinacies and qualitatively in the same way, it is clear that in any case a *terminus* is needed which designates the bare unity of *quality and matter*.

On the other hand it is now also useful to form a concept of *more comprehensive content*. We define accordingly the *cognitive essence* of an objectifying act (*erkenntnismäßiges Wesen*, as against its merely meaningful essence) as *the entire content coming into consideration for the knowledge-function*. To it then belong the three components *quality, matter, and fullness* (or intuitive content); or, if we want to avoid the overlapping of the two latter and have disjunct components: *quality, matter, and intuitively representing content* — of which the latter, and with it the "fullness," *falls away* with the empty intentions.

*All objectifying acts of the same cognitive essence are, for the ideal interest of the critique of knowledge, "the same" act.* When we speak of *objectifying acts in specie*, we have the corresponding *idea* in view. Likewise with the restricting talk of *intuitions in specie*, and the like.

### **§ 29\. Complete and lacunary intuitions. Adequate and objectively complete illustration. Essence**

In an intuitive presentation a *different measure of intuitive fullness* is possible. This talk of a different measure points, as we have discussed, to possible *fulfilment-series*; progressing in them, we come to know the object ever better, by means of a presenting content that is ever more similar to the object and grasps it ever more livingly or fully. But we also know that intuition can take place where whole sides and parts of the meant object do not fall into appearance at all — i.e., the presentation is equipped with an intuitive content that contains *no* presenting representants of these sides and parts, so that they are presentational only "*improperly*," by means of woven-in signitive intentions. With reference to these differences — which still determine very different modes of presentation for one and the same object meant according to the same matter — we spoke above of differences of the *extent* (*Umfang*) of fullness. Two important possibilities are here to be distinguished.

(1) The intuitive presentation *presents its object adequately*, i.e., with an intuitive content of such fullness that to each component of the object — *as* it is meant in this presentation — there corresponds a *representing* component of the intuitive content. (2) Or this is *not* the case; the presentation contains only an *incomplete adumbration* of the object — it presents it *inadequately*.

Here it is a matter of adequacy and inadequacy of a presentation *to its object*. But since adequacy is also spoken of, in the fulfilment-context, in a wider sense, Husserl introduces yet another terminology: we will speak of *complete* (*vollständig*) and *lacunary* (*lückenhaft*) intuitions (more specially: perceptions or imaginings). All *pure* intuitions are complete. That the converse does *not* hold — that the division carried out thus does not simply coincide with that into *pure* and *impure* intuitions — the following shows at once.

Namely, whether the presentations are *simple* or *complex* is in no way presupposed in the distinction carried out. The intuitive presentations can be composed in a *twofold* way.

(A) So that the relation to the object is *simple*, insofar as the act (more specially, the matter) exhibits *no part-acts* (or no separate matters) which *for themselves already present the same whole object*. This does not exclude that the act is built up of partial intentions — though homogeneously fused — which refer to the single parts or sides of the object. *Such* composition one will scarcely be able to avoid assuming with the "external" perceptions and imaginations, and accordingly we have proceeded.

On the other side stands (B) the kind of composition which builds up the total act out of *part-acts each of which is for itself already a full intuitive presentation of this same object*. This concerns the exceedingly remarkable *continuous syntheses* which join a manifold of perceptions belonging to the same object into a single "many-sided" or "all-sided" perception, viewing the object continuously "*in changing position*"; and likewise the corresponding syntheses of imagination. In the continuity of continued identity-fusion not falling apart into separate acts, the identical *pure* object appears here only a *single* time, and not as often as single acts are distinguishable. But it appears in continuously *changing* content-fullness; and at the same time the *matters* — and likewise the *qualities* — remain in continuous coincidence, so that throughout the whole continuous synthesis the consciousness of *the same* object, determined in the same way, is maintained, while the fullness alone flows and changes.

To these continuous syntheses the distinction between *adequacy* and *inadequacy* also refers. For example, of an external thing an adequate presentation of the all-sided surface-shaping is possible in the form of the *synthesis*, but impossible in the form of the *objectively-simple* presentation.

Of the *complete* intuitions, the objectively *simple* are manifestly *pure* intuitions, but not always the objectively *composite* ones. The pure intuition corresponding to an empirical thing (and denied us) is, to be sure, in a certain way contained *in* the complete synthetic intuition of it, but, so to speak, in *scattered* fashion and ever again mingled with signitive representants. But if we *reduce* this synthetic intuition to its pure content, there results not the pure intuition of the objectively simple presentation, but a *continuity* of intuitive contents in which each objective moment comes not once but *repeatedly* to presenting representation, to ever-changing adumbration — and only the continuity of identity-fusion constitutes the phenomenon of the *singularity* of the object.

When an intuitive act functions as fullness-giving — in respect of a signitive one, say an explicit meaning-intention — analogous possibilities show themselves. The object, as it is meant, can be *adequately* or *inadequately* illustrated. To the former belong, in the case of complex meanings, *two separable perfections*. *First*, that to all parts (members, moments, forms) of the meaning which themselves have the character of meanings there accrues fulfilment through corresponding parts of the fulfilling intuition. *Second*, that now, on the side of the fulfilling intuition, there obtains for itself adequacy in respect of the object, insofar as it is somehow meant in the articulations and forms of this meaning drawn into the fulfilment-function.

The first thus determines the *completeness of the adaptation of signitive acts to corresponding intuitions*; the second the *completeness of the adaptation of signitive acts — by means of complete intuitions — to the object itself*. Thus the expression *a green house* can be illustrated by a house being really intuitively presentational to us *as* a green one; this would be the first perfection. For the second there would be needed an *adequate presentation* of a green house. Mostly one will have only the former in view where there is talk of adequate illustration of expressions. But to mark the *double* perfection terminologically, we will speak of *objectively complete illustration* of the signitive presentation, as against its *adequate but objectively lacunary* illustration.

Similar relations obtain also *in the case of the conflicting, instead of fulfilling, illustration*. When a signitive intention disappoints itself on the ground of intuition — say in that it means a *green* A, while the same A (and perhaps even an A überhaupt) is red and just now intuited as red — then the *objective completeness* of the intuitive realization of the *conflict* demands that *all components* of the meaning-intention find their objectively complete illustration. It is therefore necessary that not only the *A*\-intention objectively-completely fulfil itself in the given intuition of the A, but also that the *Green*\-intention fulfil itself — though, of course, in *another* intuition "incompatible" with that intuition of the red A. Then it is not the bare signitive, but rather the objectively-completely-fulfilled *Green*\-intention that enters into conflict with the *Red*\-intuition, whereby at the same time these two appearance-moments themselves enter into *total*, and the belonging intuition-wholes into *partial*, "*contest*" (*Wettstreit*). Above all this concerns, as one may well say, the *intuitive* (or the *presenting*) contents of these fulfilling acts.

Where nothing is specially indicated, in the following, under the title *illustrations*, those of the kind of *fulfilments* are meant.

The differences of fullness at equal quality and matter give rise to yet another important concept-formation. We say *two intuitive acts possess the same essence* (*dieselbe Essenz*) when their *pure intuitions have the same matter*. Thus a perception, and the whole — possibly unbounded — series of fantasy-presentations each of which presents the same object with the same *extent of fullness*, have one and the same essence. All objectively complete intuitions of one and the same matter have the same essence.

A *signitive presentation* has in itself *no* essence; one ascribes to it, however, in the *improper* sense, a certain essence when it admits *complete fulfilment* through an intuition out of the possible manifold of intuitions of this essence — or, what is the same, when it has a "*fulfilling sense*." With this, Husserl notes, the true meaning of the scholastic terminus — which means the *possibility* of a "*concept*" — should be laid clear.

## **Chapter Four: Compatibility and Incompatibility**

### **§ 30\. The ideal distinction of meanings into possible (real) and impossible (imaginary)**

Not to *every* signitive intention can intuitive acts adapt themselves in the manner of "*objectively complete illustration*." Accordingly the *meaning-intentions* fall apart into *possible* (in-themselves compatible) and *impossible* (in-themselves incompatible, *imaginary*). This division — or the law underlying it — concerns (exactly as holds for all the other propositions set up here) *not the individual acts*, but *generally their cognitive essences* and therein their generally-to-be-grasped *matters*. For it is not possible that a signitive intention of the matter M should find the possibility of fulfilment in some intuition, and another signitive intention of *the same* matter M should lack this possibility. The possibilities and impossibilities do not speak of the intuitions actually findable in any empirical consciousness-complexions; they are *not real but ideal* possibilities, grounding purely in the specific characters. In the sphere of explicitness — to which one can restrict oneself without essential loss — the *axiom* runs: *the meanings (in specie the concepts and propositions) fall into possible and impossible (real and imaginary)*. (In a footnote Husserl warns that the understanding of these analytic clarifications depends wholly on the strict concepts established hitherto being kept securely in view and not having the vague presentations of popular talk slipped under them.)

The *possibility* (reality) of a meaning lets itself define, if we draw in the above concept-formations, by this: *that to it, in the sphere of the objectifying acts in specie, an adequate essence corresponds — namely one whose matter is identical with its own*; or, what is the same, that it has a *fulfilling sense*; or also, that *there is a complete intuition in specie whose matter is identical with its own*. This "there is" has here the same *ideal* sense as in mathematics; to lead it back to the possibility of corresponding individuals does not mean leading it back to something *other*, but expressing it through a mere *equivalent turn* — at least when the possibility is understood as *pure* (hence not as empirical) and in *this* sense as "real."

The idea of the possibility of a meaning expresses, on closer view, properly the *generalization of the fulfilment-relation in the case of objectively complete illustration*; and the above definitions are, instead of bare verbal explanations, *expressions of an insight* into the essential connection between the species *meaning* and the *fulfilling essences* belonging to it.

Therein lies the particular *law* that, where that relation between the matter of a meaning and the matter of an essence obtains, the "*possibility*" too takes place; and conversely, that in every case of possibility this relation obtains. Further: that such an ideal relation occurs at all — i.e., that the generalization objectively takes place, hence is for its part "*possible*" — therein lies again a lawfulness, simply stamped in the words: *there are "possible" meanings* (where it is to be noted that "meaning" does not mean "act of meaning"). Not every *empirical* relation permits such generalization. If we find this intuited paper rough, we cannot pronounce generally "paper is rough," as we may pronounce, on the ground of a certain actual meaning, "*this meaning is possible (real).*"

Just for this reason, in the proposition *every meaning is either possible or impossible* there lies not a single case of the law of excluded middle in the familiar sense — which pronounces the exclusion of contradictory predicates of *individual subjects* and can pronounce such exclusion outright only for such subjects. The exclusion of contradictory predicates in an *ideal* sphere (e.g., the arithmetical, the meaning-sphere, etc.) is not self-evident at all, but must in every such sphere be proved anew or set up axiomatically. Husserl recalls that we may *not* say "every *kind* of paper is either rough or not rough"; for therein would lie that each single paper of any kind is rough, or each single one not rough — and such assertions are naturally not correct for arbitrary kind-formations. Accordingly, behind the division of *meanings* into possible and impossible there really lies a proper, content-rich generic *law*, a law that governs the phenomenological moments in ideal fashion, namely by connecting their species in the manner of generic propositions.

To pronounce such an axiom one must *see* it (*einsehen*), and that we possess evidence in our case is certain. Realizing, e.g., the meaning of the expression *white surface* on the ground of intuition, we experience the *reality* of the concept; the intuitive appearance really sets something white and a surface — and indeed precisely *as* a white surface — before us; and therein lies that the fulfilling intuition presents not merely a white surface überhaupt, but brings it through its content to intuitive *givenness* as completely as the meaning-intention demands.

To possibility there ranges *impossibility* as an equally-warranted idea, which is not merely to be defined as the *negation* of possibility but is to be realized through a *proper phenomenological fact*. This is in any case the presupposition for the concept of impossibility ever finding application, and especially for its being able to occur in an axiom — including the axiom: *there are impossible meanings*. The equivalence of the talk of *impossibility* and *incompatibility* (*Unverträglichkeit*) points us to the fact that this phenomenological fact is to be sought in the *domain of conflict*.

### **§ 31\. Compatibility or consistency as an ideal relation in the widest sphere of contents überhaupt. Compatibility of "concepts" as meanings**

Husserl starts from the concept of *compatibility* or *consistency* (*Verträglichkeit oder Vereinbarkeit*), which has validity in the widest sphere *of contents überhaupt* (of objects in the very widest sense). Two *contents* which are parts of any whole are united in it, hence they are also *compatible*, consistent in the unity of a whole. This seems an empty self-evidence. But these same contents *would be* compatible even if they were *contingently* not united. Surely it has a good sense to speak of the compatibility of contents whose actual union always remained and will remain excluded. *But if* two contents *are* united, their unity proves not only their own compatibility, but also that of an ideal multitude of others — namely all pairs equal to them and generically similar.

It is clear what this aims at, and what, pronounced as an *axiom*, is by no means an empty assertion: that *compatibility belongs not to the scattered individuals but to the content-species*; that, when e.g. the moments *redness* and *roundness* have once been found united, there can now, through *ideating abstraction*, be *won* and thereby *given* a *complex species* which encloses the two species *redness* and *roundness* in their likewise specifically grasped *connection-form*. It is the ideal "*existence*" of this complex species which *a priori* grounds the compatibility of redness and roundness in every conceivable single case — a compatibility which is thus an ideally valid relation, whether or not empirical union occurs anywhere in all the world. If the valuable sense of the talk of compatibility thus everywhere determines itself as the ideal *being* of the belonging complex species, an important point is still to be noted: *that the talk of compatibility has at all times relation to some kind of whole* (the kind just decisive for the logical interest). We use this talk in connection with the consideration whether given contents let themselves fit together according to certain forms or not — a question decided affirmatively with the intuitive exhibition of a whole of the kind in question.

The *correlate* of this contentual compatibility is the "*possibility*" of the complex meanings. This results from the above criteria of possibility. The adequate essence (or the complete illustration of the corresponding complex content) grounds the compatibility of its parts, as, conversely, to this compatibility there belongs an essence and a corresponding meaning. The reality of a meaning thus says the same as: the meaning is an *objectively complete "expression"* of an intuitive contentual compatibility. In the limiting case of a *simple* content one may define the validity of the simple species as compatibility "*with itself*." That the connection between expression and expressed (meaning and corresponding, i.e. "objectively completely adequate," intuition) is itself again a connection of compatibility, whose particular specific content we determined above, is self-evident. On the other hand, in the talk of *compatibility in respect of the meanings* ("concepts") it is a matter not merely of their compatibility into a whole — be it even into a meaning-whole (that would rather be the *purely logical-grammatical* compatibility in the sense of the Fourth Investigation) — but, according to the above, of the compatibility of the *meaning* into a "*possible*" meaning, i.e. into a meaning which is compatible with corresponding intuition into the unity of objectively adequate knowledge. Accordingly it is here a matter of a *transferred* sense.

The same will have to be said of "*possibility*." The *originary* possibility (or reality) is the validity, the *ideal existence of a species*; at least it is thereby fully guaranteed. Then the *intuition* of a single instance corresponding to it — and again the single thing to be intuited itself — is called possible. Finally the *meaning* fulfilling itself with objective completeness in such an intuition is called possible. The difference between the talk of compatibility and of possibility lies merely in this: that while the latter designates the simple *validity of a species*, the former (before the extension of the concept by the limiting case) designates the *relation of the part-species* of a unitarily valid species — and, with reference to that, also the relation: of the part-intuitions of a unitary intuition; of the part-contents to be intuited within a total content to be intuited as unitary; of the part-meanings to be fulfilled within a total meaning to be fulfilled as unitary.

Finally Husserl remarks that, like the concepts *possibility* and *compatibility*, so also the concept of *essence* first *lends* its originary sense to the meaning-domain through *transference*. This originary concept of essence is expressed through the proposition: *every valid species is an essence*.

### **§ 32\. Incompatibility (conflict) of contents überhaupt**

*Incompatible*, then — to pursue the opposite case into its general grounds — contents are when they conflict, in the unity of the same conjoining (the same connection-form), with one another; when, in the synthesis aiming at their union, no full coincidence but only a conflict comes to pass.

Whence, however, are we to know this? In empirical single cases we attempt to bring contents to unity; sometimes it succeeds, sometimes not — we experience an *irresistible resistance*. But the *factual failure* does not prove the *necessary* failure. Could not greater force finally overcome the resistance? Yet in the empirical striving for the contents in question and for the removal of their "*contest*," we experience a *singular relation* of the contents which again grounds in their specific stock and, in its ideality, is independent of all empirical striving and of everything else of the single case. It is the *relation of conflict* (*Widerstreit*).

This relation thus sets quite *determinate content-kinds* — and indeed *within quite determinate content-combinations* — into relation. Colours do not conflict with one another *überhaupt*, but only in determinate contexts: several colour-moments of different specific difference are, as simultaneous complete overspreadings of one and the same bodily extension, *incompatible*, while in the manner of *alongside-one-another* within the unitary extension they are very well compatible. And this holds generally. Never is a content of the kind *q* *simply* incompatible with a content of the kind *p*; rather, the talk of their incompatibility always refers to a content-connection of *determinate kind* G(α, …, p) which contains p and into which q too is now to be knit. In the "is to" there lies, to be sure, the reference to an *intention* — a presentation- and mostly also will-intention — which thinks the q (given in some intuition A(q)) carried into the present intuition of the G, i.e. presents it *signitively* in it. But from this intention we now abstract, just as with compatibility we abstracted from the intention upon union, and likewise from the process of carrying-over and uniting. We hold fast merely that here a descriptively peculiar relation enters between the q (the rest of the A is arbitrarily variable and plays no further role) and the p of the content-whole G, and that this relation is independent of the individual of the case — in other words, that it grounds purely in the species G, p, q. The specific feature of the *conflict-consciousness* belongs to these species; i.e., the generalization of the situation is real, is realizable in an intuitively-unitary generality-consciousness; it yields a unitary, valid ("possible") species which, on the ground of the G, unites the p and q *through conflict*.

### **§ 33\. How conflict too can found unity. Relativity of the talk of compatibility and conflict**

To this latter expression and proposition there attaches a chain of disquieting doubt-questions. A conflict *unites*? The *unity* of conflict a unity of *possibility*? Certainly, unity überhaupt grounds possibility; but does not this simply *exclude* conflict, incompatibility?

The difficulties resolve themselves when we recall that not only the talk of an *incompatibility* but also that of a *compatibility* necessarily has reference to a certain whole G governing — subjectively speaking — the intention. Looking at its specific content, we call the parts compatible. We would call the same contents p, q… (functioning here as parts) incompatible if, in the symbolic intention upon their unity within just such a whole, we experienced, instead of intuitive *unity*, rather intuitive *conflict*. The correlation of the two possible cases in their reference to the respectively determinate kind of whole or connection of the compatible (or incompatible) contents is clear. This reference co-determines the sense of these termini. We call the p, q… *compatible* not simply and with mere regard to their being united somehow, indifferently how, but with regard to their being united in the manner of the G, and to this union of the p, q… excluding the conflict of the same p, q… with reference to the same G. And again, contents p, q… are called *incompatible* not simply, but with regard to their "not agreeing" within the frame of some unity of the unity-kind G just interesting us — i.e., because the intention upon such a unity brings about a conflict *instead* of such unity; whereby the exclusion of correlate unity through correlate conflict again plays its role.

The conflict-consciousness grounds "*disunity*," since it excludes the G-unity of the p, q… in question. In this direction of interest, the conflict itself counts not as a unity but as *separatedness*, not as "connection" but as "separation." But changing the references, an *incompatibility* too can function *as* unity — e.g. as unity between the character of the conflict and the contents which are "*separated*" through it. This character is compatible with these contents and perhaps incompatible with others. If the ruling intention goes upon the *conflict-whole* as a whole of the just-named parts, then, where we find it — where the conflict thus takes place — there obtains *compatibility* of these parts, i.e. of the p, q… in their connection and in that of the conflict separating them.

Where the conflict is lacking and this lack becomes intuitive, there attaches to the now-in-different-intuitions-scattered elements a *new* conflict-consciousness. *This* conflict is not conflict between the members of the intended conflict (whose lack it precisely indicates), but a conflict that attaches to the contents p, q… united conflictlessly in *one* intuition and to the moment *conflict* becoming intuitive in *another* intuition.

The paradox of the talk of a uniting *through conflict* thus clarifies itself through heed to the *relativity* of these concepts. One may no longer object: conflict excludes unity *simply*; in the form of conflict ultimately anything and everything can be "united"; where unity is lacking, there conflict obtains, and to let it count again as unity would mean to liquefy the absolutely rigid opposition between unity and conflict and to devalue its true sense. — No, we may now say, conflict and unity exclude each other *not* "simply," but in a respectively determinate correlation varying only from case to case. In *this* they exclude each other as rigid opposites; only if one restricts the "simply" to such an always-tacitly-presupposed correlation can we be satisfied with the opponent's assertion. Further: in the form of conflict not everything lets itself be united, but only all that precisely *founds* a conflict, and nothing of what is united and compatible. For in the sense of this talk of uniting in the form of a conflict it lies that the *form of conflict* of some p, q… thought in a certain connection G₀ is to count *as* unity — a unity which as unity really establishes union, compatibility, and thus corresponds to our above G. But if between the p, q… in respect of the connection G₀ unity obtains, then these p, q… cannot, in respect of *this* connection, be brought into a relation of conflict, since connection überhaupt is union.

Hence in the form of conflict not everything is in truth to be united — and least of all because (as the further objection ran), where unity is lacking, this is attested by a contradiction which would establish unity through conflict. We understand the confusion here committed, the jumbling-together of the founding relations. The *lack* of the unity G₀ is characterized by the conflict attaching to the p, q… in the connection determined by the idea G₀. But this conflict creates not the unity G₀ but *another* unity. In respect of the former it has the character of "separation," in respect of the new unity that of "connection." Here everything is in order. An example for elucidation: in respect of a known phenomenal connection, *red* and *green* are called incompatible, *red* and *round* compatible. The character of conflict determines, in the first case, the incompatibility; it establishes "separation" between red and green. Notwithstanding, it helps establish a unity in respect of *another* kind of connection — namely the connection-kind "*conflict between sensuous marks of a phenomenal object*." Now, then, conflict between red and green is *unity*, and naturally unity in respect of the elements *conflict, red, green*. By contrast, now "conflict of red and round" is *disunity*, and indeed in respect of these elements *conflict, red, round*.

### **§ 34\. Some axioms**

After this clarification — very important for our fundamental analysis — of the sense of the compatibility-relations, we can fix the *primitive axioms* and carry out their phenomenological elucidation. First would come into consideration the axiom *of the existence of compatibility-relations* (compatibility or incompatibility), which, after our analysis of the underlying phenomenological relations, is without further ado clear.

Closer consideration requires the next axiom to be set up: that *unity and conflict* (or *compatibility* and *incompatibility*) — the respective pairs referred to *the same* foundation of correlation — *mutually exclude* each other (i.e. again: that they are with one another *incompatible*). It now need no longer be stressed that incompatibility means not the mere privation of compatibility — not the mere fact that some union objectively does not occur. Union and conflict are phenomenologically differently founded ideas; and a content-rich proposition is therefore really pronounced therewith, namely that if a p *conflicts* with a q according to the unity-form G(p, q, …) (and the conflicting is a phenomenologically positive character), then not at the same time is the union of the p with the q in the sense of the same G "possible." And conversely: if this union takes place, the corresponding conflict is "impossible." Phenomenologically there underlies this what has already come to light in the above discussion: namely that the actual *conflict* between p, q, … — when we try to unite it with the *corresponding unity* p, q, … (i.e. to lay the unity-kind G, somewhere actually intuited by means of certain m, n, …, actually under the p, q, … in the case of the belonging conflict) — gives rise to a *new conflict*, which has its foundations in the first conflict and in the elsewhere-intuited unity-character. The analogue shows itself in the converse case (which, moreover, is also to be recognized as an application of the first axiom).

The proposition *there obtains a conflict* and the proposition *there obtains not unity* between the same but arbitrary p, q, … say one and the same. *Every "not" expresses a conflict.*

When the conflict attaches to the p, q being-to-conflict with one another — hence to p, q, … being one in the form of conflict — then p, q, … are *united*. In other words: *when p, q do not conflict, are not not-united, then they are united* (axiom of double negation); from which follows: *one of the two takes place, either union or conflict — a "third" there is not*. Four possibilities are here to be distinguished, expressing themselves thus: conflict / union takes place; conflict / union does not take place. But "not-conflict" is, by the previous axiom, another word for, and equivalent to, *union*.

The last clarification of these axioms, and their relation to the purely logical ones, goes beyond the boundary of the present inquiry. What we have adduced is only to point to the inner relations we will pursue later, and to give us a living consciousness that we are here already working at the *phenomenological foundation of pure logic*.

### **§ 35\. Incompatibility of concepts as meanings**

In thinking, incompatibility, like compatibility, occurs in connection with *signitive* intentions directed upon certain connections, and thus in connection with signitive and intuitive identifications. The concept of incompatibility delimited in the last paragraphs refers, however, *not* to intentions; rather, the concept referred to intentions — and that means: to the meanings (concepts, propositions) themselves — is a *derivative* one, to be defined by recourse to the foregoing.

This transferred concept is a special case of the original one, but of a quite determinate content restricted to the *disappointment-relations*. The analogue holds here of what we set forth above concerning compatibility. Again the talk of incompatibility, in application to meanings ("concepts"), does *not* mean every arbitrary ideal incompatibility of them — e.g. not the *purely grammatical*; it concerns solely the relation of *part-meanings* of a complex meaning which does *not* fulfil itself in objectively complete illustration, but disappoints itself, or *can* disappoint itself. Manifestly there underlies the disappointment a *conflict of the illustrated contents*, whereby it is to be heeded that the conflict itself is *not* meant and expressed; otherwise the conflict would belong to the fulfilling "intuition," and the expression would, as a thoroughly *possible* one, *adequately express the objective impossibility*.

The connection between the meaning and *each* of the unitary intuitions which relieve one another in the process of intuitive conflict is likewise that of conflict (under partial coincidence).

The ideal laws of possibility to be set up for the *meanings* ground themselves on the originary and more general concepts (or on the axioms set up for these same above, and still further to be completed). Here belong propositions such as: that incompatibility and compatibility of the same meanings and with reference to the same connections *exclude each other*; that of a pair of *contradictory* meanings (i.e. such that the one means *as incompatible* the same as the other means *as in-itself-united*) one is possible and the other impossible; that the *negative of a negative* — i.e. a meaning which presents the incompatibility of a certain matter M itself again *as* an incompatibility — is *equivalent* to the corresponding *positive* (this positive being defined as the meaning which presents the inner consistency of the same M by means of the same presentation-matter, remaining after deduction of the negations).

Self-evidently a real *theory of meanings* according to their logical relations requires that all such propositions be set up and proved in *systematic order*.

Husserl breaks off these lacunary considerations, reserving their completion for later inquiries. Above all, in the logical interest, a much more widely and completely carried-through *phenomenology and theory of the identifications and distinctions* (and quite especially of the *partial* ones), and of their visibly close relations to the doctrine of unity and conflict, will be required.

## **Chapter Five: The Ideal of Adequation. Evidence and Truth**

### **§ 36\. Introduction**

Of the *qualities* of the acts there was, in the considerations hitherto, no talk; nothing was presupposed of them. Possibility and impossibility have no special relation to the qualities. On the possibility, e.g., of a *proposition* it has no influence whether we realize the proposition-matter as the matter of a *positing* act (not of an assenting, approving, or accepting act, but of a simply taking belief-act) or whether we have it given in qualitatively modified fashion as the matter of a mere *presenting*; it always holds that the proposition is "possible" when the concrete act of propositional meaning admits the fulfilling identification with an *objectively complete intuition of equal matter*. And likewise it is irrelevant whether this fulfilling intuition is a *perception* or a mere *fantasy-formation* and the like. Since the production of fantasy-images is subject in incomparably greater measure to our discretion than that of perceptions and positings überhaupt, we are wont to refer possibility preferentially to *fantasy-imagery*. As possible there then counts what — objectively spoken — lets itself be realized in the manner of an *adequate fantasy-image*, whether or not it ever succeeds for us, the empirical single individual. But by virtue of the ideal connection between perception and imagination — according to which to every perception there corresponds *a priori* a possible imagination — this proposition is equivalent with ours, and the restriction of the concept to imagination is *inessential*.

It will now be a matter of weighing, in all brevity, the influence of the just-indicated differences (of quality, and of perception versus imagination) on the *fulfilment-relations*, in order to gain at least a provisional conclusion for our considerations and an outlook for the further inquiries.

### **§ 37\. The fulfilment-function of perception. The ideal of the ultimate fulfilment**

In respect of the way in which the objective becomes presentational in the presentation, the *perfection-differences of fullness* have proved significant. The lowest level is formed by the *signitive* acts; they have no fullness at all. The *intuitive* acts have fullness, but in gradual differences of more and less.

But the still-so-great perfection of an imagination leaves a *difference* as against perception subsisting: it does not give the object *itself*, not even in part; it gives only its *image*, which, so long as it is image at all, is never the thing itself. This we owe to *perception*. Perception too "*gives*" the object in various gradations of perfection, in various degrees of "adumbration." The intentional character of perception is, in opposition to the mere presentification of imagination, the *making-present* (*Gegenwärtigen*, presenting). This is, as we know, an *inner* difference of the acts, and more closely one of the *form of their representation* (apprehension-form). But the presenting makes in general not a *true being-present*, but only an *appearing-as-present*, in which the objective presence — and with it the perfection of perception — shows gradations. This is taught by the regard to corresponding *increase-series of fulfilment*, to which here, as elsewhere, all exemplification of perfection in the making-presentational of the object is directed. We thereby bring to clarity that over the perception-fullness there spreads a difference which we tried to do justice to through the talk of perceptive *adumbration* — a difference that concerns not the fullness according to its *sensation-content*, its inner character, but means a *graded spreading-out of its character as "fullness,"* hence of the apprehending act-character.

Accordingly there counts for us (always independent of all that is genetic, for that these, like all similar differences, have grown associatively we know very well) many an element of the fullness as the *definitive presentation* of the corresponding objective element: it gives itself as *identical* with it, not as its mere representant, but as it *itself* in the absolute sense. Others again count as mere "colour-adumbration," "perspectival foreshortening," and the like — whereby it is clear that to such talk something corresponds also in the phenomenological content of the act and *before* all reflection. We have already touched on these differences of adumbration and found them also with imagination, only transposed into the pictorial. All adumbration has representative character, and indeed it represents *through similarity*; but the *way* of this representation through similarity is different according as the representation apprehends the adumbrating content as *image* or as *self-presentation* (self-adumbration) of the object. The ideal limit which the increase of the adumbration-fullness admits is, in the case of perception, the *absolute self* (as in imagination the absolutely-equalling image), and indeed for every side, for every presented element of the object.

Thus the consideration of the possible fulfilment-relations points to a *concluding goal of the fulfilment-increase*, in which the *full and total intention has reached its fulfilment* — and indeed not an intermediate and partial, but a *definitive and ultimate fulfilment* (*endgültige und letzte Erfüllung*). The intuitive content of this concluding presentation is the *absolute sum of possible fullness*; the intuitive representant is the *object itself, as it is in itself*. Representing and represented content are here *identically one*. And where a presentation-intention has procured itself ultimate fulfilment through this *ideally perfect perception*, there the genuine *adaequatio rei et intellectus* has established itself: *the objective is really "present" or "given" exactly as that as which it is intended*; no partial intention is any longer implied that lacked its fulfilment.

And therewith *eo ipso* is also drawn the ideal of every — and hence also of the *significative* — fulfilment; the *intellectus* is here the conceptual intention, that of the meaning. And the *adaequatio* is realized when the meant objectivity is *given* in the intuition in the strict sense, and given exactly as that as which it is thought and named. No conceptual intention that did not find its fulfilment, and indeed its *ultimate* fulfilment, insofar as the fulfilling element of the intuition itself implies nothing further of unsatisfied intentions.

One notes that the *perfection of the adequation* of the "thought" to the "thing" is a *twofold* one. On the one hand the adaptation to the *intuition* is a perfect one, since the thought means nothing that the fulfilling intuition does not present completely as belonging to it. Manifestly the two perfections distinguished earlier are here comprised: they yield what we designated as the "*objective completeness*" of the fulfilment. On the other hand, in the complete intuition itself there lies a perfection. The intuition fulfils the intention terminating in it not itself again in the manner of an intention still in need of fulfilment, but establishes the *ultimate* fulfilment of this intention. We must therefore distinguish: the perfection of the *adaptation to the intuition* (of the adequation in the natural and wider sense) from the perfection — presupposing it — of the *ultimate fulfilment* (of the adequation to the "thing itself"). Every faithful and pure description of an intuitive object or process offers an example of the former perfection. If the objective is something inwardly experienced and grasped, in reflective perception, as it is, then the *second* perfection can join — as when, e.g., in regard to a categorical judgment we are just passing, we speak of the subject-presentation of this judgment. By contrast, the former perfection is *lacking* when we call the tree standing before us an "ennobled" apple tree, or speak of "the vibration-number" of the just-sounding tone, and in general of determinacies of a perception-object which, even if co-meant in the perception-intention, do not at least fall into appearance in *more or less adumbrated* fashion.

Husserl remarks further: since the ultimate fulfilment may simply include *nothing* of unfulfilled intentions, it must ensue on the ground of a *pure* perception; an objectively complete perception which, however, carries itself out in the manner of a continuous synthesis of *impure* perceptions cannot suffice for it.

Against this way of considering — which sets the ultimate fulfilment of all intentions in perceptions — the objection will arise that the realized *generality-consciousness*, which gives the general-conceptual presentations their fullness and sets the "general object" "itself" before our eyes, builds itself up on the ground of mere *imaginations*, or is at least insensible to the difference between perception and imagination. The same holds manifestly, and in consequence of what was just said, for all evident generic statements that become evident in axiomatic fashion "on the ground of the mere concepts."

This objection points to a gap, occasionally already touched on, in our inquiry. Perception counted for us, at first as if self-evidently, as equal to *sensuous* perception, intuition as equal to *sensuous* intuition. Tacitly, without rightly bringing it to consciousness, we have often overstepped the limits of these concepts — e.g. also in the context of the considerations on compatibility: it happened everywhere we spoke of *intuition of a conflict or of a unity or of some other synthesis as such*. We shall, in the next chapter (which concerns the categorial forms überhaupt), prove the *necessity of an extension* of the concepts of perception and other intuition. For the removal of the objection we now remark only this much: that the imagination which is the basis of the generalizing abstraction does *not* for that reason exercise the actual and proper function of fulfilment — hence does not present the "corresponding" intuition. The individual single of the appearance is, as we have repeatedly stressed, not itself the general, nor does it contain it in itself in the manner of a real piece.

### **§ 38\. Positing acts in fulfilment-function. Evidence in the lax and strict sense**

Under the title *Intentions* we have hitherto treated positing and non-positing acts equally; the differences of *quality* remained out of play. We now bring them into account, in order to gain the concept of evidence in its full and proper sense.

Although the general feature of the fulfilment-character is essentially determined through the *matter*, and for a series of most important relations only the matter comes into consideration, in others the *quality* shows itself as co-determining — and this so much so that the talk of an *intention*, of an *aiming*, seems properly to fit only the *positing* acts. The *meaning* aims at the thing, and it reaches its goal or does not, according as it agrees in a certain way with the *perception* (which is here a positing act) or not. Then positing agrees with positing; the intending and fulfilling acts are *equal* in this quality. The mere *presenting*, however, is *passive*, "*it leaves the thing undecided*." Where, by chance, an adequate perception joins the mere presenting, there ensues, on the ground of the matching matters, fulfilling coincidence; but already in the transition the presentation appropriates to itself the positing-character, and the coincidence-unity itself certainly has it in homogeneous fashion. *Every actual identification (or distinction) is a positing act*, whether it be itself founded in positings or not; and this proposition adds, in few words, a fundamental characteristic determining all results of the last inquiries on the relations of compatibility — by which the theory of identifications and distinctions shows itself in still much higher measure than hitherto as a main piece of the theory of judgment. With reference to whether precisely *positing* or also *non-positing* acts function as intending and fulfilling, there clarify themselves differences such as that between *illustration* (possibly *exemplification*) and *confirmation* (corroboration, and in the opposite case refutation). The concept *confirmation* refers exclusively to *positing acts* in *relation* to their *positing* fulfilment and ultimately to their *fulfilment through perceptions*.

To this especially distinguished case Husserl devotes a closer consideration. In it the ideal of adequation yields *evidence* (*Evidenz*). In the *laxer* sense we speak of evidence wherever a positing intention (especially an assertion) finds its confirmation through a corresponding and fully-adapted perception — be it also a fitting synthesis of connected single perceptions. To speak of *degrees and levels of evidence* then has a good sense. In this respect there come into consideration the approaches of the perception to the objective completeness of its objective presentation, and then further the advances toward the last perfection-ideal: that of the *adequate perception*, the full self-appearance of the object — insofar as it was somehow meant in the intention to be fulfilled. But the *epistemologically pregnant sense* of evidence concerns exclusively this last, unsurpassable goal: the *act of this most perfect fulfilment-synthesis*, which gives the intention (e.g. the judgment-intention) the *absolute content-fullness*, that of the object itself. The object is not merely *meant*, but, as it is meant and set in one with the meaning, *given* in the strictest sense; for the rest it is indifferent whether it is a matter of an individual or general object, of an object in the narrower sense or of a *state of affairs* (the correlate of an identifying or distinguishing synthesis).

Evidence itself is, we said, the act of that most perfect coincidence-synthesis. Like every identification, it is an objectifying act; its objective correlate is called *being in the sense of truth*, or also *truth* (*Wahrheit*) — unless one prefers to allot this latter terminus to another of the series of concepts which all root in the said phenomenological situation. But here a more exact discussion is needed.

### **§ 39\. Evidence and truth**

Holding fast first the just-indicated concept of truth: *truth*, as correlate of an identifying act, is a *state of affairs*, and as correlate of a coinciding identification an *identity* — *the full agreement between meant and given as such*. This agreement is *experienced* in evidence, insofar as evidence is the actual performance of the adequate identification. On the other hand, one cannot without further ado interpret the proposition "evidence is the *experience* of truth" to mean that it is (if we grasp the concept of perception widely enough) perception, and in strict evidence: *adequate perception of truth*. For, with regard to the doubt expressed earlier, we shall have to concede that the performance of the identifying coincidence is not yet an *actual perception* of the objective agreement, but that it first becomes so through a proper act of objectifying apprehension, through a proper looking-toward the present truth. And "present" it indeed is. *A priori* the possibility exists here, at any time, of looking toward the agreement and bringing it to intentional consciousness in an adequate perception.

A second concept of truth concerns the *ideal relation* prevailing, in the coincidence-unity defined as evidence, *between the cognitive essences of the coinciding acts*. While truth in the previous sense was the *objective* corresponding to the act of evidence, truth in the present sense is the *idea belonging to the act-form* — namely the *cognitive essence, grasped as idea*, of the empirically contingent act of evidence, or the *idea of absolute adequation as such*.

We further experience, on the side of the fullness-giving act, in evidence *the given object in the manner of the meant*: it is the *fullness itself*. This too can be designated as *being, truth, the true* — namely insofar as it is here experienced not as in the mere adequate perception, but as *ideal fullness* for an intention, as *truth-making* (or as ideal fullness of the *specific cognitive essence of the intention*).

Finally, from the standpoint of the intention, the apprehension of the evidence-relation yields truth as the *correctness of the intention* (specially, e.g., *judgment-correctness*), as its being-adequate to the true object; or as the *correctness of the cognitive essence of the intention in specie*. In the latter respect, e.g., the correctness of the judgment in the logical sense of the proposition: the proposition "*directs*" itself according to the thing itself; it says, *so it is*, and it really *is* so. Therein, however, is pronounced the *ideal, hence general, possibility* that a proposition of such matter can fulfil itself at all in the sense of *strictest adequation*.

Husserl warns that the being which here comes in question (as the *objective* first sense of truth) is *not to be confused* with the *being of the copula* of the "affirmative" categorical statement. In evidence it is a matter of *total coincidence*; but this copulative being corresponds, if not always, then mostly (the property-judgment), to *partial* identifications. Yet even where a total identification comes to predication, the one being does not coincide with the other. For we note that with a *judgment-evidence* (judgment \= predicative statement) the *being in the sense of judgment-truth is experienced but not expressed*, hence never coincides with the being meant and experienced in the "*is*" of the statement. This latter being is the synthetic moment of the existent in the sense of the true — how should it express *its* being-true?

We find here *manifold agreements brought to synthesis*. The *one*, partial, predicative, is meant assertively and adequately perceived, hence itself given (what this means will gain clarity already in the next chapter through the more general doctrine of the categorial objectivations); this is the agreement between subject and predicate, the fitting of the one to the other. But *secondly* we have the agreement which *makes up the synthetic form of the act of evidence* — hence the *total coincidence* between the meaning-intention of the statement and the perception of the state of affairs itself, a coincidence carrying itself out, naturally, step by step (which does not further matter here). *This* agreement is manifestly not stated; it is not objective like that first one belonging to the judged state of affairs. Doubtless it *can* at any time be stated, and stated with evidence. It then becomes the *truth-making* state of affairs of a new evidence, of which the same holds, and so on. But at every step one must distinguish between the *truth-making* and the *evidence-constituting* state of affairs — between the objectified and the not-objectified.

The distinctions just carried out lead to a general discussion. In our presentation of the relations of the concepts *evidence* and *truth* we did not, on the *objective* side of the acts which find their strict adequation in evidence (whether in the function of intention or of fulfilment), distinguish between *states of affairs* and *other objects*. And accordingly we took no account of the phenomenological difference between *relating* acts — acts of agreement and non-agreement, predicative acts — and *non-relating* acts; hence also not of the difference between relating and non-relating meanings and ideally-grasped intentional essences überhaupt. The strict adequation can set into one *non-relating* intentions, just as well as relating ones, with their perfect fulfilments; to single out specially the domain of expressions, it need not be precisely *judgments* as statement-intentions or statement-fulfilments that come in question — *nominal* acts too can enter into an adequation. Mostly, however, the concepts *truth, correctness, the true* are grasped more narrowly than we have done, being referred to *judgments and propositions* (or their objective correlates, the *states of affairs*); at the same time *being* is spoken of preponderantly in reference to absolute *objects* (non-states-of-affairs), though without secure delimitation. The right of our more general grasp of the concepts is incontestable. The nature of the matter itself demands that the concepts *truth* and *falsehood*, at least for now, be set so wide that they span the *total sphere of the objectifying acts*.

It thereby appears most fitting to differentiate the concepts *truth* and *being* so that the concepts of *truth* (a certain latitude of equivocation will be unavoidable, but, after clarification of the concepts, will scarcely remain harmful) are referred to the *side of the acts* themselves and their ideally-to-be-grasped moments, and the concepts of *being* (being-truly) to the belonging *objective correlates*. Accordingly we would have to define *truth* — after (2) and (4) — as the *idea of adequation*, or else as the *correctness* of the objectifying positing and meaning. *Being* in the sense of truth would then be determined — after (1) and (3) — as the *identity* of the object meant and at the same time given in the adequation; or else (more in accord with the natural word-sense) as the *adequately perceivable* überhaupt, in indeterminate reference to some intention to be made true (adequately fulfilled) thereby.

After the concepts are grasped in this width and phenomenologically secured, one can proceed, in heed of the difference of the *relating* and *non-relating* acts (predications — absolute positions), to delimit *narrower* concepts of *truth* and *being*. The narrower truth-concept would then restrict itself to the ideal adequation of a *relating* act to the belonging adequate state-of-affairs-perception; likewise the narrower being-concept would concern the *being of absolute objects* and separate it from the "*subsisting*" (*Bestehen*) of states of affairs.

Accordingly the following is clear: if one defines judgment as *positing act überhaupt*, then, subjectively spoken, the sphere of judgment coincides with the united spheres of the concepts truth and falsehood in the widest sense. If one defines it through the *statement* and its possible fulfilments — hence comprises under judgment only the sphere of the *relating* positings — then the same coincidence obtains again, provided only the *narrower* concepts of truth and falsehood are laid at the basis.

One-sidedly we have hitherto preferred the case of *evidence* — the act described as total coincidence. But to evidence there corresponds, with regard to the correlate case of conflict, *absurdity* (*Absurdität*), as experience of the complete conflict between intention and quasi-fulfilment. To the concepts *truth* and *being* there then correspond the correlate concepts *falsehood* and *non-being*. The phenomenological clarification of these concepts is, now that we have prepared all the foundations, to be carried out without special difficulties. There would first have to be exactly delimited the negative ideal of the *ultimate disappointment* (*letzte Enttäuschung*).

With the strict grasp of the concept of evidence laid at the basis here, it is manifest that *doubts* of the kind that came to occasional expression in recent times are *absurd*: namely whether, with the same matter A, the experience *evidence* could be linked in the one person and that of *absurdity* in another. Such doubts were possible only so long as one interpreted evidence and absurdity as peculiar (positive or negative) *feelings* which, contingently adhering to the judgment-act, confer on it that special distinction which we logically value as truth or falsehood. If someone experiences the evidence A, it is *evident* that no second person can experience the absurdity of the same A; for that A is evident means: A is not merely meant, but truly *given* exactly as that as which it is meant — it is in the strictest sense itself present. How should this same A be meant for a second person, but the meaning that it is A be truly excluded by a truly-given non-A? One sees, it is a matter of a pure *essence-situation* — the same that the *law of contradiction* brings to expression (into whose multivocities the correlations treated above naturally enter).

From our analyses it emerges with reliable clarity that *being* and *non-being* are not concepts which, according to their origin, express *oppositions of the judgment-qualities*. In the sense of our conception of the phenomenological relations, *every* judgment is positing, and positing is no character of the *is* that would find in the *is not* its *qualitative* counterpart. The qualitative counterpart to *judgment* is mere *presentation* of the same matter. The differences between *is* and *is not* are differences of the *intentional matter*. Just as the *is*, in the manner of meaning-intention, expresses the predicative *agreement*, so the *is not* expresses the predicative *conflict*.

# **Second Section: Sensibility and Understanding**

## **Chapter Six: Sensuous and Categorial Intuitions**

### **§ 40\. The problem of the fulfilment of categorial meaning-forms and a leading thought for its solution**

In our expositions hitherto a great *gap* repeatedly made itself rather palpable. It concerned the categorial *objective forms* — or the "synthetic" functions in the sphere of the objectifying acts, through which these objective forms constitute themselves, through which they are to come to "intuition" and accordingly also to "knowledge." Husserl ventures the attempt to fill this gap somewhat, attaching again to the inquiry of the first chapter, which was directed at a restricted goal of the clarification of knowledge — namely the relation of expressing meaning-intention and expressed sensuous intuition. He lays at the basis again, provisionally, the simplest cases of perception- and other intuition-statements, and on their ground makes clear the theme of the next considerations.

In the case of the perception-statement, not only the nominal presentations interwoven into it fulfil themselves; the statement-meaning *as a whole* finds fulfilment through the underlying perception. Of the whole statement, too, it is said that it gives expression to our perception; we say not merely *I see this paper, an inkpot, several books* and the like, but also *I see that this paper is written on, that here an inkpot of bronze stands, that several books are opened*, and so forth. If the fulfilment of nominal meanings appears to someone as sufficiently clear, Husserl poses the question how the fulfilment of the *whole* statements — especially in what reaches beyond their "matter," i.e. here beyond the nominal *termini* — is to be understood. What is to and can procure fulfilment for the *meaning-moments* which make up the sentence-form as such, and to which, e.g., the *copula* belongs — the moments of the "*categorial form*"?

More closely seen, this question transfers also to the *nominal* meanings, provided they are not precisely *formless* like the proper meanings. Like the statement, the name already possesses, in its grammatical appearance, its "matter" and its "form." If it falls apart into words, the form lies partly in the manner of stringing-together, partly in proper form-words, partly in the formation-manner of the single word, which then in itself still lets moments of "matter" and moments of "form" be distinguished. Such grammatical distinctions point back to *meaning*\-distinctions; at least roughly, the grammatical articulations and forms express the articulations and forms grounded in the essence of the meaning. We find, then, in the meanings parts of very different character, and among them there strike us here specially those expressed through *form-words* such as *the, a, some, many, few, two, is, not, which, and, or*, and so on; further through the substantival and adjectival, singular and plural formation-forms of the words.

How does all this stand in *fulfilment*? Is the ideal of completely adequate fulfilment, formulated in the third chapter, to be upheld? *Do all parts and forms of the meaning also correspond to parts and forms of the perception?* In that case there would obtain, between the meaning-meaning and the fulfilling intuiting, that *parallelism* which the talk of *expressing* suggests. The expression would be an *image-like counterpart* of the perception (in respect of all its parts or forms that are to be expressed), though produced out of a new *stuff* — an "*ex-pression*" in the *stuff of meaning*.

The prototype for the interpretation of the relation between meaning and intuiting would, then, be the relation of the *proper meaning* to the corresponding perceptions. Whoever knows Cologne itself, and accordingly has the true proper meaning of the word *Cologne*, possesses in the respective actual meaning-experience something exactly corresponding to the future confirming perception. It is not the proper *counter-image* of the perception, like the corresponding fantasy; but as in the perception the city is (supposedly) itself present, so the proper name *Cologne* in its proper meaning means the same city "*directly*," it itself, as it is. The simple perception here brings to appearance — without help of further acts built upon it — the object *which* the meaning-intention means, and *as* it means it. The meaning-intention finds therefore in the *mere perception* the act in which it fulfils itself *completely adequately*.

Considering, instead of the directly naming, formless expressions, rather *formed* and *articulated* expressions, the matter seems at first to stand likewise. *I see white paper and say white paper*; thereby I express, exactly fitting, only what I see. Likewise with whole judgments: *I see that this paper is white, and just this I express*: I state *this paper is white*. We shall not let ourselves be deceived by such — in a certain way correct, yet easily misunderstood — talk. With it one might even want to ground that the meaning here lies *in the perception*, which, as we have established, does not hold. The word *white* certainly means something on the white paper itself, and so in the status of fulfilment this meaning coincides with the partial-perception bearing on the white-moment of the object. But the assumption of a *mere coincidence* with this partial-perception will not suffice. One is wont to say here that the appearing *white* is recognized and named *as white*. Yet the normal talk of recognizing designates rather the *subject-object* as the "recognized." In *this* recognizing there manifestly lies *another* act, which perhaps includes that first one but is in any case different from it. The *paper* is recognized as white, or rather as a white one, where we, expressing the perception, say *white paper*. The intention of the word *white* coincides only *partially* with the colour-moment of the appearing object; there remains a *surplus* in the meaning, a *form* which finds nothing in the appearance itself to confirm it: *white, i.e. white-being paper*. And does this form not repeat itself — though remaining more hidden — also with the substantive *paper*? Only the mark-meanings united in its "concept" *terminate* in the perception; here too the whole object is recognized *as* paper, here too a *complementary form* which contains the being (though not as the only form). The fulfilment-achievement of the simple perception can manifestly not reach up to such forms.

We need only further ask what corresponds, on the side of the perception, to the difference of the two example-expressions carried out on the ground of the same perception — *this white paper* and *this paper is white* — hence to the difference of the *attributive* and *predicative* statement-form; what it actually stamps on the perception, and in the case of adequate fitting stamps with special exactness: we notice the same difficulty. In short, we see, the matter does not stand as simply with the formed meanings as with the proper meaning and its simple coincidence-relation to perception. Certainly one can say, intelligibly and unambiguously for the hearer, *I see that this paper is white*; but the meaning of this talk need not be that the meaning of the spoken sentence gives expression to a *mere seeing*. It can also be that the *cognitive essence* of the seeing — in which the appearing objectivity attests itself as self-given — *grounds* certain connecting or relating or otherwise *forming* acts, and that *these* are the ones to which the expression with its changing forms fits itself, and in which it, in respect of these forms (as carried out *on the ground of* actual perception), finds its fulfilment. Taking these *founded* acts (or rather act-forms) together with their *founding* acts, and comprising under the title *founded act* the whole complex acts that grow through that formal founding, we can say: under the presupposition of the just-indicated possibility, the *parallelism* re-establishes itself — only it is *not* a parallelism between the meaning-intentions of the expressions and *mere perceptions* corresponding to them, but between the meaning-intentions and those *acts founded in perceptions*.

### **§ 41\. Continuation. Extension of the sphere of examples**

Thinking the circle of examples extended so widely that it comprises the *total domain of predicating thinking*, there result manifold confirmations of the just-attained provisional view, and at the same time the conviction that the *categorial forms* of meaning find their fulfilment not in mere sensuous perception but only in founded acts.

There then enter especially also the *judgments* which have no determinate relation to an individual, by some intuition to be given, single thing, but express *in generic fashion* relations between *ideal unities*. The generic meanings of such judgments too can carry themselves out on the ground of "corresponding" intuition, as their origin too lies, immediately or mediately, in intuition. But the intuitively single is here *not the meant*; it functions at best as a singular case, as *example*, or only as a rough analogon of an example — of the *general*, upon which alone it is aimed. So, e.g., when we speak generically of colour, or specially of redness, the appearance of a singular red thing may furnish us the corroborating intuition.

Occasionally it happens here, moreover, that one designates the generic statement outright as expression of the intuition — as when one says, e.g., that an arithmetical axiom expresses what lies in the intuition; or when one reproaches a geometer that, instead of deducing formally, he merely expresses what he sees on the figure, drawing from the drawing and suppressing proof-steps. Such talk has its good sense (the objection in fact touches, to no slight extent, the formal conclusiveness of *Euclidean* geometry); only the *expressing* means here something other than in the earlier cases. If already in those the expression is no mere counter-image of the intuition, then here all the more not, where the intention does not direct the thought upon the intuitively given appearance and its intuitive properties or relations at all — indeed cannot direct it so in the example: the figure in the geometrical sense is, as is known, an *ideal limit* not in concreto intuitively exhibitable at all. With all this, the intuition has also here, and in the generic domain überhaupt, an essential relation to the expression and its meaning; these form, therefore, an experience of *general knowledge referred to intuition*, no mere togetherness but a palpably belonging-together unity. Here too concept and proposition orient themselves according to the intuition, and only thereby grows, with corresponding adaptation, the *evidence*, the value of the knowledge. On the other hand, it needs no long consideration to see that the meaning of the pertinent expressions lies *not at all* in the intuition, but that the latter only confers on it the *fullness* of clarity and, in favourable case, of evidence. It is too well known that the incomparable majority of generic statements — especially the scientific ones — function significantly without any clarifying intuition, and that only a vanishing part (even of the true and grounded ones) is and remains accessible to a full illumination with intuition.

Similarly as in the individual domain, the natural talk of *knowledge* refers also in the generic domain to the *intuitively founded thought-acts*. If the intuition falls away entirely, the judgment knows indeed *nothing*; but in its purely conceptual manner it still means exactly what, with the help of intuition, would come to knowledge — if the judgment is a true one at all. But the knowledge has — we can observe this in every case of subsequent corroboration of the generic judgment at the intuition — like every other knowledge, the character of *fulfilment and identification*.

For the solution of the difficulty — how identification is here to come about, since the form of the generic proposition (especially the form of generality) would seek in vain in the individual intuition for elements sympathetic to it — there offers itself, analogously as above, the possibility of *founded acts*, which, more closely set forth, would be shaped somewhat thus.

Where generic thoughts find their fulfilment in intuition, there build themselves, *upon* the perceptions and other appearances of equal order, certain *new acts* — and indeed acts which relate to the appearing object in *quite another way* than the intuitions constituting it in each case. The difference of the relation-manner expresses itself in the self-evident turn, already used above, that here the intuitive object does not itself stand there as the meant, but functions only as a *clarifying example* for the proper, generic meaning. As now the *expressing* acts follow these differences, their *significative* intention too goes, instead of upon something intuitively to be presented, rather upon a *general*, only to be corroborated through intuition. And where the new intention fulfils itself adequately through underlying intuition, it proves its *objective possibility* (or the possibility or "reality" of the general).

### **§ 42\. The difference between sensuous stuff and categorial form in the total sphere of the objectifying acts**

After these provisional considerations have made us know the difficulty and at once given us a leading thought for its possible overcoming, Husserl attempts the proper carrying-through of the consideration. We started from the point that the idea of a kind of *image-like expressing* is quite unusable for describing the relation that obtains, in the case of formed expressions, between the expressing meanings and the expressed intuitions. This is doubtless correct and is now only to undergo a closer determination. We need only seriously consider what can possibly be a matter of the *perception* and what a matter of the *meaning*, and we must become attentive that *only to certain statement-parts — specifiable in advance in the mere judgment-form — does something in the intuition correspond, while to the other statement-parts nothing in it can correspond at all*.

Let us look at this situation somewhat more closely. The perception-statements are, a normal complete expression presupposed, *articulated discourses of changing shape*. We easily distinguish certain types such as *E is P* (where E may stand as indication of the proper name), *an S is P*, *this S is P*, *all S are P*, and so on. Manifold complications arise through the moderating influence of negation, through introduction of the difference between absolute and relative predicates (or attributes), through conjunctive, disjunctive, determinative linkages, and so on. In the difference of these types sharp *meaning-differences* stamp themselves. To the various letter-signs and words in these types there correspond partly *members*, partly *connecting forms* in the meanings of the actual statements belonging to these types.

It is now easy to see that *exclusively at the places indicated by letter-symbols* in such "judgment-forms" can there stand meanings that fulfil themselves *in the perception itself*, while for the complementary *form-meanings* it would be hopeless — indeed fundamentally wrong — to seek in the perception directly what could give them fulfilment. To be sure, the letters can, by virtue of their merely *functional* meaning, also take on the value of *complex thoughts*; very intricately built statements can be apprehended under the viewpoint of very simple judgment-types. Accordingly there repeats itself, in what we unitarily take into view as "*terminus*," the same difference between "stuff" and "form." But finally we come, in every perception-statement (and likewise of course with every other statement giving expression, in a certain primary sense, to intuition), upon *last elements present in the termini* — we call them the *stuff-elements* (*stoffliche Elemente*) — which find direct fulfilment in the intuition (perception, imagination, and the like), while the complementary *forms*, although as meaning-forms they likewise demand fulfilment, find in the perception and the coordinate acts *immediately nothing* that could ever be adequate to them.

This fundamental difference Husserl designates, in the natural extension over the whole sphere of objectifying presenting, as the *categorial — and indeed absolute — difference between form and stuff of the presenting*, and distinguishes it at the same time from the intimately connected *relative or functional* difference (already co-indicated above).

We just spoke of the natural extension of this difference over the whole sphere of objectifying presenting. We grasp, namely, the components of the *fulfilment* corresponding to the stuff- or form-components of the *meaning-intentions* likewise as "stuff-like" or "formal"; and thereby it is clear what is to count, in the sphere of the objectifying acts überhaupt, as stuff-like and as formal.

Of *stuff* (or also *matter*) and *form* there is talk, otherwise, in manifold sense. Husserl expressly points out that the customary talk of the *matter* which finds its opposite in the categorial *form* has nothing to do with the talk of the *matter* in opposition to the *act-quality* — as e.g. when we distinguish, in the meanings, from the positing or merely undecided quality, the matter which tells us *as what*, as how-determined and \-grasped, the objectivity is meant in the meaning. For easier separation Husserl, in the categorial opposition, speaks not of *matter* but of *stuff*, and says, on the other hand, where the matter in the previous sense is meant, accentuatingly *intentional matter* or also *apprehension-sense*.

### **§ 43\. The objective correlates of the categorial forms are no "real" moments**

It now matters to gain clarity about the just-designated difference. Husserl attaches, for this purpose, to the earlier examples. The form-giving inflection, the *being* in attributive and predicative function, fulfils itself, we said, in *no* perception. Here we recall the *Kantian proposition*: *being is no real predicate* (*das Sein ist kein reales Prädikat*). Even if it relates to *existential* being, the being of "absolute position" (as Herbart called it), we can no less appropriate it for *predicative and attributive* being. In any case it means exactly what we want here to lay clear. The *colour* I can see, not the *being-coloured*. The *smoothness* I can feel, not the *being-smooth*. The *tone* I can hear, not the *being-sounding*. *Being* is nothing in the object, no part of it, no moment dwelling in it; no quality or intensity, but also no figure, no inner form überhaupt, no constitutive mark however to be grasped. But being is also nothing *on* an object; as it is no real inner mark, so it is no real outer mark, and therefore in the real sense no "mark" at all. For it concerns also not the *factual unity-forms* which connect objects into more comprehensive objects — colours into colour-shapes, tones into harmonies, things into more comprehensive things or thing-orders (garden, street, phenomenal outer world). In these factual unity-forms the *outer* marks of the objects ground themselves — the right and left, the high and low, the loud and soft, and so on — among which something like the *is* naturally is not found.

We just spoke of *objects*, their constitutive marks, their factual connection with other objects (which creates more comprehensive objects and at the same time, on the part-objects, outer marks); we said that something corresponding to *being* is not to be sought among them. But all that are *perceivabilities*, and they exhaust the extent of possible perceptions so that thereby it is at once said and ascertained that *being is simply nothing perceivable*.

But here a clarifying supplement is needed. *Perception* and *object* are most intimately connected concepts, which mutually assign each other their sense, extend and narrow it together. But it must be stressed that we have here used a certain naturally bounded, nearest-lying, but *very narrow* concept of *perception* (or of *object*). One speaks, as is known, of perceiving — and especially of seeing — also in a very *extended* sense, which comprises the grasping of whole states of affairs and finally even the *a priori* evidence of laws (as "*insight*," *Einsehen*). In the narrower sense, perceived is — popularly and roughly spoken — everything objective that we can see with eyes, hear with ears, grasp with any "*outer*" — or also "*inner sense*." As "*sensuously perceived*," however, count, by common usage, only the *outer* things and thingly connection-forms (together with the immediately belonging marks). Consistently, after the introduction of the talk of "inner sense," one would have had to extend the concept of sensuous perception fittingly too, so that also all "*inner*" perception — and, under the title *sensuous object*, the correlate circle of *inner* objects (hence the egos and their inner experiences) — would be comprised.

In the sphere of sensuous perception so understood — and correspondingly of *sensuous intuition* überhaupt (this width of the talk of sensibility we hold fast) — a meaning like that of the word *being* finds *no possible objective correlate* and therefore, in the acts of such perception, *no possible fulfilment*. What holds of *being* holds manifestly of the remaining *categorial forms* in the statements, whether they connect components of the termini with one another or the termini themselves into the unity of the sentence. The *a* and the *the*, the *and* and the *or*, the *if* and the *so*, the *all* and the *none*, the *something* and *nothing*, the *quantity-forms* and the *number-determinations*, and so on — all these are *meaning-bearing sentence-elements*, but their *objective correlates* (if we may ascribe such to them at all) we seek in vain in the sphere of the *real* objects — which means nothing other than: of the objects of possible *sensuous perception*.

### **§ 44\. The origin of the concept being and of the remaining categories does not lie in the domain of inner perception**

This holds — Husserl stresses it expressly — both of the sphere of the "outer" senses and of that of the "inner" sense. It is a near-lying doctrine, generally widespread since *Locke*, but *fundamentally erroneous*, that the meanings in question (or the nominally self-rendered meanings corresponding to them — the *logical categories*, like being and non-being, unity, plurality, totality, number, ground, consequence, and so on) arise through *reflection on certain psychic acts*, hence in the domain of the *inner sense*, of "inner perception." In such a way arise concepts like *perception, judgment, affirmation, negation, colligating and counting, presupposing and inferring* — which are therefore one and all "*sensuous*" concepts, namely belonging to the sphere of "inner sense" — but never the concepts of the earlier series, which can count as anything but concepts of psychic acts or their *real components*. The thought *judgment* fulfils itself in the inner intuition of an actual judgment; but the thought of the *is* does not fulfil itself therein. *Being* is no judgment and no real component of a judgment. As little as being is a real component of any outer object, so little is it a real component of any inner one — hence also not of the judgment. In the judgment (the predicating statement) the *is* occurs as a *meaning-moment*, just as, only in another position and function, *gold* and *yellow*. The *is* itself does not occur therein; it is in the little word *is* only *meant*, i.e. signitively meant. *Self-given*, or at least supposedly given, it is, however, in the *fulfilment* fitting itself, under circumstances, to the judgment: the *becoming-aware* (*Gewahrwerdung*) of the supposed state of affairs. Not only does what is meant in the meaning-part *gold* now itself appear, and likewise the *yellow*, but there appears *gold-is-yellow*; judgment and judgment-intuition unite themselves thereby into the unity of the *evident judgment*, in favourable case of the evident in the sense of the ideal limit.

If one understands by *judging* not only the meaning-intentions belonging to actual statements, but also the eventual fulfilments completely adapted to them, then it is surely correct that *a being is graspable only in judging*; but thereby it is by no means said that the concept of being must be, or ever can be, won "*in reflection*" on certain judgments. *Reflection* is otherwise a rather vague word. In epistemology it has the at least relatively fixed sense Locke gave it, that of *inner perception*; only to this, then, can we hold in interpreting the doctrine that believes it can find the origin of the concept *being* in reflection on the judgment. Such an origin we therefore *deny*. The relating being, which predication brings to expression (e.g. as "is," "are," and the like), is a *non-self-sufficient*; shaping it into the full *concretum*, there grows the respective *state of affairs* (*Sachverhalt*), the objective correlate of the full judgment. We can then say: *as the sensuous object to sensuous perception, so the state of affairs stands to the act of becoming-aware that "gives" it* (more or less adequately) — we feel pressed to say outright: *so the state of affairs stands to the state-of-affairs-perception*.

As now the concept *sensuous object* (the real) cannot arise through "reflection" on *perception* — because then precisely the concept *perception*, or a concept of some real constituents of perceptions, would result — so also the concept *state of affairs* cannot arise from reflection on *judgments*, because thereby we could obtain only concepts of judgments or of real constituents of judgments.

That there perceptions, here judgments (or judgment-intuitions, state-of-affairs-perceptions) must be *experienced* for the respective abstraction to proceed, is self-evident. *Being-experienced is not being-objective*. "Reflection" means, however, that what we reflect on — the phenomenological experience — becomes *objective* to us (inwardly perceived by us), and that it really furnishes, out of this objective content, the determinations to be generalized.

*Not in the reflection on judgments (or rather on judgment-fulfilments), but in the judgment-fulfilments themselves, lies truly the origin of the concepts state of affairs and being (in the sense of the copula);* not in these *acts as objects*, but in the *objects of these acts*, we find the abstraction-foundation for the realization of the said concepts; and naturally the conform *modifications* of these acts then furnish us an equally good one.

It is from the outset self-evident: as any other concept (an idea, a specific unity) can only "*arise*" — i.e. be given to us *itself* — on the ground of an act which sets some single instance corresponding to it at least *imaginatively* before our eye, so the concept of *being* can only arise when some *being* is set before our eyes, really or imaginatively. If being counts for us as *predicative* being, then some *state of affairs* must be given to us, and this naturally through an act *giving* it — the *analogon of common sensuous intuition*.

The same holds of all *categorial forms* (or of all *categories*). An *aggregate* (*Inbegriff*), e.g., is given and can only be given in an actual *comprehending-together*, hence in an act that comes to expression in the form of the conjunctive connection *A and B and C* …. But the concept of the aggregate grows not through reflection on this act; instead of the *giving act*, we have rather to attend to *what* it gives — to the aggregate it brings in concreto to appearance — and to raise its general form into general-conceptual consciousness.

### **§ 45\. Extension of the concept intuition, especially of the concepts perception and imagination. Sensuous and categorial intuition**

If the question is now posed — wherein do the categorial forms of the meanings find their fulfilment, if not through perception or intuition in that narrower sense which we provisionally tried to indicate in the talk of "sensibility" — the answer is already clearly prefigured by the considerations just carried out.

First, that *the forms too really find fulfilment*, as we presupposed without further ado — or that the *whole*, so-and-so-formed meanings, and not merely the "stuff-like" meaning-moments, find fulfilment — the presentification of any example of a faithful perception-statement makes doubtless; and so it also explains itself that one calls the whole perception-statement an *expression of the perception* and, in the transferred sense, an expression of what is intuited and self-given *in* the perception. But if the "categorial forms" of the expression present alongside the stuff-moments do *not* terminate in the perception (insofar as it is understood as mere *sensuous* perception), then the talk of expression of the perception must here have another sense at its basis; there must in any case be an *act* which renders to the categorial meaning-elements the same services as mere sensuous perception to the stuff-like. The essential like-kindedness of the *fulfilment-function*, and of all the ideal relations lawfully connected with it, makes it unavoidable to designate every act fulfilling in the manner of *confirming self-presentation* as *perception*, every fulfilling act überhaupt as *intuition*, and its intentional correlate as *object*.

In fact we can answer the question — what it means that the *categorially formed meanings* found fulfilment, confirmed themselves in perception — only thus: it means nothing other than that they are referred *to the object itself in its categorial forming*. The object with these categorial forms is not merely *meant*, as in the case of a merely symbolic function of the meanings, but is itself set before our eyes precisely *in these forms*; in other words: it is not merely *thought* but precisely *intuited* (or *perceived*). Accordingly, as soon as we want to lay out at what the talk of fulfilment here aims — what the formed meanings, and in them the form-elements, express, what the unitary or unity-creating *objectivity* corresponding to them is — we strike unavoidably upon "*intuition*" (or "perception") and "*object*." We cannot dispense with these words, whose extended sense is admittedly unmistakable. How else should we designate the correlate of a non-sensuous (or not-sensuous-forms-containing) subject-presentation, if the word *object* were denied us; and how its actual "being-given" (or appearing-as-"given"), if the word *perception* were denied us? So there become — and in generally usual talk — *aggregates, indeterminate multiplicities, totalities, numbers, disjunctiva, predicates* (the *being-just*), *states of affairs* into "*objects*," the acts through which they appear as given into "*perceptions*."

Visibly the connection of the wider and narrower, the *supersensuous* (i.e. building-itself-over-sensibility, or categorial) and the *sensuous* perception-concept is no external or contingent one, but one grounded in the matter. It is spanned by the great class of acts whose peculiarity it is that in them something appears as "*real*," and indeed as "*self-given*." Manifestly this appearing-as-real-and-self-given (which can very well be a deceptive one) characterizes itself everywhere through its difference from the essentially kindred acts, and only thereby gains its full clarity — namely through the difference from the *pictorial presentifying* and from the *purely significative thinking-thereof*, both of which exclude the being-present (the so-to-speak appearing *in persona*), though not the holding-for-being. As regards the latter, the pictorial as well as the symbolic representation is possible in a twofold way: in *positing* fashion, as pictorially or symbolically holding-for-being, and in *non-positing*, as "*mere*" imagining or thinking-thereof without holding-for-being. Into the closer discussion of these differences we no longer need enter, after the sufficiently general analyses of the previous section. In any case it is clear that with the concept of perception also that of *imagination* (in its manifold particularizations) must undergo a *corresponding extension*. We could not speak of a *supersensuously or categorially perceived* if there did not exist the possibility of *imagining* the same also "in the same way" (hence not merely sensuously). We shall therefore have to distinguish, quite generally, between *sensuous and categorial intuition* — or have to exhibit the possibility of such a distinction.

The extended perception-concept admits, moreover, again a *narrower* and a *wider* grasp. In the widest sense even *general* states of affairs are called perceived ("insighted," in evidence "beheld"). In the narrower sense perception goes only upon *individual*, hence *temporal* being.

### **§ 46\. Phenomenological analysis of the distinction between sensuous and categorial perception**

In our next considerations, only *individual* perceptions, and in further sequence the coordinate *individual* intuitions, come for the present into consideration.

The separation between "sensuous" and "supersensuous" perceptions was above only superficially indicated and carried out with quite rough characterization. The antiquated talk of *outer and inner senses* — which does not deny its origin from everyday life with its naive metaphysics and anthropology — might serve for the moment to indicate the domain to be excluded; but the *actual determination and delimitation* of the sphere of sensibility is not thereby carried out, and so the concept of *categorial perception* still lacks the descriptive substructure. The securing and clarification of the distinction in question is the more important as on it such fundamental separations as that between *categorial form* and *sensuously founded matter* of knowledge — and likewise the separation between *categories* and all other concepts — wholly depend. It is therefore a matter of seeking *deeper-lying descriptive characteristics* which open to us some insight into the essentially different constitution of the sensuous and categorial perceptions (or intuitions überhaupt).

For our next purposes it is not necessary to carry through an *exhaustive* analysis of the pertinent phenomena — a work that would require extraordinarily comprehensive considerations. It suffices here to attend to some more important points which can serve to characterize the bilateral acts in their relation to one another.

Of every perception it is said that it grasps its object *itself* or *directly*. But this *direct grasping* has a different sense and character according as it is a matter of a perception in the narrow or one in the extended sense — or according as the "directly" grasped objectivity is a *sensuous* or a *categorial* one; otherwise expressed: according as it is a *real* or an *ideal* object. The sensuous or real objects we shall be able to characterize as *objects of the lowest level of possible intuition*, the categorial or ideal as the *objects of the higher levels*.

In the sense of the narrower "*sensuous*" perception, an object is directly grasped or self-present which *constitutes itself in simple fashion* in the perception-act. But thereby the following is meant: the object is also in the sense an *immediately given* object, that it, as *this* one *perceived* with this determinate objective content, does *not* constitute itself in *relating, connecting, or otherwise articulated acts* which are founded in *other* acts bringing *other* objects to perception. Sensuous objects are present in perception *in one act-level*; they are not subject to the necessity of having to constitute themselves *many-rayed* (*vielstrahlig*) in acts of higher level which constitute their objects by means of *other* objects already constituted *for themselves* in other acts.

But every simple perception-act can now function — be it alone for itself, be it together with other acts — as the *ground-act* of new acts, now including it, now only presupposing it, which in their new mode of consciousness at the same time *bring forth a new objectivity-consciousness essentially presupposing the original*. As the new acts of *conjunction, disjunction*, of determinate and indeterminate *single-apprehension* (*the — something*), of *generalization*, of simple, relating, and connecting *knowing* set in, there do not arise arbitrary subjective experiences, nor acts überhaupt linked to the original ones; but acts which, as we said, *constitute new objectivities* — acts in which something appears *as real* and *as self-given*, such that the same, as which it here appears, was not yet, and could not be, given in the founding acts alone. *On the other hand the new objectivity grounds in the old*; it has *objective relation* to what appears in the ground-acts. Its mode of appearance is essentially determined through this relation. It is a matter here of a sphere of objectivities which can come "*itself*" to appearance *only in such founded acts*.

In such *founded acts* lies the *categorial* of intuiting and knowing; in them the *stating thinking*, where it functions as expression, finds its fulfilment: the possibility of perfect adaptation to such acts determines the *truth* of the statement as its *correctness*. To be sure, we have hitherto considered only the sphere of *perception*, and in it only the most primitive cases. One sees without further ado that our distinction between simple and founded acts transfers from the perceptions to *all* intuitions. There already becomes evident also the possibility of *complex* acts of such kind, which are grounded in *mixed* fashion, partly on simple perceptions, partly on simple imaginations; further the possibility that upon *founded* intuitions *new* foundings constitute themselves — hence whole graded sequences of founding build themselves one over the other; further, that the *signitive* intentions shape themselves according to such foundings of lower or higher level, and that then there shape themselves again *mixtures* between signitive and intuitive acts through founding — namely founded acts built on acts of the one and the other kind. For now, however, it matters to consider the *primitive* cases and their fully sufficient clarification.

### **§ 47\. Continuation. Characterization of sensuous perception as "simple" perception**

Husserl takes more closely into view the acts in which *sensuous concreta and their sensuous components* present themselves as given — in contrast to which, afterward, the quite other-kinded acts through which *concretely determined states of affairs, collectiva, disjunctiva* are given as complex "*thought-objects*," as "*objects of higher order*" which *include their founding objects really in themselves*; and again acts of the kind of *generalization* or of *indeterminate single-apprehension*, whose objects too are of higher level, but do *not* include their founding objects in themselves.

In *sensuous* perception the "outer" thing appears to us *in one stroke*, as soon as our glance falls on it. Its manner of letting the thing appear as present is a *simple* one; it does not need the apparatus of founding or founded acts. From which, and from how complicated, psychic processes it may genetically have arisen is naturally without significance here.

We do not overlook, either, the manifest *complexion* that lets itself be demonstrated in the phenomenological content of the simple perception-act, and especially in its unitary intention. Certainly there belong to the thing — as the one appearing thus-and-so in content — manifold *constitutive properties*, of which a part "*itself* falls into the perception" while another is merely *intended*. But we by no means *experience* all the *articulated* perception-acts that would grow if we attended to all the thingly singulars (more closely, to the determinacies of the "side turned to us") *for themselves*, if we made them *objects for themselves*. Certainly the presentations of the complementary determinacies, not themselves falling into the perception, are "dispositionally aroused"; certainly the intentions bearing on them flow into the perception and determine its whole character. But as the thing in the appearance does not stand there as a mere *sum* of the countless single determinacies which subsequent single consideration may distinguish — and as this consideration too cannot *splinter* the thing into singulars, but can only attend to them on the always-finished and unitary thing — so the perception-act too is at all times a *homogeneous unity* which makes the object present in simple and immediate fashion. *The unity of perception thus does not grow through proper synthetic acts*, as if only the form of synthesis through founded acts could procure for the partial intentions the unity of objective relation. Of articulation, and hence also of actual connection, there is no need. *The perception-unity comes about as simple unity, as immediate fusion of the partial intentions and without the accession of new act-intentions.*

It may further be that we do not let ourselves be content with "one glance," but rather contemplate the thing all-sidedly in a *continuous perception-course*, as if palpating it with the senses. But every single perception of this course is *already* perception of *this* thing. Whether I look at this book here from above or below, from inside or outside, I always see *this book*. It is always the one and the same thing, and indeed the same not in the mere physical sense, but according to the *meaning of the perceptions themselves*. Even if single determinacies thereby predominate, and at each step changing ones, the thing itself, as perceived unity, does *not* essentially constitute itself through an overarching act founded in the single perceptions.

But exactly seen, we may not present the matter as though the one sensuous object *could* present itself in a founded act (namely in the continuously running perceiving), while it is merely not *necessary* that it *must* so present itself. Even the continuous perception-course proves, on more exact analysis, a *fusion* of partial acts into *one* act, *not* a *proper act founded in the partial acts*.

To show this, Husserl makes the following consideration. The single perceptions of the course are *continuously united*. This continuity means not merely the objective fact of temporal bordering; rather the course of single acts has the character of a *phenomenological unity* into which the single acts are *fused*. In this unity the many acts are not merely fused into a phenomenological whole überhaupt, but into *one act*, and more closely into *one perception*. In the continuous course of the single perceptions we perceive continuously *this one and same object*. May we now designate the continuous perception, since it builds itself up from the single perceptions, as a perception *founded* in them? Founded it is, of course, in the sense in which a whole is founded by its parts; but not in the sense decisive for us here, according to which the founded act is to establish a *new act-character* grounded in the underlying act-characters and unthinkable without them. In the present case the perception is, as it were, only *stretched*; it lets parts be detached from it which could already function for themselves as full perceptions. But the unity of these perceptions into the continuous perception is *not* unity through a *proper act* that would constitute a new objectivity-consciousness. Instead we find that in the stretched act objectively nothing new at all is meant, but continuously *this same object* which already the partial perceptions, *taken singly*, meant.

One could now lay weight on this *selfsameness* and say: the unity is, after all, *unity of identification*. The intention of the strung-together acts continually coincides, and so the unity comes about. This is surely correct. But *unity of identification* — it is unavoidable to make this distinction — *does not say the same as unity of an act of identification*. An act *means* something; the *act of identification* means *identity*, presents it. In our case identification *is carried out*, but *no identity is meant*. The object meant in the various acts of the continuous perception-course is indeed continually the same, and the acts are united through coincidence; but what is *perceived* in this course, what *becomes objective* in it, is exclusively the *sensuous object*, never its *identity with itself*. Only when we make the perception-course the *foundation of a new act* — only when we *articulate* the single perceptions and set their objects *into relation* — does the unity of continuity prevailing between the single perceptions (i.e. the fusion through coincidence of the intentions) serve as *foothold* for a *consciousness of identity*; the identity now itself becomes *objective*; the moment of the coincidence connecting the act-characters now serves as *representing content* of a *new perception*, founded in the articulated single perceptions, which brings us to the *intentional consciousness*: the now- and previously-perceived is *one and the same*. Naturally we then have to do with a *regular act of the second group*. The act of identification is in fact a new objectivity-consciousness which brings us a new "object" to appearance — an object which can be "self-grasped" or "given" *only* in a founded act of this kind.

But before we go more closely into the new class of acts and objects, we must bring the consideration of the *simple* perceptions to an end. May we regard the sense of the *simple* — or, what counts for us as the same, the *sensuous* — perceiving as clarified, then thereby the concept of the *sensuous* or *real* object (real in the most original sense) is also clarified. We define it outright as the *possible object of a simple perception*. By virtue of the necessary *parallelism* between perception and *imagination* — according to which to every possible perception there corresponds a possible imagination (more exactly, a whole series of imaginations) of the same essence — to every simple perception there is coordinated also a simple imagination, whereby at the same time the wider concept of *sensuous intuition* is secured. That we can accordingly define the *sensuous* objects as the possible objects of sensuous *imagination* and sensuous *intuition* überhaupt means, self-evidently, no essential generalization of the previous definition; on the ground of the parallelism just stressed, both definitions are *equivalent*.

Through the concept of the *real object* the concept of *real part* — more specially, the concepts *real piece, real moment* (real mark), *real form* — is also determined. *Every part of a real object is a real part.*

In the simple perception the whole object is called given "*explicitly*," each of its parts (part in the widest sense) "*implicitly*." The totality of the objects which can be given *explicitly or implicitly* in simple perceptions makes up the *widest-grasped sphere of the sensuous objects*.

Every concrete sensuous object is simply perceivable in the manner of an *explicit*; and so too every *piece* of such an object. But how does it stand with the *abstract moments*? By their nature they cannot be *for themselves*; it is therefore evident that their perception and imagination is a *non-self-sufficient*, insofar as the representing content — even where mere representation through analogy takes place — can be experienced not for itself but only in a more comprehensive concretum. But thereby it is not yet said that the intuition must be a *founded* act. It would be so if the grasping of an abstract moment necessarily had to be *preceded* by the *grasping* of the concrete whole (or that of the complementary moments) — the grasping as an act of intuitive turning-toward; and this I do not hold to be self-evident. By contrast, it is certain that the grasping of a moment, and überhaupt of a part, *as part* of the given whole — hence also the grasping of a sensuous mark *as mark*, of a sensuous form *as form* — points to nothing but *founded* acts, and indeed to such of the *relating* kind; with that, then, the sphere of "sensibility" would be left and that of "understanding" entered. This group of founded acts Husserl will at once subject to a closer consideration.

### **§ 48\. Characterization of the categorial acts as founded acts**

A sensuous object we can *apprehend* in various ways. First, of course, in *simple* fashion. This possibility is exhausted in the simple perception (or imagination), which sets the object as a self-present (or presentified) one before us in one stroke, without bringing this or that determinacy specially to grasp and without setting the object into relation with other objects.

So apprehended, the sensuous object stands, as it were, *singly* (simply) before us: the parts that constitute it are indeed *in* it, but they do not become *explicit objects* for us in the simple act. But the same object we can also apprehend in *explicating* fashion: in *articulating* acts we "lift out" the parts; in *relating* acts we set the lifted-out into relation, be it to one another, be it to the whole. And only through these new modes of apprehension do the connected and related members gain the *character* of "parts" (or of "wholes"). The articulating acts — and, in the back-relation, the simple act — are not merely experienced *in succession*; rather, there are at each moment *overarching act-unities* in which, *as new objects*, the part-relations constitute themselves.

Taking first into view the relations between *part and whole* — hence, in restriction to the simplest cases, the relations *A is (has) α* and *α is in A* — to demonstrate the founded acts in which these typical states of affairs constitute themselves as given, and to clarify the just-used forms of categorical statements (i.e. to lead them back to their intuitive origin, to their adequate fulfilment), is one and the same. But here it is not the *act-qualities* that matter to us, but exclusively the *constitution of the apprehension-forms*; and to that extent our analysis, considered as analysis of judgment, will be incomplete.

A perceiving act grasps A as a whole, in one stroke and in simple fashion. A second perception-act directs itself upon the α, the part or non-self-sufficient moment constitutively belonging to the A. But these two acts carry themselves out not merely simultaneously or successively in the manner of "connectionless" experiences; rather they knit themselves into a *single act*, in whose synthesis the A is *first given as having the α in itself*. Likewise, with reversed "direction" of the relating "perception," the α can come to self-givenness as *accruing to the A*.

Husserl seeks to penetrate somewhat deeper. The intuitive *total-meaning* of the object includes *implicitly* the intention upon the α. The perception means, after all, to grasp the object itself, and so its "grasping" must, in and with the whole object, strike all its components. (In a small-print note: it is here a matter only of the components of the object *as it appears in the perception*, as which it stands there *in it itself*, and not of those belonging to the object existing in "objective reality," which only subsequent experiences, knowings, sciences set forth.)

In the restriction of the total-perception to the *single*\-perception, the partial intention upon the α is now *not torn out* of the total appearance of the A, as if its unity went to pieces; rather, in a *proper act* the α becomes its own perception-object. At the same time, however, the continuing total-perceiving "*coincides*," in accordance with that implied partial intention, with the single-perceiving. The representant bearing on the α functions as *identically the same in a twofold way*; and in so functioning, the coincidence carries itself out as the peculiar *unity of the two representative functions* — i.e. the two *apprehensions* whose bearer this representant is coincide. But this unity now itself takes on the function of a *representation*; it does not thereby count for itself, as this experienced association of the acts; it is not itself constituted as object, but helps constitute *another* object; it *represents*, and in such a way that now the A appears *as having the α in itself* — or, in reversed direction: the α as *being in A*.

According to the "standpoint of the apprehension" (or the "direction of the transition" — from part to whole or conversely; and these are *new* phenomenological characters contributing to the intentional total-matter of the relating act), there are *two a priori prefigured possibilities* according to which "the same relation" can come to actual givenness. To these correspond the two *a priori possible "relations"* as different but, by ideal lawfulness, necessarily belonging-together objectivities, which constitute themselves *directly only in founded acts of the indicated kind* — i.e. can come to "self-givenness," to "perception," only in acts so built.

This presentation visibly fits all particularizations of the relation between a *whole* and its *parts*. All these relations are of *categorial*, hence ideal nature. It would be wrong to lay them into the simple whole and to want to find them in it through analysis. In the whole the part is indeed *contained* prior to all articulation, and is therein *co-grasped* in the perceiving grasp of the whole; but this fact — *that* it is contained therein — is at first merely the *ideal possibility* of bringing it, and its being-part, to perception in the corresponding articulated and founded acts.

The matter stands manifestly similar with the *outer* relations from which the predications of the kind *A right of B, A greater, brighter, louder than B*, and the like, stem. Wherever sensuous objects — simple perceivabilities for themselves — notwithstanding their self-separating closedness find themselves together into *associations*, into more or less intimate unities (hence at bottom into more comprehensive objects), there grows the possibility of *outer relations*. They are one and all to be comprised under the type of the relation of *part to parts of a whole*. Again it is *founded acts* in which the primary appearance of the pertinent states of affairs, the outer relations, carries itself out. For it is clear that neither the simple perception of the whole complexion nor the single-perceptions belonging to its members are in themselves already the *relation-perceptions* which are possible only in this complexion. Only when a member is preferred as *chief member* and considered under retention of the other members does its phenomenal — and, according to the particularity of the prevailing unity-kind, changing — *being-determined* through the correlate members emerge, which thereby manifestly must themselves come to lifting-out. Here too the choice of the chief member (or the direction of the relating apprehension) in general determines phenomenologically different and correlatively characterized *relation-forms*, which in the unarticulated perception of the association (in the association as it appears as a simple object) are *not* truly, but only as *ideal possibilities*, enclosed — namely the possibility of carrying out the relevant founded acts.

The *real* insertion of these part-relations into the whole would mean a *mingling* of fundamentally different things: of *sensuous* or *real connection-forms* and *categorial, ideal* ones. The sensuous connections are *moments* of the real object, actual moments of it, present in it — though only implicitly — and to be lifted out of it through an *abstractive perception*. By contrast, the forms of *categorial* connection are forms belonging to the *manner of the act-synthesis* — hence forms which constitute themselves objectively in the *synthetic acts built upon sensibility*. In the formation of outer relations the sensuous form may give the foundation for the constitution of a categorial form corresponding to it — as when we apprehend, and possibly express, the sensuous *bordering* of the contents A and B (given in the intuition of a comprehensive G) in the synthetic forms *A borders on B*, or *B borders on A*. But with the constitution of the latter forms *new objects* have grown, belonging to the class *state of affairs*, which comprises only "objects of higher order." In the sensuous whole the parts A and B are united through the moment of bordering sensuously connecting them. But the lifting-out of these parts and moments, the formation of the intuitions of A, B, and of the bordering, does *not yet* yield the presentation *A borders on B*. This requires a *new* act mastering these presentations, fittingly forming and connecting them.

### **§ 49\. Addition on nominal forming**

Husserl appends to the analysis an important addition concerning the *forming* which the synthetically connected presentations, each taken for itself, undergo. In a special class of cases we have already studied this important point: in the Fifth Investigation we remarked that a *statement* can never become, in unmodified form, the foundation of a synthetic act built upon it — the subject- or object-member of a new statement. The statement must, we said, first take on the *nominal form*, whereby its state of affairs is, in a new — and indeed *nominal* — manner, presentational and made the *object* of a new predication.

In this fact, then, the *intuitive* difference stamps itself which Husserl here has in view, and which holds not merely for the relation-members of the hitherto-considered syntheses of lowest level (immediately built on sensibility), but for *all* presentations of which *many-rayed* syntheses of arbitrary kind and level take possession.

We can perhaps say at first generally: *objectifying acts purely for themselves, and "the same" objectifying acts in the function of constituting relation-points of any relations, are not truly the same; they differ phenomenologically — and indeed in respect of what we have called the intentional matter.* The *apprehension-sense* has changed, and hence the *meaning-change* in the adequate expression. It is not as though, between the unchanged presentations, only an intermediate piece were inserted, as a bond merely externally knitting the presentations to one another. The function of synthetic thinking (the *intellective* function) *does something to them*, *forms them anew* — though, as categorial function, in *categorial fashion*; accordingly so that thereby the *sensuous* content of the appearing object remains *unchanged*. The object appears not with new real determinacies; it stands there as *this same one*, but *in a new way*. Its ordering into the categorial context gives it therein a determinate *place and role* — the role of a *relation-member*, specially of a *subject*\- or *object-member*; and these are differences that attest themselves phenomenologically.

To be sure, it is easier to notice the meaning-changes of the *expressing* expressions than the modification of the *direct presentations* themselves; the situation, e.g., is not quite clear in the circle of the *simple intuitions*, in comparing them in and outside a relation-function. Husserl therefore did not yet claim it in the previous Investigation: the individuated perceptions of sensibility were there equated with the nominally functioning acts. Similarly as the object is directly set over against us in the simple perception, so in the nominal act the state of affairs or some categorially-formed object otherwise. The gradual constitution of the object has carried itself out; as a *finished* one it is *then* made into a relation-member — it retains, it seems, its constitutive sense wholly unchanged. Certainly one can *say*, however: at first, with perception, the phenomenological change it undergoes with the entry into the relating act escapes us, *just because* the new form is something that *includes in itself the whole old apprehension-sense* and confers on it only the new one of a "role." The perception remains perception, the object is given as it was, "*only*" that it is "*set in relation*." Such formings of the synthetic function do not *alter the object itself*, and so they count for us as belonging to our *mere subjective activity*; and so we *overlook* them in the phenomenological reflection directed at the clarification of knowledge. Consistently we must then say: also the state of affairs, in the subjectival and überhaupt *nominal* function, is *the same* state of affairs, and *at lowest ground* also constituted *through the same act in original intuition* by which it was constituted in the *isolated* function; but in the higher-level act, in which it functions as relation-member, it is constituted *with a new form* (as it were, with the characterizing costume of its role), which attests itself, in the adequate expression, through the *nominal* expression-form. Here, for the last clarification of the phenomenological situation only just touched, further inquiries are needed.

### **§ 50\. Sensuous forms in categorial grasp, but not in nominal function**

Hitherto we have spoken only of *formings* which the *relation-members* undergo — say whole and part. But in the *outer relations* we see how *sensuous forms* enter into the unity of the relation (into its predicate) and determine the relation-form sensuously, *without undergoing the nominal self-rendering*. E.g. *A brighter than B, A right of B*, and so on. The phenomenological differences — differences of the *apprehension-sense* — between the cases where, so to speak, the brightness-form is simply attended to and then made into a *nominal* object in the manner of the expression "*this brightness-relation \[between A and B\] is more easily noticeable than that \[between M and N\]*," and the quite other-kinded cases where the same brightness-form is meant in the manner of the above expression "*A is brighter than B*" — these differences, Husserl says, are unmistakable. In the latter cases we again find a *categorial form* pointing to a peculiar function in the whole of the relation. To the differences of such forms, as we have come to know them here and in the previous section, there manifestly lead back concepts like *relation-member, relation-form, subject, object*, and other concepts not always distinctly stamped and in any case hitherto not sufficiently clarified.

### **§ 51\. Collectiva and disjunctiva**

As examples of categorial — and indeed synthetic — object-forms, we have hitherto considered only some of the very simplest *state-of-affairs forms*, namely the total and partial identity-relations and the simple outer relations. Husserl now takes into view, as further examples, two synthetic forms which, not themselves states of affairs, play a great role in the context of states of affairs: the *collectiva* and *disjunctiva*. The acts in which they constitute themselves as givennesses are the ones which offer the meanings of the conjunctions *and* and *or* the fulfilling intuition.

What corresponds intuitively to the words *and* and *or*, *both* and *one of the two*, cannot — as Husserl put it above somewhat roughly — be grasped with hands, grasped with any sense; just as it also cannot properly present itself in image, say be *painted*. I can paint A and paint B, can paint both in the same picture-space; but the *both*, the *A and B*, I cannot paint. Here there is only the *one* and ever-open possibility that, on the ground of the two single intuition-acts, we carry out the new act of *conjoining* (colligating) and thereby *mean* the *together* of the objects A and B. In it constitutes itself — in the situation we just had before our eyes as example — the *pictorial* presentation of the *A and B*, while this aggregate is given "*itself*" in the manner of *perception*, and can only be given, in just such a merely conformly modified act which is, however, *founded in the perceptions of A and B*.

That we speak of an *act* uniting these perceptions, and not of any *connection* or even of a *togetherness* of these perceptions in consciousness, lies of course in that here a *unitary intentional relation* is given and, corresponding to it, a unitary object which can constitute itself only in this act-connection — quite as a state of affairs can constitute itself only in the *relating* connecting of presentations. One recognizes here at the same time the essential *error* into which prominent recent logicians have fallen, in believing they might lay under the conjunctive connection of *names* (or of statements) a mere *consciousness-of-togetherness* of the nominal and propositional acts, and thus surrendering the *And* as objective logical form. (In a footnote Husserl cites Sigwart's *Logik*, which treats the linguistic connection of sentences with "And" as expressing merely the subjective fact of being-together in one consciousness, to which no objective meaning accrues.)

One must also guard against confusing the *simple* perceptions of *sensuously-unitary multitudes, rows, swarms*, and the like, with the *conjunctive* perceptions in which alone the *multiplicity-consciousness* itself and properly constitutes itself. Husserl recalls that in his *Philosophy of Arithmetic* he tried to show how the sensuous unity-characters (which he there called *figural* or *quasi-qualitative moments* of the sensuous intuitions) serve as *sensuous signs of plurality* — i.e. as sensuous footholds for the (through them signitively mediated) recognizing of the plurality as such and as plurality of the relevant kind; which recognizing then no longer needs the *articulating* single-apprehension and single-recognition, but for that reason also does not possess the character of proper *intuition of the collection as such*. (A second footnote credits v. Ehrenfels, who treated such intuitive unity-characters and called them *Gestalt-qualities*.)

### **§ 52\. Universal objects constituting themselves in universal intuitions**

The simple synthetic acts with which we have hitherto occupied ourselves were so founded in simple perceptions that the *synthetic intention was co-directed upon the objects of the founding perceptions*, in that it brought them ideally together ("aggregate") or to relating unity. And this is a *general* character of the synthetic acts überhaupt. Husserl now considers examples from *another group of categorial acts*, with which the objects of the founding acts do *not co-enter* into the intention of the founded act, and would attest their close relation to it only in *relating* acts. Here belongs the domain of *universal intuition* (*allgemeine Anschauung*) — an expression which, to be sure, will to many sound no better than "wooden iron."

On the ground of primary intuitions, *abstraction* operates, and with it there occurs a new categorial act-character in which a new kind of objectivity comes to appearance — one which again can come to appearance, as really or pictorially given, *only in such founded acts*. Naturally Husserl does not here mean abstraction in the mere sense of the *highlighting* of some non-self-sufficient moment on a sensuous object, but the *ideating abstraction* in which, instead of the non-self-sufficient moment, its "*idea*," its *general*, comes to consciousness, to *actual givenness*. This act is presupposed in order that, as against the manifold of single moments of one and the same kind, this kind *itself* — and indeed *as one and the same* — can stand before our eyes. For we become conscious, in the repeated performance of such an act on the ground of several individual intuitions, of the *identity of the general*, and this manifestly in an overarching act of *identification* bringing all the single abstraction-acts to synthesis. By means of such abstraction-acts there then grow further, in their interweaving with new act-forms, the acts of *general determination* (the determination of objects *überhaupt* as standing under certain kinds A), likewise the acts in which *indeterminate* single objects of a *kind A* become presentational, and so on.

In the abstraction-act — which need not necessarily carry itself out by means of a *naming* — the general is *self-given* to us; we think it not in merely significative fashion (as in the case of the mere understanding of general names), but we *grasp* it, we *behold* it. Certainly, then, the talk here of the *intuition* — and more closely of the *perception of the general* — is a well-warranted one.

Yet from another side *doubts* arise. The talk of a perception presupposes the possibility of a corresponding *imagination*, and the separation between the two belongs, we said, to the natural sense of the general talk of *intuition*. Just this distinction we *miss* here. This seems to lie in the fact that the abstracting acts do not *differentiate* themselves according to the character of the founding simple intuitions — that they are wholly *insensible* to whether these founding acts are positing or non-positing, perceptive or imaginative. The *red*, the *triangle* of mere fantasy is specifically the same as the red, the triangle in perception. The generality-consciousness builds itself on the ground of perception and of conform imagining equally well; and if it builds itself at all, then the general — the *idea* red, the *idea* triangle — is grasped *itself*; it is intuited in the one and only manner which admits no differences between image and original.

Yet it is to be noted that the examples adduced were precisely of the kind of *adequate* perception of the general. The general was here, on the ground of really corresponding single cases, also really grasped and given. Where the matter stands so, there does indeed seem to be lacking a parallel imagination with the same intuitive content — as in *every* case of adequate perception. How should, even in the individual domain, a content *analogize itself*, since it, taken as itself, cannot at the same time be meant as analogon of itself? And how should the character of *positing* be lacking where the meant content is precisely the experienced and given one? It is otherwise where we have, e.g., conceived through mathematical analysis the *idea* of a certain genus of third-order curves indirectly, without a curve of this genus ever having been intuitive to us. Thereby an intuitive *figure* — say of a familiar particularization of third-order curves, whether drawn or merely imagined — may serve us as *intuitive image, as analogon* of the intended generality: i.e. the generality-consciousness builds itself, as intuitive but as *analogizing*, on the individual intuition. And does not even the ordinary rough drawing, compared with the ideal figure, work analogizingly, co-conditioning the *imaginative character of the general presentation*? Likewise we intuit, on the ground of a *model* of a steam-engine, the idea of the steam-engine, where naturally there can be no talk of adequate abstraction or conception. In such cases we have to do with no mere significations, but with *general representations through analogy* — hence with *general imaginations*. But if there is lacking — as may occur, e.g., with the intuition of a model — the *consciousness of mere analogy*, then there is precisely a case of *perception of the general*, even if of *inadequate* perception.

Likewise we now find the previously-missed differences between *positing* and *undecided generality-consciousness*. Where we conceive a general object merely analogically, imaginatively, we can *mean* it in positing fashion, and this act can, like every positing meaning, confirm or refute itself in future adequate perception. The former, when the general meaning fulfils itself in an adequate perception (i.e. in a new generality-consciousness which constitutes itself on the ground of a "real" abstraction of the corresponding single case). The general object is then not merely presented and posited, but is *itself given*. Again we can present the general in analogical fashion, but without *positing* it: we conceive it but leave it undecided. The intention upon the general, built on intuitive ground, now *decides* not over "being" or "non-being," but over whether the general and its givenness *is possible* or not in the manner of adequate abstraction.

## **Chapter Seven: Study on Categorial Representation**

### **§ 53\. Back-reference to the researches of the First Section**

The *founded acts* we analyzed in selected examples counted for us as *intuitions* — and indeed as intuitions of the new-kinded objects which they bring to appearance, and which can also be given only in founded acts of the kind and form corresponding to them. The clarifying value of this *extension* of the concept of intuition can manifestly consist only in this: that it is not a matter of an extra-essential, merely *disjunctive* concept-extension (which permits expanding the sphere of a given concept over the spheres of arbitrary heterogeneous concepts), but of a *genuine generalization* resting on the *community of essential marks*. We call the new acts *intuitions* because they, under mere surrender of the "simple" relation to the object (that determinate *kind* of "immediacy" which we defined as simplicity), have *all the essential peculiarities of intuitions*; we find with them the same essential separations, as they also prove capable of essentially the same fulfilment-achievements. (In a footnote Husserl distinguishes the worthless *disjunctive* extension — "something that is α or β" — from genuine generalization, and remarks that the disjunctive can nonetheless be useful, e.g. in mathematical technique, a field of fundamental but rigorously soluble problems that logicians have hardly noticed.)

This last-mentioned is for us especially important; for the sake of these achievements we have conducted the whole inquiry. *Knowledge*, as fulfilment-unity, carries itself out not on the mere ground of *simple* acts, but as a rule on the ground of *categorial* acts; and accordingly, when we oppose *intuiting* to *thinking* (as meaning), by intuiting cannot be understood the mere *sensuous* intuiting.

Only through the apprehension of categorial acts *as intuitions* does the relation between thinking and intuiting — hitherto brought by no critique of knowledge to tolerable clarity — become really transparent, and thus knowledge itself become intelligible in its essence and achievement. The provisional findings of the First Section receive, in consequence of this concept-extension, only now their adequate confirmation. To all intuitions in the now-widest sense — however near or far they may stand to sensibility — there correspond, as their possible ideal counter-images, *expressing meanings*. The distinctions we made within the *cognitive essence*, and the concepts connected with them which we formed, retain — though delimited with regard to a narrower sphere — their validity also in the wider one.

Hence every categorial act of intuition has: (1) its *quality*; (2) its *(intentional) matter*, i.e. its *apprehension-sense*; (3) its *representants*.

This distinction does not reduce to the distinctions belonging to the *founding* acts. The quality of the total act can be another than that of a ground-act — as indeed the ground-acts, if there are several, can be variously qualified: e.g. with the presentation of a relation between a fictive and a held-for-real object.

Further, not only does each among the founding acts have a matter, but the *founded* act brings a *proper matter*; whereby there holds the proposition that this *new matter* — or, insofar as it includes the matters of the ground-acts, the *new element* in it — is *founded* in the matters of the ground-acts.

Finally, the new act also has its *representants*. But in reference to these we find — as soon as it comes to the question whether for the new matter *new representants* too must be assumed, and which these are — serious difficulties.

### **§ 54\. The question of the representants of the categorial forms**

Approaching the analysis of the categorial acts, there at first forces itself, as seemingly incontestable, the remark that — quality apart — all differences of the categorial acts reduce to the corresponding differences of the acts founding them; i.e. that the *new* which the categorial function brings in is a *surplus of content* admitting no differentiation. Whereby else, indeed, should the fantasy-presentation of a collection differ from the perception of the same collection than through the *intentional manner* in which its members are given? In the *connection-form*, one will say, no intelligible difference is any longer to be made on either side. Or should the *collection-form* (which the little word *and* stamps) differentiate itself in the *appearance-mode* as perception or imagining? Then we should have to hold it possible that fantasy-appearances were united through the collection-form of *perception*, perception-appearances through the collection-form of *fantasy*, and indeed in differentiated fashion. That is manifestly unthinkable, indeed unintelligible.

One could, to be sure, object that nothing is easier than that. Who hinders us from thinking some perception-objects collectively together, but thereby meaning *imaginatively* another aggregate; and again thinking some fantasy-appearances together, but meaning only *this* aggregate of fantasy-appearances, hence *perceiving* it? — Certainly nothing hinders us in this respect. But then those perception-objects are *images*; i.e. the collective act is then directly founded not in the perceptions, but in the imaginations built upon them. And likewise, in the other case, it is not the objects of the fantasy-presentations, but these presentations themselves, that are colligated; i.e. the collection-act is directly founded not in the fantasy-presentations but in the inner perceptions referred to them. Between the "real" colligating on the ground of perceived, and the "imagined" colligating on the ground of fantasied objects, this proves no difference, and such a difference does not exist at all — except as a difference of the *founding acts*.

The same seems to hold for all other modifications which the collection-consciousness can exhibit. The generality or particularity, the determinacy or indeterminacy, and whatever else of categorial forms may come into consideration with the founding objects, also determines the character of the collective presentation — but so that in the *connection-character* no phenomenological difference is to be found; it is always the same *and*. According to the kind of the founding presentations, there appears to us accordingly a collection of *general* objects (e.g. colour-species: *red and blue and yellow*) or of *individual* objects (*Aristotle and Plato*), of *determinate* objects (as in the previous examples) or of *indeterminate* ones (*a man and another man; a colour and a tone*). It is not to be seen how differences of the collection-acts should be possible otherwise than through those of the founding acts.

Just the same then seems clear, without further ado, also with the *relating* intuitions. The *relating* shows continually the same form; all changes hang on the underlying acts.

But can we, in this situation, still expect *ascertainable differences* between *representants* and *apprehension-sense* in respect of the *new element* in the founded act — hence, with the synthetic acts, in respect of their *connection-form*? With the simple intuitions, apprehension-sense (matter) and representant were indeed intimately united; they were referred to one another and, in their variations, also not quite independent; but thereby they could nonetheless undergo ample *displacements* against one another. At changing apprehension-sense the sensuous representant could remain the same, but at constant apprehension-sense also vary; thus, e.g., a fantasy-presentation can remain identical with itself not merely in matter but even in *extent of fullness*, and yet noticeably change in respect of *liveliness*. In the sphere of sensibility, then, the difference between matter and representant is easily exhibitable and to be claimed as indubitable. But how with the *categorial* acts, where — the founding acts apart — *variability* überhaupt seems to be lacking? Are we to say that, in respect of the form, they lack the difference in question entirely — that they have no representants whatever reaching beyond the representants of the founding acts? And where the founding acts are themselves already categorial (e.g. acts of ideation), there would be lacking to these too the representation; it would lie only in the *ultimately-founding* simple intuitions.

### **§ 55\. Arguments for the assumption of proper categorial representants**

For taking a position on this question it is above all to be noted that the complete *indifference of the forms* — as against the manifold changes of the total act and its foundations — was in the above presentation perhaps *exaggerated* and even *misunderstood*. For if the total act is a perception (categorial perception), the founded act has the character of a self-presentation, not merely a presentification — and this character it does not borrow simply from the founding acts but possesses for itself, as a character of its own form.

If the *form* is the properly *new* and essential in the categorial presentation, it must be co-grasped by every essential character that pervades the whole and belongs to it *as a whole*. If reflection does not show us the differences of the apprehension-sense *in the form* — or at least not in the form of the *synthetic* acts (with the abstractive ones the matter is in fact already settled through the considerations of § 52\) — this explains itself probably by our *involuntarily abstracting* from these apprehension-characters (since they do not single out and delimit the moment of synthesis, but pervade the complete founded act uniformly), in order to attend exclusively to the *common* element that presses forward in all formations, e.g. of the collective synthesis. And just this *common* element could be the sought *representant*. As in simple sensuous perception the perception-sense is a homogeneous unitary something which pervades the whole representation — which has indeed determinate relation to each delimitable part of the representing content and yet, in inner reflection, does not appear as a composite of delimited part-apprehensions — so here, with the categorial intuitions, the apprehension-sense pervades the total act and its whole representation, without delimiting itself distinctly according to the representants distinguishable in reflection.

In the above presentation, then — if we admit this interpretation — there would lie the important truth *that, through all change of founding acts and apprehension-forms, the representing content is, for each kind of founded act, a single one*. To simple sensuous intuition there stands at disposal, for purposes of representation, the over-rich manifold of sense-qualities, sensible forms, and so on. In the sphere of the collective intuitions, or the identity-intuitions, etc., we would each be restricted to *one* kind: the *And-form* is everywhere the same, likewise the *Is-form*, and so on. But these forms would here be understood as the *analoga of the sensuous kernel*, of the sensible in sensuous intuition; from the quality and the apprehension-sense it would be abstracted.

One could harbour the suspicion that here the wish is father to the thought, and make us attentive how, from our earlier considerations, it emerges that the representants make up no *thoroughly essential* components of the acts. It is, after all, the peculiarity of all *signitive* acts that they *lack* representants — to be precise, the *proper* representants, which have relation to the contentual stock of the object itself. For *improper* representants — which present not the object meant in the act, but some other, the object of a founding act — the signitive acts too have. But if *improper* representants suffice, then we are no longer in embarrassment; for such are self-evidently not lacking in our case — the founding acts offer them to us at any time; their proper representants could be apprehended, in regard to the founded act, as improper ones.

Yet just the comparison with the merely signitive acts brings us to *living consciousness* that with the founded acts *without proper representation* — namely in respect of the categorial form — there is no getting by; it reminds us of the relations of possible fulfilment, of the "fullness" which the intuitive acts offer the signitive, of the increase-series which within the intuitive acts are conditioned by the changing fullness, with the last adequation as the ideal limit. It is the representants that make up the difference between "empty" signification and "full" intuition; to them the "fullness" is owed, for which reason they outright determined the one word-sense of fullness. Only the intuitive acts bring the object to "appearance," to "intuition" — namely by a *representant* being present which the apprehension-form grasps *as analogon* or *as the self* of the object. This is a situation grounded in the *general essence of the fulfilment-relation*, and it must therefore be demonstrable also in the present sphere. In it too we find the opposition between signitive and intuitive: the opposition between objectifying acts which mean a categorial objectivity *signitively*, and parallel acts which present the same objectivity, in the same apprehension-sense, *intuitively*, be it "in image" or "itself." Since the intentional matter is on both sides the same, we can grasp the *new* on the side of the categorial intuition again only thus: that it is precisely *representation*, that it sets the objective contentually before us, that it apprehends *experienced contents* as representants of the meant object. But the representation cannot be carried out in the founding acts *alone* — not merely their objects are presentified, but the whole *state of affairs*, the whole *aggregate*, and so on.

### **§ 56\. Continuation. The psychic bond of the connected acts and the categorial unity of the corresponding objects**

One could for a moment think that, e.g., in the case of a *relation*, only the relation-points are presentified, and the new lies in a mere *psychic character* connecting the two appearances. But a *connection of the acts* is not without further ado a *connection of the objects*; at best it can help such a connection to appearance: it itself is not the connection that *appears* in it. The psychic bond between the acts can be established, and thereby the objective relation appear, while this relation — even if it sets into one really existing objects — does not subsist at all. Judging *significatively* and without intuitive presentification of the judged state of affairs (as, say, with the ordinary arithmetical judgments), the relating unity of the act is an articulated one; it has its psychic connection-form, and the exactly analogous one as in the case of corresponding intuition. But the state of affairs does not, pregnantly speaking, "*appear*"; it is merely *meant*.

Taking, by contrast, the case of *intuitive presentification* — as when we identify the colour of two perceived (or through memory re-presentified) surfaces, or, say, the person presented in two imaginative presentations — the identity is again *meant*, but meant *in the manner of perception* (which gives the object), or in that of *imagery* (which images it). What makes such differences possible? Are we to say that the whole difference lies in the founding acts? But against this our objection would rise: that, e.g., in the signitive identification the identity of the meant objects is not *experienced*, but this identity is merely *supposed*; further, that in the case of *intuition* of the objects the identity is indeed perceived or imagined identity, but only in the case of *adequation* in the full and strict sense *given and experienced* identity. *The psychic bond that establishes the synthesis is, then, meaning, and as such is more or less fulfilled.* It is indeed a mere and non-self-sufficient component of the total meaning — a significative one of a significative, an intuitive one of an intuitive meaning; but, with all that, a component that itself shares the character of meaning, and thereby also the differences of fullness. Accordingly we interpret — probably not unwarrantedly — the situation so that this component too exercises the *function of a representation*: the psychic bond experienced in the actual identifying or colligating and the like (in the "actual," i.e. the proper, intuitive one) we believe we can, in the comparative consideration of various cases and in the manner of the above-weighed possibility, reduce to an everywhere-*common* element — to be thought apart from quality and apprehension-sense — which in this reduction yields that representant belonging specially to the moment of the categorial form.

### **§ 57\. The representants of the founding intuitions not immediately connected through the representants of the synthetic form**

Here some not-quite-unimportant remarks naturally fit in, which concern the relation of the categorial representant to the representants of the founding acts.

*Objectively* considered, the synthesis — e.g. the synthesis of identity, of the attributive relation, and so on — belongs to the *founding objects*; the identity is, say, identity of the *person*, the attributive relation, say, relation between the subject *tree* and the predicate *fruit-bearing*. The connected objects now appear to us by means of their *representants*, and so one might think that the synthetic bond — in which (or by means of which, likewise in the manner of a representant) the connection appears to us as form — binds those representants of the founding objects phenomenologically simply and directly to one another.

Against this we establish, however, that *the moment of synthesis establishes no direct connection of the representants belonging to the ground-acts*, but that, e.g., the phenomenological form of the identification grounds essentially *in the founding acts as such* — hence grounds in what these *are and contain beyond their representing contents*.

Were the experienced identity-moment, the psychic character, an *immediate bond* of the representing sensuous contents (we can restrict ourselves to the simplest case, where the founding acts or objects are sensuous), then the unity established through this moment would also be a *sensuous* unity, just as, e.g., the spatial or qualitative configurations or the other unity-kinds which the relevant sensuous contents otherwise still ground. But all sensuous (real) unity is unity *founded in the content-genera of the sensuous*, as was already set forth in the Third Investigation. The concrete contents are indeed many-sided; they carry various abstract moments in themselves, they ground manifold possibilities of alteration and connection. Accordingly we lead some connection-kinds back to these, some to those moments. But even if the respective unifications are not always founded in the genera of the complex wholes according to their full specific content, they are at any rate founded in the *primitive genera* which correspond to the *moments* of the respective wholes. By contrast, the *factual relationlessness* of the categorial act-forms to the sensuous contents of their bases shows itself in this: that the genera of these contents are *limitlessly variable* — in other words, that *a priori* no content-genus is possible which could not function in the foundation of categorial acts of every kind. The categorial belongs precisely not to the representing sensuous *contents*, but — and indeed necessarily — to the *objects*, and yet not to them according to their sensuous (real) content. Therein, however, lies: *the psychic character in which the categorial form constitutes itself belongs phenomenologically to the acts in which the objects constitute themselves.*

In these acts the sensuous contents are present as representants, and to that extent they too belong *to* these acts. But they do not form the *characteristic essence* of the acts; they can also be *without* the apprehension which first makes them representants — they then *are*, but with them nothing *appears*, and consequently nothing is present which could be connected, which could be grasped as subject or predicate, etc., in categorial fashion. *Not these extra-essential elements of the founding acts does the categorial moment of the synthetically founded act connect, but their bilaterally essential element; it connects under all circumstances their intentional matters and is in them, in the true sense, founded.* So we already pronounced it above generally: in all categorial acts, we said, the matter of the founded acts is founded in the matters of the founding acts. *Identity*, e.g., is immediately no unity-form of sensuous contents, but a "*unity of consciousness*" grounded in the one or other (the "repeated" or content-wise different) consciousness of the same object. And so everywhere. It is now indeed correct that intuitions of every kind — whether simple or categorial — can undergo, according to kind, the same categorial formings; but thereby it is only said that the categorial forming is phenomenologically founded in the *general* of the objectifying act, that it is a function essentially bound to the *generic* of the objectifying acts. Only experiences of this genus admit categorial syntheses, and the synthesis connects *directly the intentional essences*.

Especially in the case of *adequate* synthetic intuitions — immediately founded in individual intuitions — one must keep at a distance the deceptive *appearance* as though, at least on this lowest level of categorial synthesis, an *immediate* phenomenological connection ran from the sensuous representants of the one founding act to those of the other. By virtue of the functional dependence of the adequation (evidence) of the total act on the adequation of the founding intuitions, the situation seems here to shape itself thus: since the founding acts are adequate, the representing content coincides with the represented object. If now, on such a basis, the intuition of a *relation* takes place — e.g. of a relation between part and whole — then the relating act too has the character of *evidence*; the relation is, with the truly given contents themselves, truly given. Hence here the psychic bond of the relating, apprehended on the sensuous contents and objects as relation, connects these experienced sensuous contents in the manner of a *direct bond*.

By no means, we would object. *Not the sensuous contents, but the adequate intuitions of these contents, are what here found the unity of the relation-act.* As everywhere, so here we must look toward the *objects* — those at once representing and represented sensuous contents — in order to be able to carry out the relating act, to set this content *as whole* in relation to that content *as part*. Relations can be given only on the ground of *given objects*; but objects are given to us not through mere *experiencing* (which is in itself blind), but solely and alone through *perceiving* — and here, in the example-case, through perceiving the experienced contents that no longer represent beyond themselves.

With this, however, only our *original introduction of the categorial acts as founded ones* corroborates itself. It is essential to these acts — in which everything intellectual constitutes itself — to carry themselves out *in levels*; objectivations carry themselves out on the ground of objectivations and constitute objects which, as objects in the extended, intellectual sense (as objects of higher order), can appear *only in such founded acts*. But this excludes, with the synthetic acts, the *immediate unity of representation* such as unites all representants of the simple intuition. The whole synthetic intuition then comes about (if the above-attempted interpretation, in need of the most careful re-examination, is correct) in such a way that the *psychic content* connecting the founding acts is *apprehended* as objective unity of the founded objects, as their relation of identity, of part to whole, and so on.

### **§ 58\. The relation of the two distinctions: outer and inner sense as well as sense of \[sensibility\] and sense of category**

It is of great importance now to bring also the relation of those two distinctions — introduced at the very beginning of our present considerations — to last clarity: namely the distinctions between *outer and inner sensibility* on the one side, and between *simple and categorial acts* on the other.

The *presentation* as psychic experience — indifferent whether it is simple or founded, hence sensuous or categorial — belongs to the sphere of the "*inner sense*." But does not a *contradiction* lie herein? Is an inner perception which "reflects" on an act, and even on a *founded* act — e.g. on the actual insighting of the identity 2+1 \= 1+2 — not *eo ipso* a founded, hence *non-sensuous*, perception? In the act of this perception the founded act, together with the acts founding it, is given, and given in the strictest sense. It belongs to the *real stock* of the perception. Insofar as the perception thereby directs itself upon it, it is *referred* to it, and is therefore itself a *founded* perception.

Manifestly we shall have to say: the perceiving of an act (or act-moment or act-complex), however constituted, is called a *sensuous* perceiving because it is a *simple* perceiving — i.e. it grasps its object (the act on which it reflects) in *one* level, without the necessity of building itself, in the manner of the categorial acts, upon other objectivities through which alone its object would come to constitution.

This it doubtless is, because the relation of the perceiving act to a perceived is *no founding-relation* and does not become one even when as the perceived act a *founded* one is assumed. The *being-founded* of an act does not say that it is, in whatever sense, *built upon* other acts, but that the founded act is, *by its nature* (i.e. its genus), possible only as one that builds itself upon acts of the genus of the founding ones, and that consequently the objective correlate of the founded act has a *general*, a *form*, with which an object überhaupt can appear *intuitively* only in a founded act of this genus. Thus the intuitive generality-consciousness cannot subsist without underlying individual intuition, an identification cannot subsist without underlying acts concerning the identified objects, and so on. But the perceiving directed upon a founded act can be directed exactly likewise upon a *non-founded* act and upon arbitrary objects of outer sensibility — upon horses, colours, etc.

In every case this perceiving consists in the *simple looking-toward* the object. The matter of the perceiving (its apprehension-sense) stands in *no* necessity-connection with the matter of the perceived act; rather the whole phenomenological content of this act has the mere character of a *representant* — it is, according to the apprehension-form of the perception, objectively interpreted, namely as *this act itself*. For this reason, also *every abstraction* that builds itself on *inner* sensibility — say in the *looking-toward* a founded act — is a *sensuous* abstraction. By contrast, an abstraction that builds itself *on a founded act itself* (insofar as it itself possesses the character of an intuition, though a categorial one) is a *categorial abstraction*. Looking toward an intuitive *act of identification* (i.e. an intuition of identity) and abstracting thereby the moment of *identifying*, we have carried out a *sensuous abstraction*. But looking, *living in the identification*, toward the *objective identity* and making this the basis of an abstraction, we have carried out a *categorial abstraction*. The objective moment "identity" is no act and no act-form; it is an *objective categorial form*. By contrast, the moment of identifying, which unites the founded acts phenomenologically, is a *sensuous act-form*. In essence the same difference separates also otherwise the concepts formed on the ground of *reflection* on some intuitive acts, and the quite other concepts formed on the ground of these intuitive acts *themselves*. I perceive a house, and reflecting on the *perception*, I form the concept *perception*. But if I simply look toward the *house* — hence use, instead of the perception *of* this perception, rather this perception *itself* as founding act of the abstraction — there arises the concept *house*.

Accordingly there is nothing striking when we say: *the same psychic moments which are sensuously given in inner perception* (functioning in it thus as sensuous representants) *can, in a founded act of the character of categorial perception (or imagination), constitute a categorial form* — hence thereby bear a quite other, *categorial representation*.

The non-self-sufficiency of the categorial forms *as forms* mirrors itself, in the domain of inner sensibility, in this: that the moments in which a categorial form can constitute itself (and these moments are, for each form, so narrowly bounded that to each *form-species* there corresponds a single species of such moments) present *non-self-sufficient psychic contents* founded in act-characters. But since all act-characters are ultimately founded in *externally sensuous contents*, we notice that *in the domain of sensibility an essential phenomenological separation obtains*. There determine themselves: (1) the *reflexion-contents*, as those contents which are themselves act-characters or are founded in act-characters; (2) the *primary contents*, as those contents in which all reflexion-contents are immediately or mediately founded. These would be the contents of the "*outer*" sensibility — which here, however, appears defined not through any relation to the difference of outer and inner (which is a metaphysical one), but through the *nature of their representants*, as ultimately-founding phenomenologically-experienced contents. The primary contents form a *single*, though in many kinds splitting, highest *genus*. The manner in which the reflexion-contents undergo founding through primary contents is manifestly the loosest conceivable — namely such that the reflexion-contents are *never* bound to a *narrower* genus of the primary.

There then corresponds to the difference between purely sensuous and purely categorial *objects* of intuition also a difference of the *representing contents*: as purely categorial representants there can function *exclusively reflexion-contents*.

The concept of *category* one could now also attempt to determine so that it *comprises all objective forms which stem from the apprehension-forms and not from the apprehension-stuffs*. Admittedly the following doubt grows. Would then not the *sensuous* intuition too have the character of a categorial act, insofar as it constitutes the *form of objectivity* (*Gegenständlichkeit*)? In perception the perceived not only *is*, but it *is given in it as object*. However, the concept *object* constitutes itself in *correlation* with the concept *perception*, and presupposes, then, not merely an act of abstraction but also acts of *relation*. Insofar, this concept too is a categorial one in the previous sense.

## **Chapter Eight: The A Priori Laws of Genuine and Improper Thinking**

### **§ 59\. Complication to ever new forms. Pure form-doctrine of possible intuitions**

The various forms of *founded acts* — in which, instead of the simple, sensuous-intuitive objects, rather the categorially-formed and synthetically-connected ones constitute themselves — permit manifold *complications* to new forms, insofar as categorial unities can ever again (and indeed on the ground of certain categorial lawfulnesses of *a priori* kind) become objects of new categorial acts.

So one can, e.g., connect general objects collectively, connect the collections so formed again collectively with others of equal or different kind, and so *in infinitum*. The possibility of *unbounded* complication is here *a priori* and *evident*. Likewise one can unite states of affairs — though only within lawful limits — into new states of affairs; one can überhaupt and into the unbounded seek the inner or outer *relations* between all possible unities, use the results of this establishing again as objects of new relations, and so on. Self-evidently the complication carries itself out in founded acts of ever-higher level. The lawfulness here prevailing is the *intuitive counterpart of the purely logical-grammatical lawfulness*. Here too it is not a matter of laws which want to *judge* the truthful *being* of the presented objects of various level; these laws say in any case nothing direct about the ideal conditions of the possibility of adequate fulfilment. To the *pure form-doctrine of meanings* there corresponds here a *pure form-doctrine of intuitions*, in which the primitive types of simple and complex intuitions would have to be shown as possible through *intuitive generalization*, and the lawfulnesses of their successive complication to ever new and more complicated intuitions determined. Insofar as the *adequate* intuition itself presents a type of intuitions, the pure form-doctrine of intuitions überhaupt comprises also all the laws concerning the forms of *adequate* intuitions; and these then have special relation to the laws of the adequate *fulfilment* of significative or already intuitive intentions.

### **§ 60\. The relative or functional difference between matter and form. Pure and sensibility-mixed acts of understanding. Sensuous concepts and categories**

With the possibility of making categorial intuitions themselves again into foundations of new categorial intuitions — and then also of expressing them in corresponding expressions or meanings — the *relative, merely functional* difference of *stuff* and *form* hangs together. We have already fleetingly indicated it above. In the *absolute* sense, a *founding sensibility* gives the *stuff* for the acts of categorial form built upon it. In the *relative* sense, the *objects of the founding acts überhaupt* form the *stuff*, namely relative to the categorial forms *newly* growing for them in the founded acts. Setting two already-categorial objects (e.g. two states of affairs) into a relation, these states of affairs are the *stuff* relative to the relation-form setting them both into one. To this determination of the concepts *stuff* and *form* there corresponds exactly the traditional distinction between *matter and form of the statements*. The termini express precisely the founding acts of the whole "relating presenting" — or, what is the same, they name the founding *objects* — and *therefore* they also present the place at which contributions of sensibility alone can be sought.

But the founding objects can themselves already be of categorial kind. Manifestly *the fulfilment then carries itself out in a chain of acts which lead us down the graded sequence of foundings*; for in any case *indirect presentations* here play an essential role, whose exact investigation would be a very significant task for the clarification of the complicated forms of cognizing thinking.

The acts of simple intuition we called *sensuous*, the founded acts (immediately or mediately leading back to sensibility) *categorial*. But it is of importance to distinguish, within the sphere of the categorial acts, between *purely categorial acts* — acts of the "*pure understanding*" — and *mixed*, with-sensibility-"mingled" acts of understanding. It lies in the nature of the matter that ultimately all the categorial rests on sensuous intuition — indeed that a categorial intuition (hence an understanding-insight, a thinking in the highest sense) without founding sensibility is a *Widersinn* (a countersense). The idea of a "*pure intellect*," interpreted as a "*faculty*" of pure thinking (here: categorial action) and *wholly detached* from every "faculty of sensibility," could only be conceived *before* an elementary analysis of knowledge according to its evidently irremovable stock. Nevertheless the indicated distinction — hence the concept of the purely categorial act, and, if one will, the concept of a pure understanding — has a good sense.

Considering, namely, the peculiarity of *ideating abstraction* — to rest necessarily on individual intuition, but for that reason not to *mean* the individual of this intuition; heeding that it is rather a new mode of apprehension which, instead of individuality, constitutes *generality* — there grows the possibility of *general intuitions which exclude from their intentional content not only everything individual, but everything sensuous*. In other words, we distinguish between *sensuous abstraction*, which gives us *sensuous concepts* (whether purely sensuous or mixed with categorial forms), and *purely categorial abstraction*, which gives us *purely categorial concepts*. *Colour, house, judgment, wish* are purely sensuous concepts; *colouredness* (being-coloured), *virtue, the axiom of parallels*, and the like, are categorially mixed; *unity, plurality, relation, concept* are purely categorial. Where we speak simply of categorial concepts, always *purely* categorial ones are meant. The sensuous concepts find their immediate basis in givennesses of *sensuous* intuition, the categorial in such of *categorial* intuition — and indeed with pure reference to the categorial form of the whole categorially-formed object. If, e.g., a relation-intuition underlies the abstraction, the abstraction-consciousness directs itself perhaps upon the *relation-form in specie*, such that everything sensuous of the relation-foundations remains out of play. So grow the *categories* — which title, understood in the pregnant sense, comprises, however, only the *primitive* pertinent concepts.

We have just — this lay in the whole sense of the discussion carried out — identified *concept* and *species*. If, however, one understands by concepts the *general presentations* instead of the general *objects* (be it the *general intuitions* or the *general meanings* corresponding to them), the distinction transfers without further ado also to them; likewise to presentations of the form *an A*, namely with regard to whether the species A can contain or, on the contrary, exclude something sensuous. Purely categorial, accordingly, are all *logical forms and formulae*, like *all S are P, no S is P*, and so on; for the letters S, P, and the like, are mere indirect *indications* for "certain," indeterminate and "arbitrary" concepts, so that to them there corresponds, in the total meaning of the formula, a complex thought built out of nothing but categorial elements. Like the whole of *pure logic*, so the whole of *pure arithmetic*, the *pure doctrine of manifolds* — in short, the *pure mathesis* in the most comprehensive sense — is *pure* in the sense that it contains, in its whole theoretical stock, no *sensuous* concept.

### **§ 61\. The categorial forming no real transformation of the object**

The talk of *categorial form* we use, as is visible from the last series of considerations, in a *natural* and (given our consistent distinction between act and object) harmless *double sense*. *On the one hand* we understand by it the founded *act-characters* which give form to the acts of simple (or even already-founded) intuition and transform them into new objectivations. These latter constitute an objectivity modified, in comparison with the founding acts, in peculiar fashion; the original objects are contained in the new ones — they are present in them in changed but recognizable form. *On the other hand*, we understand by categorial form the correlative objective moment, the *form of the objectivity* itself, as it constitutes itself in those founded act-characters.

The conjunctive connection *A and B*, which as a unitary act means a categorial unity of objects (the aggregate, the "all both"), can serve us as example.

The expression *A and B* illustrates to us — and indeed in special regard to the meaning of the "and" — moreover yet a further sense of the talk of categorial form, according to which also the *significative forms* (which find their possible fulfilment in the founded act-characters) are designated as categorial forms, and, more cautiously, as categorial forms in the *improper* sense.

This presupposed, Husserl wants to bring an already-pronounced proposition — in view of his whole presentation properly self-evident — to fully developed clarity for the sake of its importance: namely that *the categorial functions, in "forming" the sensuous object, leave it untouched in its real essence*. The object is intellectively *grasped* through the intellect, and specially through knowledge (which is itself a categorial function), but not *falsified*. To make this clear, Husserl recalls the difference, already touched in passing, between the categorial unities understood in the *objective* sense and the *real* unities — as, e.g., the unity of the parts of a thing, of the trees of an avenue, and the like. Also the unity of the real components of a psychic experience, and likewise of all coexistent experiences in the individual consciousness, belongs to the *real* unities. All these unities, considered as wholes, are, like their parts, *objects in the primary and simple sense*; they are intuitable in possible *simple* intuitions. They are precisely not merely *categorially* united; they do not constitute themselves in any mere *together-considering* through colligating, disjoining, relating, and the like; rather they are "*in themselves*" united — they have a unity-form which is perceivable *on the whole* in the manner of a *real* unity-moment (hence a real determination), and perceivable in the same sense as any of the connected members and *their* inner determinacies are.

Quite otherwise it stands with the *categorial* forms. The new objects they create are *not objects in the primary and original sense*. The categorial forms do not glue, knit, join the parts together so that out of it a real, a sensuously perceivable whole would arise. They do *not* form in the sense in which the *potter* forms. Otherwise the originally given of sensuous perception would be *modified* in its own objectivity; the relating and connecting thinking and knowing would not be thinking and knowing *of what is*, but a *falsifying transformation* into something other. But the categorial forms leave the primary objects *untouched*; and they can also do nothing to them, cannot alter them in their own being — because the result would then be a *new* object in the primary and real sense, while evidently the result of the categorial act (say the collective or relating one) consists in an *objective grasp* of the primarily-intuited which can be given *only in such a founded act*, so that the thought of a *simple perception* of the formed, or of a givenness of it in some other simple intuiting, is a *Widersinn* (countersense).

### **§ 62\. The freedom in the categorial forming of pre-given stuff, and its limits: the purely categorial laws (laws of "genuine" thinking)**

Real, externally or internally sensuous unity-forms are lawfully determined through the essential nature of the parts to be connected, and, at fully-taken individuation of these parts, *absolutely* determined. All unity points to lawfulness, real unity to real lawfulness. What is *really* one *must* also be *really* united. Where we speak of the *freedom to unite or not to unite*, there we precisely take the contents *not* in their full reality (to which the spatio-temporal determinacies belong). While in this way consciousness — and specially the simple intuiting of the real contents — is *eo ipso* consciousness of their real connections or forms, it stands quite otherwise with the *categorial* forms. With the real contents *none* of the categorial forms to be fitted to them is *necessarily* given; here there obtains, in connecting and relating, in generalizing and subsuming and the like, *ample freedom*. We can divide a sensuously-unitary group arbitrarily and in manifold ways into part-groups; we can order the manifoldly distinguishable part-groups arbitrarily and knit them together at equal level, or also build collections of second, third … level one over the other. So there result many *possibilities of collective forming* on the ground of the same sensuous stuff. Likewise we can compare any member of one and the same sensuous complexion with these or those among the other members, or distinguish it from them; we can make each thereby into *subject-member* or, through arbitrary reversal of the relevant relations, into *object-member*; we can then set these relations themselves into relation to one another, connect them collectively, classify them, and so on.

But however great this *freedom of categorial unification and forming* is, it has nonetheless its *lawful limits*. Here too *unity and law* are inseparable from one another. Already in this — that the categorial forms constitute themselves in *founded* act-characters, and only in such — there lies a certain *necessity-connection* enclosed. How else would there be talk of categorial *perception* and *intuition*, if any arbitrary stuff let itself be brought into any arbitrary form — if the founding simple intuitions let themselves be knit arbitrarily with the categorial characters. Where we, e.g., intuitively carry out a relation between whole and part, we can indeed reverse this relation in the *normal* way, but not so that we could now intuit the *part*, at unchanged real content, as *whole* and the whole as part. It is also not free to us to apprehend this relation as one of total identity or total exclusion, and so on. Admittedly we can "*think*" to ourselves any relation between any relation-points, and any form überhaupt on the ground of any stuff — namely *think* in the sense of mere signification. But *really carry out* the foundings we cannot on every basis; we cannot *intuit* the sensuous stuff in any arbitrary categorial form; especially not *perceive*, and above all not *adequately* perceive.

In the stamping of the extended perception-concept a certain *boundness* attests itself *eo ipso*. Not as though the perception-character were *really* bound to the sensuous content. That it never is; for that would mean that nothing *is* which is not *perceived* and must be perceived. But very well is nothing which cannot be *perceived*. Therein, however, lies: the actual *performance* of the categorial acts on the ground of just these stuffs (or, more exactly, on the ground of just these simple intuitions) is, in the ideal sense, *possible*. And these possibilities are, like ideal possibilities überhaupt, lawfully bounded, insofar as certain *impossibilities*, ideal incompatibilities, lawfully step to their side.

The *ideal laws* which regulate the connection of these possibilities and impossibilities belong to the *categorial forms in specie*, hence to the *categories* in the *objective* sense. They determine *which variations of any pre-given categorial forms are possible at presupposed identity of the determinate but arbitrary stuff*; they teach, e.g., which form-transformations the available stuff *necessarily* requires or excludes, and what new forms it thereby makes possible.

They circumscribe the *ideally closed manifold* of re-orderings and re-formings of the categorial forms on the ground of the *identically* remaining stuff. The stuff comes here into question only insofar as it must be intentionally held *fast in identity with itself*. But insofar as the *species* of the stuffs are wholly freely variable, and stand only under the self-evident *ideal condition* that they are *capable of functioning* as bearers of the respectively undertaken forms, the laws in question have the character of wholly *pure* and *analytic* laws; they are *wholly independent of the particularity of the stuffs*. Their general expression therefore contains nothing of stuff-species; rather it uses only *algebraic symbols* as bearers of indeterminate-general presentations of certain, otherwise arbitrary, but self-identically-to-be-kept stuffs überhaupt.

For the *insight* into these laws there is therefore no need of the actual performance of a categorial *intuition* that really makes its stuffs intuitive; rather there suffices *any* categorial intuition which sets before our eyes the *possibility* of the relevant categorial formation. In the generalizing abstraction of the total *possibility*, the unitary intuitive "*insight*" into the law carries itself out, and this insight has, in the sense of our doctrine, the character of *adequate generic perception*. The general object self-given in it is the *categorial law*. We may say: *the ideal conditions of the possibility of categorial intuition überhaupt are correlatively the conditions of the possibility of the objects of categorial intuition and of the possibility of categorial objects simply.* That a categorially so-and-so-formed objectivity is possible — this stands in essential correlation with the fact that a categorial intuition (be it even a mere imagining) can set such an objectivity completely adequately before our eyes; in other words, *that the relevant categorial syntheses and other categorial acts are, on the ground of the relevant founding intuitions (be they even imaginings), really performable*.

But *which* categorial forming an arbitrary stuff — indifferent whether perceptively or imaginatively pre-given — *de facto* admits (i.e. which categorial acts are really performable on the ground of the sensuous intuitions constituting it) — about this the ideal conditions in question, the *analytic laws*, say *nothing*. That here no arbitrariness can prevail limitlessly, and that the "real" performability has not the character of empirical reality but of *ideal* possibility, the above examples teach. And they teach also that it is the respective *particularity of the stuff* which circumscribes the possibilities, so that we *can* say, e.g., *G is really a whole of γ*, or *γ is really a property of G*, and the like — whereby, however, the categorial form, *unlike* the real, is *not* restricted to the *content-genera* of the G, γ, and the like, as though it did not come into consideration for contents of other genera at all. On the contrary, it is evident that *contents of all genera can be formed through all categories*. The categorial forms are precisely *not* founded in the stuff-contents, as we have already set forth above. Those pure laws can therefore *not* prescribe which form a *given* stuff can take on; they teach only this much: that *if* it — and any stuff überhaupt — has taken on a certain form, or is capable of taking it on, a *fixedly-circumscribed circle of further forms* stands at command for this same stuff; or, that *there is an ideally-closed circle of possible transformations of the respectively prevailing form into ever new forms*. The *ideal possibility* of the new forms on the ground of the same stuff the said "analytic" laws guarantee, under this presupposition, *a priori*.

These are the pure *laws of "genuine thinking"* (*eigentliches Denken*), understood as laws of the *categorial intuitions according to their purely categorial forms*. The categorial intuitions function precisely, in theoretical thinking, as actual or possible meaning-fulfilments (or \-disappointments), and confer, according to their function, on the statements the logical value of *truth* or *untruth*. The *normative regulation* of the (whether purely signitive or signitively clouded) thinking depends, then, on the just-discussed laws.

But for the more exact exposition of the state of affairs, and for the clarification of the distinguishing talk of laws of "genuine" thinking, a closer regard to the sphere of the *meanings* (or meaning-intentions) is needed.

### **§ 63\. The new validity-laws of signitive and signitively clouded acts (laws of improper thinking)**

The categorial acts we have hitherto thought free of all significative accessory — hence performed, but founding no acts of recognition and naming. And surely every prejudice-free analyst will concede that we can, e.g., intuit aggregates or various primitive states of affairs without bringing them to nominal or propositional expression. We now oppose to the case of mere *intuition* the case of mere *signification*; we heed that to all the acts of categorial intuition, with their categorially-formed objects, *purely significative* acts can correspond. This is manifestly an *a priori* possibility. There is no pertinent act-form to which a possible meaning-form would not correspond; and every meaning can indeed be thought as performed *without* correlate intuition. The *ideal of the logically adequate language* is that of a language which would procure unambiguous expression for all possible stuffs and all possible categorial forms. To the words there belong then, unambiguously, certain significative intentions, which can live even in the *absence* of the "corresponding" (i.e. of course the fulfilling) intuition. There then runs parallel to all possible primary and founded intuitions the *system* of the primary and founded *meanings* (possibly) expressing them.

But *the domain of meaning is very much more comprehensive than that of intuition* — i.e. than the total domain of possible fulfilments. For on the side of the meanings there enters in addition that *unbounded manifold of complex meanings which lack "reality" or "possibility"*; these are *formations of meanings* which do indeed close together into *unitary meanings*, but into such as can correspond to *no possible unitary fulfilment-correlate*. Accordingly there obtains also *no full parallelism* between the *categorial types* (or the types of categorial intuition) and the *types of meaning*. To every categorial type of lower and higher level there corresponds a meaning-type; but there does not correspond, given our freedom to connect the types significatively into complex types, to every type so growing a type of categorial *objectivity*. Husserl recalls the types of *analytic contradictions*, like *an A which is not A; all A are B and some A is not B*, and so on. Only in respect of the *primitive* types can and must the parallelism subsist, since all primitive meanings überhaupt have their "*origin*" in the fullness of correlate intuition.

Or, to express it more distinctly: since compatibility and incompatibility are spoken of only in the sphere of the *composite* (or of what is to be composed), the *simple* meaning, as expression of a *simple*, can never be "imaginary"; and this holds therefore also of every simple meaning-form. While an *at-once-A-and-not-A-being* is impossible, an *A and B* is possible; the *And-form*, as a simple one, has a "real" sense.

Transferring the terminus *categorial* to the meaning-domain, there corresponds to every *proper* categorial form (be it one in the objective sense, be it the belonging categorial form of the *intuition* — in which, namely, the categorial objective constitutes itself perceptively or imaginatively) a proper *significative form* (or also a proper *meaning-form in specie*). In this form of signification the significative *meaning* of a collectivum or disjunctivum, of an identity or non-identity, and the like, carries itself out. Speaking of the opposition of *proper* and *improper presentation*, one has usually the opposition of *intuitive* and *significative* in view (provided it is not aimed, as occasionally also occurs, at the other opposition of *adequate* and *inadequate*). Accordingly the present cases would be those of "*improper*" collection, disjunction, identification, abstraction, and so on.

Comprising all these categorial acts — through which the judgments (as predicative significations) appropriate to themselves their fullness and finally their whole knowledge-value — under the title *thought-acts*, we would have to distinguish between *proper* and *improper thought-acts*. The improper thought-acts would be the meaning-intentions of the statements and, in naturally *extended* grasp, all the significative acts which can possibly serve as parts of such predicative intentions: but so, as is self-evident, *all* significative acts can serve. The *proper thought-acts* would be the corresponding *fulfilments*; hence the state-of-affairs-intuitions and all intuitions which can function as possible parts of state-of-affairs-intuitions: and these in turn can be *all* intuitions überhaupt; there is especially no categorial form that could not become a component of a state-of-affairs form. The *general doctrine of the forms of the symbolic judgments* (the statement-meanings) comprises that of the *meaning-forms* überhaupt (the purely logical-grammatical forms); likewise the general doctrine of the *pure forms of the state-of-affairs intuitions* (or of the pure state-of-affairs forms) comprises that of the *categorial forms of the intuitions* (or of the objective categorial forms) überhaupt.

Identifying, as often happens, *thinking* and *judging*, there would be to distinguish between *proper* and *improper judging*. The concept of judgment would then be determined through the *common* of statement-intention and statement-fulfilment — hence through the intentional essence as unity of quality and intentional matter. As thought-acts in the *wider* sense there would naturally count not merely the judgment-acts, but all possible *part-acts* of judgments, so that we would come back to a delimitation of the concept *thought-act* equivalent to the previous one.

In the sphere of *improper thinking*, of mere signification, we are free of all the limits of the categorial laws. In it everything and anything lets itself be brought to unity. But exactly seen, this freedom too is subject to certain restrictions. We spoke of this in the Fourth Investigation; we pointed to the *purely logical-grammatical laws* which, as laws of complication and modification, separate the spheres of *sense* and *nonsense*. In the improper categorial forming and re-forming we are free, provided only we do not *nonsensically conglomerate* the meanings. But if we want to keep at a distance also the *formal* and *real countersense* (*Widersinn*), then the widest sphere of improper thinking — of the significatively connectable — narrows very much. It is now a matter of the *objective possibility* of the complex meanings, hence of the possibility of their adaptation to an intuition fulfilling them as wholes unitarily. The *pure laws of the validity of the meanings* — of the *ideal possibility of their adequate illustration* — run manifestly *parallel* to the pure laws which regulate the connection and transformation of the *proper* categorial forms.

In the pure laws of meaning-validity it is again not a matter of laws in which the validity of arbitrarily pre-given meanings could be read off, but of the purely categorially determined possibilities of meaning-connection and meaning-transformation which, in any arbitrarily pre-given case, may be undertaken *salva veritate* — i.e. without in any way damaging the possibility of meaning-fulfilment, provided it had existed from the outset at all. If, e.g., the statement *γ is a part of G* is *valid*, then a statement of the form *G is a whole of γ* is also valid. If it is true that *there is an α which is β*, then it is also true that *a certain α is β*, or that *not all α are not β*, and so on. In such propositions the *stuff-like* is *limitlessly variable*; hence we replace all stuff-meanings by indirectly and wholly indeterminately meaning *algebraic signs*. Thereby, however, these propositions are characterized as *analytic*. In this situation it again does not matter whether the stuff constitutes itself in perceptions or imaginings. The possibilities and impossibilities concern the *production* of the acts adequately illustrating the meaning-form on an arbitrary stuff-basis; in short, it is a matter of the *pure conditions of the possibility of completely adequate signification überhaupt*, which for their part point back to the *pure conditions of the possibility of categorial intuition überhaupt*. Naturally, then, these validity-laws of meanings are *not identical* with, and themselves the *proper categorial laws*, but they *follow* them faithfully, on the ground of the lawfulness which regulates the connections of meaning-intention and meaning-fulfilment.

The whole consideration just carried through demands a natural and self-evident *extension*. We have simplified the situation by drawing only the two *extremes* into consideration; we set over against each other the *thoroughly intuitive*, hence really performed, categorial act-formations on the one side, and the *purely signitive*, hence properly not-performed-at-all and only-in-processes-of-possible-fulfilment-to-be-realized act-formations on the other. But the *ordinary* cases are *mixtures*; thinking runs in some stretches intuitively, in some signitively; here a categorial synthesis, a predication, generalization, and the like is really performed, there a merely signitive intention upon such a categorial synthesis attaches to the intuitively or only verbally presentational members. The complex acts thereby growing have, taken as wholes, the character of *improper categorial intuitions*; their whole objective correlate is made presentational not really but only "improperly"; their "possibility" (or the objective one of their correlate) is not guaranteed. The sphere of "improper thinking" must accordingly be grasped so widely that it can take up also these *mixed* act-formations. All that we have set forth then holds, *mutatis mutandis*, under presupposition of this extension. Instead of validity-laws of mere meanings, of mere symbolic judgments, etc., we have then to speak of validity-laws of *signitively clouded* presentations or judgments. Where one speaks of mere symbolic thinking, one has these mixtures mostly also in view.

### **§ 64\. The purely logical-grammatical laws as laws of every and not merely of the human understanding überhaupt. Their psychological significance and their normative function in respect of inadequate thinking**

Self-evidently the one as well as the other laws are of *ideal* nature. That a sensuous material lets itself bring only into certain forms — that the categorial forms are rooted in the genus of the sensuous (or, more generally, of the founding) only in respect of their *performability*, not their abstract content — this is an ideal lawfulness, independent of all empirical-psychological facticity, of all factual constitution of human (or any other) thinking-faculty.

That a sensuous material lets itself be grasped only in certain forms and connected only according to certain forms, and that the possible transformation of these stands under pure laws in which the stuff-like is freely variable; that thus also the *expressing meanings* can take on only certain forms (or transform their forms only according to prescribed types) if they are not to forfeit their proper *expressive capacity* — all that lies *not* in the empirical contingencies of the consciousness-course, nor in those of our intellectual and even general-human *organization*. It lies rather in the *specific nature of the relevant act-kinds*, in their intentional and cognitive essence; it belongs not to the nature of just *our* (individual or general-human) sensibility, or of just *our* understanding, but rather *to the ideas Sensibility and Understanding überhaupt*. An understanding with other than the *purely logical* laws would be an *understanding without understanding*. If we define the understanding, in opposition to sensibility, as the *faculty of categorial acts* — and, if need be, as the faculty of the expressing or meaning that directs itself according to these acts and is then "correct" — then the generic laws grounding in the species of these acts belong to the *definitory essence* of the understanding. Other beings may look into other "worlds," may also be equipped with other "faculties" than we; if they are psychic beings at all, and possess intentional experiences at all with all the differences here in question — between perceiving and imagining, simple intuiting and categorial intuiting, between meaning and intuiting, between adequate and inadequate knowing — then they have both *sensibility* and *understanding*, and "stand under" the belonging laws.

Naturally, then, the laws of genuine thinking belong *also* to the stock of the human consciousness, to the general-human "psychic organization." On the other hand, they are *not characteristic* for this organization in respect of its *peculiarity*. The laws ground, we said, in the purely specific of certain acts; therein lies: they concern the acts not merely insofar as these find themselves together precisely in a *human* organization; they belong rather to *all possible organizations* überhaupt which are to be built out of so-constituted acts. The differentiating peculiarities of the respective *type* of a psychic organization — all that, e.g., which delimits the *human* consciousness as such, in the manner of a natural-historical species — is not touched at all by *pure* laws such as the thought-laws are.

The reference to "our" psychic organization, or to the "consciousness überhaupt" (understood as the general-human of consciousness), defines not the pure and genuine, but a *grossly falsified a priori*. The concept of the general psychic organization has, like that of the physical organization, a merely "*empirical*" meaning, the meaning of a mere *matter of fact*. But the pure laws are *pure* precisely of the matter of fact; they say not what is, in this or that province of the real, more general usage, but what is simply *withdrawn from all usage* and all delimitations according to reality-spheres, and is so because it belongs to the *essential equipment of the existent*. And so the *genuine logical a priori* concerns all that belongs to the *ideal essence of the understanding überhaupt*, to the essences of its act-kinds and act-forms — hence to that which cannot be abolished so long as the understanding (or the acts defining it) is what it is: so-and-so-constituted, keeping its conceptual essence identical.

To what extent, accordingly, the logical laws — and in the first line the ideal laws of "genuine" thinking — also claim a *psychological* significance, and to what extent they too regulate the course of the factual psychic happening, is without further ado clear. Every genuine "pure" law which expresses a compatibility or incompatibility grounded in the nature of certain species *restricts*, when it bears on species of psychically-realizable contents, the empirical possibilities of the psychological (phenomenological) *coexistence and succession*. What is insighted *in specie* as incompatible cannot, in the empirical single case, be united, hence compatible. Insofar as the empirical-logical thinking carries itself out, in incomparably greatest part, *inadequately and signitively*, we now *think, suppose* much which in truth (i.e. in the manner of genuine thinking, of the actual performance of the merely supposed syntheses) is not at all to be united. And just for this reason the *a priori laws of genuine thinking and of genuine expressing* become *norms* of the merely supposing and improper thinking (or expressing). Or, somewhat otherwise turned: on the "genuine" thought-laws there ground themselves new laws — also to be formulated as practical norms — which, appropriated to the sphere of the signitive or signitively-clouded presenting, pronounce the ideal conditions of a possible truth überhaupt (= correctness überhaupt), namely the ideal conditions of "logical" (because referred to possible adequation) compatibility within this sphere of the signitively-clouded supposing. *Psychologically* the laws of "improper" thinking again value not as *empirical laws* of the becoming and changing of such thinking, but as *purely ideally founded possibilities or impossibilities* of the adequation of so-and-so-formed acts of improper thinking to corresponding acts of genuine thinking.

### **§ 65\. The absurd problem of the real meaning of the logical**

We now also understand completely why the thought that the *course of the world* could ever *deny* the logical laws — those analytic laws of genuine thinking, or the norms of improper thinking built upon them — or that *experience*, the matter of fact of sensibility, would have first to *ground* these laws and prescribe to them the limits of their validity, is nothing but *countersense*. We disregard that even the *probability-grounding* on facts is itself grounding, which as such stands under ideal laws (laws which, as we foresee, are founded in the "genuine" probability-experiences according to their specific stock and as generic laws). Here it matters rather to point out that the so-to-speak *factual* of the fact belongs to *sensibility*, and that the thought of grounding *purely categorial laws* by help of sensibility — laws which by their sense exclude all sensibility and facticity and make merely pure essence-statements about categorial *forms*, as forms of possible correctness or truth überhaupt — presents the clearest *μετάβασις εἰς ἄλλο γένος* (transition into another genus). Laws which mean no fact can be confirmed or refuted by no fact.

The problem of the "*real or formal meaning of the logical*," so earnestly and profoundly treated by great philosophers, is therefore an *absurd problem*. *There is need of no metaphysical or other theories to explain the agreement of the course of nature with the lawfulness "inborn" to the "understanding"*; instead of *ex-planation* there is need of the mere phenomenological *eluci-dation* of meaning, thinking, knowing, and of the ideas and laws springing therein. The *world* constitutes itself as a *sensuous unity*; according to its sense it is unity of the actual and possible *simple* perceptions. But according to its true being it is never given to us, in any closed perception-process, unreservedly or even adequately. It is for us at all times only a quite *inadequately*, partly through simple and categorial intuition, partly through signification, supposed unity of *theoretical research*. The more our knowledge advances, the better and richer the *idea of the world* determines itself, the more incompatibilities separate themselves out of it. To *doubt* whether the world really is as it appears to us, or as which it is supposed in the respective theoretical science and counts to it in grounded conviction, has its good sense; for inductive science can never *adequately* shape the world-presentation, however far it may bring us. But it is *countersense* to doubt whether the actual course of the world, the real connection of the world *in itself*, could *conflict* with the *forms of thinking*. For therein would lie that a determinate, hypothetically supposed sensibility — namely the one which (in an ideally closed manifold of endless perception-processes) would bring the world in itself to adequate self-presentation — would indeed be *capable* of taking on the categorial forms, but would *impose* on these forms unifications which are, through the general essence of these same forms, generally *excluded*. *That* they are so, and that the *laws of the categories* hold as pure laws which abstract from all stuff of sensibility (hence cannot be touched at all by its limitless variation) — this we do not merely *suppose*, we *insight* it; it is given to us in fullest *adequation*. The insight carries itself out subjectively, of course, on the ground of some contingent empirical intuition; but it is *generic* and purely form-related insight; the abstraction-foundation here, as elsewhere, harbours no presupposition for the ideal possibility and validity of the abstracted idea.

Husserl could point further to the *absurdity* lying in one's positing the *possibility* of an anti-logical course of the world in signitive thinking — and thereby raising the claim that this possibility is *admissible* — while in one breath abolishing the very laws which lend validity to this, as to any possibility überhaupt. He could point further that, from the *sense of being* überhaupt, the *correlation* to being-able-to-be-perceived, \-intuited, \-meant, \-known is inseparable, and that thus the ideal laws belonging to these possibilities *in specie* can never be abolished by the contingent content of the respectively existent itself. But enough of argumentations, which are finally only turns of one and the same situation, and which already guided us in the *Prolegomena*.

### **§ 66\. Separation of the most important distinctions confused in the customary opposition of "intuiting" and "thinking"**

Through the foregoing inquiries the much-used and so-little-clarified relation between *thinking* and *intuiting* should, in general, have been brought to satisfactory clarity. It remains to separate expressly the most important distinctions which are wont to be confused in the customary opposition of these two titles.

Husserl here assembles the following oppositions, whose confusion has in special measure perplexed epistemological research, and whose separation has become wholly distinct to us:

(1) The opposition between *intuition* and *signification*. The intuition, as perception or imagination (indifferent whether categorial or sensual, whether adequate or inadequate), is brought into opposition to the mere *thinking* as the mere *significative meaning*. The differences set in parenthesis are, to be sure, in the ordinary view overlooked; Husserl lays the greatest weight on them and adduces them now specially.

(2) The opposition between *sensual* and *categorial intuition*. We thus set over against each other: the *sensuous intuiting*, the intuiting in the common, simple sense, and the *categorial intuiting*, the intuiting in the extended sense. The founded acts characterizing the latter count now as the "*thinking*" that *intellectualizes* the sensuous intuition.

(3) The opposition between *inadequate* and *adequate intuition*, or, more generally, between adequate and inadequate *presentation* (taking together intuitive and significative presenting). In the inadequate presentation we merely think it is so (it *seems* so); in the adequate one we *behold* the situation itself and intuit it, for the first time, in its full *selfhood*.

(4) The opposition between *individual* intuiting (usually, and in visibly unfounded narrowness, grasped as sensuous intuiting) and *general* intuiting. According to this opposition a new concept of *intuition* determines itself; it is set over against *generalization*, and then forthwith over against the categorial acts which imply generalizations, and, in unclear confusion therewith, also over against the *significative* counterparts of just these acts. The "*intuiting*," it is now said, gives mere *singularity*; the "*thinking*" goes upon the *general*, carries itself out through "*concepts*." One speaks here usually of the opposition between "*intuition and concept*."

How great the inclination is to let these oppositions flow into one another, a *critique of Kant's epistemology* would show — whose whole stamp is determined through the lack of any firm separation of these oppositions. In Kant's thinking the categorial (logical) functions indeed play a great role; but he does not arrive at the fundamental *extension* of the concepts perception and intuition over the categorial domain — and indeed not, because he does not appreciate the great difference between *intuition* and *signification*, in their possible separation and ordinary fusion, and therefore does not carry through the analysis of the difference between *inadequate* and *adequate* adaptation of the meaning to the intuiting. He therefore also does not distinguish between *concepts* as general *word-meanings* and concepts as *species* of the genuine general presenting, and again concepts as general *objects* (the intentional correlates of the general presentations). Kant gets from the outset into the fairway of *metaphysical* epistemology by aiming at the critical "rescue" of mathematics, natural science, and metaphysics, *before* he has subjected knowledge as such — the total sphere of the acts in which the pre-logical objectivating and the logical thinking carry themselves out — to a clarifying *essence-analysis* and critique, and led the primitive logical concepts and laws back to their phenomenological origin. It was fateful that Kant (to whom we feel ourselves, despite all this, very close) held the purely logical domain in the strictest sense to be disposed of with the remark that it stands under the principle of contradiction. Not only did he never notice how little the logical *laws* anywhere possess the character of analytic propositions in the sense he himself definitorily fixed; he did not see how little is *gained*, for a clarification of the *achievement* of analytic thinking, by the reference to an evident principle of analytic propositions.

**Zusatz (Addition).** Ultimately all the principial obscurities of Kant's *Critique of Reason* hang together with the fact that Kant never made clear to himself the peculiarity of the pure *ideation*, the adequate beholding of conceptual essences and of essence-lawful general validities — that the phenomenologically genuine concept of the *a priori* was therefore lacking to him. Hence he could never appropriate to himself the only possible goal of a strictly scientific critique of reason — namely the goal of investigating the *pure essence-laws* which regulate the acts as *intentional* experiences according to all their modes of the objectifying sense-giving and of the fulfilling constitution of "true being." Only through the insightful cognition of these essence-laws can all questions of understanding that can be meaningfully posed to the "possibility of knowledge" find their absolutely sufficient answer.

# **Third Section: Elucidation of the Introductory Problem**

## **Chapter Nine: Non-Objectifying Acts as Apparent Meaning-Fulfilments**

### **§ 67\. That not every meaning includes a knowing**

Having sufficiently investigated, in connection with much more general problems, the relation between *meaning* and corresponding *intuition* — and thereby at the same time the essence of *proper* and *improper expressing* — the difficult questions now come to full clarification which disquieted us at the entrance of this inquiry and offered for it the first stimulus.

Above all, we shall no longer be able to succumb to the *temptation* which a thought-train (already touched above, and ever again pressing itself forward in important epistemological contexts) harbours in itself — namely that the *meaning* of expressions must in a certain way be regarded as a *knowing*, and even as a *classifying*. One says: an expression must, after all, give expression to some *act* of the speaker; but in order that this act find the fitting speech-form, it must be apperceived, recognized, in a belonging way — more closely, the presentation *as* presentation, the attribution *as* attribution, the negation *as* negation, and so on.

We answer: The talk of *knowledge* refers to a *relation* between *thought-act* and *fulfilling intuition*. But thought-acts come to expression in *statements* and statement-parts (e.g. in names) *not* by their being in turn *thought and recognized*. Otherwise these new thought-acts would be the *meaning-bearers*; they would at first be expressed, would thus need again new thought-acts, and so *in infinitum*. If I name this intuitive object *watch*, I carry out, in the naming, a thought- and knowledge-act; but I recognize *the watch* and not *the recognizing*. So it stands, of course, with all meaning-conferring acts. If I say, in the context of expressing discourse, *or*, I carry out a *disjunction*; but the thinking (whose part the disjoining is) refers *not* to the *disjoining* but to the *disjunctivum* — as it belongs to the unity of the state of affairs. This disjunctivum is recognized and objectively designated. Accordingly the little word *or* is no name, and also no non-self-sufficient designation for the *disjoining*; it only *makes known* this act. Naturally this holds also of whole judgments. If I *state*, I think of the *things*; *that* the things are so and so: *that* I express, and possibly I also *recognize* it. But I do *not* think and recognize the *judging*, as though I likewise made it into an object and now even classified it *as judgment* and named it through the expression-form.

But does not the grammatical *adaptation* of the expression to the act to be expressed point to an act of *recognizing* in which this adaptation carries itself out? In a certain way surely — or in certain cases, namely wherever that sense of the talk of expressing finds application which occupied us at the beginning of the present inquiry. But not where the expressing concerns the mere *making-known*, according to which any meaning-giving acts count as expressed *through the words* (the word-sounds); and again not where *expressing* says as much as *meaning*, and the expressed is the *identical meaning*. In this latter double sense, every statement — whether merely significative or intuitively fulfilled — expresses something, namely the *judgment* (the conviction) or the "*judgment-content*" (the identical sentence-meaning). But in the first-indicated sense only the intuitively fulfilled (or to-be-fulfilled) statement expresses something — whereby not the word-sound, but the already sense-animated discourse, presents the "expression" for the corresponding intuition. The meaning-conferring function is exercised, in the first line and in every case, by the *unitary complexion* of the signitive intentions hanging on the words. These make up the mere signitive *judging*, where every fulfilling intuition is lacking to them; the synthesis of *agreement or non-agreement* which the signitive total-intention "*expresses*" (or pretends to express) is here not carried out "*properly*," but only *signitively meant*.

If otherwise it comes to this *proper performance* of the indicated synthesis, then the "proper" coincides with the "improper" synthesis (the synthesis in the signification): both are one in the *identical intentional essence* which presents the one and same meaning, the one and same *judgment*, whether one now judges merely signitively or intuitively. Manifestly the analogous holds for the cases where only single ones of the word-intentions are provided with intuitive fullness. The signitive acts comprise the same meaning as the intuitive ones, without their fullness; they merely "*express*" it — and the simile fits the better, as they preserve for us, even after the falling-away of the intuitive acts, the *sense* of the intuition, as an empty husk without the intuitive kernel. The coincidence-unity is now indeed, in the case of intuitive judging, really *knowledge-unity* (though not unity of the *relating* knowing); but we know that in the knowledge-unity überhaupt it is not the *fulfilling act* (here the "proper" judgment-synthesis) that is the *known*, but its *objective correlate*, the *state of affairs*. In the intuition of the things we carry out a judging synthesis, an intuitive *so it is* or *so it is not*; through the expressing intention with the associated word-sounds (hence the grammatical expression) fitting itself to this act of state-of-affairs-intuition, the *recognizing* of the intuited state of affairs carries itself out.

### **§ 68\. The dispute over the interpretation of the peculiar grammatical forms for the expression of non-objectifying acts**

Husserl now turns to the last consideration of the inconspicuous, but on closer view as-important-as-difficult, *disputed question* whether the known grammatical forms which language has stamped for *wishes, questions, will-meanings* — generally speaking, for acts which do *not* belong to the class of the objectifying — are to be regarded as *judgments about* these acts, or whether also these themselves (and not merely objectifying acts) can function as "*expressed*," i.e. as sense-giving (or sense-fulfilling). It is therefore a matter of sentences like *Is n a transcendent number? May heaven stand by us\!* and the like.

The captiousness of the question shows itself in that the most significant logicians since *Aristotle* could not become united in their decision. As is known, already Aristotle raised objection against the *equating* of such sentences with the *statements*. Statements are expressions for *that something is or is not*; they *assert*, they *judge about* something. Only with them is there talk of *true* and *false*. A wish, a question asserts nothing. To the speaker here it cannot be objected: what you say is *false*. He would not understand the objection at all.

*Bolzano* would not let this argumentation count. He said: a question — e.g. *in what relation stands the diameter of a circle to its circumference?* — says indeed *nothing* about that *about which* it asks; but for that reason it nonetheless still *says* something: namely our *desire* to obtain an instruction about the object we ask after. It can thus be both true and false; the latter it is when that desire is incorrectly indicated through it. (Husserl cites Bolzano's *Wissenschaftslehre*.)

But the doubt stirs whether Bolzano does not here *jumble* two things: namely the *adequacy* (or inadequacy) of the *expression* — i.e. here of the word-sound — to the thought, and the *truth* (or falsehood), which concerns the *content* of the thought and its adequacy to the *thing*. Of the inadequacy of an expression (as word-sound) to the thought one can speak in a double sense: either in the sense of *unfitting* discourse — the speaker chooses, for the expression of the thought fulfilling him, words whose language-customary meaning conflicts with this — or in the sense of *untruthful* (i.e. intentionally deceiving, lying) discourse: the speaker does *not* want to express the thoughts that actually fulfil him, but certain *other* thoughts, conflicting with these and only *presented* by him; and indeed he wants to express them in such a way *as if* they fulfilled him. The talk of *truth* has with such things nothing to do. A fitting and truthful expression can still say both, truth and falsehood, according as it expresses through its sense *what is* (or is not) — or, what says the same, according as its sense is adequately to be fulfilled or disappointed through possible adequate perception.

One could now hold against Bolzano: of *truthfulness* or untruthfulness — and überhaupt of adequacy and inadequacy — there can be talk with *every* expression equally. But of *truth* and untruth only with *statements*. To the one stating, one can therefore object several things: *What you say is untrue* — this is the *factual* objection. And: *You do not speak truthfully*; or also: *You express yourself unfittingly* — that is the objection of the *untruthful* and of the *inadequate* discourse. To the one questioning, one can make only objections of the latter kind. He perhaps dissembles, or uses his words incorrectly and says other than he really wants to say. But one will not make to him the *factual* objection, since he precisely represents no thing.

If one wanted to let the objection bearing on the *inadequacy of the expression* count as proof that the question-sentence *states a judgment* — namely the judgment which would express itself completely in the form *I ask whether* … — then one would have to proceed consistently with *every* expression überhaupt in the same way; hence also lay under *any* arbitrary *statement*, as its proper sense, the one which would find its adequate expression in the speech-form *I state that* …. But the same would have to hold for the transformed discourses, and so we would come upon *infinite regresses*; whereby it is easy to see that the *flood* of ever-new statements is no mere word-flood, but rather furnishes *modified* statements which are not equivalent with the original ones — let alone meaning-identical. Does not, then, the *countersensical consequence* compel us to acknowledge an *essential* difference between the one and the other sentence-forms? (A footnote remarks that how this difference is truly to be grasped will be taught by the next section.)

Here, however, one can still take a *double* position. *Either* one says: the question of *truthfulness* concerns every discourse; hence to every discourse as such there belongs a *judgment*, namely the one bearing on the *experience of the speaker to be made known*. Whoever speaks *makes something known*, and to this corresponds the *making-known judgment*. But what is made known or expressed is something *different*: in the question-sentence the question, in the command-sentence the command, in the statement-sentence the judgment. Every statement-sentence accordingly *implies a double judgment*, namely a judgment about this or that state of affairs, and a *second* judgment which the speaker as such passes about *this judgment as his experience*.

This seems to be *Sigwart's* position. Husserl quotes: the *imperative* includes, to be sure, also an assertion — namely that the speaker now precisely *wills* the action demanded of him; the *optative*, that he *wishes* what is pronounced. But this assertion lies in the *fact of the speaking*, not in the *content* of what is pronounced; likewise every statement-sentence of the form *A is B* contains, merely through the fact of the speaking, the assertion that the speaker *thinks and believes* what he says. These assertions about the *subjective state of the speaker* — which lie in the fact of his speaking and are valid under presupposition of his truthfulness — *accompany in like manner all speaking* and can therefore *found no difference* of the various sentences.

Another conception, however, would be that one rejects the *making-known judgment* (and thus the judgment-doubling in the case of the statement-sentence) as a *contingent* complication, only exceptionally playing in and otherwise first carried in through descriptive reflection, and against this teaches: in every case of adequate and not occasionally abbreviated discourse the *expressed* is essentially *one* — namely in the question-sentence the question, in the wish-sentence the wish, in the statement-sentence the judgment. This position Husserl himself held, before the carrying-through of these inquiries, to be *unavoidable*, however hard it seemed to be reconcilable with other phenomenological facts. By the following argumentations, which he now accompanies with fitting critique, he held himself *bound*.

### **§ 69\. Arguments for and against the Aristotelian conception**

(1) According to the doctrine *turning away from Aristotle*, whoever utters a *question* is to *communicate* to the other his *wish* to be instructed concerning the question-state-of-affairs. This communication, bearing on the actual experience of the speaker, is, one says, the proper meaning of the question-sentence; the question-sentence is thus, at bottom, a *statement-sentence about the wish of the questioner*.

This communication, bearing on the actual experience of the speaker, is, one says, like every communication, a *statement*. Now, in the question-form itself it is admittedly not expressly said *I ask whether* …; it only characterizes the question *as* question. The discourse is precisely an *occasionally abbreviated* one. The *circumstances* of the utterance make it without further ado intelligible that it is the speaker himself who there asks. The full meaning of the sentence thus lies not in what it itself, according to its word-sound, means, but is determined through the *occasion* — namely through the relation to the person speaking at the moment.

In favour of the *Aristotelian* conception several things may now be replied. (a) The argument would fit no less *statement*\-sentences; hence we would have to interpret the expression *S is P* as an occasional *abbreviation* for the new expression *I judge that S is P*, and so *in infinitum*.

(b) The argument supports itself on the fact that the *express sense* of the question-sentence is another than the *actual* one. It cannot be denied that in the question- and wish-sentence itself the relation of the wish to the wisher does not necessarily *stamp* itself, as little as in the statement-sentence the relation of the judgment to the judger. But if this relation lies *not* in the express sense of the sentence, but only in the occasionally-changing one, then already as much is conceded as one could wish. Under circumstances the express meaning can *modify* itself; but there will also be circumstances under which the express meaning is exactly the *intended* one. Then the *mere question* (and likewise the mere request, the mere command, etc.) is expressed in completely adequate fashion.

(c) For the Aristotelian conception speaks the more exactly carried-through *comparison with the normal statement-sentences*. In *communicative* discourse such a sentence *makes known* a *judging*, and it is the grammatical form of the statement-sentence which brings the *judgment as such* to stamping. Hence with the utterance of a discourse of such grammatical form there is without further ado linked the effect that the one addressed apprehends the speaker as *judging*. But this effect cannot *constitute* the meaning of the expression, since it means in *solitary* discourse the same as in communicative. The meaning lies rather in the *judgment-act* as the identical *judgment-content*.

The same will now be able to hold of the *question*\-sentences. The meaning of the question-sentence remains the same, whether it is a matter of an *inner* question or of an *inquiry*. The relation to the speaker and the one addressed belongs here, as in the comparison-case, to the merely *communicative* function. And as there the "judgment-content," so here the *question-content* makes up the meaning of the question-sentence. In both cases the normal meaning can undergo *occasional modification*. We can pronounce a statement-sentence while it is not our *primary* intention to communicate the relevant state of affairs, but rather the *fact* that we have this conviction and intend to represent it. This intention may, perhaps supported by *heterogrammatical* means (emphasis, gesture), be understood. Here a judgment *referred to the express judgment* underlies. Likewise the primary intention can, in the case of a question- or wish-sentence, lie not in the mere wish, but rather in the fact that we want to bring the wish to the hearer's *expression*. Naturally this interpretation will not be able to fit everywhere. It cannot fit in cases where, e.g., a *hot wish* spontaneously wrings itself from the heart. The expression is then intimately *one* with the wish; it fits itself to it simply and immediately.

**Critique (Paragraph 7).** Looking more closely, by these argumentations only this is proved: that *not* to the sense of *every* sentence can there belong a thought which has relation to the *communicative* relationship. The *counter-argument*, which builds itself on the false assumption that every expression is a *communication* and every communication a *judgment* about the inner (made-known) experiences of the speaker, is refuted. But *not* its *thesis* — at least not given fitting modification. The possibility is not excluded that the disputed sentences (the wish-, request-, command-sentences, etc.) are for that reason nonetheless *judgments about* the relevant experiences (the acts of wishing, requesting, willing), and only by being so are able to give the expression adequate to these experiences. If there is found no room for judgments in the narrower sense of *predications* (for which Aristotle indeed took the disputed sentences), then perhaps for judgments in the wider sense of *positing objectivations* überhaupt.

To point (a) Husserl notes that the situation for statements and, e.g., questions is *not* the same. With the transformation of the sentence *S is P* into the sentence *I judge that S is P* (or into any kindred sentence that expresses, however indeterminately, the relation to a judger), we obtain not merely *changed* meanings, but such as are not even *equivalent* to the original ones; for the simple sentence can be *true*, the subjectivized one *false*, and conversely. Quite otherwise in the comparison-case. However much one may decline to speak of true and false in it, one will always find a *statement* which "*says essentially the same*" as the original question-, wish-form, etc. — e.g. *Is S P?* \= *I wish*, or *one wishes to know whether S is P*, and so on.

Should there not, then, in such sentence-forms, nonetheless be implied a relation — even if an indeterminate or only incidentally co-meant relation — to the *speaker*? Does not the preservation of the "*essential meaning*" in the transformations into statement-sentences point to the fact that the meaning-giving acts must at least belong to the *same class* as the judgments? And thereby point (b) will also dispose of itself: it will be not the *mere wish-* or will-experience, but the *inner intuition* of it (and the signification adapted to it) that comes in question for the meaning. But just this conception touches the next argument.

(2) In yet another way one could try to interpret the sentence-forms in question as *judgments*. In pronouncing a wish — be it even in solitary discourse — we grasp it and the wished-for content in words, hence *present* it and what constitutes it. But the wish is not an arbitrary merely *presented* one, but rather the just-*perceived*, the *living* wish. And of *it* as such we want to give *news*. Consequently not the mere presentation, but the *inner perception* — hence really a *judgment* — comes to expression. It is admittedly not a judgment of the kind of the ordinary statements, which *predicatively* say something about anything. In the wish-expression it is also only a matter of grasping, in simple *positing*, the inwardly-perceived experience conceptually (= meaningwise) and stamping its simple *existence*; not, however, of carrying out a *relating predication* about the experience which would set it into relation to the experiencing *subject*.

**Critique (Paragraph 11).** Against this conception rises the *objection* that the situation for the *stated judgments* is exactly the same as for all other express experiences. In stating, we *judge*; and in words we grasp not only the presentations underlying the judgment, but also the *judgment itself* (namely in the form of the statement). So we would have to conclude *here* too: the judgment is inwardly perceived, and the meaning of the statement lies in the *simply positing judgment about this perceived* — that is, about the judgment. If no one will find this conception acceptable in the case of the statement, then it can also not seriously come in question in the case of the other self-sufficient sentences. We recall what was set forth in the last section: the expressions that attach to the *expressed experiences* cannot refer to them *as names* (or analogously to names) — as though the experiences were first *objectively* presented and then brought under concepts, as though with every newly-entering word a *subsumption and predication* took place. Whoever judges *that gold is yellow* does not judge that the *presentation* he has together with the word *gold* is gold; he does not judge that the *judgment-mode* he carries out at the little word *is* falls under the concept of the *is*, and so on. In truth the *is* is no word-sign for the *judgment*, but a sign of the *being* belonging to the state of affairs. And again, *gold* is no name for a presentation-experience, but a name for a *metal*. Expressions are names for experiences only where the experiences become, in *reflection*, *objects* of the presentation (or judging). The same holds for all words — also the syncategorematic — with reference to the objective which they, after their kind, *designate*, even if not, *as names*, *name*.

Hence to the act which in each case *fills* us, in which we *live* without reflectively *judging* it, the expression does *not* enter in the manner of a *nominal signature*; rather the expression belongs to the *concrete stock* of the act itself. *Express judging is judging, express wishing is wishing.* To *name* a judgment or a wish is not to *judge* or *wish*, but precisely to *name*. The named judgment need *never* be judged by the namer, the named wish never wished by him. And even in the opposite case, the naming is not the *expression* of the judgment (or wish), but the expression of a *presentation* referred to it.

**Critique (Paragraph 13).** This objection too lays bare the *weakness* of the premised and at first so near-lying argumentation. It is certain — by it as already by our earlier considerations — that *not every expression as such presupposes a judgment* (or some other act making the made-known experience into an object). But again, thereby the *thesis* itself is not refuted; it is not proved that *not* precisely the disputed sentence-forms are nonetheless *judgments* about the respective wish-, question-, request-experiences (or expressions of their simple *existence* in the speaker).

Certainly, to *name* a wish is *for that reason* not yet to *wish*; but is to *experience* a wish and in one therewith to *name* it not *also* to *wish*? Hence even if express wishing is *necessarily* a *naming* or *stating* wishing, there holds the proposition that express wishing is precisely *wishing* and not mere *naming*.

(3) The disputed expressions have the *form* of sentences, and under circumstances also that of *categorical* sentences with subject and predicate. From this it already emerges that one can grasp them also content-wise as *predications* — and indeed not exactly as predications in reference to an always-same but unspoken subject *I*. E.g. *May God protect the emperor. Franz should spare himself. The coachman shall harness up.* A *may* or *shall* is stated; the relevant subject is apprehended as standing under a *demand* or *obligation*.

One could here reply: where the *shall* counts as an *objective predicate* and is as such in fact attributed, there the *shall*\-sentence has not the meaning of a wish or command, or has not this alone. An objective *obligation* can be stated as valid without the one stating needing himself to experience an act of the kind which makes up the actual *consciousness* of the obligation. Knowing the will of a person bound through their service-relation or through custom and morality, I can *judge* that they *shall* and *must* do something. But thereby I express no living *wishing, desiring*, or *shall*. To be sure, shall-statements can in *occasional* function also serve to express such acts — e.g. *Johann shall harness up\!* It is clear that here not merely the objective obligation, but *my will* is expressed. In the words themselves it does not come to expression, but indeed through the *tone* and the *circumstances*. Doubtless the predicative form under such circumstances very often *surrogates* for the wish- or command-form — i.e. the shall-predication that lies in the word-sound is not carried out at all, or becomes a *side-matter*. Finally it is also unmistakable that the predicative interpretation has even *only in some cases* a semblance. Surely not with *questions* — as indeed B. Erdmann, who otherwise inclines to it, did not recommend it for questions.

**Critique (Paragraph 17).** It is questionable whether this refutation suffices at all. That the *shall*\-predicate often has an objective sense and value is indubitable; but that, where this does *not* take place, *nothing* is predicated and in any case *not* judged, is by no means proved. One could say: when we direct a command at someone — e.g. at the coachman Johann, that he *shall* harness up — he counts for us as one *standing under our will*; as such he is apprehended by us and accordingly addressed in the expression-form. We say: *Johann, harness up\!* As one who is *to-harness-up* he is here predicated; and naturally he is so in the *expectation of corresponding practical successes*, and not in intent upon the mere *establishing* of this fact, that he counts to me as such a one. The expression of the command is a *relative* one. We can present no one as *commanded* without *co-presenting* a *commander*, be it in determinate or indeterminate way. Where we ourselves command, we apprehend *ourselves* as commanding. But this requires, as a self-evidence, no *explicit* expression. Instead of the cumbersome form *I command* …, we use the short *imperative*, which through its form points to the communicative relationship. The speech-form with *shall* (and *must*) is originally used *not* by the commander in the actual address to the (facing) commanded one, but everywhere where it is a matter of a more *objective* expression of one's own or another's will-meaning — so, e.g., by third, command-transmitting persons, or as expression of the *legislative will* in the *law*. Outside the communication between command-holder and command-receiver, the imperative (adapted to the consciousness-situation of the former) loses its applicability. This conception lets itself carry through everywhere. One will say: in the *optative* the wished-for is presented *as* wished-for, named, and then in any case stated; likewise in the request-form the requested *as* requested, in the question-form the questioned *as* questioned, and so on. These acts are presentationally set into relation to their *intentional objects* and thus become, *as reflexion-predicates*, objective *on themselves*.

In the *communicative* relationship, like the commands, so many other of the expressions in question have the function of telling the hearer, in the manner of essentially *occasional* expressions, that the speaker carries out the made-known acts (of the request, the wish, the condolence, etc.) *in intentional reference to him, the hearer*. Insofar as expressions of any kind can be borne, fully consciously, by the *wish* to communicate oneself with them to the other — to give him knowledge of one's own convictions, doubts, hopes, etc. — they are possibly all accompanied by acts of *reflection* on these inner experiences, and more closely by acts of their intuition relating them to the *I* and to the addressed person. This holds also of communicative *statements*. For that reason these acts of reflection and relation do *not yet* belong to the *meaning* of the statement and of all other expressions überhaupt; but very well is this to be said of the expressions of the *disputed class*, as which they are thoroughly directed upon *inner experiences* of the speaker.

In the *solitary* soul-life (disregarding the exceptional cases of speaking-to-oneself, questioning, wishing, commanding oneself), the relation to the addressed one falls away, and the relevant *subjective* expressions, still applicable then, become expressions of the *simple being* of the inner experiences with more or less distinct reference to the *I*. The *monological* question says either *I ask (myself) whether* …; or the regard to the I falls away entirely, and the question-expression becomes a mere *name* — or at bottom not even that. For the normal function assigns the name a place in a predicative or attributive relation, of which here there is no talk. In that the expression sets itself, in the manner of a *knowledge*, into one with the *intuited inner experience*, there grows a *complexion* having the character of an *in-itself-closed phenomenon*. Insofar as in this complexion the *question* is the act in which we *preferentially live*, while the expression fits itself to it only as the saying, articulating one, we call the *whole complexion* a question. The knowledge functions here *not theoretically* — that it does only in *predication*, while here it is not predicated, the question indeed *recognized* and *expressed*, but not *subjected*, not made into subject or object of predicative acts. Manifestly this directly stamping sense of the question-sentence is a *component* of the *predicative* question-sentence (or of the meaning corresponding to the changed circumstances).

### **§ 70\. Decision**

If one understands by *judging* predications, then, according to these considerations, the disputed sentences are *not in all cases expressions of judgments*. Nonetheless, even in these cases an *unbridgeable gulf* separates us from the logicians attaching to *Aristotle*. According to them, names, statements, wish-sentences, question-sentences, commands, etc., would be *coordinated* expression-forms — and indeed in the following sense: *names* give expression to *presentations*, *statements* to *judgments*, *wish-sentences* to *wishes*, and so on. As meaning-conferring acts there could function, in exactly like manner, presentations, judgments, wishes, questions, etc.; in short, acts of *every* kind; for to *give expression to acts* means here everywhere the same, namely to *find in these acts one's meaning*.

We, by contrast, find, in comparing the names and statements with the expressions of the disputed group, a *fundamental difference* therein: that the acts of presenting (or judging) "expressed" in names and statements are indeed *meaning-giving* (or meaning-fulfilling), but for that reason precisely *not meant* — that they, in the naming and predicating, are not *objective*, but *object-constituting*. On the other side, and in straight opposition thereto, we find with all the disputed expressions that the "expressed" acts, although they are allegedly meaning-giving, become *objective* to us. This happens, as we recognized, on the one hand by virtue of *inner intuitions* which reflectively direct themselves upon these acts (and mostly also by virtue of *relating* acts founded in these intuitions); and on the other hand by virtue of certain — possibly only partly pronounced — *significations* which fit themselves to the inner intuitions and relations in the manner of *knowing*, so that their objects (the acts of questioning, wishing, commanding, etc.) become *named* and otherwise-said objects, possibly *components of predicated states of affairs*. In these *objectifying acts* lie now the true meanings of the disputed expressions. It is with them *not* a matter of meaning-conferring acts of fundamentally *new genera*, but of contingent *particularizations* of the *one and only* genus *meaning-intention*. And likewise the meaning-fulfilling acts belong not to *various* genera, but to the *one and only* genus *intuition*. *The wishes, commands, and the like, are not themselves expressed through the grammatical formations and their significations; rather, the intuitions of these acts are what serve as fulfilments.* Comparing statement-sentence and wish-sentence, we may not coordinate *judgment* and *wish*, but *state of affairs* and *wish*.

Accordingly there results the result: *the alleged expressions of non-objectifying acts are practically — and especially communicatively — exceedingly important, but for the rest contingent, particularizations of statements (or other expressions) of objectifying acts.*

Therein lies the fundamental importance of the treated disputed question: that on its decision depends whether one can represent the doctrine that *all meaning, in intention and fulfilment, is of one genus* — namely of the genus of *objectifying acts* with their fundamental separation into *significative* and *intuitive* acts — or whether one must rather resolve to admit acts of *every* genus as meaning-giving (or \-fulfilling). And again this disputed question is of no slight significance in that it makes attentive, in the very first place, to the fundamental *threefoldness* of the equivocal talk of *expressed acts*, with whose analysis the present inquiry set in.

Accordingly, under "expressed acts" there can be meant: (1) the *significative* acts which confer meaning on the expression überhaupt and, in their significative way, mean a certain objectivity; (2) the *intuitive* acts which often *fulfil* the significative meaning of the expression — hence present the significatively-meant objects intuitively, and indeed in an equal intuitive "sense"; (3) the acts which, in every case where an expression expresses *the own momentary experiences* of the speaker (in the second sense), are the *objects* of the signification and at the same time intuition. If these acts do not belong to the *objectifying* ones, they can by their nature *never* stand in the functions designated under (1) and (2).

The ground of all the difficulty lies, however, in this: that in the *direct* application of the expressions (or expressing acts) to the intuitively-grasped *inner experiences*, the significative acts are *completely fulfilled* through the inner intuitions belonging to them — hence the two are most intimately *fused* — while at the same time the intuitions, as *inner* ones, go up in the *simple presentation* of the meant acts.

Finally Husserl notes that the difference brought against Bolzano above — whether only the *subjective* objection (bearing on truthfulness or adequacy of the expression) can be made, or also the *factual* objection (which goes upon objective truth and falsehood) — exactly seen does *not* essentially hang together with the question here disputed. For it concerns quite generally the difference between expressions that refer to the own, intuitively-grasped act-experiences, and such as do not. But of the former, many are quite *undisputed predications* — so all statements of the form *I ask whether* …, *I command or wish that* …, and the like. And, well to note: even with the so-formulated *subjective* judgments no *factual* objection can be made. They are indeed true or false, but *truth* here *coincides with truthfulness*. With other statements, which go upon "objective" (i.e. not upon the self-pronouncing subject and its experiences), the *factual* question concerns the meaning; the question of *truthfulness*, however, hangs together with the possibility of *apparent* stating, where the proper and normal act of meaning is lacking — it is not judged at all, but the statement-meaning is *presented* in the context of a deception-intention.

# Husserl, *Erste Philosophie* (1923/24), Second Part: Theory of the Phenomenological Reduction

The source is Hua VIII, ed. Rudolf Boehm, 1959. The division into Sections and Chapters and the lecture titles are the editor's; they are reproduced here in translation. Retained terms: epoché, ego cogito, ego, and Descartes' Latin. Evidenz is rendered "evidence" in Husserl's sense of self-giving insight, never "proof"; Stellungnahme is "position-taking"; Einfühlung is "empathy"; Vergegenwärtigung is "presentification".

Five misreadings are guarded against throughout. First, the epoché is not doubt, denial, or a hypothesis that the world does not exist; it is an abstention from using the world's validity as a premise, and the lectures say repeatedly that the world goes on being experienced. Second, the "possible non-existence of the world" established in Section Two is an eidetic claim about the structure of external experience (its presumptive, never apodictic character), not a sceptical worry. Third, apodictic evidence is not adequate evidence: the course starts by treating them together and then prises them apart, and the whole critique of transcendental experience hangs on the difference. Fourth, the reduced subjectivity is not the psychological interior of a human being in the world; the "second way" through psychology is valuable precisely because it shows where the psychological and the transcendental coincide in content and differ in sense. Fifth, the closing transcendental idealism is not a solipsistic or Berkeleyan idealism; it ends in a monadology of communalized subjects, and its "absolute being" is the being of constituting subjectivity, not a mind that manufactures things.

## Section One: Preliminary Meditations on the Apodictic Beginning of Philosophy

### Chapter One: Introduction. The Motivation of the Beginning Philosopher in the Absolute Situation

#### Lecture 28: The Idea of Philosophy in History and the Motivational Situation of the Subject Beginning to Philosophize

Husserl summarizes the first part of the course (Hua VII), the critical history of ideas delivered before Christmas. That history followed one idea of philosophy, born in the Socratic-Platonic reaction against the Sophists: knowledge grounded in the knower's ultimate self-reflection and self-responsibility, hence absolutely self-justifying and universal science. History failed to realize it, and the symptom of failure was a scepticism that never went away, sometimes open, sometimes hidden. The critique of that history yielded a single lesson: every justification has its final source, and its unity, in knowing subjectivity grasped in transcendental purity. Hence the need for a science of origins, a "first philosophy" of transcendental subjectivity, from which every genuine science would draw its basic concepts, principles, and method, and by which the sciences would become branches of one philosophy. The course so far has produced only a pre-concept, a goal-idea still to be worked out in theories. The second part turns from history to execution: it will bring this philosophy into actual becoming "from its primal beginnings". The shift is from narrating the idea to enacting it, and the first-person plural already invites the listener to perform the beginning rather than watch it.

Descartes is named as the historical seed of transcendental philosophy, and his Meditations are to help with the attempt at a correct first beginning. The editor's note sends the reader back to lectures 9–11 of the first part, where the Cartesian critique was given in historical form. What follows will reuse the Meditations as a pattern, not as an authority.

Husserl states his hermeneutic principle for Descartes: a genius's false theories and even his apparently trivial arguments carry a truth in the making, felt but not yet formed, recognizable only by those who later possess it in mature form. That is why the first two Meditations keep their hold despite having found almost no real successors. Two achievements are credited. Descartes denied every science, mathematics included, final grounding and demanded a new grounding from one absolute source, so that all sciences would become branches of a single universal wisdom (*universalis sapientia*) unified by the unity of reason. And he discovered the ego cogito and tied absolute grounding back to it, which amounts to the conviction that transcendental self-knowledge is the source of all other knowledge. The paragraph ends with a double verdict that governs the whole course: Descartes found the basic form of the beginning of scientific philosophy and at the same time misunderstood its sense, and so missed the real beginning. The misunderstanding, spelled out in Hua VII and again in the Cartesian Meditations of 1929, is that Descartes took the ego for a piece of the world, a thinking substance from which the rest could be inferred.

Husserl also values the style of the Meditations. Descartes presents his route to the ego cogito as an autobiographical report of reflections in which the true method came to him; its meaning is supra-personal. The first-person narrative is not a literary accident but the form philosophy must take. This is the thread Husserl picks up.

Whoever wants to become a philosopher in the Platonic and Cartesian sense, as the author of a universal science from absolute justification, can do so only through such self-reflections and such rational self-shaping. These reflections are not a ladder to be kicked away: they belong to the systematic content of philosophy, whose subjective sources cannot be severed from its objective form. The contrast with positive science follows. Natural knowledge and positive science can seize their objects directly and proceed in naively exercised evidence, because a world is pregiven to them through experience and a domain of objects precedes all their thinking. The philosopher cannot begin that way. He may count as valid only what he has given himself in absolute justification; he has no objects in advance, no self-evident right of natural experience, and he may not let any naively exercised evidence pass unexamined, even one that raises no suspicion of its own accord. The last clause matters: the demand does not wait for a reason to doubt. Its target is the pregivenness of the world as such, and this is the first form of what Section Two will call the critique of mundane experience.

Since the philosopher has no theoretical material yet, he must first reflect on how he can arrive at a beginning at all: how he can give himself something he may accept as absolutely justified, and what would be first in itself. Before everything stands the question of what absolute justification means, and that question stays open throughout, because its sense differentiates as the work proceeds. So the philosopher, having nothing outside himself, must begin by reflecting on himself as the one who wills philosophy, and must stop being a naive knower absorbed in objects and unconcerned with himself. The editor refers to Beilage III on tradition and the proclamation of an "epoch-making" beginning. The move is from world to self, but it is not yet the transcendental reduction: the self reached here is the willing philosopher, not the transcendental ego.

The first reflection is a reflection of the will. By deciding to become a philosopher the subject takes a resolution covering its entire future cognitive life: from now on it wills only absolutely justified, systematic, universal knowledge. From this reflective will arise the reflections on the goal's sense and on the ways of realizing it, and their contents form the first stretch of the path, the basic piece of method, from whose exercise philosophy will later grow as a system of justified theories in endless progress. Husserl gives method a voluntative root. The beginning is a resolution before it is an insight, and this is why the next lectures read like an ethics of the philosophical life.

Philosophy cannot therefore arise from naive cognitive activity but only from free self-reflection and self-determination, from radical clarity about what the subject as philosopher really wants and about the path it must follow. Husserl distinguishes two stages carefully. First the philosophizing ego must become the theme of its own will. Only "in further consequence" does it become its own first theme of knowledge, by grasping itself, through a methodical apperception, as the transcendental or pure ego and finding there the basic field of its theoretical work. He stresses the phrase: the transcendental ego does not belong to the very first beginning but to the results of the meditations, as their first summit. The order forestalls the Cartesian error of starting from the ego as a datum; the ego becomes available only through method, and Section Two will show what that method costs.

Husserl lingers on the form of the first meditative beginning. The resolution founds a habitual form of life, the life of the philosopher in the making, and this life is characterized as cognitive life in complete and constant self-responsibility. "Founding" (Stiftung) is the technical term for an act whose sense persists as a habituality; Ideen II and Hua XIV use it for the ego's lasting convictions, and here it is transferred to the decision that institutes a vocation.

The motivation of the beginner is examined in its formal generality. The beginner is still a naive knower but no longer entirely naive, since he has become aware of his naivety and is dissatisfied with it. He has a stretch of cognitive life behind him, because all waking life contains knowing in some form, and he has been a scientist who not only produced knowledge but criticized it and reshaped it toward final values. As a scientist he knows the difference between a judgment that merely means its state of affairs and one in which what is meant is realized in the knowing itself, as truth seen. There the meaning intention is fulfilled; the knower is conscious of having reached the goal and sees "it itself". He knows that bare opinions lack evidence, that they can be taken in hand and a justifying grounding attempted, in which the self-given truth becomes the measure of their right, and that the attempt can fail and expose the opinion as wrong. He takes for granted that every judgment is true or false and that truth, once achieved, is a lasting possession, reattainable as the same and never turning into falsehood. Husserl here compresses the doctrine of fulfilment from the Sixth Logical Investigation and of evidence as self-giving from Ideen I into a portrait of what any scientist already lives by. The status of the portrait is important: these are "self-evident" assumptions, and self-evidence is exactly what the absolute situation will suspend. Bivalence and the permanence of truth are listed as unexamined possessions of the beginner, not as axioms of the course.

Truth deserves to be called an attainable good. It is a value, falsity a disvalue, and as a practical value truth becomes the goal of cognitive striving and cognitive action. Like any action, knowing can reach its goal imperfectly or miss it openly or unnoticed; knowledge can remain at a distance from its matter, with a clarity that already sees but only anticipates, or that does not yet give the meant state of affairs in all its sense-moments. This motivates a new striving: to test cognitive action reflectively and turn a merely supposed attainment into a real one. Husserl treats knowing as a species of acting, and this lets him import the whole structure of practical reason (goals, success, failure, self-criticism) into epistemology. It is the bridge from the analysis of evidence to the ethics of vocation in Lecture 29.

All this the beginner knows concretely as a scientist, even without the logical insight into laws that only general analysis and description would supply. It belongs to the basic character of science, which does not want to be naive but to pass through critique of knowledge. What the scientist asserts he asserts in consciousness of its conformity to norms, or at least with that pretension: it has gone through the purgatory of critique. The editor's reference to Beilage IV, on the principle of sufficient reason for every scientific judgment, shows where this points. Science already contains, in germ, the demand for justification that philosophy will radicalize; the difference will lie in how far critique goes.

#### Lecture 29: On the Founding of the Habitual Form of Life of the Philosopher in the Making

The beginning philosopher takes over the scientist's motivations; they simply live on in him. He does not want to be anything other than a scientist, only a genuine, radically genuine one. What moves him is the love of wisdom that gives him his name, which at first is nothing but the scientist's love of truth: a habitual devotion to the realm of values contained in the sphere of judgment. He too lets this love determine a lasting life decision aimed at what is greatest and best in that realm, within practical limits. The claim is deliberately modest. Philosophy is not a different kind of cognition from science; it is science that has become what it claims to be.

Yet there is an essential difference. Science and philosophy were originally one, the special sciences living branches of a single philosophy. They separated through nothing but a change in the attitude that animates the work: the spirit of radicalism was lost, the will to go to the end in the critical justification of knowledge and hence in the scientist's justification of himself. The independent sciences leave the true philosopher, the scientist in the eminent sense, unsatisfied, and Husserl gives two reasons. The lesser one is that each science covers only a limited universe of objects and aims at a limited system of truths, not the full unity of all truth. The decisive one is that all sciences remain half-naive and so fail their own sense as sciences, the sense of thorough conformity to norms. They have pregiven data, and even the ultimate pregiving, experience, is already knowledge of the lowest level; they accept it without ever having examined it critically. They criticize their methods, but they never make theoretical accomplishment as such the theme of the most radical reflection. From this arises the philosophical drive toward a presuppositionless beginning and a life of knowledge from absolute justification, in which the knower can stand before himself "in an absolutely good conscience" and answer for every cognitive decision. Two points carry forward. Calling experience the lowest level of knowledge prepares Section Two, where external experience itself is put on trial. And the language of conscience shows that "presuppositionlessness" is not a logical ideal of premise-free deduction but a demand for full self-accountability, which is how Husserl defended the principle against critics from the Logical Investigations onward.

This absolute radicalism requires a corresponding absolute life decision, which turns the would-be philosopher's life into a life from absolute vocation. The subject decides, from the innermost centre of its personality, for the best in itself within the universal realm of cognitive values and for a consistent life directed toward that idea. In a sense it "absolutely identifies" itself with this best. Correlatively, it chooses highest knowledge as the final end of its striving, its true calling, to which it has devoted itself once and for all as a practical ego. The philosopher is always conscious of this end in the sense that it persists as a habitual validity: he can at any moment make evident to himself that this is his lasting aim, valid from the founding decision and still at work as the ideal pole governing all his cognitive acts. Husserl specifies the mode in which a life-goal is "always present": not as a constantly thought content but as a habituality that can be reactivated. The same structure (founding act, persistent validity, reactivation) will later explain how the epoché, once performed, can be sustained as an attitude.

For someone who is a philosopher only through this ideal centring, every deviation from the final end is a deviation from himself, an act of self-betrayal. Correlatively, every exercise of the will to absolute justification is at the same time a self-justification of the philosopher as such. Justifying knowledge and justifying oneself become one act. This is the point at which epistemology and ethics fuse in the Erste Philosophie, and it anticipates the Crisis, where philosophers are called "functionaries of mankind" answerable for reason's self-realization.

Husserl warns that stressing the absoluteness of the decision has named its most important feature inadequately and ambiguously, and announces a clarification. The ambiguity is that "absolute" could mean merely intense or unconditional, whereas what he wants is the specific structure of a vocation.

Giving philosophy the personal validity of a final life-goal is not just choosing a goal like any other. Wealth, honour, power, or fame can also be personal ends and shape a so-called professional life, but in such a life no higher calling is manifest that would give the profession a kind of consecration, as it does for the philosopher, the genuine artist, or the genuine statesman. Still, the same formal features recur wherever one speaks of a profession in the ordinary or the higher sense. The merchant who resolves to rise as far as possible in wealth and standing also decides in the form of "once and for all"; he too has before him an endless chain of values and purposes that build on one another, where later totals absorb earlier ones, preserve them in a sense, and devalue them by surpassing them; and the endless ascent itself, a *gradus ad Parnassum*, is a value chosen under the title of a profession. In every freely chosen profession there is an idea of final validity and, for the person as willing subject, an identification with this system of ends, a creative devotion to it "out of love" and a will to live oneself out in it for ever. Husserl establishes the common form before marking the difference, so that the difference will lie in the content and purity of the value, not in the structure of commitment. The formal analysis of an open, self-absorbing series of values will return in the account of theory as an infinite progress.

Yet an ordinary profession and a profession from vocation are worlds apart. The home of genuine vocation is the heavenly place (*topos ouranios*) of the absolute idea, of pure value as opposed to merely supposed value, which may contain something genuine but not the purity of completion. The other home of vocation is the self, which not only values but, through every one-sided finitization and clouding, anticipates and intends the pure values themselves and unites with them in creative realization. The pure resembles a mathematical limit, an idea lying at infinity, at least if every pure value already seen still keeps a distance from the ego, with unfulfilled components in itself or its horizons that draw the lover of ideas onward. Each realm of value has its pure value, its *kalon* as idea; in cognition it is pure truth, which rises through the systematic building of theories. Truths produced at lower levels are preserved and yet absorbed by higher ones and reduced to surpassed values. Viewed as a whole, the infinite progress of theory is itself a pure value that surpasses every stage and does not itself change: an absolute and abiding value. The highest would be the infinite totality of pure truths and grounding theories, embracing every possible progress of theorizing. The Platonic vocabulary is joined to the Kantian regulative idea: the pure value is an idea "in the infinite", as in Ideen I § 143, where the complete givenness of a thing is an idea in the Kantian sense. The resulting picture explains why the philosophical life is essentially unfinished without being futile: each stage is a relative end, and the series as a whole is a fixed, absolute value.

The totality of theoretically connected truth is an infinite realm of pure beauty, disclosed in pure seeing, but not in passive looking. It discloses itself in loving creative activity in which the pure and beautiful actualizes itself as the fulfilment of anticipating intentions and is then seen as the goal the ego has reached by its own activity. Every such goal is only a relative end (*telos*) and drives on to new horizons and their undisclosed anticipations. In this way the abiding guiding idea of the infinite progress becomes actual in relative, finite form in the actual movement of knowing, whose acts fulfil longing, but only relatively. Truth is called beautiful not as an ornament but because Husserl assimilates theoretical evidence to the creative realization of value: to see a truth is to have produced its fulfilment. The claim anticipates Formal and Transcendental Logic, where ideal objects are said to be "produced" in judging activity without being made up.

The beautiful is loved, and love has no end. It is love only in the infinity of loving and carries as its correlate the infinity of pure value. As the love of the creative ego it is infinite longing for the beautiful: unhappy when blocked and unfree, blessed in free, self-active fulfilment, in the steady realization of the beautiful that is first anticipated, then foreseen, and finally, though always only relatively, shaped by the ego itself. The editor reports Husserl's marginal verdict "inadequate" on this and the following passages (Beilage II). The paragraph reads as Platonic eros (the Symposium's ascent) cast in the phenomenology of fulfilment: anticipation, foresight, and self-giving are the stages of intentional fulfilment, and love names the ego's striving through them. Husserl's own dissatisfaction suggests he saw that the affective register had outrun the analysis.

Husserl draws a series of distinctions. Being lovingly open to pure beauties and already surveying a world of beauty in anticipation is one thing; being devoted to such a world is another. Being devoted to it at times, shaping or re-shaping it with pure joy and so purifying and raising oneself, is again different from giving oneself wholly to the infinities of such a world of values, pouring oneself into them, and willing, and having, to appropriate its infinite reaches by one's own action in unconditional love. The gradation runs from aesthetic receptivity through intermittent devotion to total self-surrender, and only the last is vocation. The distinction between intermittent and total devotion prepares the point of Lecture 30, where the philosopher is contrasted with the scientist who loves truth but whose love has limits.

Once love of pure beauty is awakened in one sphere, love of beauty in other spheres will generally follow, where understanding permits. But the lover cannot decide practically for every realm of value equally or for all at once. Each realm is an infinite world; decision for one, as it passes into the labour of realization, obstructs realization in another and so the possibility of unconditional decision for it. Is the choice then a matter of chance? The word "vocation" answers. I may see several worlds of pure beauty and grant them equal value in themselves, and yet for me, as the one I am, they cannot count equally: I can and must decide for this one. Not merely because I must choose something, and not merely because I cannot do everything, but because I have a special, unconditional affinity with this realm, because from my innermost self a personal valuation meets it and stamps it as an absolute value for me. This realm belongs to me and I to it; it calls me personally, and realizing it is my affair. Following the call, I lose myself as finite, sensuous, inauthentic self to gain my true, infinite self, purified of the earthly; divining the eternal in the earthly and realizing it as pure beauty, I gain not mere happiness but blessedness, the only satisfaction in which I am satisfied, and so realize myself as the one I can alone call myself being "in spirit and in truth". The decisive distinction is between the value of a realm in itself and its value for me, an "absolute value for me" that is not arbitrary. Husserl locates the individuating ground of vocation in personal affinity rather than in comparative value, which lets an objective hierarchy of values coexist with an irreducibly personal choice. The Johannine phrase and the language of losing and finding oneself show how far he is willing to borrow religious form; the same themes recur in the Kaizo articles of 1923–24 (Hua XXVII), written in the same months, where the ethical life is defined as the life of absolute self-responsibility.

The general talk of vocation meant the special case at issue: the true philosopher, who is one only if he follows the call issuing from the idea of universal wisdom and demanding absolute devotion. Only he who consecrates himself to philosophy is a philosopher, as only he who consecrates himself wholly to art is an artist. Being interested in philosophy, reflecting on questions of truth now and then, even working on them continuously, is not yet being a philosopher, just as lifelong amateur painting is not yet being an artist. What is missing is the radicalism of the will to the ultimate, which has before it the infinity of the pure idea and of a whole world of ideas and is satisfied only in a life directed toward the eternal poles, in which it realizes itself as an eternal ego. The criterion of the philosopher is thus not competence or output but the form of the will. The point bears directly on the method to come: the epoché will be demanded of someone defined by this will, and its radicality is the radicality of the will that institutes it.

#### Lecture 30: Pure Cultural Disposition in General and the Primal Founding of a Philosophical Radicalism

The previous lecture treated pure realms of value as fields of creative realization and put philosophy, the universal and absolute science, on a level with other ideal forms of pure culture such as fine art. In every such realm the realization of pure values demanded a radical disposition that is never satisfied with the finite and strives toward the eternal poles of the idea. Now Husserl marks the first difference. Science and art are both universal concepts, covering all scientific and all artistic works, but philosophy as universal science wills to make possible, or even achieve, the purposive realization of all actual and possible sciences, and even each special science takes the whole infinite set of truths of its domain as the theme of its will. So the philosopher is guided not only by the infinity of one idea underlying a single work, the idea of its perfection, but by the infinity of the totality of such perfected works. Every researcher intends each result as a new piece of the universal realm of truth whose conquest science wills. Nothing like this holds in other cultural fields; art, even pure art, is not the name of a will directed at the totality of possible artworks. Husserl leaves open whether universally directed cultural intentions might be awakened in other fields too. The asymmetry lies in the structure of the will: science is the only cultural form whose single achievements are intended as parts of a totality, which is why it alone can become the vehicle of a universal philosophy.

A second difference sets philosophy apart from all other pure culture. Pure culture does not exclude a certain naivety; except in philosophy, it grows and remains in naivety, however much reflective self-criticism at each stage belongs to it. Positive science, in this respect, stands on the same level as the rest of culture. Philosophy rises above this whole level and parts ways with naivety in principle. Hence its radicalism is essentially different from the radicalism of any other cultural disposition: the general radicalism of unconditional devotion to seen and loved pure values is not yet philosophical radicalism. Accordingly the life decision that makes a philosopher is motivated in a different way from the artist's or the scientist's. Husserl now withdraws part of what Lecture 29 claimed. The ethics of vocation was a genus; philosophy is not just one species of it, since its radicalism concerns naivety itself rather than degree of devotion.

The philosopher's objection to the dogmatism of positive science need not touch the ideality of the scientist's disposition. Modern science may be too much under Bacon's utilitarian motto that knowledge is power, but one cannot deny scientists pure love of truth, and the best of them have a genuine research vocation. In science, however, as in art, this love and the life decision that follows can grow unnoticed. Someone can develop a pure love of art in youth, turn it into practice, specialize, say, in landscape painting, and slip into the profession without any solemn decision; a later explicit choice of career merely confirms and articulates a habitual will that had already grown naturally. The same happens with precocious scientific talent. The contrast being prepared concerns genesis: ordinary vocations can arise by passive, unnoticed development, and their founding decision can come after the fact.

With the philosopher it is entirely different. He needs a resolution of his own, one that first creates him as a philosopher, a primal founding (Urstiftung) that is an original self-creation. No one can slip into philosophy. Urstiftung is the key term of Husserl's genetic phenomenology and later of the Crisis, where the Greek primal founding of philosophy is renewed in every genuine re-founding; here it is applied to the individual. The philosopher is not a natural kind that matures; he is instituted by his own act.

The reason is that a certain naivety of the love and creation of knowledge necessarily comes first, and it carries an imperfection hidden from itself, felt dimly at best but not understood. Scepticism first exposes it. The imperfection appears only when the knower attends to the relation of the objects known and the truths achieved to the knower, and falls into the well-known epistemological difficulties. He must finally convince himself that all knowledge is affected: no cognitive value can be asserted as absolute in the naive attitude, since each is inseparably related back to the knowing subject and its cognitive shaping, and if any pure cognitive value can be secured, it must be grasped within this correlation with cognitive accomplishment. Occasional reflections and mixtures of naivety and reflection achieve nothing and produce only absurdity. Merely logical critique, the scientist's own, cannot help, because logic itself carries the same unintelligibility. So there arises the need for a new, universal radicalism that targets all naivety in principle and seeks ultimate truth, the only truth in the full sense. This produces the will to begin a wholly new, all-embracing, thoroughly transcendental science with no sceptical abysses, in which everything is clear and certain. Plato faced sophistical scepticism and sought a new science against it; scepticism accompanied ancient and medieval thought, constantly urging the radicalism that alone could overcome it; sceptical nominalism reached into the modern age and was opposed by the Platonism renewed in the Renaissance. Two theses matter for the reduction. First, the problem that motivates philosophy is the correlation of object and knowing, discovered through scepticism; this is the "transcendental problem" of the Idea of Phenomenology (1907) and of Ideen I, restated as the drama of a biography. Second, logic is not exempt: Husserl refuses the neo-Kantian route of grounding knowledge in a logic of validity, since logic too is an accomplishment whose sense needs clarifying, which is the programme of Formal and Transcendental Logic five years later.

In Descartes a will to absolutely radical grounding of science comes alive with original force and defines the modern epoch: the idea of a philosophy that has nothing in advance that it has not given itself. Yet English empiricism follows and lives on as open or veiled scepticism, with Hume and the positivism he inspired. Before us also stands modern independent science with its ideal taken from mathematics, while mathematics itself exhausts itself vainly trying to clarify its elementary concepts, axioms, and principles of inference. Seeing the inadequacy of all this, we want a philosophy. Its idea therefore contains a finality and a radicalism of finality that cannot grow naturally and unnoticed from any naive love of knowledge, including the half-naive love of science in the positive sciences. What it presupposes in the subject is a kind of collapse of all naive cognitive and scientific values: the insight that all science so far suffers from imperfections that are incurable as long as it keeps its type, that positive science must be called into question and in a certain way put out of validity in advance, and that a completely new beginning is needed if science in the full sense is to be possible. Even that possibility may not be presupposed; at the beginning it too must be put in question. The editor refers to Beilage V on whether the goal of universal knowledge is meaningless. Two phrases are anticipations. "Put out of validity in advance" is the first formulation of the epoché over the sciences, which Section Two extends to the world of experience. And putting the very possibility of philosophy into question ensures that the beginning does not rest on an assumed guarantee; it will have to secure its own possibility, which is why the apodictic critique of the ego cogito cannot be skipped. The foundations crisis in mathematics, fresh in 1923 (Brouwer, Weyl, Hilbert), is the concrete reference.

This is the unique situation of the beginning philosopher, and not by historical accident. Even ideally, the idea of an absolutely grounded science must draw its sense from such a situation. Without it, the philosopher, however pure his love of knowledge, falls back into naivety, and whatever the factual course, the development would take the same general form: it would bring the knower face to face with the transcendental abysses of knowledge and entangle him in sceptical snares and puzzles, thereby producing the classical situation for the beginning of genuine philosophy. Husserl turns the history of Hua VII into an eidetic claim. Any path of knowledge carried far enough reaches the sceptical crisis; the absolute situation is the necessary form of the start, not a Western accident. The later Crisis will make the European history of reason more specifically historical, but here historicity is subordinate to essence.

In this situation a great life decision is needed: to take the risk and stake one's life on seeking truth and science in the radicalism of the ultimate, or rather to attempt such a science from oneself and in association with the like-minded; a science from an ultimate good conscience, which alone can satisfy the pure love of knowledge. It must create complete clarity in every direction. It cannot rest content with the naive fulfilment of straightforward intentions; above all it must produce transcendental clarity, which dispels the transcendental ghosts and cobwebs of scepticism on one side and dogmatic metaphysics on the other. That happens by studying all scientific accomplishment in the full concrete context of the accomplishing subjectivity and never apart from this correlation. Three details carry weight. The decision is a "risk", since its success is not guaranteed in advance, as the previous paragraph required. The phrase "with the like-minded" keeps a place for the community of philosophers, which returns as the intersubjective reduction of Lecture 53. And scepticism and dogmatic metaphysics are paired as two symptoms of one naivety: both treat knowledge and its objects apart from the correlation that constitutes them.

Picture the beginning philosopher in the absolute situation, after the primal decision to meet its demands. The first necessity is to set going meditations on possible method. In their course the empty generality of the idea of a beginning will fill out, the concrete sense of philosophy will unfold, and the same will hold for method, whose elements and connections will be determined ever more richly. The ideal and the method become concrete together. Husserl rules out a method fixed in advance and then applied; method and goal clarify each other, and this explains why the course will return to its own method in the "phenomenology of the phenomenological reduction" of Section Three.

At this point one could enter the meditations and perform the first philosophical act, which follows for the beginner as an absolute demand and from the consistency of his will: the universal overthrow of all previous convictions, however acquired, the overthrow Descartes demanded "once in a lifetime" of all who seriously strive for a good mind (*qui serio student ad bonam mentem*). Husserl glosses the good mind as the absolutely good cognitive conscience, the philosopher's self-justification. The Cartesian formula (from the Principles and the Search for Truth, and the opening of the First Meditation) is thus reinterpreted in the terms of Lectures 28–29: the overthrow is an ethical-epistemic act of self-responsibility, not a sceptical exercise. Lecture 33 onward will replace "overthrow" by the more careful critique of mundane experience.

Before beginning, Husserl closes the introduction with a consideration showing that philosophy, though it concerns the realm of cognitive values, has a significance spanning all pure culture and hence all rational humanity, and that this is an essential necessity, not a historical fact. Knowing in its full breadth covers the whole sphere of judgment, predicative and prepredicative, every act of belief and all its modalities (conjecture, holding possible, and so on). Other kinds of ego-acts remain: loving and hating, liking and disliking, wishing, desiring, willing. These functions do not lie side by side but interpenetrate. Scientific knowing is not just judging: a line of striving and willing runs through unclear judging and terminates in insightful judging, giving the truth reached the character of a goal of the will; valuing is involved too, since whoever aims at truth values it positively. Take pure liking, as of a flower. The flower is given in perceptual belief with its material features, but liking adds a further content, the character of charming, beautiful, lovely. We easily pass from the attitude of liking, a pure turning of feeling, into the attitude of judgment, in which "beautiful" is grasped in an experiencing belief and then predicated. All such predicates originate in feeling before they are predicated or even experientially grasped, just as predicates of good and bad, useful and harmful, originate in the will bound up with valuing feeling. The same holds for culture: cultural works are understood by re-understanding the feeling and willing that produced them, and what is so understood can become the object of experience, predication, and science, the aesthetic and practical attitude turning into a cognitive one. Only by deliberately and abstractively excluding all predicates whose source lies in feeling and will, as the natural scientist does, do we get sciences of pure knowledge. There feeling and will operate only as the will to know and the valuation of truth, not as a source of objective predicates; cultural sciences, by contrast, deal precisely with such predicates. This is the doctrine of Ideen II §§ 2–11 in compressed form: the "merely material thing" of natural science is an abstraction from the fully valued thing of the life-world, and the naturalistic attitude is an artificial restriction of the personalistic one. Its role here is to prepare the claim that cognition, through its capacity to turn any attitude into a cognitive one, reaches over all the rest.

So the realm of knowledge encompasses every realm of accomplishment originating in feeling and will, and correlatively valuing feeling and willing reach over the whole of subjectivity and all its intentional functions. For science this means that all valuing and practical reason is reflected and objectified in it, as the objectification of cognitive reason: every truth about value and every practical truth can be expressed in predicative forms and take on forms of cognitive grounding. Feeling values purely in itself, and the acting will shapes the beautiful. The genuineness of a value and a work shows itself originally and naively in feeling, in pure satisfaction; practical realization shows itself in the founded satisfaction of achievement. But genuineness of value and truth of achievement are ultimately answered for in knowledge, which predicates value and disvalue in logical form and refers the contingent intuition of value back to general norms, gaining a higher responsibility. The highest responsibility arises in knowledge from the transcendental attitude toward the ultimately constitutive accomplishments of feeling and will. Husserl distinguishes where a value shows itself (feeling) from where it is answered for (knowledge). This is the basis for the primacy of theoretical reason in his system, which Heidegger and Scheler contested: not that valuing reduces to judging, but that responsibility for any claim to validity is discharged only in knowing, and ultimately in transcendental knowing.

Hence a philosophy referring to science in general, if it seeks full universality and keeps the whole transcendental problematic and all possible worlds of knowledge in view, is not a "mere" theory of knowledge or of science dealing with something limited or with a logical and noetic generality. Every essential generality and every essential particular in the play between subjectivity and objectivity, every kind of reason and its products, every essential form of social subjectivity and its cultural systems, falls within a full philosophy. Pure questions of knowledge come first only because radical philosophy, like logic, naturally proceeds from the most general to filled particulars. The most general form of science does not yet prejudge the basic divisions of what is, in the formal sense of being as what is true: it does not yet know distinctions such as bare fact, value, and what ought to be, or within facts, nature and spirit. Husserl pre-empts the complaint that transcendental phenomenology is epistemology in disguise. The priority of the cognitive is an order of exposition, starting from the formally universal concept of "what is", which is neutral with respect to fact and value; the course will indeed begin with the most general, the ego cogito, before any regional distinctions.

### Chapter Two: The Idea of Apodictic Evidence and the Problem of the Beginning

#### Lecture 31: Natural and Transcendental, Apodictic and Adequate Evidence

Husserl restates the starting point in the first-person plural. We are philosophers in the making, in the absolute situation. Behind us lies our scientific life and its products, truths and theories once valued as absolute, which no longer satisfy. We have woken from the naivety of positive grounding and felt the sting of scepticism, which taught us to look at knowing subjectivity: all being meant or grounded as true, and every theory, originates subjectively in its accomplishments of consciousness, in its pretheoretical passivity and its theoretical activity. We have seen that the world and its true theory cannot be fully known without studying the transcendental subjectivity in which world and theory are constituted. Every positive science is abstractly one-sided, because in it the transcendental life of experiencing, thinking, and grounding consciousness remains anonymous: unseen, untheorized, not understood. "Anonymity" is the key term. The failing of positive science is not error but a blind spot: the functioning subjectivity that makes its results possible does not appear among them. The pairing of passivity and activity signals that constitution includes pre-predicative synthesis, the subject of the Analyses concerning Passive Synthesis delivered in the same years.

One group of sciences has already thematized subjectivity and its knowing: psychology and the biological, sociological, and cultural disciplines interwoven with it. But these too are positive or "dogmatic". They proceed with the same naivety, in which knowing as the originating function of the objects known, their sense-giving and grounding of being, does not receive its theoretical due. They treat animal and human, individual and social subjects and their products in the world (embodied spirit, culture) as worldly objects, categories of mundanized spirit. The point is the core of Husserl's anti-psychologism in its transcendental form: psychology has subjectivity as its theme but only as a part of the world, so it presupposes the world whose constitution is in question. The whole of Section Four will turn on exactly this difference between psychological and transcendental subjectivity.

All sciences thus came under review, and the same failure appeared everywhere, not as an accidental imperfection of the existing sciences but as a feature of their method in principle. It would therefore belong in advance to any new science founded by the same method, and no improvement within that method could change it. The criticism is eidetic, not empirical: it concerns the type "positive science", and so rules out the reply that science will fix its foundations in due course.

This was already a universal and in a sense radical critique of science: of the history of philosophy, which includes the history of all sciences and the origin of the very idea of positive science, and of present-day science with its hopeless philosophical annexes. But it was only a first necessary piece of the meditations on first philosophy. It could only make us feel acutely the insufficiency of positive science and the impossibility of a positivistically grounded philosophy of the absolute sense of the world, and, by turning our gaze toward sense-giving subjectivity and the dark correlations of cognitive accomplishment, cognitive sense, and known being, let us divine a new science. More: the reference back to transcendental subjectivity as the field of origin of all reason had to give the ancient idea of a universal, absolutely grounded, self-justifying science new sense and force, as it had already done in Descartes. The history of Hua VII has been a motivating critique; its results are "felt" and "divined", not established. Husserl carefully withholds from it the status of a premise, since the beginning must not borrow from anything unjustified.

This created the motivation, the absolute situation. Despair over the finality of positive science did not end in sceptical despair about genuine science as such. On the contrary, the critique of every type of scepticism, whose sources lie in the insufficiency of positive knowledge, was our main means of foreseeing what was missing: the theoretical exhibition and systematic investigation of the unitary ground of all knowledge, transcendental subjectivity, in whose accomplishing life all unities of knowledge arise. Scepticism is valuable as a symptom. It is right about positive knowledge and wrong about knowledge as such, and diagnosing why it is right points to the missing field.

The gain of all previous reflections is therefore the idea of a universal science of this ground, of what is originary in the strictest sense for the sense and validity of all possible being and all possible sciences, and of a universal philosophy that produces all possible sciences from this origin, lets them arise in absolute intelligibility, and embraces them as branches. "Produces" is strong: philosophy is not to endorse existing sciences but to regenerate them from the origin, a programme Husserl never gives up (compare the closing pages of the Cartesian Meditations on the sciences reborn from transcendental phenomenology).

This is the highest guiding goal-idea of all cognitive effort. But as it has arisen from evident motivation it is not yet an explicit conception of the science. We have no clear archetype of philosophy, only a formal methodological guiding thought: a science is needed that, as a genuine "archaeology", systematically investigates the ultimately original and all origins of being and truth, and teaches how every cognition can be brought from this source into the highest form of reason, absolute foundation and justification, the last conceivable clarification of sense and certification of right. In that form knowledge can be acquired as "final", not merely valid, and set aside as settled once and for all. It then joins our fixed possessions, reactivatable at any time in its original form and defensible in its invariable finality, not only from the naive clarity usually called evidence (natural evidence, the evidence of positivity) but from the higher-level evidence of transcendental clarity about origins, in which the origin hidden in positive evidence, with its horizon of motivations that fixes and limits its original right, is uncovered and understood. Two technical points. "Archaeology" is Husserl's word for the regressive inquiry into constitutive strata; it recurs in the manuscripts of the 1930s and was picked up by Fink and, through him, by Foucault's vocabulary. And the distinction between natural evidence and transcendental evidence of origin is the thesis that governs what follows: an evidence can be perfect in its own direction and still be incomplete, because its constitutive horizon is not yet uncovered.

As philosophers in the making we decide practically for this idea and draw the consequences: we try to unfold what it contains as an intention and indicates as an indeterminate demand, and so bring the intended science into realizing form. Only then can we have it itself and know what we were aiming at. The goal is known only in its fulfilment, which applies the structure of intention and fulfilment reflexively to philosophy itself: the idea of philosophy is an empty intention that only the working-out can fill.

Enriching the first sketch of the guiding idea, we notice through the distinction between natural and transcendental evidence that "universal science from absolute justification" contains several components of sense not clearly distinguished in the ambiguous phrase. The editor reports Husserl's marginal note "rework and shorten greatly" and points to Beilage VI, "The Questions of the Beginning", as an alternative version of this whole stretch from the start of the lecture. The note is a warning that the present text is a lecture draft Husserl found prolix, not a finished statement.

"Absolute" points on one side to the unitary source of all knowledge, transcendental subjectivity, so far known only as a distant idea. On the other side, "absolute justification" means a perfect accounting that tolerates no lack of clarity and distinctness, no lack of evidence, nothing that could cloud certainty or later put the results in question. My knowledge is to be final, so that I never have to give up what I hold certain because I later see it is not so, or not as I believed. And it is to be evident in every respect, including respects not now actual for me: what is known should not have essential determinations outside the direction of my otherwise perfect evidence, which, being unknown, bring treacherous obscurities and doubts. The two senses are distinct: absoluteness of source (transcendental subjectivity) and absoluteness of quality (evidence without defect). The second sense splits again, into indubitability (no later cancellation) and completeness (no unseen sides). These are the germs of the later distinction between apodicticity and adequacy, which this lecture is about to identify and which the Cartesian Meditations (§ 6) will separate.

The double sense of absolute justification has deeper grounds. Every evidence not yet referred back to absolute subjectivity, even one as perfect as mathematical evidence, necessarily has sides of possible questionability, puzzlement, and doubt. These do not alter its content in its original direction or cancel it in its positivity, but they mean an imperfection of knowledge that cannot be tolerated. This clarifies the distinction drawn earlier between defects of evidence that leave open the cancellation of knowledge and defects that do not, but mark a missing piece of a one-sided evidence, and hence unseen abysses where one may slide away from the natural directions of knowledge. That distinction was meant to capture the defects of all merely natural evidence, the evidence of positivity, which fails with regard to the transcendental. The editor refers to Beilage VII on logification and making intelligible. The claim is sharp: even mathematics is not self-sufficient. A theorem is not threatened with falsity, but what it is to be a mathematical object, and how that sense is constituted, remains obscure, and in that obscurity the paradoxes of foundations and the sceptical abysses take root. Husserl distinguishes a defect of certainty from a defect of intelligibility; the transcendental critique concerns the second.

In the search for a true beginning both aspects must be kept in view. But first comes the most general principle of justification, to which all knowledge, naive and transcendental alike, is subject, and which expresses the demand for genuine grounding from pure evidence. As beginning philosophers we will to follow the principle of pure evidence with the strictest generality of will. The formulation follows. Husserl states the principle as an object of volition ("we will"), in line with Lecture 28: it is not an axiom but a maxim adopted by the philosopher's founding decision.

We will accept nothing as finally known, nothing as being or being so in any mode, unless it stands before us itself, grasped exactly as it is meant and posited in our cognitive belief. We let the radicalism of the ultimate govern and try always to pass to the limit of evidence. Our cognitive satisfaction is to be absolute: an absolute attainment of what we aimed at. That requires pure certainty in having the being itself; while uncertainty remains we are unsatisfied. And the certainty must be absolutely evident at the limit: not a wholly certain opinion but seeing certainty, finally absolutely self-giving. Self-giving is the measure, and its absolute optimum the last measure, by which we test every judgment. This lies in the sense of all scientific activity; we only make it conscious and turn it into a first principle of deliberate method. The principle is the "principle of all principles" of Ideen I § 24, restated as a norm of the will and radicalized: self-givenness now has an optimum, and the optimum is the standard. Husserl insists on the continuity with ordinary science, which only lacks the reflective explicitness.

Felt cognitive satisfaction is not enough; evidence must justify itself as evidence. We must convince ourselves whether and how far our intention is fulfilled, whether the known is really given itself, and whether anything is still meant only in anticipation without corresponding self-given components. In principle this can always be done in a subsequent reflection that measures the components of the meant sense against what is self-given, testing each for fulfilment. An evidence with this ideal perfection is called adequate. That it is adequate we know only in a second, reflective evidence, which must itself be adequate. That adequate reflections are possible in infinitum and that justifications seem needed in infinitum should not trouble us yet, though the problem, like the problem of the possibility of adequate givenness, must be faced in due course. Perhaps such self-givenness is a mere "idea", as pure red is: seen red is always only more or less pure red, in series of increase through which we approach pure red while remaining at a distance; beyond the last member a further increase is always conceivable, and yet in the progress we have evidence of approaching the distant pure red, hence, in an essentially grounded ambiguity, an evidence of the being of this idea as idea, though not a having of pure red itself. Perhaps every evidence as self-giving has such a relativity, so that "adequate evidence" is always a continuous, freely continuable process of increasing relative evidences, a consciousness of approach to a goal that becomes evident only as an idea and evidently remains unreached. Regress questions arise here too, but they cannot be questions of the beginning. This is one of the most important paragraphs of the course. Husserl concedes, against the programme just stated, that adequacy may be an idea in the Kantian sense, approached but never reached, and that the evidence of an idea is evidence of approach, not possession. If so, a beginning cannot rest on adequacy. That is why the next lectures shift the weight to apodicticity, and why Lecture 53 will demand an apodictic critique of transcendental experience itself. The deferral "not questions of the beginning" is methodical, not evasive: the beginning must be possible without having solved these problems, but they are marked for return.

As a beginner I have no knowledge; I seek it, and not just any "knowledge" but genuine knowledge that I can justify as such. By the first reflection this can only mean evident knowledge, at best adequately evident or justifiable by adequate evidence, as far as that is possible. I do not want to opine at random but to draw what I mean from having the thing itself, or, where my opinion was not so drawn, to seek the corresponding self-having and "verify" it by converting what is merely meant into the self-grasping of the same. The first-person singular replaces the plural here, and it will dominate from now on: the meditating "I" is the only standpoint the method permits. The qualifier "as far as that is possible" carries the concession of the previous paragraph.

Is it not clear, even with the unavoidable naivety of a beginning, that striving and acting proceed as aiming and attaining, and that attainment is conscious to the agent, in the acting itself, as the becoming-certain realization and self-having of the goal? Is it not clear that in cognitive action the practical intention strives through mere opinion toward self-having of the meant being, and that there is such a thing as evidence, in degrees up to the limit of adequacy, as the condition for degrees of satisfaction? Nothing is clearer than that in perceiving a thing I grasp it, this thing there, itself; and that in "seeing" that 2 < 3 I have and grasp the meant state of affairs itself and am at the goal, behind which, in its adequacy, there is nothing more to seek. The "seen" is simply the "itself" of what is meant, at once meant and self-grasped. The two examples are chosen to differ. Perception gives the thing itself but, as Lecture 33 will show, not adequately; the arithmetic insight is offered as a case of adequacy. Husserl here still uses "itself" for both, and this blurring of self-givenness with adequate self-givenness is exactly what the critique of mundane experience will undo.

A further mark of adequate evidence appears in the test of passing through negation or doubt. If I try to negate an adequate evidence or posit it as doubtful, the impossibility of the non-being or doubtfulness of what is evident, grasped in absolute self-giving, leaps out, again in adequate evidence. This property may be called apodicticity. Conversely, every apodictic evidence is adequate, so the terms can be used as equivalent, preferring one or the other according to the emphasis. The editor refers to Beilage VIII on the apodictically given as presupposition of all cognitive striving. The test of negation defines apodicticity as the evident inconceivability of the contrary, a reflective property. The claimed converse (apodictic implies adequate) is the thesis that Husserl will later reject: in the Cartesian Meditations § 9 the ego cogito is apodictic yet inadequate, surrounded by a horizon of undetermined past and future. Lectures 39–40 below, with the living present and its horizons, already strain the equivalence, and Lecture 53 will need the separation to formulate the critique of transcendental experience. The equivalence here is a provisional thesis of the beginning, not doctrine.

This reflection yields the principled sense of Descartes' maxim of indubitability, his method of rejecting as unjustified everything that could give the least occasion for doubt, used at the start of the Meditations as the principle of perfect justification. Husserl reconstructs the Cartesian rule of doubt from his own principle of evidence: to reject whatever admits the slightest doubt is to accept only what passes the test of negation, that is, only the apodictic. The Cartesian procedure is thus rehabilitated as a consequence of the phenomenology of evidence, not of sceptical tactics.

#### Lecture 32: The Possible Starting Points: "I Am" and "I as Beginning Philosopher"; "I Am" and "This World Is"

Husserl restates the guiding principle of adequate and therefore apodictic evidence, which the last lecture adopted. It is a formal and unsurpassable ideal of justification: a judgment can be perfectly justified only if we not only make it evident but convince ourselves that what we mean is in our grasp exactly as meant, seen and grasped in person, and, as the test of adequacy, that every positing of its non-being or doubtfulness is cancelled in absolute evidence. Applying the principle may allow us to restrict a given evidence to its absolutely justifiable content and so turn an inadequate evidence into an adequate but restricted one. The last sentence names the operation the course will actually perform. The reduction to the ego is such a restriction: from the full, inadequate experience of self-in-the-world, subtract what cannot pass the test and keep the residue.

We will try to carry out this absolute ideal of adequacy, however much it demands, and see how far it takes us. The phrase is experimental. The ideal is adopted as a working norm whose reach is itself to be tested.

Perhaps later we will have to distinguish many forms of justification without the character of adequate evidence; perhaps we can then show that adequate evidence must function in all of them as the last source of justification, if justification is to be real and serious. For now we know nothing of this, and the absolute ideal is prescribed as the guiding principle of the beginning. Husserl wrote "Why?" in the margin beside the last sentence, and the editor points to a longer critical note in Beilage II. The self-query targets exactly the weak joint of the argument: why must the beginning be held to the absolute ideal if other forms of justification may prove legitimate? By 1929 the answer shifts from adequacy to apodicticity, and the ideal of adequacy survives only as a regulative idea.

The next reflection concerns the beginning itself: which adequate evidences must we secure first, as the ground on which philosophy is to be built? All sciences have been put out of validity. Should we search them for adequate evidences, or test which of their evidences can be made adequate? Since sciences are structures of mediate knowledge whose justification leads back to immediate basic knowledge, should we criticize the evidence of all their basic cognitions? The question is the classical foundationalist one: work down the chain of inference to first premises and test those.

It is evident that a universal justification of knowledge would have to follow the order of mediation, so that any universal critique of knowledge would first demand a critique of immediate knowledge. Yet the proposed procedure becomes doubtful if we recall the motivations of the preliminary meditations. Is our aim a science of the traditional methodological type, only carried out perfectly? If so, no radical rebuilding would be needed, no real overthrow, only a general questioning by evaluative critique, with universal re-examination and correction: cognitions proving themselves in perfect evidence would be readmitted, the rest replaced. Husserl accepts the principle of mediation but rejects its sufficiency. Checking premises is the scientist's own procedure; it cannot address a defect that belongs to the type of science itself.

Such an enterprise would have little prospect. Every scientist practises this kind of critique continually, and through it positive sciences rise from lower to higher perfection. The need for it has long since produced general reflections on the methodological features of science as such: logic, as universal theory of science, exists precisely to be the universal critique of all sciences. If the task were only premise-checking, logic would already have accomplished it or could. The paragraph removes the most obvious rival to transcendental philosophy, the view that logic and methodology are the critique of science.

But neither this general logic nor any system of perfected special sciences of the familiar type can be our aim. As the historical-critical meditations showed, that type has a defect in principle, transcendental naivety, and overcoming it defines the radical sense of philosophy. That all knowledge is an accomplishment of knowing subjectivity is a platitude, yet it is the origin of all transcendental confusion and all absurd metaphysics. Even the most perfect evidence of positive science is surrounded by transcendental and metaphysical fog in which scepticism and mysticism play. Every positive cognition, insofar as it is genuine, is an evidence and at the same time a riddle; it needs transcendental illumination. Since this holds for all knowledge, there is a universal task: to investigate knowing subjectivity as accomplishing knowledge, in all its kinds and forms. This task, which cannot be refused, came to us from the preliminary meditation. "Evidence and riddle at once" is the formula of the whole transcendental problem. The platitude that knowledge is the subject's accomplishment is harmless until one asks how an accomplishment inside subjectivity can reach something that is not subjective; the naive answers to that question produce scepticism (it cannot) and dogmatic metaphysics (it must, through some mechanism), both of which Husserl blames on failing to investigate the accomplishment itself.

How is this task to be taken up? What method first makes its pure sense graspable and allows the transcendental accomplishments of knowing to be investigated scientifically? How can subjectivity attain the self-knowledge in which it understands all truth and science as formations shaped within itself? And how can transcendental self-knowledge satisfy the principle placed at the head, adequate evidence? Perhaps, too, the demand for transcendental inquiry is itself a consequence of the demand to bring all knowledge to final fulfilment. The two perfections required for absolute and universal knowledge are closely linked. Perhaps the extreme demand to fulfil every cognitive intention would by itself force the passage into the transcendental problematic, so that a self-justifying science according to the ideal of ultimate evidence would eo ipso be transcendental philosophy. The conjecture links the two senses of "absolute" distinguished in Lecture 31: absoluteness of evidence and absoluteness of source. If pursuing perfect fulfilment necessarily leads back to the constituting subject, the Cartesian way and the transcendental way are one way. That is the thesis Section Two will test and, in Lecture 46, partly abandon.

We know from the preliminary meditations that there are great difficulties. Above all, the self-knowledge in question is quite different from the natural self-knowledge of a human being and from a psychology drawing on it as inner experience. But nothing learned in the preliminary meditations may be presupposed in a strictly systematic construction, except as a motive guiding our gaze and our new findings. To reach a real beginning we may have nothing except what we have acquired originally ourselves. The distinction between results and motives is the methodological hinge between Hua VII and Hua VIII. The history may direct attention; it may not supply premises.

Our task is therefore to work out the transcendental ground afresh in this original way: to force upon ourselves the positing of the "I am" with its peculiar and absolutely necessary sense, as transcendental self-positing. Later we must carry out, against the natural-naive, objective-logical critique of knowledge, the differently founded transcendental critique, and show that all positive science and its critique are encompassed by the transcendental frame and critique, but now performed in the spirit of finality. "Force upon ourselves" (abnötigen) signals that the "I am" is not assumed but extracted under the pressure of the principle of evidence. The second task, the incorporation of positive science into transcendental critique, is the programme of the later Formal and Transcendental Logic, and it also answers the worry that the reduction loses the world: positive knowledge is to be recovered, not discarded.

There are several ways to proceed in the meditation that is to create the beginning. One can steer straight at the goal already foreseen: begin with the knowledge "I am", see how it stands with its adequate evidence, and, if the natural first evidence of "I am" proves imperfect, try to convert it into an adequate and therefore apodictic one. By methodically excluding everything that is not adequately given in principle, one would reach pure or transcendental subjectivity, the content of subjectivity accessible to itself in pure adequacy. This is the direct Cartesian path: start from the ego and purify it. Husserl presents it and then declines it for systematic reasons.

The second way refuses all guidance from the preliminary meditations and uses only the idea of a universal science from absolute justification. "I" would then have to say: I as beginning philosopher. At the beginning must stand an immediate cognition, or a field of immediate cognitions indicated by one, and these must be apodictically certain. I have put all sciences out of validity, but the stream of my life and immediate experience goes on, assuring me of myself and of the world I live in. These are cognitions too and would have to be included in the overthrow, given my claim to universal justification; but I can resurrect them at once, since here I have undeniable evidence: I am, this world is; how could I doubt it? Of course I must test them for real apodicticity from real stocks of adequacy. The test leads to transcendental subjectivity as the only stock of apodictic immediacies, absolutely indubitable data of experience, and so to the same result as the first way; conversely, the critique required on the first way makes it unavoidable to criticize the supposed indubitability of the world's existence. The two ways must soon coincide. The second way is chosen because it assumes less: it starts from what the natural attitude itself offers as most certain, the world and myself in it, and lets the principle of evidence do the work of separating them. The "first way" of the Section title below is this second way of the present paragraph, the way through the critique of world-experience, later known as the Cartesian way.

Preferring the second way for systematic reasons, the question of the immediate evidences preceding all sciences, and then of the experiences preceding them, seems to lead to the world of experience as indubitable being. We experience ourselves as particulars within it, so there seems no reason to single ourselves out and state "I am" separately. The world is there in constant experience; our cognitive efforts, cares, and actions relate to it and its events; nothing is more certain. Experience sometimes deceives: what stood before us in person does not exist or is otherwise, and we speak of illusion and deception of the senses. But if sensuous experience cannot claim indubitability in a given case, the existence of the world does not become questionable. Experience can be careful, aimed at evidence, carried out with the requisite completeness, and so gives legitimate basic knowledge for rational science. It seems that the title "the world is", with the universe of deliberate and precise experience, contains the universe of all ultimate, immediate principles of knowledge, or of all experiences needed for a universal science. I, with my existence and the experiences that grasp me directly, am naturally included; with the negation or real annihilation of the world, I would be negated and annihilated. Husserl gives the natural attitude its strongest voice, the one of Ideen I § 30 ("the general thesis"): local error never touches the general certainty of the world, and the ego is a part of the world. The final sentence states the thesis Section Two will overturn: that my being depends on the world's.

However evident this natural reflection, and however much "I am" seems a contingent, unprivileged specification of the world's existence, one can hold, with much better reasons, that "I am" must be the true principle of all principles and the first proposition of philosophy. One can show that the whole argument for the indubitability of the world's existence and the right of world-experience does not pass the test of genuine apodicticity; so for a philosophy to be absolutely grounded, the universe taken in its totality must remain out of validity, questionable for the time being as lacking absolute grounding, like the propositions of all world-sciences. The editor refers to Beilage IX on empirical and transcendental critique of experience. The reversal is announced: the principle of principles is no longer a formal rule of evidence (as in Ideen I § 24) but a first proposition, "I am". The world is not denied; it "remains in question", which is the exact status the epoché will give it.

We easily convince ourselves that every perception of a spatial thing is inadequate. It gives the thing only from one side, with some of its determinations, partial qualities belonging to the partly visible shape. We are conscious of the existence of the thing itself, but that does not change the incompleteness; nor does passing from perception to perception in continuous observation and counting the whole connection as one perception. Each phase has actually seen only its own content, and in new phases something of what was seen is inevitably lost; moreover observing perception never reaches an end. This lies in the sense of experience itself: the perceiver is well aware of horizons of manifold possible experience reaching beyond what is actually perceived. The editor points to a critical note of Husserl's in Beilage II. The argument goes from inadequacy to non-apodicticity through the horizon. Inadequacy here is not a failing of attention; it belongs to the sense of perceiving a spatial thing, whose givenness in adumbrations was analysed in Ideen I § 41 and in Thing and Space (1907). The lecture ends at the point where this structure will be converted, in Lecture 33, into the possibility of the thing's non-being.

## Section Two: Critique of Mundane Experience. First Way to the Transcendental Reduction

### Chapter One: World-Perception and World-Belief

#### Lecture 33: The Irremovable Contingency of the Proposition "The World Is"

Husserl recalls that the last lecture subjected the indubitability of the world's existence, based on continuously advancing mundane experience, to an apodictic critique: it asked whether external experience, however careful and experimental, can ever be adequate perception. For every perception of a single spatial thing there proved to be an unavoidable inadequacy. In every perception the perceiver is conscious of grasping the thing in person, and also of not grasping it fully in this bodily presence. This is not a fact established from outside. Every perception, as experiencing meaning, has a content of determinations conscious as actually self-grasped (a piece of the shape actually seen, with its qualities) and a content of empty co-meaning and pre-meaning, conscious as something that would come to bodily self-givenness only in further perception, which can be freely set going. The inadequacy is available to the perceiver in the perception itself: every perception knows itself to be partial. The empty intentions are not guesses added to perception but belong to its sense, as Ideen I and the Analyses concerning Passive Synthesis show.

This holds with strict necessity and generality. Every perception of a spatial thing is, and counts for us as we perceive, a mixture of genuine self-giving and co-meaning. The co-meaning gives every observing and experimenting intention its practical sense: it supplies open horizons, the not yet grasped or imperfectly known, and indicates freely available series of perceptions in which what was merely co-meant would become bodily grasped. As perception of a thing expands, disclosing new parts and sides, this structure remains; it is still inadequate perception, and a closure in the form of a perception that would be genuine perception throughout, with no anticipation and no horizon of co-meaning, is inconceivable. The claim is eidetic ("inconceivable"), and it grounds the positive role of the horizon: the same co-meaning that makes perception inadequate makes it motivate further perception. Inadequacy and the practical openness of inquiry are one structure.

Bound up with this are open possibilities that what is experienced is otherwise or does not exist. Every external perception can deceive, even though by its own sense it is direct self-grasping. By its own sense it also anticipates, and so radically that even the content self-grasped in a given moment contains moments of anticipation: strictly, nothing in what is perceived is purely and adequately perceived. We depend on continuing confirmation. I really see an evenly red plane surface, yet on approaching, unevenness of colour may appear, though confirmation is equally open. Even when confirmation has held so far, at each moment the situation is the same: further disconfirmation remains possible. So it is no wonder that experience deceives, nor that experience justifies itself through experience, though only presumptively. The step from "not adequate" to "possibly not so" is taken through the claim that anticipation pervades even the actually seen: there is no adequate core within perception to fall back on. The word "presumptive" names the positive status of experiential justification. Experience is not unjustified; its right is the right of a presumption that holds until overridden, and this is the exact status the next pages extend from the single thing to the world.

The same holds for the total perception in which all single perceptions are embedded as moments. Through this universal and continuously advancing perception we are constantly conscious of one world, enduring through one infinite time, persisting through particular changes, extending through one space. This world is constantly experienced perceptually, but only in the manner of anticipating meaning, with open possibilities of being otherwise and even of not being. The unit of analysis shifts from the thing to the world. The world is not the sum of perceived things but the correlate of one total perception, a concept Husserl develops here for the first time on this scale; in the Crisis it becomes "world-consciousness" as the universal horizon.

In fact the continuous stream of external perception, with its contingent and ever-threatening disappointments, runs in the form of consistent correction. Every anticipating apprehension that fails to be confirmed can be, and of itself is, replaced by a modified one that restores harmony and is confirmed for the time being. A perception turns out to be illusion: in the course of perceiving it is cancelled by an emerging discordance, and with the cancellation goes a change of sense, as when I see a tree stump in the fog instead of a man, and harmony returns. That this always happens and can go on happening in this style is a remarkable fact and anything but an apodictic necessity. An endless stream of perception runs through my waking life and keeps this structure of becoming, resolving all disharmony into harmony, so that one and the same world remains consciously there. We always say "the" world and experience it as grasped in person, though this grasping is through and through anticipation, geared to confirmation, sometimes confirmed, sometimes dissolving into doubtful or null semblance. Correction always occurs or at least is possible: doubt is resolved, a correct sense is substituted for the null one, and harmony restored. Correlatively the world as experienced after each correction counts as the true one. This truth is forever on the march; it too is provisional and may have to be overcome, but it always can be, in the form of a new correction and a newly harmonious world. The earlier truth is never wholly abandoned, as the word "correction" implies. A world is there in a truth that is always relative, but knowable in relative truth. Two claims are kept apart. Correction is the internal mechanism by which local illusions leave the world intact. That the stream always has this style is a fact, not a necessity. The first claim explains why the natural attitude is untroubled by illusion; the second prepares its critique. "Truth on the march" states Husserl's non-sceptical fallibilism: relative truth is still truth about the one world.

From the harmonious course of self-correction arises the idea of a definitively true world, the same world as it would be experienced in a final, ideally attainable harmony needing no further correction. Herbart's saying, "so much semblance, so much indication of being", expresses the factual structure of universal experience. Semblance does not have the last word; continuous experience brings out a relative truth that cannot claim finality, since further correction stays open, but can be placed in a graded series and count as an ever better approximation of a final truth that is itself unreachable. In deliberate, experimenting, systematically recorded experience, we think, such a progress of approximation could be fruitfully realized. Final truth, the world as definitively true, is an idea: unthinkable as something adequately perceivable, yet not a fiction or arbitrary ideal but an ideal motivated by the universal form of experience and, as long as that form holds, necessarily posited: a pole to be seen, never saturated by belief, to which all relative empirical truths validly refer. This analysis illuminates how the world is constantly given as actually existing yet necessarily imperfectly, under the idea of always possible perfection and correction. The world-in-itself is reinterpreted as the correlate of an idea of completed experience (the doctrine of Ideen I § 143), not a hidden reality behind appearances. Its necessity is conditional: "as long as that form holds". This conditional is the lever of the next paragraph.

Is this harmonious unity of universal world-perception an apodictic necessity? Is it not a constantly open possibility that this unity, itself only an empirical presumption, dissolves, that experience no longer runs in the form of continued and mutually fitting corrections? Even if we were apodictically certain that it has run so far, that it must run so is not apodictically certain. It is conceivable that a continuity of fitting appearances dissolves into a mere "swarm" of appearances, as Kant put it. Then single perceptions would lose their anticipating belief and their whole structure of anticipation; their pre-meanings and co-meanings, which in normal perception have the character of expectations, would lose their hold and certainty. If expectations were disappointed again and again, nothing would be expected; anticipation would cease. That would end external perception: the course of perception would lose its character as perception of things. Neither things nor world would be there for the experiencer, neither as actually experienced nor as available to freely activated perception. At best there would remain a memory of a world that once appeared in harmonious experience and was believed with certainty, but whose continued validity had lost every experiential motive. Without actual external perception and without readiness for perception, without the certainty "I can" look, listen, search, and find spatial things, the old world-belief would be uprooted. Every representation of a world would be an arbitrary fiction, each equal to every other. The editor points to Beilage X on the possibility of the actuality of the world. This is the famous thought-experiment of the "annihilation of the world" of Ideen I § 49, now argued in full. Kant's "swarm" (Critique of Pure Reason A 111) marks the continuity: without synthetic unity there would be no object. But Husserl's claim is weaker and more precise than Kant's. He does not deduce the necessity of unity; he shows its contingency, and he describes what would remain: a stream of experiences still running, with its "I can" frustrated. The persistence of that stream in the thought-experiment is what the reduction will isolate.

Has it not been shown that the presumptive character of external experience, from which the positing of the world draws its original right, leaves open for the knower the constant possibility that the world, however it is now given as experienced, does not exist at all? If I, the experiencer, can bring to evident clarity the possibility that what I experience does not exist, notwithstanding and during the experience, there can be no talk of apodictic necessity of its being. We cannot separate the being-so of the world from its being and save at least its existence as absolutely indubitable. Apodictic knowledge excludes the possibility of the non-being of what is known, and where nothing of the being-so of external experience can be adequately given, no room is left for an apodicticity of mere existence. The editor refers to Beilage XI on the possibility of the non-existence of the world. Husserl blocks an obvious retreat: one might concede that we never know what the world is while insisting that we know that it is. The retreat fails because existence is not given apart from content. There is no bare "that" in experience, only the "that" of something appearing as so-and-so, and if the "so" is presumptive, so is the "that".

Every fact, including the fact of the world, is contingent as a fact: it could be otherwise or not be. Whether this holds for every fact is not at issue. For the world a different contingency is in question. While I perceive the world, however perfectly, and it is conscious to me in unbroken certainty as self-given, as something whose existence I cannot doubt, it has a constant cognitive contingency: its bodily self-givenness never excludes its non-being in principle. A double indubitability must be distinguished. Where I perceive in unbroken harmony I believe, and cannot arbitrarily turn belief into disbelief; while nothing speaks against the experienced existent I do not doubt, and I cannot doubt, since doubt essentially involves "something speaks against it". Apodictic indubitability means far more: where I see as I see, and holding fast that I see thus, I cannot even think the possibility that what is seen does not exist or is otherwise, or that it will later turn out not to exist. I can think such a possibility only by thinking that I do not see, or not in this apodictic way. With the seeing of external experience it is different: it is evidently compatible with it that what is perceived does not exist. That is the irremovable contingency of the proposition "the world is". The distinction between empirical and apodictic indubitability is the conceptual centre of Section Two. Empirical indubitability is psychological and motivational: doubt needs a counter-motive, and there is none. Apodictic indubitability is eidetic: the contrary is unthinkable while the seeing holds. Husserl can therefore say, without contradiction, that the world is completely beyond doubt and that its non-existence is possible. The contingency at issue is not the metaphysical contingency of creation (that the world might not have existed) but the "cognitive" contingency of its givenness, which is why it is compatible with perfect certainty.

#### Lecture 34: Transcendental and Empirical Illusion. On the "Objection from Madness"

We established an irremovable cognitive contingency of the world, bound essentially to the structure of world-perception without which a world could not be there for us or be knowable in any other way. Husserl proposes to examine the character of this contingency, or of the contingent structure of world-experience responsible for it. The rider "or knowable in any other way" matters: no science could escape the contingency, since all knowledge of the world goes through experience.

At issue is not only the structure of single perceptions and of the unifying total perception at each moment but also the universal structure of the course of perception. Every thing-perception is anticipation throughout, and so is universal world-perception: at each moment it consciously carries the anticipation of a course that will be harmonious or brought to harmony by correction. The perceiver constantly expects confirmation of his forward-directed expectations and knows that he can freely direct perception in many other dimensions and turn other co-meanings into intuition. He knows that his anticipations may fail and that correction is practically possible, restoring harmony. The correlate is that he looks into an ever-existing, identical world, bodily there in unbroken total belief, a world not only present now but becoming from the past into an open future. Because of this structure the world carries a double sense of being and truth. Just as all experience admits correction, and every correction further correction in infinitum, the world actually perceived at any time is a "mere world of appearance", the appearance of a world in itself. This is the plain, not metaphysical or mystical sense of the correlation between world of appearance and world in itself, read off directly from the sense-structure of mundane perception. "In itself" means an ideal of approximation in the openly possible sequence of corrections, which a freely experiencing subject can approach but never reach. The distinction between appearance and thing-in-itself is naturalized into the structure of experience: "in itself" is the index of possible correction, not a noumenon behind the senses. Husserl also brings freedom into the structure: the horizon is a field of possible self-directed perceptions (the "I can" of kinaesthesis), so the world's givenness depends on the perceiver's capacities, a theme of Ideen II §§ 18 and 59.

This whole structure has, relatively speaking, an absolute necessity. As long as perceptual life runs in this style, the perceiver lives consciously toward a world constantly there, one and identical, unaffected by frequent illusions and corrections. Conversely, if the perceiver is to remain in the belief in one existing world, if a world is to remain experienced and experienceable for him at all, the style must last to infinity. A world cannot be without becoming into a horizon of infinite future and being experienceable for subjects in that infinity. The correlate of the true being of the world I now perceive is the never-breaking harmonious structure of my perceptual course for all future. The necessity runs in both directions: harmony of experience is necessary and sufficient for the being of the world for me. This is the strongest form of the correlation thesis in the course: the true being of the world is correlated not with a finite experience but with an infinite, harmonious one, and "being" becomes inseparable from "experienceability ad infinitum". Lecture 54 will build transcendental idealism on this equivalence.

However much we see this correlation and the essential necessity of the structure as necessary, necessary on the condition that a truly existing world is to be and be experienceable for us, the necessity is relative. Absolutely speaking, the universal structure of mundane perception is contingent, a mere fact that could be otherwise. The distinction is between eidetic necessity of a conditional (if world, then harmonious experience) and the factual status of the antecedent. Eidetics gives the form; it cannot guarantee that the form is instantiated.

Now, however, a world is bodily given, and we live, and as memory tells us have lived, in a harmony that kept or restored the concordance of all perceptions. This past and continuing style necessarily motivates the anticipating belief that it will stay so. So we believe, and must believe; this belief belongs to the structure of streaming world-perception. Yet it is empirical belief, mere anticipation, leaving open that it will not be confirmed and that the structure of consistent correction dissolves. So while we live in harmonious perceptual belief, we can see the possibility that this bodily perceived world is mere semblance, and not, as it self-evidently is, mere appearance of a true world contained in it as idea. This is a semblance of a special kind, transcendental semblance, distinct from every empirical semblance. Standing on the ground of empirical world-certainty, we meet empirical semblance here and there and can ask for its explanation, for the hidden truth that always underlies it; that this question has a sense and can be decided is certain in advance. Transcendental semblance is different. That the world given now in person might in truth not exist is a constantly open possibility, but it belongs to this semblance that seeking a corrective truth, a true being to set in place of the non-existing world, would be senseless. It would be like asking what real thing exists in place of the water nymph I have just freely imagined, how this nothing could be corrected into a something that falsely appears as a nymph. The distinction recalls Kant's transcendental illusion but inverts it. For Kant transcendental illusion arises when reason oversteps experience; for Husserl it would be experience itself as a whole losing its object. Empirical semblance presupposes the world (the stump replaces the man); transcendental semblance would leave nothing to replace it with. Appearance, by contrast, is the normal mode of the world's givenness. The three terms (appearance, empirical semblance, transcendental semblance) should not be conflated.

No perverse sense should be read into this, as if the world "perhaps" does not exist in the ordinary sense of "quite possibly" or even "presumably", or as if we should be prepared for the world to end as a clear sky can cloud over. On the contrary: the existence of the world is completely beyond doubt, and this indubitability lies in the world-perception in which we constantly live. Whoever, confused by sceptical arguments, judges that the world does not truly exist, or that one must constantly be prepared for this, follows theoretical, perhaps verbal, arguments and does not look at the sense of world-experience and the unbreakable world-belief in it, a belief that tolerates beside it not the faintest conjecture or real possibility of being otherwise. Nothing speaks against the world's existence and everything for it. What matters is that this perfect empirical certainty, being empirical, leaves open the possibility that the world does not exist; the possibility can be seen, though absolutely nothing speaks for its realization. With our empirically indubitable certainty of the world's existence, the proposition that this world is a pure nothing, a mere transcendental semblance, is compatible. So the hypothetical positing of the non-existence of the world I experience with indubitable certainty is not an arbitrary hypothesis like "1 is more than 2" or "a square is round". Any absurd proposition can be turned into a hypothesis, but then it is an absurd one. Here the positing is evidently possible, seen to be free of absurdity, and that it concerns the universe I experience, while it is given in person and indubitably, is of great interest for the beginning philosopher. This is the principal misreading-guard of the whole section, written by Husserl himself. Three modalities are separated: real possibility (something speaks for it: none), empirical doubt (something speaks against the world: nothing), and pure conceivability without absurdity (granted). Only the last is claimed. Its philosophical weight is that it distinguishes the world's existence from every necessary truth: the non-existence of the world is consistent in a way the non-existence of a mathematical truth is not. The sceptic's mistake is to read this pure possibility as a real one.

This cannot yet be counted as a secure result, since an obvious objection remains. Surely it is a contingent fact that a subject's perceptions run in the universal harmony that makes consciousness of a single true world possible, and it is possible that someone's harmonious stream turns into a senseless jumble. But what does that mean except that a person, and finally everyone, could go mad? The possibility of madness says nothing about the possible non-existence of the world. On the contrary, it shows that we must hold to the world's own absolutely necessary being, since the possibility of a madman presupposes the existence of the world. The objection reinterprets the dissolution of experience as a psychological event in a human being, and so places the whole thought-experiment inside the world. It is a serious objection because it is the natural attitude's standard response, and the lecture turns it into a lesson in method.

This is not a very strong counter-argument, but criticizing it will clarify matters. Husserl does not refute the objection by force; he uses it to expose a presupposition in his own mode of presentation.

First, the objection motivates correcting our natural but inadmissible way of speaking in the communicative plural. I, the meditator, must say: before all science, which I have put out of validity, the world is given to me from original experience, from my external perception. Grasping things and the world in person is nothing but performing external perception. If I carry out for myself, on my own world-perception, which belongs to my own inner life, the series of meditations that occupied us jointly in the natural "we" attitude, I find that I cannot grant apodicticity to the existence of the world I experience, and I see the possibility that my perceptual course could become disharmonious in such a way that the world previously experienced in me dissolves into nothing. The plural smuggled in other people as co-perceivers whose harmonious experience could guarantee the world if mine failed. The first-person restatement removes that guarantee. It is a methodological, not a metaphysical solipsism, and the next lecture names it so.

What of other experiencing subjects, whose experiences might proceed normally regardless of the disharmony of mine? I am given human beings originally only on the basis of certain of my external perceptions: some things in the thing-world I harmoniously experience are distinguished as bodies (Leiber) in which "psychic life", sensing, representing, feeling, willing, is embodied. I first find myself as a human being by experiencing this embodiment most originally in the body I call mine. If I then find things of the same spatial type, behaving in the same typical way as my body, I experience them through empathy as expressions of other subjects, and so experience other people like me. It is therefore presupposed that my experience runs as harmonious spatial experience in which a spatial world is there for me. If, as I evidently see, my stream of experience could change so that it loses its self-confirming belief and sense, so that there can be no talk of existing or experienceable things, then there is no talk of my body, of animals and human beings, nor of people whose harmonious experience I could appeal to. I can appeal only to others who are experienceable within my open but still valid experience, to be found in space, the form of freely accessible horizons, by setting perceptual series going. If I have no horizons of belief but only fictions of worlds with fictional horizons, "world" names infinitely many empty possibilities of phantasy, and others are such empty possibilities, each equal to every other and all equal to nothing. Note the kind of emptiness. That people live on Sirius is, for me with an existing spatial world, an "empty" possibility in that nothing in my experience speaks for it; but not completely empty, since I can take paths of experience that would decide the matter. No such paths, no "I can" look and decide, lead into the realm of absolute fictions that are not drawn into world-space and do not populate its horizons of real possibility. The argument is that other subjects are given to me only through my experience of their bodies, founded on my experience of spatial things; so their existence cannot be more secure than my world-experience. The distinction between real and pure possibility, sharpened by the Sirius example, explains why an appeal to others fails: others are real possibilities only within a world whose horizons my experience keeps open. The sketch of empathy here anticipates Lecture 35 and the Fifth Cartesian Meditation.

So if as beginning philosopher I am to carry out a universal critique of mundane experience, I may not do so in the communicative attitude, in which I presuppose the actual or really possible existence of other people. That would presuppose what is itself in question and to be criticized. More precisely: as other people are there for me through my experience, so their experiences are there for me only as experienced in mine, but hidden, experienced in the mode of co-meaning. My perception of others is directly only perception of their physical bodies; their co-meant psychic life, and specifically their perceiving, can never become mine as my own perception. For me the other psychic subjects and their perceptions are necessarily only co-meant mediately in my perceptions, expression understood on the body seen before me. The point is structural. Another's experience is given to me in the mode of appresentation, never presentation, and would lose its otherness if it were presented. So the harmony of another's experience is available to me only as an interpretation of my own experience, and it cannot serve as independent evidence for the world.

The universal critique of my experience is therefore the universal critique of experience in general that falls to me and could ever fall to me. If I have reason to criticize another's experience as experience, this can make sense only as a critique entering into the mediations of my experience, a critique of experiences co-experienced in certain of mine on such-and-such foundations. The editor refers to Beilage XII on the problem of objective world-science and communicative intersubjectivity. The universality of the critique is secured by its being first-personal: my experience is the only access I have to any experience, so criticizing it exhausts the field.

Having learned to avoid a specifically epistemological circle that contradicts the sense of the critique, I now try to appropriate more adequately what I previously considered, willed, and achieved. The circle is to use the world (in the form of other people's experience) to vouch for the world. The next chapter returns to the analysis in strictly first-person form.

### Chapter Two: Supplements and Clarifications following the "Objection from Madness"

#### Lecture 35: On the Doctrine of "Empathy"

Husserl recapitulates. The last lecture considered an objection to the result that the experienced world might not exist even while it is experienced with empirical certainty, a result reached by showing that the harmonious style which unites the infinite web of anticipations into one total perception could dissolve. The objection said that this possibility means only that the subjects concerned have gone mad, which does not exclude that others remain sane. It served to show that the self-reflection of the beginning philosopher must be carried out not in the natural communicative attitude but, so to speak, in the solipsistic one. What this demands was being developed. So each of us now shifts to speaking as "I". The instruction "each of us" is deliberate: the solipsism is a method that every listener is asked to perform for himself, not a doctrine about one privileged mind.

I who meditate on the evidence of the world's existence as a possible beginning, naively aware that I reflect among like-minded companions, even jointly, find that I have nothing to think or say in the communicative plural. I must note that this whole "we" belongs to the experienced content of my mundane total perception. The universe in the full sense, including all people, is given to me originally, before all science, from my universal perception, the unifying manifold of particular perceptions that makes up my perceiving life. The "we" is not denied; it is relocated as a content of my experience.

In truth, even when I spoke communicatively, I carried out all my reflections on my own actual external perceptions, especially spatial, physical ones; I drew my companions in only inessentially, assuming each would find the same. Now I must remove every trace of naivety and consider how my companions, and people generally, are perceptually given to me, since I can know of them only through experiences in which they would be originally given. Examining the experiences that give me people, and already animals, and how they fit into full mundane experience, yields a confirmation and a supplement. It supplements, since I had ignored this distinctive stock of experience; it confirms, since the result reached on thing-perception will hold in the extension. It also purifies the result: the necessary reduction to the universal stream of my own world-perception, in which everything that can be originally given to me is given, and hence everything of which I can later gain mediate knowledge. The word "reduction" appears here for the first time in the section, in a narrow sense: a reduction to my own perceptual stream as the source of all original givenness, a precursor of the "sphere of ownness" of the Fifth Cartesian Meditation. Note the order: the result is established on physical things first and then extended to others, because experience of others is founded on experience of things.

Animals and people are there for me only because among my perceptions some give me not mere things but bodies (Leiber): in my phenomenal spatial surroundings I find things distinguished as bodies, in which psychic life, sensing, perceiving, thinking, feeling, willing, is expressed or embodied. They have this distinction for me because I experience them as bodies, not as mere things, in a mode of experience that confirms itself harmoniously. One body is strangely privileged, and so one animal being, one human being: it is my body, and so I am distinguished for myself above all objects of experience as I in the ordinary empirical sense, this human being to whom this body belongs. My body is the only one in which I experience the embodiment of a psychic life, my own, in an absolutely immediate way: I perceive at once the thing-body and its behaviour, my psychic life, and the unity of both, the embodying of the latter in the former. Every movement of my hands or legs that I perceive is at once a thing-like, mechanical movement, as far as mechanics speaks of such things, and, seen from within, a subjective "I move" that animates the mechanical being-moved. Step by step the appearing body and its changing modes of appearance give themselves as bearing this or that psychic content, as an exterior that has in itself originally the interiority expressed in it. Both are given undivided, in coincidence; so in one experiencing look the hand, and in its movement the two-sided psychophysical movement, the specifically bodily movement. This is the analysis of the lived body in Ideen II §§ 36–42 compressed: the body is experienced from within and from without in coincidence, and "I move" (kinaesthesis) is the inner side of a physical motion. The analysis matters here because it shows where the psychic is originally given: only in my own body.

This two-layered givenness, with exterior, interior, and their union all originally perceived, holds only for my body. How are other bodies, and so animals and other people, experienced within my world-perception? My body plays the role of the primal body from which the experience of all others derives, and I am constantly, for myself and my experience, the primal human being from whom the experience of all others derives its sense and possibility of perception. Only because my body is always there as body in its original psychophysical, two-layered givenness can other bodies be there as bodies for me and count in a way as perceived. Only as far as things around me resemble my body and what gives its physical behaviour the rank of animated expression can and must they be apprehended as bodies. I say this not on the basis of objective psychological theories, of which I may know nothing here, but from reflecting on my perception itself and its structure as perception of my own and others' bodies. Perception of another's body is perception, since I grasp its existence as immediately there itself, and the other person is there for me perceptually. I express the immediacy most sharply by saying that a person stands here before me in person. No inference leads to positing the other's body and the fellow human; that would mean he is only "there" in a wider sense, findable, experienceable. No: he is really experienced, standing at his place, and it is inconceivable that I could experience him more immediately. So I rightly say that I perceive him in person. Two theses coexist: other bodies are apprehended by analogy with mine ("primal body", "primal human being"), and the apprehension is perception, not inference. Husserl rejects the argument from analogy (Mill, Lipps's critics) while keeping the analogical founding: the similarity works as an apperceptive transfer of sense, not as a premise. This is the position of the Fifth Cartesian Meditation (§§ 50–51), where the same transfer is called "pairing".

Yet the sense of this perception contains a mediacy that essentially distinguishes it from perception of my body. There both the thing-body and the psychic life embodied in it are originally perceived, since the psychic life is mine. In the other's case the physical body is perceived as originally as mine, but not the psychic life embodied in it: that is not given itself but only appresentatively co-meant. There is a resemblance to the anticipation contained in every external perception, such as the unseen back of a thing, but the analogy is incomplete: this is indication, not an anticipation that could become a grasp of the thing itself. The indicating intention neither demands nor permits a redeeming perception, as the anticipating moments of thing-perception do. Perception of another's body is, by its essence, perception through original interpretation. The originality lies in its inseparable relation back to my own primal bodiliness, in which I have the primal experience of the incorporation of the subjective in something appearing as a thing. Perceiving that thing there, which resembles my body in all its behaviour, I cannot help apprehending it as embodying the subjective in the indicated way (a moving of the hand, a shaking of the head, sensing by touch), and so as a body to which a full, more or less indeterminate subjectivity belongs, one that must live itself as "I am" but that I am not. This combination of spatial seeing and original interpretive seeing-as, understanding of expression, is a basic form of experience of its own, distinct from simple external perception and from the already founded perception of one's own body, and still properly called perception. Husserl adds in a note that this experience is nowadays, not very aptly, usually called "empathy". It has its own modes of confirmation: interpretive perception is confirmed again through interpretation, not to be pursued here. It is a secondary form of perception, since it presupposes perception of one's own body and constantly relies on it. So I participate in perceiving the other in a special way: understanding him, I am as it were in coincidence with what I understand, the more vividly the more clearly I approach his interiority. "Appresentation" is the exact term: co-presentation of what can never be presented, unlike the back of a thing, which can be brought round. This asymmetry is what gives the other his otherness and at the same time makes his experience unusable as independent testimony for the world, which was the point of the objection from madness. The note on "empathy" records Husserl's dissatisfaction with Lipps's term, which he continues to use for want of a better. The final remark on "coincidence" anticipates the later analysis of empathy as a quasi-presentification in which I live in the other's perspective without becoming him.

#### Lecture 36: Transcendental Solipsism. The Negative Result of the Critique of Mundane Experience

Surveying mundane experience after these supplements, I find running through it a layer of spatial experience that bears all other mundane experience; every possible experience of people presupposes it. If I follow its stream purely, looking at its harmonies, discords, and corrections, and see the possibility of its turning into a mere "swarm" of appearances in which all unity of belief dissolves, so that there could be no talk of an empirically indubitable world with existing things, it follows necessarily that there could be no talk of existing animals and people. So there is the evident possibility not only that the spatial world, pure physical nature, does not exist while I perceive it with empirical certainty, but also, as a consequence, that no people exist with the psychic life expressed for me in their bodies. This possibility of the absolute non-existence of the world of my experience must not be confused with any real possibility grounded on the world posited as existing. A real possibility, such as that a masked procession is now moving along the Kaiserstraße, may be an otherwise empty conceivability, but it stands on the general ground of experience and can be decided within experience as an objective falsehood. Here the possibility is that experience as a whole loses all harmony and so could not be a valid ground for any positing, even of real possibilities. Real existence and possibility of other subjects may therefore be used in a critique of experience only as long as the critique is ordinary empirical critique, historical criticism or the assessment of witnesses; the possibility of people going mad is such a real possibility, presupposing the world. But if the world itself is universally in question, and the experience that gives and verifies it is to be criticized universally, the critique cannot presuppose the existence, or even possible co-existence, of others; that would presuppose what is implicitly part of the theme. If I adopt the evidently possible hypothesis that my experience turns into a senseless swarm, there are for me, as subject of this swarm, no possible others, mad or sane, to appeal to. To think myself into this hypothesis and still hold people really possible is inconsistent, since the swarm excludes every motive for belief in real possibilities. If the other people are only empty fictions thought along with it, their harmonious or disharmonious experiences are fictions too, and mean nothing. The masked procession on the Kaiserstraße (Freiburg's main street) is the counterpart of the people on Sirius: a real possibility, decidable within experience. The layering argument is now complete: spatial experience founds bodily experience, which founds the experience of others. The objection from madness is thus disqualified not as false but as a category mistake: it answers a transcendental question with a real possibility.

A universal critique of experience, as it falls to me as beginning philosopher, can therefore only be solipsistic in a good sense: a critique of my experiences that knows other subjects and their experiences only as experienced in mine, and does not presuppose them as existing, since they are in question. If I later have reason to criticize others' experience philosophically, this makes sense only after I have criticized the mediacies of my universal experience. Since others' experience is there for me only as indicating itself through interpretive mediacy on what I experience directly, the primal ground of all critique must be the investigation of these mediacies as experienced and traceable in my own consciousness. The long discussion of the madness objection has also alerted us once and for all to the specifically epistemological circle: using in a particular argument, unnoticed, what the generality of the critical theme puts in question. "Solipsistic in a good sense" is the formula of methodological solipsism. It concerns the order of justification, not the population of the universe, and it is provisional: Lecture 53 will name the result "transcendental egology" and pass beyond it to the intersubjective reduction. The circle named here, using a special case of what is universally in question, is the recurring charge Husserl levels at all naturalistic epistemology.

With this meditation concluded, I can use its corrected and purified result. The world is there for me beyond doubt, thanks to my harmonious perception. It extends through an endless past, thanks to my endless series of awakenable memories of my former perceptions, and into an open endless future, as far as I foresee the stream continuing in the same harmonious style. Each phase of this certainty includes the certainty that what is actually perceived has manifold horizons of perceivability, that every actual perceiving has around it possible perceivings, a region of free access through perceptual series I can set going. Of this too I have certainty in general, reaching beyond all possible particular disappointments of this "being able": things will always somehow fit together and let the unity of this, my world, hold firm. None of this alters the fact, shown by closer examination, that world-belief, with its whole objective content, the sense that shapes and redetermines itself in experience, is through and through presumptive. It leaves open that the harmonious style now running leads to a senseless swarm, despite all indubitability; and also, not to be overlooked, that despite past and present harmony, the horizons of perceivability that never became perceived lack the fulfillability constantly presumed. Correlatively the world need not be, need never have been, and need not continue to be even if it was and is. This world, which I experience as present in continuously self-confirming perceptual belief, and as past in indubitable empirical memory, need be no more than transcendental semblance. That it is so is an absolutely empty possibility, against which all experience speaks with the full force of its harmony and for which nothing at all speaks. I cannot believe that it, or any of the infinitely many lawless particular possibilities it covers, is the truth, since I live in the force of world-experience and cannot annihilate it at will. So I can say this possibility is beyond doubt a nullity, considered as to its actuality. Yet it is a possibility: there could in truth be nothing real, no world ever, now or in the past, while I experience this one with certainty. The final statement of the negative result adds a second variant: not only the future collapse of harmony but the non-fulfilment of past horizons, which extends the contingency backward to the world that "was". Husserl carefully sets the possibility's nullity "as to actuality" against its standing as possibility, which is exactly the double status required for the epoché: it gives no motive to disbelieve, only a reason not to use.

Relating this to the question of the right beginning: after the overthrow of the sciences, the beginning philosopher was offered "I am" and "the world is", the second seemingly preferable since it contains the first. The first methodological meditation laid down that absolutely justified knowledge must be adequate and hence apodictic: every negation and doubt is excluded, and the exclusion itself adequately graspable; correlatively, the possibility of the non-being of what is adequately given is excluded in absolute evidence by its mode of givenness. The recapitulation restates the criterion in its reflexive form: apodicticity requires not only that doubt be absent but that its exclusion be seen.

The world's existence is given originally in an experience that is inadequate through and through and cannot in principle be made adequate, in essence or existence, and so leaves the world's non-being always open. The proposition "the world exists", and the universe of particular experiential propositions, is therefore judged. The existence of the world and everything in it must be included in the universal overthrow. The juridical "judged" (gerichtet) marks a verdict of the critique. The overthrow, which Lecture 30 applied to the sciences, now reaches the world, and with it the ego as a human being in the world, since "the world exists" included "I am" in that sense.

How world-experience and world-sciences can later be restored to philosophical validity I do not know now. I know only that it would be forever impossible, and that empirically founded knowledge could never be justified, if it were required to satisfy the norm of apodicticity in its plain sense as empirical certainty; that would be an evidently absurd demand. This may worry me and raise the question whether I shall have to modify my radical demand for apodictic justification, or whether experience might, in a modified sense, prove an apodictically justifiable source, so that fully justified empirical sciences might draw their true right from apodictically evident principles, and only from them. As a beginner I cannot yet tackle such questions. I am still seeking the Archimedean point on which I can rely absolutely, the ground for a first, absolute work. I hold to the radical demand and follow the thoughts that the negative result and the course of the critique awakened in me. The editor refers to Beilage XIII on how far apodicticity can be demanded for knowledge of beings at all. Husserl concedes openly that the principle adopted in Lecture 31 cannot justify experience on its own terms and that either the principle or the notion of experience will need revising. The second alternative, apodictic principles of experience, points to the eidetic laws of the life-world and of transcendental experience (the "apodictic structure" of experience in the Cartesian Meditations). He defers the question but does not hide it.

### Chapter Three: Opening a Field of Transcendental Experience. Transcendental, Phenomenological, and Apodictic Reduction

#### Lecture 37: The Apodictic Certainty of the World's Possible Non-Existence and the Setting-Off of the Transcendental Life of Subjectivity

If I make the evidently possible supposition that the universe, the whole infinite space with everything in it, does not exist despite harmoniously flowing world-experience, I can ask: what would remain, unaffected and perhaps apodictically existing? But does not "the universe" mean "the totality of what is"? Is my question not absurdly: what if there were nothing at all? On closer consideration my critical insight concerns not the totality of beings in the widest sense but the beings of mundane experience, objective beings. The experience itself, and I the experiencer with my life, to whose concrete stream mundane experiencing belongs, I have constantly posited and presupposed as existing. What I considered were forms of my actual experiential life and their evidently conceivable variations, including those in which there would be for me no mundane objects and no mundus. I did not presuppose my existence and that of my experiences as a hypothesis; while questioning the experienced world I looked at my lived life, my flowing perceiving, remembering, anticipating, and took it as I found and perceived it, in its lived selfhood. Did I commit an epistemological circle? Throughout the meditation I performed self-knowledge regarding my mundane experiencing: how I find it in my life, describe its style, consider its variations. How could I, since I belong to the world, and the apodictic critique of world-knowledge should therefore have included that of self-knowledge? The turn of the whole course happens here. The thought-experiment of Lecture 33 needed a subject in whom experience dissolved; that subject was never put in question. The worry about a circle is the right one to raise, since the madness objection was dismissed for exactly that fault.

The situation is remarkable. On one hand, I undeniably presupposed myself and my experiencing life in the critique. During the meditation it was the focus of attention, perceptually there and accepted, without my thinking of subjecting this being-there-for-me to apodictic critique, that is, my awareness of myself and of my mundane experiencing, the constant looking at it, considering it, reflecting on it, the whole course of self-knowledge by which it was there and thematic for me. There was a certain naivety in this: I carried out a universal apodictic critique of mundane experience, and did not in turn criticize the self-experience, the experience of my mundane experience, nor the reflection on it in which the critique consisted. Husserl concedes naivety, not circularity. The self-experience that performed the critique was itself uncriticized, which is a gap, not a contradiction; filling it is the task of the "apodictic reduction" named in the chapter title and taken up again in Lecture 53.

Such a critique, above all of self-awareness and self-perception, which gives mundane critique its ground, may also be needed, and I already suspect it will be very important. But the question now is whether this omission really involved an epistemological circle. Is it not rather clear that two meanings of "I", and hence of "my psychic life", "self-experience", and "self-knowledge", begin to separate? The answer to the circle charge is a distinction, not a defence. If the "I" presupposed in the critique is not the "I" included in the world, no circle arises. The two meanings of "I" are the empirical and the transcendental ego, and the next paragraph draws the line.

"I" in ordinary speech means the human ego. Taken concretely I am an animated body, a psychophysical reality belonging to the world, the totality of realities; I am one object of my mundane experience among others. Must I not distinguish from this the ego that is the subject of that experience, the subject-ego for the object-ego? Living through continuous world-experience, I find the manifold unified world and so am, as the subject who finds everything, the subject for all objects, for the universe. Ordered within it I also find myself as object: this human ego with its psychic life, belonging psychophysically to the body I call mine and embodied in it objectively; and this whole psychophysical reality is what it is in world-space, woven into the chains of causality that bind all worldly realities. The distinction is between the ego as the correlate of the world as a whole and the ego as an item in it. It is the "paradox of human subjectivity" of the Crisis (§§ 53–54): being a subject for the world and an object in it.

This is not an accidental equivocation. Passing from the subject-ego to the object-ego and back, I must recognize with apodictic evidence that I, the subject of experience, am identical with the ego objectified in the human being. Going from objective self-experience of myself as this embodied human ego to reflection on myself as the subject performing it, and back, I must at once incorporate everything found in subject-reflection into myself as the objectively experienced human being and identify subject-ego and human ego. I, the subject-ego, am the same who belongs psychophysically to this body and so to the world; and I the human being perform this reflection and disclose my hidden interiority through it. The mundane experiencing in which I as human being am an object is not visible in the objective content while I am so directed; it comes into my grasp only through reflection. Yet it is evidently mine, this human being's, as the reflection is again mine when I grasp it in higher-level reflection. Husserl takes the objection to the distinction seriously: in the natural attitude there is a constant, apodictic self-identification, by which whatever reflection discloses is at once assigned to the human being. This "incorporating" apperception is exactly what the reduction will suspend; the next paragraphs will not deny the identity but show it to be the product of an apperception.

Despite this, and whatever difficulties closer study may find, I cannot escape the distinction. Criticizing world-experience by looking at it and at the variations I produce in free phantasy, I really gain the apodictic insight that the world actually experienced need not exist. Even if I naively took my perceiving as actually running, it is apodictically certain, at least in hypothetical and general form, that if mundane perceiving runs in the normal style, what is perceived as world need not exist. Suppose the world did not exist, and so not my body nor I as human being: not nothing would remain. All the presupposed world-perceiving would be, and I as its subject and subject of the whole concrete psychic life in which it runs would remain what I am, with all this life. I would remain unaffected in my being by any nullity of the world, never to be annihilated by an epistemological annihilation of my body and the universe. I must not say that I would remain if I were removed from the world or detached from my body; that sounds as if an angel of death lifted me out of an existing, persisting world as a pure soul. It would be better to say, though religious notions are inadmissible for the beginner since they fall under the overthrow, that if the created world, the objective world of my experience, were annihilated, I, the pure ego of its experience, and this experiencing itself would not be. And more: had it pleased God, if this is conceivable, to create instead of the real world only a transcendental world of semblance, given to me as indubitable reality yet a nothing, I would remain exactly who I am in my pure selfhood. I, by transcendental semblance this human being, would in truth have no body; and if I lost even the semblance-body, I would still be the subject of experience now turned into a senseless swarm. The editor refers to Beilage XIV on nature and the nature-experiencing ego. The residue is not a soul surviving the world; Husserl explicitly rejects the image of a disembodied soul departing, since that would be an event within a persisting world. The theological variants are used as illustrations while being declared out of bounds. The phrase "not nothing would remain" answers the absurd question raised at the opening of this lecture: the totality of beings is not the totality of worldly beings. This is the famous residuum argument of Ideen I § 49 ("consciousness as the residuum of world-annihilation"), now carefully stripped of any suggestion that the ego is a separable substance.

So in the purely philosophical meditation I must distinguish my human existence, given originally in mundane self-experience, from my transcendental being, given originally in transcendental self-experience, the self-perception of pure reflection. In mundane self-experience I am a soul with a psychic ego, really belonging and psychophysically bound to the sensibly experienced body. If there is no body, if it is transcendental semblance, there is no psychophysical causality that could weave the ego and its life into physical events and give them the objective real character of soul and psychic life. The ego as psychic, or as soul, would vanish with the world, and so would the souls of others and they themselves, as long as "soul" keeps its natural sense, which refers to an animated body that functions psychically and embodies subjectivity. The soul is by definition a worldly reality, correlated with a body. That is the ground of the fourth misreading-guard: the transcendental ego is not a soul, and the reduction does not isolate a psychic interior.

On the other hand, the cognitive contingency of the world and all that follows from it do not affect my ego and my ego-life in their purity. Positing this universe as null lets my empirical soul vanish, but not the purely psychic, purely egoic element, which no longer has real co-existence as animating and no longer draws its sense of existence from mundane perception, but has its existence for me from a pure self-experience whose validity is not touched by whether, or in what way, mundane experience is valid. When I put all mundane experience out of play as to its objective validity, as I do when I stand on the supposition that the world does not exist and see the possibility apodictically, the self-experience in which this mundane experience is there for me as my experiencing is not released from validity. And while mundane experience is out of validity universally, I have no possibility, in passing from reflection to straightforward looking, of placing my subjectivity in a body or ordering it into the world, since there is nothing mundane any more. My ego and its experiencing life, by contrast, are anything but out of validity: they are so constantly valid that self-experience supplies the whole ground on which my reflection moves, and without it the reflection would be senseless and baseless. This will give me more to think about. The operative phrase is "out of play as to its objective validity": the world is not removed, its validity is not used. With that suspension the incorporating apperception described above loses its target, and what it had been incorporating stands out as a field of its own. The closing remark, that self-experience is the ground without which the critique would be baseless, is the germ of the insight that the reduction does not find the ego as a residue after the fact but presupposes it throughout.

#### Lecture 38: The Field of Transcendental Experience as Theme of a Transcendental Critique

The task now is to grasp, use, and extend this cognitive gain rightly. The editor refers to Beilage XV, "All being presupposes subjectivity". The single sentence opens a lecture devoted to the self-understanding of the method just performed.

Surveying the path: seeking the beginning of an apodictically grounded philosophy, the obvious first step was an apodictic critique of the most original self-evidences surviving the Cartesian overthrow, the existence of the world or of my ego. I preferred the first, which in its universality had to include the second, since by "I" I naturally understood myself as a human being; how could I have thought otherwise at that stage? The result was the exclusion of world-knowledge as mundane experience, which in principle cannot be apodictic. When I was then led back to "I am" and kept it after the critique, this was no longer the ego of ordinary speech, as if I, the human being, had escaped the epistemological end of the world. What remained was the transcendental ego, precisely because as subject of all world-knowledge I did not belong to the known world, and on excluding the world I became aware of myself in my purity. This was a very significant step and opened a promising horizon; Husserl notes in the margin that "this can be willed from the start". But retaining transcendental self-experience did not mean retaining the self as an apodictic residue of the critique of the world: nothing of that remained, nor of the validity of the experience that gives it. Rather, the being of this experience, and I as its subject, became visible through a turn of theoretical interest: a self-experience remained untouched by the mundane critique, and when I put the whole world out of validity, I myself lie constantly ready as theme of experience and knowledge. With transcendental experience and the field of transcendental being and life opened, it is opened as theme for a transcendental critique, which I must still provide if I am to stay consistent with the principle of admitting only apodictic evidences as the beginning. Two corrections of the Cartesian picture are made. The transcendental ego is not what survives doubt; it is what becomes visible when interest turns from the world to the experiencing of it. And its being-there is not yet apodictically secured: it has been opened as a field of experience, not certified. The marginal note ("this can be willed from the start") signals a possibility Husserl will exploit in Section Three: one could go to transcendental subjectivity directly, without the detour through the world's possible non-being.

For the self-understanding of the method it is important to note that the apodictic critique of mundane experience, besides its original function of deciding whether the world's self-evidence is apodictic, has acquired a second, very consequential function: making visible, through its result, the transcendental subjectivity previously hidden. Only by this means does the transcendental ego appear as subjectivity existing purely in and for itself, a sphere of being positable for itself within my experience, even if the universe does not exist or every position-taking on its existence is inhibited. Only so can it become mine, as a sphere purely separable from the world, and yet not separated in any natural sense, as if these were separately existing, or possibly separately existing, realms of being side by side. Transcendental being is completely closed in itself and yet, according to the sense of mundane experience, which is an accomplishment within the transcendental ego, experienceable as the animation of a body. The transcendental ego is purely in itself, but it performs within itself a self-objectivation, giving itself the sense-form "human soul" and "objective reality". This paragraph states the doctrine of mundanization. Separability in the sense of the epoché is not ontological separation; the ego and the world are not two regions. The human being is the transcendental ego's own self-apperception, and so the relation between the two egos is one of sense-constitution, not of two entities. This is why the identity and the distinction drawn in Lecture 37 are compatible.

How does my transcendental ego free itself from this self-concealment? How do I free myself from the apperception produced in me that, habitually reactivated, lets me appear to myself only as a human being? How do I overcome the habitually operating force of motivation that draws me into believing mundane experience and into the mundanizing apperception of myself as human? How do I rise above losing myself in the world and dressing myself in worldly clothing, and become aware of myself in transcendental purity, as the subject in whose apperceiving life "this world is there" and "I am a human being in this world" are made as subjective accomplishments? How do I see purely the subject and its life in and through which everything objectively experienced, and anything conscious at all, exists for it? The language of concealment, self-loss, and clothing is ethical and almost religious; it echoes Lecture 29 ("losing oneself to gain oneself") and anticipates Fink's Sixth Cartesian Meditation. The problem is practical: the natural attitude is a habit with a motivational force, and it reasserts itself.

The answer is clear: through the methodical putting out of validity of the universe's existence. Having suspended all world-belief in its most effective form, an evidently possible hypothetical declaration of the whole world's nullity, I see that my self-positing as a worldly reality, as a human being, has become impossible. But I also see that my self-experience is not only possible but continues in unbroken performance and validity. I as a human being would no longer be, or I can always put my humanity out of force; but I am, and my flowing life is, and in particular my mundanely experiencing life and my apprehending of myself as "human being in the world". Putting the world out of validity therefore carries with it putting out of validity the apperception that mundanizes me. It is a method for stripping off the empirical-objective clothing that I have put on myself, or rather keep putting on in a habitual apperceiving that goes unnoticed in naive life, and for bringing this very fact to knowledge: that in my ultimate reality I live an absolutely closed life of my own, a life of constant objectivating accomplishment which, by forming mundane experiences, forms within itself an objective world as its phenomenon. The world is what it is from my transcendental shaping, as appearing, valid, existing in my verifications, confirming itself as actual. The method is justified by its double effect: suspension of world-validity automatically suspends self-mundanization, while the apperceiving itself remains as a fact of transcendental life. The world becomes "phenomenon", a term that will explain the name "phenomenological reduction". The strong phrase "absolutely closed life" should be read with the account of self-objectivation above in mind: closed as a field of sense-constitution, not a sealed container.

I notice that I may take all objectivity that appears to me, and whatever else I am or could be conscious of beyond the real world, only as what appears in appearing, what proves itself in my proving, what shapes itself as phenomenon intentionally in absolute conscious accomplishment belonging to my transcendental life. The reduction is generalized from the real world to every object of consciousness, including ideal objects, which Lecture 54 will treat alongside things.

This foresight will give me enough to think about. For now I say this in clarifying the method by which, against the empirical ego, the transcendental ego becomes visible, and against empirical psychic life and empirical self-perception, transcendental life and transcendental self-grasping. The anticipation is marked as such; its justification belongs to the later lectures.

It has also become clear that we owe access to transcendental subjectivity to the method described not only in fact but because this or a related method is indispensable for discovering it. Discovering: no one needs to discover his empirical ego; every mature, waking person finds himself as a human being with a human ego and psychic life and exercises natural self-experience whenever he says "I perceive, remember, like, desire, will". Transcendental subjectivity must first be discovered, by each for himself, and first his own. He discovers it only through a method that frees him from the motivational compulsion of natural life. Mere reflection, however careful and however directed to my purely psychic interior, remains without such a method natural psychological reflection and remains mundane experience. The purely psychic is still psychic, the interiority of a still valid exteriority. As long as the world is there for me in naive validity, the purely psychic is the animation of my body, belonging to it and so existing in the world; and as long as the world is there for me, I am in it, this human being. What occasion could I have in natural life to go beyond the natural attitude? For that the validity I give to mundane experience by naively performing it must be put out of play, and so effectively that every attempt to fall back into naive performance is blocked. Only when, in the strictest sense, nothing is "there" as existing reality any longer can I grasp myself as transcendental subject, as the irreality that all reality presupposes. This is the principal guard against the psychological misreading. Introspection, even pure introspection, is mundane, because the world's validity silently frames it. "Irreality" is a technical term: the transcendental ego is not a real being in the sense of Ideen I (a being in space, time, and causality), not a fictitious one. The requirement that the suspension be "effective" enough to block relapse explains why Husserl uses the drastic device of the world's hypothetical nullity: a gentler suspension would let the habit of world-belief reassert itself.

Historically, too, transcendental subjectivity had to be discovered. The discovery appears first, in immature form that soon lost effect or went astray, in Descartes' ego cogito, presented from the start with a claim to apodictic indubitability. A pure and real exhibition of transcendental subjectivity occurs only in the phenomenological reduction familiar to every phenomenologist, which is nothing other than the method described in the last lectures. It deserves to be called the Cartesian method of transcendental reduction, since it is a clarifying working-out of the deep content hidden in Descartes' apparently trivial first Meditations, hidden also from Descartes himself. Why it is called phenomenological reduction, and transcendental subjectivity phenomenological, will be understood later. The name "Cartesian way", used by later commentators (Kern, "Die drei Wege zur transzendental-phänomenologischen Reduktion", 1962), originates here, in Husserl's own designation. The deferral of "phenomenological" points to Section Three, where the reduced world is treated as "phenomenon".

For Descartes the ego cogito presents itself at once as absolutely indubitable being, standing out against the world's possible non-being, or, as Descartes prefers, its possible doubtfulness. I have deliberately detached the method of transcendental reduction from the question of the apodictic validity of transcendental self-knowledge. I now distinguish the transcendental or phenomenological reduction from the apodictic reduction connected with it. The latter is a task first made possible by the phenomenological reduction. Before practising apodictic critique I need a field of critique, a realm of experience, and I have this realm, transcendental self-experience, only by means of the phenomenological reduction. This is the pivotal methodological distinction of Hua VIII and its main advance over Descartes and over Ideen I. The reduction opens a field; the apodictic reduction tests that field's evidence. Descartes fused the two and so treated the ego as an indubitable premise; Husserl separates them, which permits transcendental experience to be investigated before its apodicticity is settled, and makes the critique of transcendental experience a later, distinct task (Lecture 53; Cartesian Meditations § 13).

It is hardly avoidable and in any case useful to carry out part of the apodictic reduction at once, the beginning that suggests itself, together with a first closer look at the new transcendental sphere. Questions of a possible variation of the method of phenomenological reduction also arise at once, as contributing to clarifying the sense of transcendental subjectivity. Both announcements are fulfilled in Section Three: the first by the analysis of the living present, whose apodictic core is examined, the second by the "second way" to the reduction.

We begin to look more closely at transcendental subjectivity, which in our procedure has become visible only in limited stock, at a first glance. I, the naive ego experiencing the world, passed to striking out this world entirely, and retained my world-experiencing, and so myself as experiencing ego, though my body and my humanity were struck out too. This meant: I am, and am living through these world-experiences. The editor points to a critical note in Beilage II. "Limited stock" signals the next lecture's question: the reduction as performed so far reached only the present experiencing of the world, not the whole of transcendental life.

This experiencing life is, and is mine, even if nothing real were or is, whether or not there are world and people: a flowing being as ego-life, which I do not doubt in the least and have at first no reason to call into doubt or question. I experience it continuously and immediately in perception, only this perception is transcendental, performed in the attitude of universal exclusion of the world. In this attitude my empirical self-experience as human ego belongs here too, but not performed naively with its objective validity; its validity is excluded, or as phenomenologists also say, "bracketed", and it is taken as a merely subjective fact, a pulse of my ego-life. The last sentence gives the positive side of the reduction. Nothing is lost from the field: the mundane self-apperception reappears inside transcendental life as one of its acts, bracketed. "Bracketing" is introduced here as a synonym for exclusion, as in Ideen I § 31. The claim that transcendental self-perception is at first unquestioned is exactly what the apodictic reduction will now examine.

## Section Three: On the Phenomenology of the Phenomenological Reduction. Opening a Second Way to the Transcendental Reduction

### Chapter One: The Transcendental Time-Form of the Transcendental Life-Stream of Subjectivity

#### Lecture 39: The Full Content of Universal Transcendental Self-Experience: Transcendental Present, Past, and Future

The ego as experiencer of mundane objectivity is far from the whole transcendental ego or the full content of universal transcendental self-experience. Consistent abstention from all naive world-belief leads not only to grasping each perception of something objective as a transcendentally pure "I perceive". In the natural attitude I find myself not only perceiving but remembering, expecting, thinking, doubting, comparing, liking, judging, inferring, wishing, willing, acting. To all these acts, experienced mundanely as empirical ego-acts, correspond transcendental ego-acts, which are nothing but the acts I concretely perform as the ego made visible by the reduction in its independence from world-existence. They are those objective acts freed from objectivating apperception in the naive attitude; the objectivating is recognized, through abstention from mundane co-belief, as an accomplishment taking place in the subject as transcendental, and is itself taken into the transcendental content. If I grasp reflectively "I wish the fine winter weather would last", abstention from world-belief lets me see my transcendentally pure "I wish", what really goes on as my wishing, which is what it is whether or not the world exists. It is not a second wishing beside the objective one; the objective element in it is an apperception I myself perform, with an experiential belief I myself perform. I see this when I inhibit mundane belief, and I must inhibit it if I want to become aware, as phenomenological ego, of what is really present in natural objectivating and the natural "I wish". Nothing mundane counted in natural reflection may be spared, on the side of the ego or of what is wished: the positing of the fine weather as real must be inhibited too. Since natural objective apperception always comes first, I must practise the reduction at every point in each "I do or suffer this", performing the mental "bracketing" that puts objective belief out of play; so for every ego-act and every ego-experience, which we thereby gain as what it is in transcendental subjectivity, a pulse in its true and pure life. Three points are fixed. The reduction extends to all intentional acts, not only to perception. It does not produce a double of each act; the transcendental act is the same act seen without its self-mundanizing apperception, which is now itself a component of the act. And the reduction must be performed "at every point": the natural apperception comes first each time, so the reduction is not a single switch but a continuing practice. The last point prepares the account of the phenomenological attitude as a habit in Chapter Two.

Husserl adopts Descartes' convenient ego cogito as a general title for everything egoic gained by the reduction. Cogito covers not only thinking in the narrow sense but loving and hating, wishing and willing; and unless the empirical "I think" is expressly meant, it refers to transcendental ego-life, in which everything objective appears and is posited, but whose own being retains nothing of objective being. This is the usage of Ideen I § 28 and of the Cartesian Meditations. The final clause blocks the misreading of the transcendental ego as a thing among things: objectivity appears in it without being in it as a component.

Before examining the method further, we should make sure of the universality of the transcendental field in another respect: the method leads not only to present transcendental life, the ego cogito I now perform, flowing as my present "I perceive, think, wish, do" during reflection. As in the natural attitude I know of my past and future life, looking back and forward, so, practising the reduction, I know of my transcendental past and future, and from transcendental experience. Experience is first perception, but memory and, in a way, expectation are experience too, and to them we owe all knowledge of the past as past and the future as future. The extension to past and future matters for the apodictic question: if the transcendental field were only the present cogito, it would be a point; the claim here is that it is a stream with horizons, and that memory counts as transcendental experience. The qualification "in a way" for expectation is explained two paragraphs down.

An example: I remember yesterday's walk up the Schlossberg. Bracketing puts out of play not only my present perceptual body, my whole mundane perceptual present, and the town with the Schlossberg as present existence, but the whole of yesterday's walk as to everything objectively posited in it. The reduction reaches into the past: my past empirical ego, my past body, the existential content of the past perceptions through which the town, the streets I walked, and the Schlossberg I climbed were given as objective facts. A memory gives me the transcendental in two ways. First, I remember, and if I inhibit world-belief, this reflectively perceived "I remember" remains as my present experience. Second, in this present experience my past walk is presentified. I may make no judgmental use of the past event as a psychophysical event in the world; as far as my recollection is belief in this real past, the belief is put out of play. But on closer look my "I remember" implies "I perceived", and in the past action, "I willed and did". Whether the Schlossberg, my body, my walking legs, as past being, were real or transcendental semblance, the whole continuity of perceiving through which path and goal counted for me as actuality, and the striving, willing, and doing that went with it, are not cancelled by abstention from judgment on mundane being. So I have not only the present transcendental experience "I remember" but, contained in it, the memory of my past transcendental life. This holds for every memory: each evidently admits a double transcendental reduction, one yielding the memory as my present experience, the other reaching into its reproductive content and disclosing a piece of my past transcendental life. Following the chain of recollections toward the present and practising the reduction on it, I see my continuous transcendental past up to the now, though only a stretch of it; asking back into ever more distant pasts, I see that my transcendental life reaches back continuously into an endless past. The Schlossberg is Freiburg's hill above the old town, and the example is typical of Husserl's lectures. The "double reduction" is a technical innovation of this course: the reduction applies both to the present act of remembering and, "inside" it, to the remembered act, which is itself an intentional experience with its own world-belief. The result is a transcendental past, not merely a present act of recollecting. Whether this past is apodictically given is precisely what the apodictic reduction must ask, since memory can deceive; the Cartesian Meditations (§ 9) conclude that the transcendental past is not apodictic in its content, only in its general structure.

The future is somewhat different, since the foreseeing of expectation is not real seeing, nor an exact analogue of memory's quasi-seeing-again. Still, the reduction can be practised on the expected, and it again yields a double transcendental: the expectation as present transcendental experience, and the expected content included in it. Since every present carries a horizon of expectation, we have, analogous to the endless horizon of the transcendental past, an open endless horizon of the transcendental future. With the world, objective time, the form of mundane objects, is put out of play. But I, the transcendental ego, live a transcendental life that presents itself in continuous transcendental experience in a transcendental time-form of its own, to be described more closely: the form of a present life carrying the endless horizon of memory and expectation, which, when disclosed, shows a transcendental life-stream endless in both directions. Objective time falls under the reduction; the time of consciousness does not. This is the doctrine of the Lectures on Internal Time-Consciousness applied to the reduced field: immanent time is not a region of objective time but the form in which transcendental life presents itself to itself. The asymmetry between memory (reproduction of what was perceived) and expectation (pre-sketch, not re-seeing) will matter for the status of transcendental futurity.

#### Lecture 40: Reflection as Splitting of the Ego and the Identity of the Ego in the Streaming Living Present

A much deeper reflection is needed on what I have in my transcendental life and what role is played in it by mundane objectivity, which is excluded as to judgment but does not stop appearing to me. A house I perceive does not stop being perceived in this perceiving when I pass to the transcendental attitude and grasp my "I perceive" as transcendental experience; it is still believed in it, standing there with certainty, with its red roof. But have I not bracketed the house's existence and put the belief of naive perception out of play? If I take belief from perception it is no longer perception. It is no use saying that belief cannot be removed like a detachable piece; the point is rather to put it "out of action, out of play". Quite right. But does that not mean doing something to the perception? At any rate the bracketing has changed my original experience. Yet by this bracketing, it was said, I obtain my "I perceive" as it really and purely is or was, a component of my transcendental subjectivity, which is to be nothing but the ego and ego-life as it is in and for itself, whether or not there is a world, and which in its life performs world-experience, a particular life of unbroken belief, and so first has the world consciously as existing reality. The objection is the standard one against the epoché: if the reduction modifies the act, it cannot give the act as it purely was. Husserl states it at full strength. The answer will come through the analysis of reflection that follows: the modification occurs not in the reflected act but in the reflecting ego's participation.

Following the method's instruction, I recognize and indeed see this transcendental subjectivity and what is said of it. But reflecting on the method as I just did, I become doubtful whether it is or could be suited to exhibit transcendental subjectivity and its life. The editor refers to two critical notes of Husserl's covering the pages through Lecture 43. The gap between performing the method and reflecting on it is exactly the theme of the Section: a "phenomenology of the phenomenological reduction", reflection on the reflection that performs the epoché.

I cannot tolerate unclarity here and must give an exact account of how I practise the reductive method and how I must understand it. I begin naturally with natural self-reflection, taking again the perception of a house. At first I perceive in naive devotion and a certain self-loss: I am absorbed in looking at the house. This is not the self-loss of dull sleep. The ego is awake, actual, the performing ego of an act whose only concrete expression is "I think", ego cogito, completed by naming the cogitatum. The grammatical subject-object predication expresses the unique structure of the act, with act-subject and act-object: in the act the performing ego is directed at the object conscious in it and occupied with it. Here it performs a perceiving, directed at the house. But that it is I who do so, and this is the self-loss, I do not know; I am not directed at it. That happens only in reflection, a perception of higher level, in which what is perceived is not the house but "I perceive the house", as the simple judgment of perception faithfully says. What happened in this reflection? I rose, as ego of reflection, above the act "I perceive", in whose performance I was not aware of it or of myself as performer; and as this newly appearing ego I perform again an act "I perceive", in which I make that self-forgotten ego and the previously unperceived "I perceive the house" my perceived content. The structure of reflection is described as the appearance of a new ego-act directed at the old one. "Self-loss" is not a failing but the normal mode of an absorbed act; the ego is present as functioning, not as object. Two egos now appear, which gives rise to the image of splitting.

When reflection begins, the naive perceiving of the self-forgotten ego is already past. I grasp it only by a catching grip back into the "still-having" of so-called retention, the after-memory immediately joined to original experience. Only so can I notice the naive perceiving and the self-forgotten ego, a noticing that is really an after-noticing, not quite a perceptual grasping, but a grasping still. If the house perception continues after I have established myself as reflecting ego, there is no temporal separation between the ego directed at the house and the ego of reflection directed at it, as in the phase of retentional after-noticing. In the living present I have in coexistence the doubled ego and the doubled ego-act: the ego continuously looking at the house and the ego performing the act "I am aware that I am continuously looking at the house", which may express itself as "I look at the house". This simple sentence is the reflecting ego's statement, and the ego stated in it is the reflectively grasped one. Two phases of reflection are separated. At its onset reflection reaches the reflected act only retentionally, as just-past; once established, it runs alongside the continuing act in the same living present. The first phase explains why reflection always arrives late (a point later pressed by Sartre and Henry against the whole reflective method); the second explains how it can nonetheless grasp a still-living act. "Living present" enters here as the name for the field of coexistence of reflecting and reflected.

The ego performing the reflection is itself in the mode of self-forgotten ego, and its noticing act is self-forgotten. How do we know of this higher-level self-forgetting? By a reflection of the second level, with its reflecting and in turn self-forgotten ego, which would say: I am aware that I perceive this house. Again phases of retentional grasping and subsequent phases would be distinguished, in which the ego-subjects related in the splitting belong to the same streaming present. Each new, higher reflection brings a new performing ego on stage, the third related to the second and the second to the first. The iteration is open-ended but not vicious: each level is self-forgotten while functioning and can be made thematic from above. The infinite regress Lecture 31 deferred reappears here in concrete form, as a structure of reflection rather than a demand for justification.

A terminological remark. "Self-forgetting" is not apt, since forgetting normally presupposes prior awareness, whereas here the mode of self-forgetting comes first. "Ego unconscious of itself" does not fit either, because of the ambiguities of "consciousness". Better to speak of a latent ego and, in contrast, a patent one. An ego, awake and performing an act, and an act itself become patent only through the appearance of an ego reflecting on it, which is in turn latent. Such becoming-patent is possible for every latent ego, including that of any reflection; it consists in the reflecting ego's performing an act that makes the previously latent one its intentional object. The pair latent–patent replaces the misleading vocabulary of forgetting and unconsciousness. Latency is not a deficiency but the primary mode of functioning subjectivity; this thought, developed in Beilage XVI ("Double latency of the ego") and in Fink's writings, is the source of the later problem of "anonymous" functioning subjectivity in the Crisis and of Henry's radicalization of it.

Why speak of the same ego relating to itself and becoming aware of itself and its act in "self-perception", when different acts are evidently layered and each has its own ego as its act-pole? We will see that these separate egos need not agree in their position-takings. And how could we use the image of splitting, which suggests the division of something unitary, perhaps preserving some unity, as a split tree trunk is not yet cut into separate pieces? The questions are genuine. If each act has its own ego-pole, identity needs showing; and the possible disagreement of position-takings, announced here, is what will make the epoché possible.

The answer comes from looking at actual and always possible life in ego-reflection. I can always perform higher reflection and survey together an ego caught by grasping back, an ego in a living act and at the same time reflectively observed, a reflecting ego already made patent, and so on. Then I can and must see that the many act-poles are evidently the same ego, that one and the same ego appears in all these acts, in a different mode in each; that splitting into a plurality of acts and act-subjects, it remains one and the same ego that splits. Living in activity is nothing but continually splitting in active conduct, and an all-surveying, identifying ego can always establish itself; or more originally, I can establish myself as surveying ego in higher reflection and become aware, in evident synthetic identification, of the sameness of all these act-poles and the difference of their modes of being. So I say: I am the same throughout, the same as reflecting who grasps myself as unreflecting in retrospect, who as self-perceiving watch myself perceiving the house. The editor refers to Beilage XVI on the double latency of the ego and Beilage XVII, "What kind of ego is it that I cannot strike out?". The identity of the ego is itself a result of synthetic identification, seen in reflection, not presupposed. Splitting and identity belong together: the ego is one as the pole that identifies itself across its modes. This is the ego-pole of Ideen II § 22 and the Cartesian Meditations § 31, and Beilage XVII's question ("what ego can I not strike out?") connects it to the apodictic residue.

With this clarified, and the multiplication of performing egos in every reflection secured, an important point follows. Normally, reflecting on my naive house perception, I do not merely look at "I perceive this house" as spectator of my perceiving ego; I share its perceptual belief. As reflecting ego I co-perform the belief of the perceiving ego: just as the house and what I observe of it count for me as really existing while I see it, so they count for me as reflecting. Along with the self-perception and the perception of the house-perception I let the latter hold; I am a performing subject of this validity too. The normal case of reflection is complicit reflection: the upper ego ratifies the lower ego's belief. This is why natural reflection, however pure, stays mundane (Lecture 38).

So normally every statement "I see this object" also means "I believe this object is real", for anyone in ordinary speech. But against the normal stands the abnormal, and now you will see why this whole self-clarification was needed. It need not always be so; as reflecting ego I need not always co-believe. And what is especially important for understanding the phenomenological reduction: I can, in my freedom, refuse this natural co-belief of reflection. I can behave purely as a spectator absolutely uninterested in the existence and being-so of the perceived house and of the world in general. This is the answer to the objection that opened this lecture. The epoché does not tamper with the lower act: the house perception keeps its belief, and the belief is now seen as belief. What changes is the reflecting ego's participation; it abstains from co-performing. The "uninterested spectator" (unbeteiligter Zuschauer) is born here; Chapter Two will specify what "uninterested" means, since, as Husserl immediately warns, it cannot mean a mere absence of interest.

### Chapter Two: On the Theory of the Phenomenologist's Theoretical Attitude: Sense and Achievement of the Epoché

#### Lecture 41: Reflection and Theoretical Interest; Splitting of the Ego's Position-Takings

Before clarifying this lack of interest, Husserl must clarify the more general phenomenon of a division in belief, and generally in position-taking, that can go with the splitting of the ego: unlike the usual case, the reflecting ego does not join, and may even reject, the position of the lower ego it reflects on. The sceptic is an example. Looking straight at the world, he lives through perceptual belief in the harmony of his perceiving and cannot help having things and world given as reality. But when, as philosophical sceptic moved by arguments, he has reached denial or doubt of the world and reflects on himself as perceiving the real world, the split of position-takings becomes visible in the unity of the split act: as perceiver he believes; as reflecting sceptic he does not trust the belief, does not join it, doubts or rejects it. In a note Husserl lists possible attitudes toward the harmoniously experienced world: holding its non-being possible while experiencing it with firm certainty; adopting a sceptical or negativist theory and laying over continuing belief a layer of intellectual doubt or negation; saying agnostically that experiential certainty concerns only subjective images and that the world itself is unknown or unknowable; or accepting the experienced world as the world itself while doubting that it has truth in itself, treating its harmonious style as provisional and trusting it practically "as if" there were a definitive truth. He adds, "compare Hume's backgammon". The sceptic proves that reflection can dissent from the belief it reflects on without abolishing it. Hume's backgammon (Treatise I.4.7) is the classic case: scepticism survives only in the study, while life goes on believing. Husserl's list distinguishes four positions that are all still interested in the world's being, which is exactly what will separate them from the epoché: each takes a stand on the world, whereas the phenomenologist will take none.

So far only perception has been used as example, and not all reflections have the same structure, so Husserl adds recollection. Language expresses it reflexively: "I remember". Every memory contains a kind of doubling of the ego, since what I remember is conscious not merely as having been but as having been perceived by me. I remember a fire: I saw it; a concert: I heard it; or mediately, I remember having heard or read of the fire. The doubling lies in the fact that my past ego, the ego that was there, belongs to the content of the recollection as an experience of my present waking ego. The experience turns into explicit reflection when I direct my look back to the past ego and its act, and then as present reflecting ego I say: "I watched the fire". The past perception includes a past perceptual belief. In normal recollection I, the present ego, co-perform this reproduced belief: as I believed then, I believe now. So "I remember that it was so" normally implies "it was so". Recollection is reflexive by structure: it presentifies a past act of the ego, not only a past event. This is why the "double reduction" of Lecture 39 was possible, and why memory is the second model of ego-splitting.

But I can later become doubtful, even convinced that it was not so. While the recollection is still recollection of the same and still presentifies the past perceiving and its belief, my present ego has freed itself from co-belief and takes another position. This is again like the sceptic, and exactly analogous to actual reflection, except that the sceptic declares not a single experience but universal world-perception deceptive. Correction of memory shows the present ego dissenting from a reproduced belief that remains intact as reproduced. The general point is that the belief of the reflected act and the position of the reflecting ego are separable.

What holds for perception and recollection transfers easily to all acts in which we behave "believingly", intuitive or not: empty or vividly pictured expectations, acts of depiction, empirical indication, predicative thinking, and so on. All can appear in the primal mode of certainty or be modalized as doubting, conjecturing, negating. In all these cases the reflecting ego's position will in general be interested in the being of the object, the fire, the world, whether or not it has separated its belief from the reflected ego. Even if its conduct is not co-belief, it is a position-taking toward being: a judging in the widest sense, even if a modification of the normal form, judgmental certainty; a conjecturing, holding possible or probable, doubting, or negating rejection, a striking out of being. The generalization prepares the decisive contrast. Dissent is still a stance on being. The sceptic's doubt and the corrector's negation remain within the interest in being; they only change its sign. The epoché will have to be something other than any modality of belief, as Ideen I § 31 insisted when it separated the epoché from Cartesian doubt.

Every such act of the judging ego contains, as the word "act" suggests, a doing, however fleeting. In judging I am directed at being and being-so as at a goal I strive toward. Through every conjecturing, holding probable, or doubting runs the intention toward a being to be established by certainty, finally toward what is "itself", which I can "have" only in the form of certainty called evidence. Negative certainty passes the intention on, or rather turns it into a counter-intention: if the intended is not, a striving intention turns to what is instead, toward a positive certainty. And if I already have certainty and am with the being itself, as in perception, the intending continues toward ever richer and more perfect acquaintance, fulfilling the anticipating components of perception through new self-giving perceptions of new sides and moments. Such doing, advancing from act to act, is synthetically unified into one doing by the continuity of a striving directed at the identical conscious goal. The free working-out of such striving often stalls, perhaps at the first step, when other interests distract me; what matters is that in every act I am aiming and live in a continuity of acts centred by a conscious unity of goal. As judging I am directed at certainty of being, possession of being, and the perfection of this possession; in this sense I am, as act-subject, interested in being, and I remain so in general in reflection, as the ego of reflection. "Interest" gets its technical sense: the striving character of every act toward its goal, which in judging is being itself. This is the practical construal of cognition from Lecture 28 applied to the micro-structure of acts; it is also the analysis of Experience and Judgment § 20 on "interest" as the ego's tendency toward the object. To be interested in being is not a mood but the teleological form of every doxic act.

It can also be that as reflecting ego, observing my act, I am completely uninterested in what is believed and intended as a goal of being in this act. In the split we have the ego performing the perception, or having performed the recollection, which is interested in being; and above it the ego of reflection, which observes "I perceive the house" and normally shares the lower ego's interest, even being one with it in its interested position, co-believing, co-conjecturing, co-doubting. Against this stands the possibility that this participation and unity is absent: the reflecting ego grasps and observes the act of the lower ego and its interested directedness, but is uninterested in what the observed ego is interested in. Reflecting on "I perceive this house", I am then no longer the ego for which the house is existing reality; the certainty of the lower ego is not co-performed by me. Nor do I perform any modification of it, conjecturing, doubting, or negating; I have no reason to, seeing the house in bright sunshine, untroubled by sceptical motives. If there were such motives, I would still be interested in the being of the house, in a judging, theoretical attitude aimed at knowledge of the house. But I am not. In this I, the uninterested self-observer and self-knower, differ from the ego I observe: I am interested solely in the being and being-so of the perceptual experience, the act of perceiving as such, present, past, or future, as it is, was, or will be. The central negative definition of the epoché: not co-performance, and not any modalization either. The phenomenologist does not doubt the house; he has no stance on it at all. What he is interested in is the act. This excludes the second misreading-guard (epoché as doubt) once and for all.

As reflecting ego I am not uninterested in every respect. I perform an act and exercise a cognitive interest, but it is directed at me and my perceiving in its pure being. Precisely by refraining from any interest sharing in the perceived being of the house, nothing is there for me but the purely subjective, and my theoretical interest consists in observing and determining it and its immanent contents. That this perceptual act, now my pure theme, exercises a belief that a house is there belongs to its pure content and so to my reflective interest; yet in noting this belief as a moment of the actual experience I believe nothing about the house's being and have nothing to judge about it. A note adds that everything material is in a sense preserved for the reflecting ego: performing higher-level acts, it grasps the subjective life and specifically everything subjective belonging to the things it previously had straightforwardly, the position-takings and modes of givenness of every kind, but does not thereby give up the material element. To its original thematic sense, which contained nothing subjective, it adds the subjective modes in which the thing was given, perhaps with the insight that it is thinkable only in such modes. Two corrections to the picture of "uninterested" spectating follow. It is a shift of interest, not its absence: the phenomenologist has a new theme, being-for-consciousness. And the belief of the lower act is part of that theme, so the epoché does not erase positionality but makes it visible. The note answers the charge that phenomenology loses the things: they remain, now with their modes of givenness, which is the germ of the doctrine that the noema is the object "as meant".

#### Lecture 42: The Most General Concept of Interest, of "Attitude", of "Theme"

The lack of interest by which I become an "uninvolved spectator", then a theoretical observer and investigator of myself, cannot be a mere privation, a not doing what I otherwise do, as in sleep I do not do what I do awake. Passing from naive, latent performance of "I perceive the house" into reflection, I cannot simply drop the interest in being I naively exercised. It belongs to me as I now am and remains mine in new acts until a motive makes me drop it. Reflecting simply, I cannot help sympathizing with myself, taking over my interests. A special motivation must free me from this sympathy so that I become a pure observer of myself, or of my pure self and its act taken purely in and for itself. Only through the free act of abstention from judgment, of deliberately detaching myself from the original co-interest, can the attitude of uninvolved observation arise, in which what I perceive stops being simply there as valid in its field of being or as something to be experienced. What could the motive be? "Sympathy with oneself" names the default complicity of reflection. The epoché is an act of will against a habit, which ties Section Three back to the voluntarism of Section One, and the question of motive will receive two answers: the Cartesian motive of apodicticity already used, and the new motive of pure interest in subjectivity that the second way will exploit.

Before pursuing the question it must be given its full breadth. Our concepts of interested and uninterested reflection are still inessentially restricted, since they concern only acts of *doxa*, the intellective sphere. Acts of feeling and will must be considered too, loving and hating, hoping and fearing, deliberating and deciding, with their own, genuinely new kinds of reflection. Here too the positions of reflecting and reflected ego may agree or not. This yields the widest concept of a reflecting ego that sympathizes with itself or renounces all sympathy with itself, and the narrower but for us important idea of a thoroughly uninvolved theoretical self-observer and self-knower. The universality of the epoché requires it to cover every position-taking, not only belief; otherwise a valuing act, bracketed as belief, would still carry an unbracketed valuation of its object.

Before examining reflection on feeling and will, a general consideration of the whole activity of an ego is needed, to found a fundamental concept, interest and acts of interest, together with other concepts important everywhere. The editor points to a critical note of Husserl's. The detour into a general theory of interest and attitude is not a digression; "attitude" (Einstellung) is the term that the whole distinction of natural and phenomenological attitude depends on, and it has not been defined.

We treated intellective acts separately, assuming they can function alone, like acts of feeling or practical acts. But are not acts of all spheres interwoven in each pulse of waking ego-life? What gives sense to saying we are sometimes in a theoretical attitude, sometimes in a valuing, sometimes in a practical one aimed at realization? How does the unity of an intellective action, merely experiencing or theorizing, stand out from the manifold of a stream of acts, so that we speak of one, though synthetic, act, an experiencing or a proving, a purely intellective act embracing many intellective sub-acts but not the valuings that may move the experiencer along? Even what we ordinarily call an act becomes questionable and needs self-clarification that makes the naive usage intelligible from within. Here belongs the talk of theme, usual in the theoretical sphere and easily extended: what we are especially directed at is our theme, perhaps belonging to an infinitely comprehensive sphere we are habitually attuned to, our thematic universe. Talk of directions of interest and of interest itself aims at the same. The questions show that the unit "an act" is itself an abstraction from interwoven life, and that "attitude" and "theme" must be defined through the dominant direction of interest, not through the presence or absence of certain act-types.

The acts the reflecting ego finds as its own, or as those of its unreflected ego, are as a rule, indeed always, interwoven and founded on one another. Some are obviously possible only as founded on others. The sculptor modelling clay must have it perceptually before him; he has the shape to be produced as an obscure goal-idea, and each intermediate shape as a more or less successful approximation, which involves constant valuing; what I strive for must count as a value. As naive subject of such an action I am subject of many act-intentions joined into one total act. Among these sub-acts those in serving function are distinguished from those exercising the ruling action, in which lies what the act-subject is aiming at. Acts can also stand in secondary or main action without being united in one total act. As botanist I may be delighted by a flower's beauty, but the delight is not in main action while I observe and classify it; when I am done, the aesthetic joy running alongside may become the main action, and I am in the aesthetic attitude, of feeling, instead of the theoretical attitude, of understanding. Another example is the change between aesthetic contemplation of a work of art and the art historian's theoretical study; here the two acts are not parts of one total act. The structure of "ruling" and "serving" functions, and of "main" and "secondary" action, will let Husserl define attitude as the dominance of one interest over others that remain present. The flower and art-historian examples reprise Lecture 30; now they illustrate a change of attitude as a change in which interest rules.

The relation of ruling and serving function defines a unified total act despite interweaving. When the sculptor sees and values the emerging form, approving a surface or line with pleasure, rejecting it with displeasure, striving for the most satisfying form, the actions of feeling, liking and disliking, wishing it so or otherwise, finding it beautiful, serve; but they are not performed incidentally, since the working artist lives in them in main performance. Through the serving intention passes the ruling function that governs the process: the acting will, directed through the realizing activity at a final form. "Serving" has different senses according to whether we consider the founding valuing and perceiving, or the intermediate phases of action with their unfinished shapes, though necessary foundings are in question everywhere. Such interweavings, at different levels of complexity, belong to the act-life of the naive ego devoted to its intentional objects and so latent to itself. The distinction between "serving" and "incidental" is fine but important: a serving act can be fully lived, as the sculptor's aesthetic feelings are, while still being subordinate to the will that rules. The whole architecture is lived by the latent ego without being noticed, which is why its analysis requires reflection.

We must also note the variety of characters of the intended objects corresponding to the variety of acts. The knowing, self-lost ego has before it as its thematic intended object, according to whether it believes with certainty, conjectures, doubts, or negates, something as simply being, as possibly being, as doubtful, as not being, "something" standing for the material content. In acts of feeling joined with them, the naive ego has its object with characters of pleasing or displeasing, loved or hated, esteemed or feared, beautiful, useful. The correlation of act-modality and object-character (being, possible, doubtful; beautiful, useful) is the noetic-noematic parallelism of Ideen I §§ 103–117. For the naive ego these characters appear as properties of things, which is precisely how value-predicates were said to originate in feeling in Lecture 30.

An act of interest in the pregnant sense has an object which the ego is not merely aware of, perhaps incidentally, but at which it is directed in a pregnant sense, which it has in view and aims at. Further distinctions arise. The street noise "disturbing" me is not part of my theme, the theoretical thought I am working out, though it might serve as a means for other purposes. As a mathematician I live in a special problem, but it is a mathematical one, and the unity of the mathematical field is a unity of thematic interweaving. We must distinguish the momentary actual theme, and within it what the final aim is directed at from what is a "means" (a premise), from the realm of habitual themes, which remain as lasting interests in "intellectual possession" and, reactivated, have the character of settled themes already attained, yet still of interest as acquired possessions. The concept of theme is given three dimensions: focal versus peripheral (the noise), end versus means (conclusion versus premise), and actual versus habitual (a field of settled acquisitions still available). The last connects with the habituality of vocation in Lecture 29: the philosopher's theme persists habitually between acts.

Thematic interweaving lies not only in relations of end and means; everything we call material belonging-together indicates an inner transition of interest into new interests, which becomes interweaving and, from aim to aim, keeps producing the synthetic unity of an embracing aim, whose fulfilling realizations are, in the logical sphere, the logical connection that emerges, and in the practical sphere, the connection of purposes. The unity of a field of inquiry is thus a unity of interest before it is a unity of objects; logical and practical coherence are its fulfilments.

If the ruling interest is in being and being-so, cognitive in the widest sense, or, as one says, theoretical, though mere experience belongs here, the theme is a cognitive theme, perhaps a theoretical one in the proper sense. One unity of goal runs through all acts, whatever their particular goals: cognition's relation to one object, or to the unity of an objective connection that may first emerge in the synthesis of the single thematic acts, and first as something to be attained; finally the theme drives on to embrace the whole field. The directed interest aims everywhere at attaining the relevant "being" (as posited) in verifying determination, in its properties and relations, in its truth. Through the manifold of interwoven acts, each "having" its interest (understood ontically, as goal), passes the unity of "one" interest uniting all. In the scientist's habituality there is a lasting thematic habitus, with its acquired possessions and its open infinite horizon of future themes, a habitually recurring "professional attitude" to continued work in relation to the universal field of the science. This transfers from the individual scientist to the community of colleagues constituted for each, with whom one "works together" and to whom one turns. The "professional attitude" (Berufseinstellung) is the conceptual link between this lecture and Lecture 29: an attitude is a habitual unity of interest, and a vocation is the life-long form of such an attitude. The last sentence gives the scientific community a place in the theory of interest, as a correlate of each scientist's habitus.

The interest can also be one of feeling, valuing in the widest sense, with a value-theme. The ego wants to live itself out in valuing. From an anticipatory liking of a work of art seen fleetingly, it wants to progress to the fulfilled, ever more richly fulfilled value, and in the complete fulfilment of the founding valuations to attain the value itself: to have in itself, in the mode of full, pure aesthetic enjoyment, the synthetic unity of mutually fulfilling value-intentions, the aesthetic object as this concrete value, as the actuality (*energeia*) of the earlier mere potentiality, the final and real self-giving and self-having of the value, which is nothing but pure, saturated enjoyment. This happens in valuing, not judging; perceptions play only a serving role, as foundations for the valuing feelings founded on them. The value itself in its value-truth is not perceived but, as it were, "value-taken": what perception achieves for the mere thing, value-ception (Wertnehmung) achieves for value; it is the fulfilment-mode of valuing feeling. The aesthetic observer lives in valuing interest, and only by a turning of interest, a change of attitude, does he pass into being-positing experience and theoretical observation, as art scholar; this can begin only after the aesthetic interest has been exercised and the value, the *telos* of feeling, lies ready as valued, for a theoretical interest and first for a perception directed at the aesthetic object, the perception of the value-object. The coinage Wertnehmung (formed on Wahrnehmung, "perception", literally "taking-true") names the intuitive givenness of value in feeling: it is to value what perception is to things. It formalizes the claim of Lecture 30 that value first shows itself in feeling. The order is strict: theoretical cognition of values presupposes the value already "value-taken", which is Husserl's version of the primacy of the axiological in the constitution of values, compatible with the primacy of theory in their justification.

Generally, having performed acts and actions of any kind in naive devotion, I can during or after them become aware of my doing and reflect, for example while theorizing, contemplating aesthetically, planning, or working. The act of reflection need not be intellective, as the earlier examples assumed, not merely reflective perception of the lower acts or reflective turning back in retention or recollection, on which reflective thinking may then build. There are specific reflections of feeling, which can, like other acts of feeling, undergo a thematic turn into doxic reflections of changed sense. As reflecting ego I can have myself as intentional object in liking and disliking, love and hate, striving, deliberating, deciding, realizing, myself as subject of such acts performed naively. I love, and reflecting I am glad that I love as I do, or reproach myself with displeasure. I willed and acted, and reflecting I regret having done so. Conscience is the name for a class of such reflexive relations, emotional positions of the ego toward itself, which often pass into judging oneself and one's own worth. Reflection of feeling is a new class: the reflecting act is itself an evaluation. Conscience appears as the paradigm, which ties the analysis once more to the ethics of self-responsibility of Section One: the "good conscience" of the philosopher was a reflexive valuation of this kind.

The reflecting ego in such acts can agree or disagree in its positions with the ego reflected on, as when I now condemn a hatred I once felt, or give up an earlier aesthetic judgment, regret, and the like, in any critique within feeling. The ruling act of the reflecting ego can also be of a different kind from that of the reflected one; in other words, as an extension of what was said earlier, the interest of the two egos can differ. The editor notes that part of this text was revised by Husserl in response to a critical note. The possibility of a difference in kind of interest is the step needed for the epoché: a theoretical reflecting ego can observe a valuing or willing lower ego without sharing its value-interest.

#### Lecture 43: The Possibility of a Pure Interest in Subjective Being in Phenomenological Epoché and Reflection

With the most general concept of interest in hand, we see that the interests of reflecting and reflected ego can differ even in basic kind, one of feeling, the other of understanding. What concerns us is that the reflecting ego can be a theoretically interested observer directed at the reflected ego, occupied aesthetically or practically, and at its acts, which are its theme as being and being so, a theoretical theme. The phenomenologist's interest is theoretical; the interest reflected on may be of any kind. This asymmetry makes all of subjective life, not only cognition, available as theme.

At the same time the reflecting ego can restrict its theoretical interest purely to the reflected ego and its acts and refuse all co-performance of their position-takings, ruling and serving alike. If I pass from naive enjoyment of a work of art into theoretical reflection, a direct experiencing observation of my aesthetic act, the normal case is that I also have the reflected ego's object and theme, co-interested in its objective being, its value, and the work as work; I co-perform the lower ego's acts by which the artwork is there for me not only as a thing but as a work in which a value is embodied by the artist. If, for whatever motive, I withdraw from this co-performance of the acts of feeling and will I reflect on, and become a pure, uninterested spectator and theoretical observer of them, they are all, founded and founding, ruling and serving, put out of action for me, and with them every unified theme belonging to their synthesis. As reflecting ego I am not the ego whose perceptual belief gives the artwork as thing its subjective validity as existing; nor the ego whose value-taking gives its value-form validity for feeling; nor the ego that, in a change of attitude, observes, describes, and places the work historically. The epoché applied to a complex act is total: it suspends each of its layers of validity, doxic, axiological, practical, including the art historian's theoretical validity. The phenomenologist is neither the believer, nor the enjoyer, nor the scholar of the work; he is the observer of all three.

As reflecting theoretical ego I can, by abstaining from co-performing the underlying acts, be interested in them purely as these experiences. That they confer on what they perceive validity as real, on what they value validity as beautiful, on the sensory appearance validity as a work, characterizes them essentially as act-experiences and cannot be separated from them. But only that they confer this or that validity, that they mean the existing as existing and the valuable as valuable, belongs to my interest as reflecting ego. It is a pure interest in subjective being. Whether the perceived being is rightly believed, whether the value is genuine or apparent, I have no decision to make as uninterested ego of reflection, since I take no part in the directions of interest of the straightforwardly performed acts. The phrase "pure interest in subjective being" defines the phenomenological attitude positively. The contrast between "that they confer validity" and "whether it is right" separates description from critique, which is why the phenomenological reduction must precede, and is distinct from, the apodictic reduction (Lecture 38).

Again, and now with full generality: precisely thereby I gain my purely subjective as theoretical theme, in the sense that it is what it is even if all the act-objects meant and posited as valid in it did not in truth exist; it is what it is independently of the truth-validity of the objective meaning these acts perform in themselves, prior to any critical question of their right. The formula reproduces, within reflection, the result of Section Two: the purely subjective is what would remain if the world did not exist. But it is now reached without the hypothesis of non-existence, merely by abstention. This is the seed of the "second way".

In this way I can perform a "phenomenological" epoché on any act I have performed straightforwardly, in a provisional sense, which will prove limited and not at all transcendental. I can take the attitude of the spectator theoretically interested only in the phenomenologically pure act-experience, through an epoché that refuses all thematic objects of the acts the interest of validity. This is phenomenological exclusion. The qualification "not at all transcendental" is decisive. An epoché performed act by act, while the world as a whole remains valid in the background, yields only pure psychology, the "phenomenological-psychological reduction" of Section Four. What it lacks is universality; the passage from the single-act epoché to the universal one is the problem of Lecture 48.

This concerns all possible acts and hence all objects. If I practise uninvolved reflection on acts in which I have ideal objects, numbers, mathematical manifolds, geometrical ideals, any ideals, as objects of being, for example on the evidence that 2 < 3, the ideal content is given in this act with apodictic certainty; I attain and grasp it really and beyond doubt. But establishing myself as reflecting ego purely interested in phenomenology means being completely uninterested in the intentional object as existing. Even an absolutely evident being like 2 < 3 may not count for me as phenomenological ego; I inhibit any position on the existence or non-existence of this arithmetical state of affairs. As a mathematician, moved by interest in mathematical being, I would necessarily perform the evidence; but as mathematizing ego I am not the phenomenological ego I am when I watch my mathematizing as a split-off reflective ego, interested purely in what it is in itself, without any judging position toward what counts for it objectively. There is no sceptical epoché here; the sceptic's epoché, as doubting, would be an interest in mathematical being, not in the purely subjective experience. A note adds that ontological predicates too, all regional essential determinations, now appear only as phenomenological predicates, essential determinations of the regional noema: in any harmonious experience of a thing, the thing as experienced must have all "transcendental-aesthetic" determinations, the formal structure of the regional noema "thing-appearance"; imagining further harmonious experiences unfolding the empty horizons, and comparisons, predications, measurements based on them, I would have to state geometrical and chronological "truths". But these do not concern truly existing things able to prove their being in infinitum; ontological truth indicates noematic rules for synthetically harmonious experience, however far it may reach. The mathematical example removes any suspicion that the epoché is a response to doubtfulness: it applies to the apodictic just as to the doubtful. The Cartesian way, which ran through the dubitability of the world, is here quietly surpassed; the epoché is not a verdict on evidence but a change of interest. The note is important for the fifth misreading-guard: even ontologies (geometry, the formal ontology of the thing) become, under the reduction, rules for the harmony of experience, the "clue" (Leitfaden) function of the object in constitutive analysis (Cartesian Meditations § 21).

Appealing to evidence in the logical normation of knowledge is thus a logical, not a phenomenological reflection. In the phenomenological one evidence is a form of experience whose structure interests me as a subjective fact; in the logical one I tell myself that I see the object itself, that I cannot doubt what is so seen, that I must co-believe where I see evidently. There I am in the will to knowledge, directed at the goal of being, and assure myself that I am really at the goal. But in phenomenology I put out of play, and must, all interest in the validity of being or any validity of the intentional object if I want the act-experience in its own character. The pure act-experience can be defined as what can be posited and known in experience at any time when, reflecting, I put everything straightforwardly valid out of validity; and pure phenomenological interest is the interest in being that is still possible when I exclude all other interests, those I had as straightforwardly performing ego. As reflecting ego I then have none of the real or ideal objectivities, of being, value, or practice, previously simply given, including all human persons, objective subjectivities, with their psychic life. But I have something: the subjective, the pure act-experience, in which all these excluded objectivities are posited, experienced, thought, valued, created; and in a way I still have the objectivities themselves, "as so posited" by these acts, though not posited by the phenomenological ego. The distinction between logical and phenomenological reflection on evidence reinterprets Lecture 31 in retrospect: the principle of evidence adopted there was a logical norm, performed from within the will to knowledge; the phenomenology of evidence, which studies evidence as an experience, is a different enterprise. The retained "objects as posited" are the noemata; they are not copies, and they are not the objects "posited by me", which prevents the reading of the noema as a mental representation.

This point needs care. The talk of phenomenologically excluding objects, putting them out of play (a legitimate correlate of putting acts and interests out of play), must not be misread. The being, value, or purpose that as phenomenological observer I no longer "have" in the normal sense, I constantly have in a modified sense. What is excluded from my validity and interest in validity has not disappeared from my field of consciousness; it is given to me as phenomenologist, in contrast to me as naturally attuned observer, knower, evaluator, or worker, in an essentially changed way, thanks to the method that made me a phenomenologist. On the side of the object this method is called bracketing: we attach an excluding bracket to the object, an index saying: here I inhibit all co-validating, all interest in being or value; I let the object count only as intentional object of its act, the act that confers validity on it, and am interested only in the act and what it posits as object, thematically so characterized. Doing so, I have the phenomenologically pure subjective and in it its object in the modified form of validity of a merely intentional object of its act. I do not become blind to objects as by self-hypnosis, nor, when I exclude the whole world, blind to it; I remain seeing everything. But in the split I am established as at once simply seeing and practising pure self-knowledge, and everything simply seen is there in the modification of the bracket, seen as bracketed. Have we yet considered all kinds of acts on which to clarify the method and achievement of bracketing? This is the fullest statement of the bracket in the course and the key text against the first misreading-guard. Bracketing is an index attached to a still-given object, not its removal; the world remains in view "in the modification of the bracket". "Self-hypnosis" names the caricature Husserl rejects: the phenomenologist does not look away from the world but sees it together with its being-for-consciousness. The split ego makes this possible: the lower ego keeps seeing and believing, the upper ego observes without co-believing.

Consider acts in which a picture is seen as a depiction of something. They play a founding role in aesthetic contemplation or production of depictive fine art, which presents something else in the picture. The aesthetic valuing founded on image-consciousness does not concern us now, only the self-presentation of something else in a picture, as real or as mere imagining. In a photographic or other portrait a person presents himself as real; in another picture a battle of Cyclopes, as phantasy. In any case something presents itself to me only because in the act of picture-seeing I have presenting and presented: the appearing picture and, not beside it but in it, the presented subject. That is, this nesting is there for me in its peculiarity only because it counts so for me in the naive act of image-consciousness. Under "picture" several things are joined: the photograph as paper thing lying on the table; and in another sense the sensuously intuited little violet figure, the image proper, which is anything but a real thing, in which the subject "person" presents itself. Naively I look now at this, now at that, in correspondingly turned acts. As phenomenologist I can put these acts and their validity as picture in one sense or another out of play and grasp, analyse, and describe everything inseparable from them, the purely subjective. The threefold distinction of physical image-thing, image-object (the "violet figure"), and image-subject (sujet) is the analysis of Hua XXIII (Phantasy, Image Consciousness, and Memory, 1904–05). It enters here because image-consciousness contains a layered validity (the image-object is a semblance that does not exist, yet presents a subject that may) and so tests the bracket's capacity to handle nested positings, which the next chapter pursues for phantasy.

### Chapter Three: The Conscious Activity of Natural Ego-Life and the Reduction to Pure Subjectivity

#### Lecture 44: Positional and Quasi-Positional Acts and Their Reduction; Epoché and Quasi-Epoché

Before the peculiarities of the reduction of depictive acts, Husserl takes up reproductive phantasy, with its figures floating before us in naive imagining, some arising involuntarily, some formed freely. Though we speak of "images" of phantasy, there is no pictorial presentation here, no distinction of depicting and depicted, image and subject. In both cases something non-present is conscious in a present experience. But in depiction the image is a spatial semblance; in phantasy the so-called image is not a semblance at all. A semblance is something represented as bodily present, not believed to exist, but appearing as if it were there: a fiction, but not a fiction of reproductive phantasy. Phantasy is not a presenting but a presentifying representation, and in this resembles memory. Memory, however, includes belief in the remembered, while the imagined is conscious only in the character "as if" it were. What floats before me in phantasy does not count as a representation of something else, just as in memory the past is not an image depicting something. It is no objection that the remembered or the imagined may itself be a picture, as when I imagine a sacred grove with a statue of a god. The analysis of Hua XXIII is summarized in three contrasts: depiction presents through a present semblance; phantasy presentifies without any present image; memory presentifies with belief. The rejection of an "image theory" of phantasy and memory is crucial for what follows, since the reduction of phantasy will not pass through an image but through an implied, imagined perceiving.

Phantasy and reality can be separate or mixed: the act-consciousness in which reality counts for me (perceived, remembered, judged, valued, shaped in action) and that in which a fictive reality is there for me, fictive presence, past, judgments, valuations, actions, conscious in the modified way of "as if". Mixtures occur whenever I imagine something into my perceived or believed surroundings, as when I imagine nymphs dancing here before us, or adventures on a walk through a tropical jungle. There can also be pure phantasy without co-operating consciousness of reality, where all such consciousness is out of action: in a self-loss in which not even my body and my immediate surroundings get attention, I live wholly in the world of "as if", and all my perceiving, thinking, feeling, acting is activity "as if", as when I am completely lost in dream in the jungle adventures. Then the phantasies are pure and I am in my object-field only as ego in the phantasy. In the other case, where I only re-imagine reality and perhaps imagine myself active in the fictive surroundings, there is a mixture, and as far as I belong to the phantasy, I myself am a mixture, a re-imagined ego some of whose actual content is untouched. Pure phantasy is a structural analogue of the epoché: in it "all consciousness of reality is out of action". But the analogue is misleading, and the next paragraphs show why: in pure phantasy the ego is lost in another quasi-world, not reflecting on its consciousness.

Now the reduction. Acts in which phantasy formations, things, people, myself as acting, appear "as if" are performed naively at first, but can be grasped during or after by the reflecting ego through splitting. Here too I can establish myself as phenomenological observer interested purely in the experience and its intentional contents. As with memory and every presentifying act, remarkable nestings arise for the reduction. If I imagine a landscape with centaurs, the reduction should yield my pure act-experience, in which the intentional objects are grasped purely as such, exactly as characterized. As phenomenologist I do not really imagine; the reflected ego does. I am not the dreaming ego devoted to the dreamt but the observer of dreams and dreamt, of imagining and imagined as such. As spectator of a real act I bracket the existence, value, and work-character of its objects, so now I bracket, on the "as-if" objects, the "as if" and whatever is posited under it. The "as if" is a modifying sign under which being and its modalities, values, works, and ego-acts of every kind stand. The acts subject to the epoché fall, in a division covering all acts, into two great classes, corresponding member for member so exactly that to each act of one we can assign one of the other almost as a repeating copy, though it is no mere repetition. One is an act simply, unmodified, conferring on its theme validity as actual; its parallel confers on the same content validity "as if", that of a phantasied object, value, work. Correlatively objects divide into objects simply, meant as actualities, and quasi-objects, meant as mere fictions; a world simply means an actual world, but there are phantasy worlds given in the modification of "as if". Developed phenomenology calls the first acts positional and the second quasi-positional. Imagining, as an accomplishment modifying simple validity, is itself a mode of validity, and this accomplishment of the naively imagining ego must also be bracketed to obtain the pure content of the phantasy experience. The terminology comes from Ideen I §§ 111–114, where neutrality modification and phantasy are distinguished. The decisive thesis is that "as if" is itself a validity, so the epoché cannot stop at suspending belief. It must suspend quasi-belief too. This rules out a tempting identification of the epoché with imagining: to imagine a world is to posit it "as if", which is still a stance, whereas the epoché suspends even that.

How does bracketing reach into the imagined contents? Taking an example, certain implications essential to the intentionality of "as if" appear at once. I imagine a landscape with trees, people, centaurs, and fabulous beasts in furious combat. I may belong to this world of phantasy, fighting alongside; or I may not be in the game. But even then I am in a way necessarily co-imagined, since I could not imagine this piece of a phantasy world determinately without imagining it in a determinate orientation. Some trees are in the foreground, some in the background, some right, some left; a centaur gallops toward me, a dragon swoops down on it from above. Right, left, front, back, from above are occasional expressions, essentially related to the observing, perceiving ego that carries the zero point of oriented space and all its dimensions, the space in which any piece of world can appear only in orientation. The argument for the co-imagined ego is from orientation: every intuited space is oriented around a "here", and a "here" is the location of a perceiving body. So an imagined scene implies an imagined perceiver at its zero point. The zero point of orientation is the theme of Ideen II § 18 and Thing and Space; here it becomes the key to the structure of presentification.

I find this not as the dream-lost imagining ego, unaware of itself, following the combat, but in the reflective phenomenological attitude of the uninvolved theoretical observer of myself as imagining and of the imagined contents as such. And I find that the intentionality is not simple, as if there were only the act of imagining and in it the centaur scene as its simple object in the mode "as if". I find the object conscious in a peculiar mediacy: first as intentional object of my perceiving, not my real perceiving but my necessarily co-imagined perceiving, whose subject therefore belongs to the phantasy world. The act "I imagine a centaur scene" is possible only if I perform, "as if", the act "I perceive the centaur scene". Imagining straightforwardly, my look rests only on the combat; in the pregnant sense only it is the fiction, especially in free imagining. But that it stands before me as my theme proper is possible only because I am lost in myself not only as now imagining ego but also, "as if", as the co-imagined subject of the perception of the combat. Imagining oneself into a naive perceiving is a mode of self-forgetting in phantasy. As only its object, what it perceives, stands in the look of the phantasy-ego, so what the phantasy-ego perceives in phantasy stands immediately in the look of the subject actually performing the phantasy, as its imagined object. The look of the ego in phantasy is a look "as if"; its perceiving and perceived bear the modification. But all this in its modifying validity is there for the real ego now imagining. As long as it imagines naively it finds thematically only the object of the imagined act. But it can change attitude at any time: within the phantasy it can turn from the centaur combat to its oriented modes of givenness, to its modes of appearance in general, to the perceiving ego and its whole act, and so, by reflective turns, obtain the perceptual act "as if" for the perceptual object "as if", now ready for thematic observation. Described otherwise: imagining naively, I had the combat as fiction and was co-imagined as perceiving subject, but self-lost, not reflecting. Now I re-imagine my co-imagined ego as one reflecting on itself; but I do this in actual imagining without modifying the sense of the phantasy reality, without making another phantasy reality out of it, by producing in phantasy the reflections I could have performed as phantasy-ego and through which my previously latent act would have been disclosed. This is Husserl's "intentional implication" doctrine in its canonical form: to imagine X is to quasi-perceive X, and the quasi-perceiving has its own quasi-ego. The reflection within phantasy is itself a quasi-reflection; the phenomenologist reaches the imagined perceiving only by re-imagining the phantasy-ego as reflecting. This nested structure explains the "double reduction" of memory in Lecture 39 and anticipates Lecture 47's theory of iterated implication.

All this is itself a piece of phenomenological consideration of what is really experienced in straightforward imagining and belongs to its pure content. It would also have to be worked out as belonging to the sense of the imagining accomplishment that in the example the quasi-perceiving phantasy-ego is necessarily present yet not counted as part of the phantasy reality, as against cases where it is. I am present explicitly, as belonging to the sense of the image, when I include myself as participant or fighter. Two ways of being in a phantasy are distinguished: as its implicit perceiver (always) and as an explicit character in it (sometimes). The distinction will reappear in Section Four between the ego that functions and the ego that is objectivated.

Note especially how the phenomenological epoché, as inhibiting all interested actions of the naive ego, is performed not only on the present real ego as imagining but also within the phantasy world, on the co-imagined subjectivity belonging to it and its naivety; or rather, only through the epoché under the sign of "as if" is the epoché performed on the action of the imagining ego. Nothing can float before me as phantasy, as quasi-reality, unless the ego belongs to its unity as co-imagined ego, the subject for whom this "reality" is what it is from the "validities" its acts have "performed". This holds generally and necessarily. Imagining is behaving as if one had a reality, as if one perceived, thought, valued, acted thus. This holds whether realities or idealities are imagined, if the latter are thinkable without co-imagined realities at all. Behaving "as if" is from the start to re-imagine oneself in imagining, to have oneself as a fiction; only it belongs to imagining naivety that the fiction of the imagined object is graspable as fiction in the pregnant sense before the co-imagined ego whose intentional content alone it can be. Going back to the phenomenologically pure therefore means first producing the pure structures of the intentional objectivity actually present, going back to the intentionally nested subjectivity and its acts and objects and grasping them purely under the sign of "as if". To every pure positional act and its contents corresponds the parallel pure quasi-positional act only in that its purity contains, intentionally, an exact mirror image of the same pure positional act with all its contents, under the sign of "as if". The reduction is achieved by performing the described reflection "in" phantasy and the phenomenological epoché under the sign of "as if" on the acts in the phantasy world. This implies a quasi-accomplishment mirrored into the phantasy world, a quasi-epoché. Every real epoché I practise on my phantasy and my imagining contains intentionally a quasi-epoché, as if I, the imagined ego, reflected and performed phenomenological exclusion. I can also say: performing my real reduction, I place myself for a moment, forgetting the actual present, on the ground of the phantasy world, taking it as if it were real. Then I must take its ego-acts as if they were real positings with real objects; exclude what would belong to the being of the objects if they were real, exclude every interest I would have on this ground in objects of being, value, and so on; to gain what would belong to these acts as such and remains after excluding all questions of right and truth that could be raised on this ground. Since in the phantasy world I have and can have no objects but those of the acts of my co-imagined ego, I thereby, and only thereby, obtain the full and pure intentional objectivity of my phantasy act. The "quasi-epoché" is the lecture's conceptual novelty. Because the phantasy world has its own quasi-ego with quasi-positings, a full reduction of phantasy must be performed twice: really on the imagining and quasi within the imagined. This matters beyond phantasy. It explains how eidetic variation, which works in phantasy, can yield essences of transcendental life: the phenomenologist can imagine himself as a phenomenologist and practise the quasi-epoché on imagined consciousness, which is how the Cartesian Meditations (§ 34) make pure possibility, not only actuality, the field of transcendental phenomenology.

#### Lecture 45: The Natural World-Life of the Ego as Act-Subject and the Unnaturalness of Phenomenologically Pure Self-Reflection. On the Course of Thought

Husserl has been told the last lectures were felt to be very difficult. Not without reason: principled general grounds are at work, and he interrupts the systematic course to speak of them. The lecture is a pedagogical interlude, but its content, why phenomenology is hard, is itself a phenomenological thesis about the natural attitude.

A first theoretical penetration into the intimacies of pure subjectivity must be very difficult, precisely because it concerns actually lived life in its being-for-itself and being-in-itself. Ego-life means being related in consciousness to objects, especially through specific acts in which the waking ego is directed at objects and occupied with them knowingly, valuingly, acting. In natural life, before the motives that force the passage into the phenomenological attitude are at work, everyone knows of his ego-life and its relations to real and ideal objects, from natural reflection. But natural reflection can never yield knowledge of pure subjectivity, or even a hint of it. It always has objects through prior, retained objective knowledge and relates the reflectively grasped ego as act-subject to them, apprehending the ego itself as objectively human. As long as it rules exclusively, it cannot see that every having of objects and every determination through which they are there for the ego is already an accomplishment of the ego and its conscious life, that the ego in its own essential life and doing, in sensing, thinking, valuing, creating, constantly brings about in and for itself the appearance and validity of objects. Consciousness itself, intentional life as it lives in itself, how from purely internal motivations subjective apperceptions with characters of subjective validity grow up, necessarily remains hidden: this whole life through which my world, things, people, values, works, actions, communities, are there for me as at one stroke, though, looked at more closely, there for me only in communication with others, so that we are referred to the community of all egos and their intersubjective, unified, constituting life. Natural reflection, however attentive, reaches only the ego-in-relation-to-objects, never the accomplishment by which objects are there. The final clause is an important admission within the course: the world is there for me only through communication with others, and constituting life is intersubjective. Methodological solipsism thus rests on a subjectivity that is in fact communal, which Lectures 53–54 will make explicit.

This pure life can be opened to observing and theoretically determining work only through the phenomenological epoché, which conditions a new universal way of looking, a new kind of reflection of the ego on itself and all its worlds as worlds of its conscious activity. Only through it does the pure ego and the whole realm of pure subjectivity become visible and describable. It is an entirely "unnatural" attitude. Natural life is an original, at first absolutely necessary devotion to the world, a being-lost in the world. The unnatural is radical and pure self-reflection, on the pure "I am", pure ego-life, and the ways in which whatever gives itself as objective gains this sense and mode of validity purely from the inner accomplishment of this life. "Unnatural" is not a reproach. World-loss is original and necessary, which is why the natural attitude is not an error to be corrected but a starting point to be reflected on; the transcendental attitude presupposes it.

So the difficulty of first penetrating pure subjectivity and systematically carrying out the pure "Know thyself", from which, as will become ever clearer, all philosophy flows, is intelligible. Any instruction for however complicated activity on the ground of natural knowledge can be followed relatively easily, since each step asks only for something typically familiar. From childhood, and this was the achievement of childhood development, we have learned the typology of the natural world in all directions. We stood constantly in motivations through which all kinds of consciousness and ego-acts could come into play and a typically familiar world could form for us, of things, values, and ourselves as human beings acting in and depending on it, moved by joy and suffering. Gradually we came to exercise all main kinds of objective activity, experiencing, knowing, scientific knowing, practical shaping. All this is familiar at least in type, and what one cannot do at once one can learn by following instructions. The Delphic "Know thyself" returns as the formula of phenomenology, as at the end of the Cartesian Meditations. The concept of "typology" acquired in childhood is Husserl's genetic account of the natural attitude: the world is pre-given as a structure of familiar types built up by sedimented experience, the thesis of Experience and Judgment §§ 8 and 83.

The instructions of phenomenology have no model in the life-experience of the individual or of history and rely on no elementary familiarities. Toward the worlds of pure subjectivity, our original life through which all natural being and validity has its self-evidence, we are at first like a man born blind whose cataract has been removed and who must learn to see from scratch. The successful operation does not yet make him see, that is, apprehend the spatial world in its familiar forms and visual features; visual apperceptions must first form within the inner motivations of apperception. So even once the idea of the phenomenological attitude is grasped, the method must be exercised in many ways, systematically, on subjectivity related naturally-objectively to real and ideal worlds and on the universal typology of its objective data and its objective-subjective human life. We must learn to see the universe of pure subjectivity and know it systematically, so that it stands before us as a world of its own, familiar at least in its most general typology, as the physical world is to beginners in physics as a realm of spatial experience known since childhood. The Molyneux case (the cataract patient) is used against the idea that the reduction yields immediate insight. Performing the epoché is like the operation; seeing the transcendental field requires the formation of a new typology through practice. The implication is that transcendental experience, like natural experience, has a genesis.

So before any science of phenomenological subjectivity lies a great task the naive sciences could not have. They have a ready, familiar world of experience before science; phenomenology must first work out its phenomenological world of experience, the world of phenomenological subjectivity, freely surveyable and familiar in its typology. The phenomenologist must first learn to see phenomenologically and procure the typology of the forms belonging to any phenomenological subjectivity. Given the necessary priority of natural "world-childhood", whoever wants to become a phenomenologist must systematically free himself from world-childhood and practise the reduction on all types of worldly experiencing, representing, thinking, and living, and on all correlative types of worldly existence: the systematic epoché by which everything worldly is raised into pure subjectivity, transcendentally spiritualized. With this the natural child, the child of the world, becomes the phenomenological child, the child in the realm of pure spirit. The editor refers to Beilage XVIII on the sense of the phenomenological reduction. "World-childhood" (Weltkindschaft) and "phenomenological child" recast the reduction as a second birth, with a New Testament overtone (John 3). "Transcendentally spiritualized" does not mean dematerialized: the worldly is kept, but as sense within subjectivity.

For the beginning philosopher these difficulties are increased because the pure ego, even when seen as subject of the intentional life in which all its objectivities are shaped, contains unsuspected, deeply hidden mediacies of intentional implication, without unravelling which pure life remains unintelligible. Unnoticed, even after grasping the necessity and sense of the epoché, one remains under the spell of natural habits of thought and treats the new realm of phenomenological data as an analogue of objective data. Since the phenomenologically subjective also gives itself as individual temporal being, one takes it as being like objective-temporal existence, most readily as an analogue of physical spatial existence. That is an error. Phenomenological analysis is not at all an analogue of analysis of objective things. The observer does not find pure life as an interweaving of "elements" into totalities, or totalities decomposable into coexisting and successive elements unified in abstractively discernible forms. Whatever intentionality we consider, deeper study shows that a concrete intention is possible only through a nesting of intentional, otherwise non-independent accomplishments, with a hidden nesting of intentional objects. Only through such intentional analysis do we realize that subjectivity is absolutely unique, without equal among objects foreign to the ego, and that phenomenological analysis has, in method and matter, a totally different sense from natural-objective analyses of nature. A note adds that it makes no difference whether one takes the ancients' analysis of nature or the moderns' causal and functional analysis. This is Husserl's critique of the "psychic chemistry" of associationist and elementist psychology (Wundt, Mach, the Brentano school's descriptive psychology in its atomistic forms), and of his own earlier temptation to it in the Logical Investigations' talk of real "contents". Intentional analysis is not decomposition but explication of implications: a phantasy "contains" an imagined perceiving not as a part but as an implied sense. This is the methodological basis of the claim that subjectivity is sui generis, and the reason Husserl insists (against Natorp) that phenomenological description is possible at all.

A piece of such unexpected analysis of intentional implication occupied us in the analysis of phantasy. Instead of repeating it, shorter parallel analyses, easier after the exercise, will bring out the essentials. But first Husserl recalls Herbart's pedagogical doctrine of the right alternation and tempo of absorption and reflection, without which the learner cannot build the unity of an intellectual form from single moments and make it a free possession. Where absorption in new and difficult trains of thought exhausts all powers, reflection is doubly needed to keep the general plan in view, in which these thoughts are only functioning parts. So, having left the systematic course for today anyway, Husserl adds a retrospective and prospective reflection on the course of thought, which may also strengthen interest in the next steps. Herbart's terms (Vertiefung and Besinnung) describe a rhythm of immersion and survey, and the lecture enacts it. The recapitulation that follows is thus part of the method: a reflection on the reflection, in keeping with the Section's title.

We first tried to realize the deepest sense of the Cartesian beginning of a universal, absolutely justified science, constructing the pure, truly grounded path to transcendental subjectivity only hinted at in Descartes' first Meditations. Its first guiding principle was: what I cannot negate or doubt at all shall count for me, sharpened at once into the principle of apodictic indubitability. In retrospect Husserl thinks this sharpening from natural to apodictic indubitability could have been introduced later. Trying, guided by the vague natural principle, to appropriate the always and indubitably given being of the world, which seems to lead toward an empirical world-science, one notices the difference between empirical indubitability, the empirical impossibility of giving up belief in the harmoniously experienced world, and another indubitability excluding every possible doubt in a new sense, the apodictic. Every sensory experience leaves open that further experience motivates doubt about what was completely certain; doubt is conceivable, though without empirical grounds, and the non-being of what is empirically indubitable is never excluded. Only then, perhaps, does the principle of apodictic evidence emerge as the philosophical guiding principle. But in another way of considering, which need not be epistemologically interested, it appears that the whole world need not be although and while it is harmoniously experienced, and, not in radical purity, this is essentially Descartes' path. The possible epoché regarding the whole world of experience makes visible, in reflection on the knowing subject, the pure subjectivity that remains even if the world were not. This subjectivity was then extended beyond the present into past and future, to the pure subject of transcendental life endless in both directions. The self-criticism reorders Sections One and Two: the apodictic principle should have been derived from the critique of experience, not laid down before it. It also notes that the possible non-being of the world can be seen without any epistemological interest, which weakens the link between the reduction and the quest for certainty and prepares the psychological way.

#### Lecture 46: Reshaping and Deepening of the Phenomenological Method: The Cartesian Way and the Psychologist's Way to the Transcendental Reduction

What emerged as pure subjectivity was not yet shown to be apodictically indubitable. Excluding the world because it is not apodictically given only directed the gaze to the universe of a pure subjectivity given in a new kind of experience, transcendental seeing. The critique of its apodicticity was required but postponed. That was the first, Cartesian way to the transcendental ego and its apodictic critique still to be performed. The recapitulation now names the method "the Cartesian way" and records its unfinished business, which will be taken up in Lecture 53.

The next need, to know transcendental subjectivity in its particular forms and types of transcendental life so that "ego cogito" would not remain an empty word, we satisfied in a way that step by step built a new way to the ego cogito. The guiding thought: what we retained under the possible hypothesis that the world of experience does not exist, the subjective life that would still be, we also retain if as reflecting subjects we refrain from co-positing the world and from any position on its being, non-being, or other modalities. In short, if we inhibit every interest in the being of the world, transcendental subjectivity enters our field of view. The equivalence is the crux of the second way: abstention yields the same field as the hypothesis of annihilation. The Cartesian detour through non-being can therefore be dropped.

This suggests a new, apparently simpler path. Is it not enough, without the lengthy critique of world-experience and the evidence of the world's possible non-existence, to apply the epoché of the uninterested self-observer directly to single acts, perhaps adding that I perform this bracketing on all my acts at once, which should give me my pure subjectivity? The editor points to two critical notes on this paragraph. "Apparently simpler" is the warning: the second way has its own difficulty, universalization, which the phrase "all my acts at once" glosses over.

We tried to carry out this idea. We said: set aside the earlier course. Start from the naive ego performing acts and naturally related to their objects. Without thinking of transcendental subjectivity or having any idea of it, we can perform on each single act, in an easily intelligible way, an epoché similar to the one performed on the world and world-experience on the Cartesian way. A further critical note concerns this sentence. The second way starts from the natural ego and a local operation, not from a global hypothesis. Its advantage is intelligibility; its danger is that the result stays local.

We separated the ego naively performing an act from the reflecting ego above it and showed that the latter, reflecting naturally, can also become uninterested. We clarified interest, first as experiential and cognitive, then as interest of feeling and will. Excluding all participation in the reflected ego's interest meant excluding every adoption of its positings and validities, and also inhibiting all practical intentions of the reflecting ego to criticize them and seek true being. What the theoretically attuned reflecting ego finds in such epoché is the phenomenologically pure act, pure in a first, still imperfect, not yet transcendental sense; what is gained is, as predicted and to be clarified, only phenomenological purity in the sense of an empirical psychology. The editor reports that Husserl wrote "No!" beside these two sentences and later struck out "not yet transcendental". Husserl's own hesitation is recorded here: he later rejected the claim that the single-act epoché yields only psychological purity. The disagreement concerns whether the purity of the reduced act is already transcendental, lacking only universality, or merely psychological, framed by an unbracketed world. His later view (Beilage XIX, "The second form of the phenomenological reduction", and the Crisis's "way through psychology") treats the psychological reduction as convertible into the transcendental by a shift in the sense of the whole, not by adding content.

We continued along this line, taking main types of acts one by one, reducing them to pure contents, exhibiting their wonderfully interwoven intentionality, and so bringing the peculiarity of subjective being and accomplishment in each type to first understanding. Such analyses are needed to show that the phenomenological method opens a realm of experience new relative to ordinary experience. The editor refers to Beilage XIX. The analyses of Lectures 41–44 are thus justified as the demonstration that phenomenological experience has a content of its own, not as exercises.

Husserl gives a preview of how this reduction of single acts of the empirical human ego is to open a path to transcendental subjectivity, making experienceable the ego that would remain even if the whole world did not exist, and so my body did not, and there were no talk of an ego in the ordinary sense, a human being. The target of the second way is explicitly the same as the first: the ego independent of the world's existence.

Instead of performing the reduction as reflecting ego on single acts and acts-connections, in the direction of what each act posits as valid, I constitute myself as transcendental-phenomenological ego, as the subject who practises transcendental-phenomenological reductions and makes his own transcendental subjectivity the open, endless field of his phenomenological experience and research. What does that mean? I become this transcendental observer, and my epoché becomes transcendental, by being universally comprehensive and radical in a sense the previous psychological reduction did not know. If I reduce a single "I perceive", "I remember, imagine, think, desire" to its pure subjective content (the psychological one), I still have much else at the same time. It remained to show that this reduction and purity attainable on the single act leave open a higher, new achievement of purity, and that an extension of the epoché into one that not only embraces everything but also, as it were, absorbs everything psychological must lead to the transcendental reduction; indeed to one reaching further than the one first gained on the Cartesian way. For on this way I gain not only myself, the subject of the reducing, as transcendental ego: including others' subjectivity in the method, I gain transcendental intersubjectivity, the transcendental totality of egos, the transcendental community of transcendentally grasped individual egos. The editor refers to Beilage XX, a critique of the two stages in which Husserl had reached the idea of the reduction in 1907 and 1910. The elliptical "I still have much else" means: reducing a single act leaves the rest of my life, and the world in which it is embedded, in unbracketed validity; hence the purity is only psychological. Two conditions define the transcendental epoché: universality (it embraces all acts at once) and radicality (it "absorbs" the psychological, i.e. suspends the apperception of the reduced acts as states of a human soul). The promise that the psychological way reaches further than the Cartesian one, because it includes others, is its main advantage: empathy, bracketed, yields the other's transcendental life as implied in mine, whereas the Cartesian way ended in solipsism (Lecture 36). The reference to 1907 and 1910 points to The Idea of Phenomenology and the Basic Problems of Phenomenology lectures of 1910/11, where Husserl first extended the reduction to intersubjectivity.

So our at times difficult analyses were not an exercise in dispensable single analyses, as if we had lost sight of the course of a philosophy in the making. We were in a strictly systematic course of reshaping and deepening the method, for which these analyses were required. Their single reductions supplied the foundation stones over which, from a soon intelligible basic thought, the higher, transcendental-universal reduction is to arch. The image of an arch built over foundation stones states the relation of the two reductions: the psychological analyses are not superseded but supported upon.

Husserl proposes to resume the old investigation and bring it to an end in brief and not too difficult strokes. The remainder of the lecture closes the analysis of presentifications before Section Four takes up the universalization.

The aim of the interrupted investigation was to show, on main types of presentifying acts (recollection, expectation, depiction, reproductive phantasy), that their intentional relation is not simple, as it first appears and as usually expressed. Apparently a remembered past in memory, an expected future in expectation, a depicted object in depiction, a fiction in phantasy are presentified as simply as the perceived in perception. It is not so. For memory and expectation this was shown briefly on the first, Cartesian reduction; for phantasy in careful, difficult analysis. In every such presentification one must distinguish the straightforward direction of the look, at what is in the primary sense presentified and at first alone visible, from a hidden act and act-look, itself presentified, whose ego is the necessary correlate of the presentified object, a presentified ego. To be conscious of a landscape in phantasy is not as simple as to perceive one: a co-imagined ego belongs to it, not I who am here now but a phantasy-modification of myself, quasi in the landscape, perceiving it in a certain mode of appearance. This imagined ego and its imagined seeing belong necessarily to imagining the landscape, which is not conscious in simple intentionality like one really perceived. Likewise the memory of yesterday's walk seems simple, as if I had only the present ego and the memory-image of the walk, conscious only in subjectively altered fashion. But the past as remembered can be conscious only as perceived by me, so that I am present in the reproduced past as reproduced ego, not as I am now but as I was, having lived through this and that. Likewise for expectations, with a necessarily co-posited ego and act in which the coming event will be, or would be, perceptual presence. The general law of presentification is now stated: every presentification implies a presentified perceiving and a presentified ego. It prepares the argument of Lecture 47 that intentional implication, not the psychological reduction of single acts, is what drives the reduction toward universality, since a remembered perceiving carries with it its whole horizon of a past world and past life.

## Section Four: Phenomenological Psychology, Transcendental Phenomenology, and Phenomenological Philosophy

### Chapter One: Achievement and Problems of a Phenomenological-Psychological Reduction

#### Lecture 47: Intentional Implications and Iterations

It must be made clear everywhere that, if the remembered or expected event is conscious only as perceived in a remembered past, or to be perceived in an expected future, every such intentional content includes a purely subjective content: the purely subjective of the implied past or future perception. So the reduction of a memory or expectation requires carrying it into the remembered past or expected future: placing myself naively into the past, I must reflectively grasp the past ego and its past act, and all acts it is conscious of having performed, and bring into play the disinterest of theoretical observation that yields the pure content of these acts as acts of the past ego; likewise for the future ego and its acts implied in expecting; and for all acts which, like these presentifications, carry within their intentionality an implied ego with implied acts, besides the present ego actually performing them. In phantasy such an ego is implied "as if", as co-imagined ego acting in the phantasy world. The purely phenomenological element gained from implied imagined acts is itself only imagined. Still, the reduction here achieves what must be achieved: it shows how every phantasy, as a present real act of the actual imagining ego, is conscious of its phantasy objects, and how to everything imagined as objective corresponds an imagined pure subjectivity through which alone the objective can appear and count for the subject even in phantasy. The reduction must "travel" into implied subjectivities. The last sentence is a thesis of transcendental correlation extended to the possible: even an imagined object requires an imagined constituting life. This is why eidetic phenomenology of consciousness can operate in phantasy without losing correlation.

The implications multiply because simple presentification and phantasy, like the more complicated depiction and indication (by marks), admit stacked repetition, iteration. Instead of a simple recollection, a recollection of a recollection, and later a recollection of having had a recollection of a recollection. A depiction can depict a depiction that itself depicts: a drawing of a statue, then a reproduction of the drawing. A phantasy can be a phantasy of a phantasy, as when I dream myself into a phantasy world and in it fall into imagining; ideally repeatable too. Iteration is essential because it shows the implied subjectivities form series of arbitrary depth, each with its own ego-modification, so that the reduced field has an internal structure of mediation.

Beyond iteration within one kind of act, the different modifying kinds overlap: memories of expectations, expectations of memories, phantasies of memories; and every iteration of one modification can pass into the intentionality of any other. Phantasy is the title of a universal modification opposed to all non-imagining, positional acts; to every positional act corresponds ideally a phantasy act. So all phenomenological results gained by reducing positional acts, simple or iterated memories, expectations, transfer to phantasy with the modification "as if". What is needed is the general exhibition of the peculiarities phantasy as such produces, and the systematic reduction of the basic kinds of positional acts and the typology of their iterations. The combinatorics establishes that the reduced field is closed under the modifications: phantasy doubles everything, and the phenomenology of the actual carries over to the possible. Eidetic phenomenology is thereby grounded in the structure of the field itself.

The acts considered so far contain implied acts, but acts of the same ego. When I remember, I as remembering subject am not in the act's intentional content, yet in a modification, that of my remembered past, I belong to it: I, the same who now remember, was there. Now a new and remarkable form of act, to whose accomplishment presentifications contribute: so-called empathy. Through it I, as ego, am conscious in a kind of perception of the bodily presence of other subjects, alter egos, by understanding into certain things, "other bodies", a psychic life belonging to them and co-present. Bodies are things in which psychic being and life "express" themselves; perceiving them I experience this expression and through it the other's psychic life as expressed, announced as co-present. I see the psychic in them feature by feature, though I cannot see it itself or ever really perceive it. There is here an original indication drawing its force from the perceptual presence of my body interwoven with my psychic life and from the typical similarity of the other body, first as physical, to mine. This similarity motivates a presentifying function, an indication of similar psychic life. But this is not indicated as in memory or expectation, which would allow identifying the co-presentified ego-subject with me; it is an ego, but another. The peculiar structure of this perception, which owes a main part of its accomplishment to presentification, demands an ego and yet not me as implied subject; it creates my consciousness that another ego faces me, without which "other ego" or "fellow human" would be a word without sense. The doctrine of Lecture 35 is recast in terms of intentional implication. Empathy is a presentification that implies an ego not identifiable with me, whereas memory implies an ego identifiable with me. This non-identity is the phenomenological definition of otherness, and the claim that "other ego" would be meaningless without it rules out treating the concept of other minds as a hypothesis or inference.

Unravelling the implications of empathy and extracting their contents reductively, we meet something new in principle. As we can practise the reduction on our own acts, present and implied, real and imagined, so we can on the other's acts conscious to us through empathy. Placing ourselves into them, living into their act-life, we can perform reflection and epoché in them as if we were they, and so exhibit the purely subjective, the other's, for each of their perceptions, memories, expectations, phantasies, just as with our past or imagined ego. The editor refers to a critical note. This is the decisive step of the second way beyond the Cartesian one: the other's pure subjectivity becomes accessible through the reduction of empathy. The Cartesian way had to bracket others as worldly beings; the psychological way reduces the empathic act and finds, implied in it, another pure subjectivity.

The situation is more complicated for empathically given acts, since this is an intentional modification of what is given in me more originally, through the peculiar indication by the experienced body. The modification "alter" is an intentional character that correspondingly modifies all conceivable kinds of my own acts with their implications. All intentional contents I might find in myself could meet me in this modification, as contents of another's acts. Essentially, experiences of every kind I have lived or could imagine could be understood into an alter ego. This holds for empathies too, which can express themselves through the other's body. So I can experience another as someone who experiences a third as "other", who experiences a fourth, and so on, everyday cases of intentional implication. This too is an iterable modification: the modification "alter", in which a subject and its life are given modified as object of an empathic act, as against the original unmodified self-perception, can be stacked. Something psychic experienced as empathized within an empathized has this intentional mediacy. "Alter" is treated as a modification like "past" or "as if": a universal operator on the whole range of my possible experiences. The iteration of empathy (I experience you experiencing him) is the structural root of social reality, spelled out next.

Analogously a recollected thing has, compared with a perceived one, the modified character "was perceived" or "past in memory"; the object of a recollection of a recollection has an iterated character referring to two presents, actual and past. The same is given in immediate and mediate intentionality and identifiable as the same: I remember something and at once remember it as what I remembered yesterday. Likewise I experience something, and at once experience that my neighbour is directed at the same, that a third person observes this neighbour so directed. Identity across mediations is the key point: the same object is intended through different chains of implication, and their synthesis is how objectivity is constituted, in time through memories and intersubjectively through empathies.

A special and very important case of empathic experience, in which another is given as experiencing a second, is when I am that second, and this mediate empathic experience coincides with my self-experience: I experience the one facing me as directed experientially at me. On this most original form of reciprocal being-for-one-another rest the most varied I–thou acts and we-acts, themselves empathizable as unities by others and communicative pluralities. So manifold communal life becomes possible, whose peculiarity is that many subjects live not merely side by side, but so that each, through empathic intentionality, has all the others given as his others, co-existing in his field of existence, partly in original experience, partly in determinate or open indeterminate knowledge. More: immediately or mediately, actually or as practical possibility, each is in social connection with all these others through communicative, specifically social acts, I–thou and we-acts, "dealing" or able to deal with them, experiencing personal effects from them and exerting them; all within his own, and everyone's, intentionality, so that "everyone" knows himself as member of a personal community of effects extending into indefinite distances, and finally of a humanity. Reciprocity, being seen as seeing, is the origin of the social act. The analysis of Ideen II § 51 and the Kaizo articles on social acts is compressed here. The phrase "all within his own intentionality" matters: society is constituted in each ego, which is what allows the reduction of each ego to yield an intersubjective field and what will ground the "monadology" of Lecture 54.

One more class of acts: reflection itself, including the acts of phenomenologically reducing reflection. As in a simple perception I can bracket the perceptual object and, reflecting, take it only as perceived of the pure perception, so I can bracket the object of the phenomenologically reflecting act and seek its pure content. The act of phenomenological reflection by which I obtain the perception as pure experience has this pure experience, this phenomenological datum, as object. Nothing prevents inhibiting my interest in its being and establishing, as before, an exclusive interest in fixing the pure experience of the epoché just performed on the perception. This happens in a second-level reflection with a second-level epoché. What was done on perception can be done on any act, and so we reach, by iteration, at least ideally infinite series of stacked reductions. The reduction applies to itself. This is the formal basis of the "phenomenology of the phenomenological reduction" and of Fink's "transcendental spectator" theory in the Sixth Cartesian Meditation.

Such series, starting from any simple reduction, exhibit sequences of pure data founded on one another; but higher-level results do not improve or complete lower ones. What I have exhibited as purely subjective loses and gains nothing when I notice that the exhibiting is itself an act, which, if I want it as a pure act, requires a further reduction. These acts of method are not there in advance but set in play by me, and what is remarkable is only that they can be set in play again and again, as with every iteration. If I want to delimit a field in which only the purely subjective occurs, the pure data of all reflective levels belong to it equally; and I am not required, whenever I fix a particular datum, to perform an infinite regress of reductions, as if higher reflections had to complete it and it were not already pure and complete in itself. The regress feared in Lecture 31 is defused. Iterability is an open possibility of further themes, not a condition for the validity of the first; the reduced act is already fully given. This distinguishes Husserl's position from theories on which self-knowledge would need an infinite hierarchy to be secured, and it removes an objection to the apodictic critique of reflection.

#### Lecture 48: The Problem of the Transition from the Psychological Reduction of Single Acts to the Universal Phenomenological Epoché and Reduction

Now the single analyses must be used, first by asking whether a phenomenological "beyond" can be seen from them. We know of one, "transcendental subjectivity", from the Cartesian way. This knowledge is to serve only as a means for criticizing the achievement of single reductions performed on the ground of the natural attitude, and for awakening the decisive thought that could overcome their inadequacy. It gives a guiding motive for inventing the constructive complement of the new method; the motive does not belong to the method itself, which must be grounded in itself. With it we use the long familiar main thought: everything objective that is, was, or will be there for me, that counts for me in any sense as being, can have drawn its sense, mode of appearance, and validity only from certain of my conscious accomplishments. So wherever, in objective connection with something already reduced, I have a remainder not reduced, something objective posited simply rather than as merely intentional object of its act, I do not yet have a really and completely pure subjective. The editor refers to Beilage XXI on the iterations of disclosing the latent. Two methodological scruples: the Cartesian result may guide but not ground the second way, and the criterion of purity is completeness of reduction, i.e. no objectivity left posited simply. The criterion already contains the answer, since the unreduced remainder turns out to be the world itself.

Consider the reductions of various acts from this viewpoint, and suppose all kinds of acts treated so. From the start I was in the natural attitude: I, as psychologist, begin as this human being in the familiar world, not only the real environment I have come to know but ideal "worlds" too: I have learned mathematics and know the world of numbers and types of ideal manifolds, Riemannian, Lobachevskian. No one asked me as psychologist to give up all these worlds. Continuing my reductions from act to act required only certain reductions of them and their objects; however purely I grasped the subjective, I was never asked to put all objectivities of every kind that were and are simply mine out of validity once and for all. Only those posited in the particular acts were bracketed, and only temporarily, to gain the pure contents. If the world with its people remains valid in the background as acquired habitual possession, every act I reduce, a perception, memory, empathy, at once stands there as mine, this human being's psychic act, as soon as I look again at my body. My body is always in my perceptual field, noticed or not; since it has remained valid as constantly there, like the rest of my experienced world, it and the world always have a say. Likewise when I aim at the unified interiority, the unity of the pure soul. The psychologist stands on the ground of the objective apperception "human being" and adopts an abstractive attitude, gaining the component of mere physical body purely and then, conversely, the component "pure soul", but as a component. Every new validity I set in play by new acts interweaves, according to its sense, with the old, merely reactivated validities. So every pure subjective I gain carries with it, untouched by the reduction, an objective component of validity derived from the interweaving of the never-inhibited objective validities. If people are always in my field of validity, their bodies and the acts animating them belong together in unities of validity, and the pure subjective I work out of these acts, mine or others', is the subjective co-existing with this or that body, the pure subjective of this or that human being: his purely psychic, and mine when I reduce my own. This is the precise diagnosis of why the psychological reduction does not reach the transcendental. The reduced act is pure in itself, but its placement is not: it remains "the act of a human being" because body and world remain valid as the frame. Husserl calls the pure soul a "component", the product of an abstraction within the apperception "human being". The mathematical examples show that ideal worlds are included in the unreduced background too.

So what was announced is confirmed: the procedure practised on types of single acts achieves, and can achieve, nothing but exhibiting psychic interiority in its purity and bringing to light the intentional implications hidden in it. The editor refers to Beilage XXII on the reduction to psychological interiority within the natural attitude. The result is limited but positive: a pure psychology, not transcendental phenomenology. Husserl will lecture on this "phenomenological psychology" in 1925 (Hua IX).

This is no small achievement. The phenomenological reduction first achieves what since Locke people thought they could do without methodical art, by simple experience, mere receptivity: purely psychological, so-called inner experience. Our analyses show that experience of the purely psychic, even purely psychic self-perception, is anything but receptivity; it requires the laborious exercise of a specific method, the epoché. The target is the empiricist tradition of introspection (Locke's "reflection", Brentano's inner perception). Pure inner experience is a methodical achievement, not a given, because natural reflection is always contaminated by co-belief in the objects of its acts. Psychology, not only philosophy, needs the epoché, which is the thesis of the Encyclopaedia Britannica article of 1927.

How can this method of psychologically pure experience open a path to transcendental subjectivity, leading beyond viewing human psychic life and pure psychological analysis into seeing transcendentally pure life and transcendental analysis? What modification and heightening of the method is required, what extension of the epoché to absolute universality, is already indicated. The operative phrase is "to absolute universality": what is missing is not a new kind of reflection but the extension of abstention to the whole background.

Consider the whole situation of the reduction so far, which leads to the merely psychologically pure content of an experience and, performed with natural universality, would lead to the purely psychic content of the whole world, the totality of pure souls of all human and animal beings. The editor refers to Beilage XXIII, a critique of the false presentation of the difference between psychological and transcendental reduction in these very lectures. "Natural universality" is the key qualifier: even if every act of every animate being were reduced, the result would be a universe of souls in the world, a pure psychology of the whole world, not transcendental subjectivity. Beilage XXIII shows that Husserl later found his own contrast here misleading, since it suggests that the difference lies in scope rather than in the sense of the epoché.

Starting from and maintaining the natural attitude meant that everything previously valid for me remained valid for me as phenomenologizing subject. The epoché I practised, even as universal, in the general will to subject every act of mine and others to it, left the natural validity of all these acts untouched. Perceptions, mine or others', were inevitably those of my human ego and of other people or animals. The disinterest in the being of perceived objects and of objects posited in other acts was not absolute and radically pure but relative: aimed only at exhibiting what remains of these acts if the perceiving ego were deceived, and what belongs inseparably to their actual being however it stands with their objects. My purpose was to obtain act-experiences of the respective subjects as pure experiences with their pure contents. It was not even said that I, the psychologist, simply put out of play my own belief in these perceived things and values; I did so only relatively, relative to my purpose. So I could keep the house the other sees as real and obtain his pure perception by bracketing its reality: I established that only the intentional object, house of this content and mode of appearance, belongs to his perception, the house as perceived, which remains a moment of the act even if the house did not exist. I did not inhibit absolutely my position on the being of the intentional objects, still less my other positings, my whole habituality of validity, by which the real world is constantly there with its realities, and many ideal realities recognized in my past life remain untouched in my stock of habitual convictions. The distinction between relative and absolute disinterest is the key to the second way. Relative disinterest brackets an object "for the purpose of" describing an act, while still believing in it in another capacity. Husserl stresses the ease with which the psychologist holds both: the other's house is real for me and bracketed for the description of his perceiving. The transcendental epoché cannot be relative in this way.

Now we see how something new is possible that withdraws the whole ground of the natural attitude and puts out of force all validities that create this ground. The editor refers to a critical note. The "ground" is not an object but a system of validities; the reduction must address validity as such, not objects one by one.

Nothing prevents founding a universal epoché as a universal decision of will to inhibit all my interests: those through which not only what counts for me now counts, but through which what counted for me earlier still counts habitually, and through which, on this ground, what will count for me in future would count naturally, were I not to intervene with my epoché. With this universal decision, meant as lastingly valid, I govern my further life: in its steady execution I must put out of play every validity performed or offering itself for performance, denying it the force of counting for me, by bracketing. I say "offering itself", since the universal epoché entering my life-stream changes nothing in its essential structure. I still find myself related not only to particular things, people, numbers, political or ethical ideals I am occupied with, and to personalities bound up with realities, but know myself co-related to a whole world, the real universe to which these things and people belong, and to ideal realms to which the ideal objects of my occupation belong. So the reduction I must practise in carrying out my universal will must reach beyond the particular act; the unravelling of implications leads beyond it of itself, since every object has its objective horizon and every validity its horizon of validity. This points to manifold lines of intentional involvement, for which the demand of reduction holds constantly and must be ever reconsidered. The transcendental epoché is defined as a universal, lasting decision of will, covering past habitual validities and future ones in advance, in line with the voluntarism of Section One: the epoché is a founding (Stiftung) of a new habitus. It leaves the structure of life unchanged (the world still offers itself) and so must be enacted continually on validities as they offer themselves. The bridge from single acts to the universal is the horizon: every act implies the world through its horizon, so reducing any act consistently means reducing the world.

The main role is played by the difference between actual validity, lying in the working performance of acts, and potential, perhaps habitual validity, a special mode that discloses its sense and accomplishment when converted into actuality. This difference is demonstrable before all science and theory, from natural ground, in natural reflection. Correlatively there is the difference between objects thematic in acts and all other objects belonging to the unthematic objective background. The psychological reduction reduced only actual validity; habitual and potential validity, the background, remained in force. A universal reduction must reach potential validity, and the next paragraph shows that potential validity is present in every act as its horizon.

Every object of a perceptual act directed specifically at it carries its unnoticed spatial background. A turn of attention, and one of its objects becomes grasped. "Turning" attention implies that what it turns to was already in the field of consciousness as background, only unnoticed, not the thematic object of an act. So the concrete perceiving act (and in a way every act) has a peculiar structure: foreground consciousness, the specific directedness of ego and act on the theme with all that concerns it, and background consciousness, the horizon of consciousness that makes the spatial background conscious. Horizon-consciousness is an essentially different mode from specific directedness, looking at, grasping, being actively occupied. Turning attention is the passage from the latter mode to the former. An identifying coincidence occurs, a consciousness of unity and sameness, explicated in judgment as the evidence: what I now attend to was already in my perceptual field; it was there, I had only not noticed it. The foreground–background distinction of Ideen I § 35 is invoked as the natural-reflective evidence of potential validity. The background is not unconscious; it is conscious in a different mode, and the coincidence upon turning attention proves it was valid all along. The step from here to the world is taken in the next chapter.

### Chapter Two: Opening the Realm of Transcendental Experience on the Second Way

#### Lecture 49: The Horizons of the Living Streaming Present

Staying with perception: we never have anything perceived without horizon-consciousness, however we delimit it. Not only does a whole thing have its horizon, and each separable part; each perceived thing has, so to speak, its background within itself: it is given only as presenting itself through a visible front, with invisible interior and back. Though we may attend only to the front as what is "properly" perceived, it is not the thing itself or a thing by itself but only that of the perceived thing which falls into proper self-grasping. The invisible is not in the field of the proper perceptual appearance, nor does it become grasped by a mere turn of attention. It is conscious "non-intuitively", perhaps very indeterminately, as with an unknown object; but this intuitively empty consciousness is consciousness, a horizon into which no ray of active directedness aims, though it can at any time. Then we attend to the unseen side, perhaps ask what the thing looks like from behind, wish to see it, walk around it and produce new seeing. Even the most indeterminate consciousness is not empty of sense: at least a spatial thing, something coloured, is meant. A most general pre-delineation is necessarily present, limiting sense, which actual perceiving, through syntheses of identification, fills in and fully depicts. The "inner horizon" is distinguished from the background just treated: turning attention does not fulfil it, only further perceiving does. Pre-delineation (Vorzeichnung) is the term that makes the horizon a structure of sense, not a blank: the unseen back is meant as "some back of a spatial, coloured thing", which constrains fulfilment. This is Experience and Judgment § 8 in first form.

There is also an equally necessary non-intuitive outer horizon. The outer horizon of a perceived object, its spatial surroundings, is not only the unnoticed perceptual field from which the noticed object stands out; within the outer horizon we distinguish the region still perceptually intuitive from the empty horizon continuous with it. What is perceived does not end, for consciousness, where perceiving ends: space goes on endlessly as the field of actual and possible, known and unknown objects. Attention can turn into these distances; the ego can be "affected" from them to direct itself and grasp what is consciously there though not seen, as when we now turn our attention to the vestibule. A moment of horizon-consciousness that always belonged to our perceptions is awakened and depicted in presentifying intuition. The sense with which the space of this room is perceived includes the spatial beyond, the vestibule with its familiar pillars and stairs. This is not explicit in single acts, nor a colourful picture before the mind's eye, all the less since empty consciousness and the affections arising from it are preconditions for full, intuitive object-consciousness. Yet it is a unity of validity with many special validities to be exhibited, all centred on the specific perceptual object, this lecture hall. Penetrating the empty horizons of presentifying intuitions, the same recurs. The example (the vestibule of the Freiburg lecture hall) is pointed: the audience is asked to verify on their own perception that the unseen building is meant with determinate sense. The claim that empty consciousness and "affection" precede intuitive consciousness is the thesis of the Analyses concerning Passive Synthesis on affection: the empty horizon is the field from which objects first attract the ego.

So every actually perceived thing has an empty outer horizon, first a region of determinate pre-indication, from which the perceived refers to what co-exists nearby or is to be expected, conscious only obscurely. This pre-indication can always be made intuitive and explicitly understood as possible experience, here experience to be realized next. What is intuitively presentified counts as experienceable: something I would perceive by continuing my active experience in familiar directions (walking up, around, touching), or that would emerge of itself as expected if I did not disturb the course of experience. In our examples the pre-indicated was attainable relatively directly: spatial nearness is nearness of experience. But co-present objects from far away can also be awakened by recollection; a horizon of familiarity reaches beyond the next street into the street system of our town, and immediate association can awaken these familiarities, which disclose themselves as data of possible, realizable experience. The horizon is defined through the "I can" of kinaesthetic freedom and through association: possibilities are "possibilities of experience" for me. The town example extends the horizon from perception to memory, integrating the temporal and spatial horizons.

However described in detail, these possibilities are not mere possibilities of phantasy; they are borne by a positional consciousness of validity. Attention can turn activatingly into such horizons; then the pre-indicated, perhaps made intuitive, is conscious as co-existing reality, perhaps in a modified mode (conjectural, doubtful, probable), but in some mode of validity. The empty horizon really embraces the whole world, as a horizon, an infinite horizon of possible experience. In activating disclosure, experiencing consciousness is converted into systematically connected series of possible perceiving, in which ever new regions of the world, and in an ideal totality of possible perception all mundane realities, would or must come to perception. This does not mean each perception really contains such an infinity as a real infinity of empty pre-indications and systems of possible perception. Yet the infinite realm of the unknown and undetermined is somehow pre-indicated in every perception, represented in its empty horizon and pre-delineated as valid in an improper way. Besides what is determinately pre-indicated at each moment as co-existing, at least the unified style of flowing possible experience is pre-delineated: that every new experience must bring new pre-delineations, that further experience must fulfil, determine, or re-determine them, that expectations may be disappointed and something unknown take their place, and so on. The infinite space surrounding all experience is not a form the experiencer could populate with arbitrary phantasies beyond actual experience and validity; it is a form of possible valid existence, which, however indeterminate as to number, kind, and distribution of real things, and even as to how far thing-reality extends, is still a form of validity, a form for infinite possibilities that are not mere phantasy possibilities. The central thesis for the second way: the world is present in every perception as a horizon of positional, not phantastic, possibility. The distinction between real and pure possibility from Section Two (Sirius, the Kaiserstraße) reappears as the character of the horizon. Since the horizon is a validity, and indeed the validity of the world as a whole, a consistent epoché on any act must bracket it: the universalization follows from the structure of a single perception. The claim that the horizon does not "really contain" infinities but pre-delineates a style avoids the absurdity of an actually infinite content in a finite act.

The horizon of consciousness, with its implications, determinacies and indeterminacies, familiarities and open latitudes, nearnesses and distances, embraces not only a present surrounding world but, as memory and expectation already showed, open infinities of past and future. The living streaming present always includes a region of immediately conscious past, in the immediate echo of the perception just sunk away, and a region of immediate future, consciously just coming, toward which streaming perception hastens. Behind this retentional past lies the realm of sedimented, settled pasts, also conscious now as open horizon, into which a searching, awakening look can turn, a realm reawakenable by recollection. On the other side is the horizon of open endless distant future, into which our acts of the future, presentiments, hopes, deliberations, decisions, purposes, are directed. The editor refers to Beilage XXIV on the self-giving of modalities, recollection versus expectation. The spatial horizon is joined by the temporal: retention and protention as immediate horizons, sedimented past and distant future as mediate ones. "Sedimented" (niedergeschlagen) is the genetic term for past experience deposited as habitual background, reawakenable. Together they make the living present the concrete form of world-consciousness.

So my conviction of the existence of my surroundings is documented originally in the structure of each phase of my streaming experience, as a horizon belonging to it and changing in the stream according to its inscriptions or pre-indications. It always contains, in the described way, infinities of implied validities. The editor refers to a critical note. Belief in the world is not a separate judgment added to experience but the structure of every phase's horizon. This is the phenomenological content of the "general thesis" of Ideen I § 30, now given a temporal and horizonal analysis.

Going deeper would open a large field of studies: the systematic, changeable, yet stylistically persistent structure of these implications; the explications belonging to them through intuitions; and the structure of the world exhibited as valid. One would study how lasting validity arises from originally founding acts and how continuing validity announces itself; how validity unites with validity into harmonious validity, how validities and convictions conflict, how validities can be devalued or cancelled and live on only as cancelled, as null; how the world valid as existing at a given moment is a title for the total stock of positive validities unified by harmony. One would also study the structure of the world valid as existing, which, however convictions change and reality turns into null semblance, persists as existing world in its general structure: always spatio-temporal-causal physical nature, always with a manifold of embodied-psychic beings rooted in it, animals and humans in social relations forming societies and communities. Spiritual content is constantly deposited in nature by subjects acting on it; so we never really experience mere nature and mere animality but a surrounding world spiritualized as culture, with houses, bridges, tools, artworks. These most general structures of the always valid world are constantly present for every experiencer, constantly the world of experience, ready for practical action, as intentionally implied contents in the life-horizon, in awakening and disclosing activities, and in further actions of acquaintance, knowledge, revaluation, new purposes, and practical reshaping. Two research programmes are sketched: a genetic theory of validity (founding, persistence, conflict, cancellation) and an ontology of the life-world's invariant structure. The second is the "ontology of the life-world" of the Crisis (§ 51), stated here almost verbatim a decade earlier; the claim that pure nature is never experienced continues the argument of Lecture 30 and Ideen II.

So far the world-horizon was considered as the horizon of the real surroundings. But we also have our ideal "expanses", as lasting deposits of validity from acts in which ideal objects and open infinities of ideality receive their primal founding. From acts of pure counting and of forming 2, 3, 4, with the subjectively closing consciousness of "and so on", "we could always add unit after unit", we gain the consciousness of the being of the infinite number series. Once formed, it belongs to our habitual cognitive possession, which, though unawakened over stretches of life, is represented in the distant horizon, just not a spatial one. When reawakened the empty horizon gains a special salience; in renewed counting each number or series has the character of the known, merely renewed, the reactivation of what was always ours, only sunk into emptiness. The same holds for all arithmetical formations of theoretical acts relating to the number series, with the relation of harmonious validity or exclusion through contradiction. Ideal objects have a horizon too, not spatial but habitual: they are possessions founded once and reactivatable. The "and so on" as the constituting source of infinity recalls the Philosophy of Arithmetic and anticipates the Origin of Geometry, where ideal objects are sedimented and reactivated across generations. For the reduction the lesson is that ideal validities too are implied in the horizon and must be bracketed with the world.

#### Lecture 50: The Endless Time-Stream of Life and the Possibility of a Universal Reflection and Epoché

Whether we consider the unity of the real world or the various unified domains of ideality, our life goes on in constant intentional relation to universes of subjectively harmonious validities, ultimately to the total universe of all validities of being that have arisen, in very different senses, from primally founding acts. The real world has a distinctive, fundamental position, because it belongs to the structure of the real world valid for me from my own intentionality that I, the subject of consciousness, am as a human being a member of this world. Accordingly every formation, ideal as well as real, as arising in my active life, is, notwithstanding its ideality, rooted in the world. If my life has for me the experiential validity of a human life going on at this place and time, every theoretical formation has, from its producing act, a relation back to this spatio-temporal place and to my real human existence there. In the intersubjective connection of the human community every theoretical formation, as belonging to intersubjective science, gains further world-relations: to its first discoverer and its real documentation for objective tradition, to the various learners and their original appropriation. The same holds for all ideal objects, such as those of art and all others gaining intersubjective validity through objective expressions. The priority of the real world rests on self-mundanization: because I apprehend myself as a human being, all my products, ideal ones included, acquire a place in the world through me. This is why bracketing the real world will also affect the mode in which ideal objects are valid for me, and why the Origin of Geometry will locate ideal objects in historical tradition.

This supplements what the earlier analyses of single act-types exhibited under the title of intentional implication. We see how far the title reaches, containing infinite open horizons, and how incomplete a reduction is that proceeds singly and, unaware of these infinities, brackets only the individual emerging validities of being, value, and practice, withdraws interest only from them, and looks at the pure subjective untouched by any question of their right. Hidden validities and endless ranges of validity always remain. In truth we stand in the all-unity of an endless life-connection, the infinity of our own and intersubjective historical life, which is an all-unity of validities producing themselves in infinitum and exhibited in infinitum as we penetrate the horizons of present, past, and future. A complete, freely performed unravelling and exhibition of these manifolds is out of the question; consider only the vast realms of forgetting, for which awakening associative motives are lacking over long stretches of life or permanently. The problem of the second way is now sharply posed. The single-act method would need an infinite, in fact impossible, enumeration, since validities are infinite and partly inaccessible (forgotten). "Historical life" is named as the frame: validity is always historically sedimented.

Yet there is a radical means of putting all validities contained in streaming, constituting life out of force at once, and the recent demonstrations about the constant horizon-consciousness accompanying every present, into which the attentive look can always sink, supply it. The solution is to operate on the horizon as a whole rather than enumerating its contents: since the horizon is itself conscious, it can be reduced in one act.

Active ego-life admits various reflections. After performing single acts or undergoing affections I can reflect on them, as expressed in "I perceive this", "I judge about it", "I like or dislike it", "I desire or do this"; or something arouses my interest, repels me. I can direct at earlier acts not only reflective experiences and theoretical acts but reflective valuations and volitions, for example deciding to perform a certain act in future. Reflections can also concern whole stretches of life: I survey yesterday, my happy student years, or the coming Easter holidays and their likely course. I can take corresponding decisions concerning whole stretches, subjecting the coming holidays to a rule of will, or making past stretches a theme of will, as when I criticize my life as a student. Reflection is scaled up from acts to stretches of life, and reflective willing to rules governing stretches. The Easter holidays date the lecture to early 1924.

Finally I can survey my whole life universally and take decisions for it as a whole. I can practise a universal critique of my whole life so far and will to shape my whole future life, whether from an unexamined universal value like power or success, or in the highest sense of ethical self-reflection, self-criticism, and self-government. Following this highest sense to its limit-form, we come to a remarkable reflective self-regulation related to a universal survey of life. It would contain an inhibition at once of all validities that held for me before and all that would hold in future, if performed with the same naivety. This universal epoché regarding all validities takes place here as foundation and with a view to a universal critique and a universal self-shaping, from sources of truth and genuineness, of a new and true life. The editor points to a textual note in the appendix. The ethical renewal of life, the theme of the Kaizo articles, is presented as the natural model of a universal epoché. At its limit, the ethical decision to live only from justified validities suspends all naive validity at once. The course comes full circle to Lecture 29: the philosopher's founding decision was already the limit-form of this ethical self-regulation.

What strikes us, and why we recurred to this possibility of ethical universality familiar to us as ethical beings, is that here universal reflection is linked with a universal epoché, which enters into a comprehensive regulation of will and is itself already one. Normally ethical self-regulation does not have this limit-form, and the inhibition of validities it contains, though related to the universal life-horizon, is not meant with strict universality. But ethical life and its reflective manner, which we can well think possible in its strictness, can prepare us to envisage the possibility of a strictly universal epoché, even for other purposes. The editor refers to two critical notes. The ethical case is a model of the form, not of the purpose: a universal epoché is shown to be a possible act of will because ethical life already contains its approximation. The phenomenological epoché will have the same form but a theoretical purpose.

Consider what the familiar universal survey of our life accomplishes. It is not seriously a seeing, not a real reproduction of past life in a continuity of explicit intuitive recollections, as if I had to relive my past feature by feature, still less an explicit depiction of the probabilities and possibilities of my future life. Surveying representation and grasping as existing has the character of an anticipating, vague grasp of distances, necessarily. My life in its universal temporal extension is constantly constituted for me, before any active grasping, as a unity of horizon, a vague distant representation. I can turn to this vague distance, approach it, draw out more contours in the continuous consciousness of the same, make it clearer by a wealth of reproductive single intuitions. But this process of clarifying inscription does not change the fact that all results remain vague, of a relative distance despite the approach, so that in infinitum further inscription remains possible. I always grasp the unified endless time-stream of life by anticipation, but only from afar. Its time is always an empty and vague time, occupied with memory contents which in their relative nearness determine individual stretches in vague individuality, and which have before them, at infinite distances, time itself as pure idea, with absolutely individual time-points. The editor refers to a critical note. The survey of one's life, which the universal epoché requires, is itself possible only as a vague horizonal grasp, never as an intuitive totality. Husserl turns the vagueness into a positive structure: the whole of life is given as a horizon, and that suffices for grasping it as a unit. The final sentence places exact time, with its individual points, at infinity, like the thing-in-itself of Lecture 33: an idea of the limit.

Surveying my life universally and constantly certain of it as a whole, approaching and grasping more of it, I also recognize the general character of life as intentional life, an essential character belonging necessarily to such a life and all its stretches and phases. Reflecting on myself, on stretches or on my whole life, means finding myself related to objects conscious in this life positionally or quasi-positionally, in some mode or other. Above all, positionality belongs necessarily to life, in every stretch: something is always there for the ego as valid being. Under validity of being we can include validities arising from feeling and will: feeling a value is not an act of believing that posits the value as existing, but value-ception carries the value in consciousness so that it is always ready for experience, for a simple grasping as existent. So values and practical formations belong to the general sphere of being, to the world instituted for the ego by its own accomplishments of consciousness and positing as its surrounding world. Two essential theses about life as a whole: it is intentional throughout, and it is positional throughout. Life never lacks a valid world. That necessary positionality is exactly what makes a universal epoché meaningful: there is always a universal validity to suspend. Values are included in "being" in the broad sense because every valuing can be converted into a positing.

#### Lecture 51: Transition to the Universal Epoché and Reduction. The Pure Universal Life and Its World of Experience

Surveying my life means, correlatively, surveying the world shaped and reshaped, with changing content, in my intentionality, my certainties and probabilities of judgment, my valuations and actions. Life and world are two sides of one survey.

What was said of reflection on a single act holds for universal reflection on one's whole life as intentional life. Reflection on a perception yields not a bare "I perceive" but "I perceive this house"; reflection on my whole life yields not bare life without its objects, its real and ideal worlds, but life with these as correlates, however vague and distant their representation. And by the analogy of single and universal reflection, the look at the intentional objects comes before the look at act and life: only after naively seeing the house can I reflect on "I see the house", and likewise the simple surveying look at my surroundings comes first, followed by reflection on the life that posited the world. The analogy secures two points against the misreading of the transcendental field as worldless: the reduced life keeps its world as correlate, and the path to it always runs from world to life, not the reverse.

When I now direct a universal reflection on my life, my attentive look, turning from my current theme, penetrates the completely vague horizon that is mine now, directed at the realm of my valid objects, unconscious in particular yet conscious in vague generality. More specifically, following the vaguely given time-form, I turn first to my world of memory, given at first glance as a vague distant formation: it includes the real world as I experienced it and, rooted in it, the manifolds of ideal objects I formed in various contexts, all undifferentiated. This distant formation is a potentiality for free acts of bringing closer and producing new relatively near formations, in the consciousness of ever more complete clarification of the same objective past that was mine, arising in ever new, ever wider "syntheses". Reflection on life itself can follow, and even from the start: in a single act, reflecting from the vague past that was mine to myself who experienced it as its intentional subject, I gain, in a wholly vague representation and positing, my past life as life related to its experienced objectivity; likewise for the future. The universal reflection can be performed in one act on the vague whole, which answers the problem of infinite enumeration: I do not need to clarify every memory to posit my whole past life as the correlate of my past world.

Now the universal epoché and reduction. It is the analogue of the earlier psychological single reductions on reflective acts directed at limited objects or domains, not at the universal surroundings and its correlative total life. We must see how this most universal reduction comes about and how far it achieves something peculiar that exceeds all psychological reductions in principle. The claim to watch is "in principle": the universal reduction will differ not in extent only but in kind.

First, the survey of my life and world that I now perform is also an act, whose object is the intentional relation of my whole life to the objective totality posited in it. I can reduce this act like any other, bracketing that object, and obtain the pure subjective of this act of self-survey. Here I already have a pure subjective that has put out of play the whole validity implicit in the horizon of my act and keeps in validity only the horizon-consciousness itself as positing. But this reduction yields only the pure subjective of the present. The first step shows a limitation: reducing the act of survey gives the present survey, not the past and future life surveyed. The universal reduction must reach into the horizon's contents.

My reductive interest, however, concerns not only the pure subjectivity of the present but my past and future, and for these too I gain a pure subjective in the sense of absolute purity. My past and future are there for me, as I am about to reduce, only thanks to my present horizon; so to gain something transcendentally pure from them I may not put this horizon simply out of validity. I first let it count as it gives itself. I turn my look to my past life, which includes the relation to the object-world posited in it as my surroundings, and which I co-posit as I found it then, as I then posited it. I proceed reductively as learned for other recollections: I inhibit belief in this past surrounding world, first co-posited as the total correlate of my past life, and every interest in its being. However it stands with the actual past existence of the world that counted for me perceptually or otherwise, and with the numbers and theories I meant or supposedly knew, it is certain, and remains valid, that I lived, that I experienced them in my life, took them as real, posited them as valid. Whatever I pick out, even if it did not exist, would have been meant by me; and if nothing of all these surroundings existed, it would still have been the surroundings of my life. I do not say it is possible that these past worlds did not exist, since their possible being interests me as little as their actual being. In the attitude excluding all interest in being, before any question of being or non-being, possibility or probability, I am certain of what that question presupposes: that in my past life I had this realm of objects in my field of consciousness and gave them validity of being. Abstention from all interest in being serves to show that belief in the being of my past life remains untouched by any existential position toward the object-world toward which that life took positions, experiencing, thinking, valuing, acting. The crucial refinement is in the middle: the second way does not even assert the possible non-existence of the world ("I do not say it is possible that these past worlds did not exist"). The Cartesian hypothesis is replaced by pure abstention, and the result is the same: what remains certain is that I lived and posited. This makes explicit the thesis of Lecture 46 that the two ways yield the same field.

So past life can be posited without the least presupposition of its intentional surroundings: it is in itself what it is and posits what it posits as a pure life; correlatively, it exists so as to need in advance no existence of what is posited in it as existing. Doing the same for the anticipated future stretch, I gain my whole pure life as a stream of life absolutely closed in itself, however it stands with the being of the universal surroundings that ever were, are, or will be for me. It is thereby shown that I can consider my pure universal life purely for itself, bring it progressively to pure givenness, and perhaps investigate it scientifically, without ever needing the slightest position on any objectivity as premise; or I can consider it in an epoché regarding all objectivity that equals its annihilation for any use in judgment. Of objective existence nothing at all remains for me during this epoché, not even the slightest possibility of existence, let alone actual or possible properties of naively given objects. "Equals its annihilation for any use in judgment" is the exact formulation of the relation between the two ways: the epoché does functionally what the hypothesis of annihilation did, without asserting anything. The phrase "not even the slightest possibility of existence" confirms that the epoché brackets modal claims too.

This universal epoché is made possible by the essential peculiarity of my life that in every present phase it has, and constantly produces anew, an empty consciousness of distance, a horizon-consciousness, in which everything that was, is, or will be objective for me is implied universally, as intentional correlate of my whole life, itself co-implied. Every present of life has the whole life "in itself" in its concrete intentionality, and together with what is perceptually conscious in this present it carries horizonally the universe of all objects that ever counted for me and in a way even those that will count in future. A note adds: "Thus temporalization of the whole monad and the whole totality of monads at every point of life, in every experience." The possibility of the universal epoché rests on the living present containing the whole of life and world horizonally. The note is one of the richest in the volume: every present "temporalizes" the whole monad and even the community of monads. This is the germ of the late time-manuscripts (the C-manuscripts) and of the monadology of Lecture 54.

The epoché is further possible because, as I can turn any empty special consciousness standing out in the horizon into a grasping consciousness, a specific act, and realize it step by step, so I can turn the empty consciousness of the unified horizon into a grasping total consciousness and convert it step by step into active processes of realizing intuition. An empty memory surfacing, or a non-intuitive anticipation arising in the background while I am busy with something else, can take the form of an act: I turn to the past, the expected, the thought, bring it closer, make it intuitive, perform the thinking explicitly. Likewise for the universal horizon: first turning attention to the universal objective past that was mine, then to my past living in relation to it, and then clarifying and illustrating as described. Husserl noted "imprecise" in the margin, perhaps for the whole passage. The operation that makes universal reflection possible is the conversion of horizon-consciousness into act-consciousness, the ordinary mechanism of attention applied to the whole. The marginal "imprecise" reflects the difficulty of claiming that the horizon as a whole can be grasped by one act, which Husserl will later treat as the "universal thematization" of the world as horizon in the Crisis (§§ 37–38).

It also belongs to the possibility of the epoché that the distant consciousness of the universal horizon not only implies the whole of life and its world as bearing validity from it, but that anticipating validity and still-present validity are inseparably interwoven, for example validity performed in past life and its continuing validity, in which it still holds now, perhaps modalized. Finally, it belongs to the possibility of the epoché, and of the reduction as transcendental, that in every present I can establish myself as uninterested spectator of myself not only as in psychology and its special reflection. Having cast my distant retrospective look on my whole life, as my life related to my whole world valid in it, I inhibit at one stroke, made possible by the act-unity of this universal consciousness, every validity that the whole world conscious in the horizon, the universe of all realities and idealities, ever had, has, or will have for me and through me; I inhibit every interest in being, value, and practice. Directing my look and interest to my life, which remains what it is and confers the validity it confers, I gain pure universal life, and the worldly universe turns into universal intentional objectivity as such, belonging to life as its inseparable correlate. This universal reduction remains in force through all explications and illustrations I perform. Grasping my life is not yet its explicit self-grasping in its own being. My life itself, beyond the immediately present life, even beyond what I have in original form in a special phenomenological grasping of a present act, is a limit-idea lying at eternal distance, containing an infinity of limit-forms and infinitely distant points. But in relative determinacy and approach, first and above all for the infinite past, I can presentify any details and stretches through intuitions, turning the distant past into a near one, finally "completely clear" through recollection; yet the clear always carries its empty horizon of unclarity, and even the perfectly clear has, essentially, an inner horizon of relative unclarity, relative distance, possibilities of becoming clearer, intervals of emptiness. Still, it is a near form of pure life and carries the evident ideal possibility of universal explication and of the reduction to be performed again and again in it. The editor refers to a critical note and Beilage XXV on phenomenological psychology and transcendental phenomenology. The definition of the transcendental reduction is now complete: universal (all validities, including habitual and anticipatory ones, at one stroke) and lasting (in force through all subsequent explication). The difference from psychology is that the whole world, not an act's object, becomes "intentional objectivity as such". But Husserl adds a sober qualification: pure life as a whole is itself a limit-idea, known only in relative nearness. Transcendental experience, like world-experience, has horizons and is inadequate, which is the precise reason apodictic critique cannot be skipped. The equivalence of apodictic and adequate asserted in Lecture 31 has quietly lapsed.

As for the attainability of merely relative nearness, the same holds for natural, unreduced recollection. Just as we accept it as natural experience yielding experiential judgments about the natural past, perhaps rational ones, we can say: the method of epoché and reduction, progressing from horizonal distances to nearnesses, opens a new realm of experience. Indeed it creates a new kind of experience, a new perception, recollection, and anticipation, unknown to natural human life and to humanity before phenomenology. The parallel with natural memory sets the standard for transcendental experience: it is experience, fallible in detail and self-correcting, not intuition of the absolute in one look. "Creates a new kind of experience" echoes the cataract simile of Lecture 45.

### Chapter Three: The Philosophical Sense of the Transcendental Phenomenological Reduction

#### Lecture 52: Philosophy as the Systematic Self-Unfolding of Transcendental Subjectivity in the Form of Systematic Transcendental Self-Theorizing on the Ground of Transcendental Self-Experience

Having carried out the new method completely and built it up from the lower level of a merely phenomenological-psychological reduction, we can say it has enriched us essentially in its methodical grounding. The Cartesian reduction, based on proving the possible non-being of the experienced world, directly had only a limited result. By positing the world's non-being, it directed the look at subjectivity only as experiencing and unaffected by this non-being. Further elaboration was needed: to show that no thought-positing of the world may remain valid, nor any validity of ideal objects. In short, to delimit the whole range of pure subjectivity as transcendental, supplementary investigations were needed, at whose end the method would be the equivalent of the one now gained. Even if that can be done, the new procedure has the great advantage of opening the widest and deepest understanding of the structures of subjectivity on which the possibility of the epoché rests, and so of its pure sense. Were we full phenomenologists we could say the new procedure yields not only the method of the reduction but a phenomenology of the phenomenological reduction. The comparison of the ways is Husserl's own verdict. The Cartesian way reaches the ego quickly but narrowly: it brackets only what is dubitable as experience and must be supplemented to cover thought-positings and ideal objects. The psychological way shows why the epoché is possible (horizons, implication, the living present), and so contains its own justification. The phrase "phenomenology of the phenomenological reduction" names Section Three's title and Fink's later programme.

The considerations have reached a certain conclusion, and a retrospective reflection is needed. The original aim was philosophy, universal science from absolute justification; the chosen principle was apodicticity; it demanded that the beginner "overthrow" all given sciences and all prior convictions. The attempt to gain a positive beginning, a first absolutely justifiable sphere, turned the gaze to continuous world-experience preceding all theory. But it too contains a belief, often deceptive in particular cases. So a universal critique of world-existence as posited in world-experience was needed. So we, so I as beginner, were led first onto the Cartesian way and then to the construction of the deeper, richer method of universal epoché and reduction. The recapitulation joins Sections One to Four into one path: from the idea of philosophy to the method of reduction.

Enriched by the insight gained, the beginning now appears in a new light. The overthrow of all prejudices was a meaningful and necessary demand, but as a demand of the beginning necessarily vague. To take practical effect it needed systematic clarification of what it implied. A first step is that the whole realm of natural experience and finally the universe of all natural validities, including continuing validities from past life and the anticipations they found, must be included in the "overthrow". A radical clarifying reflection on what the demand implies for me is nothing but the development of the systematic method of the epoché. The question that moves me, what I can and may claim first, necessarily directs my gaze to transcendental subjectivity, so that the method becomes eo ipso the method of transcendental reduction. For it turns out that questioning everything, all my cognitive possessions, presupposes and makes evident a universal ground of being which is always ready for experience and unquestioned. In this method it is not merely postulated or talked about from an empty distance with the sentence "ego cogito"; it appears at once concretely, in its essential character, as infinite transcendental life, which, restricted to my own life graspable in immediate transcendental self-experience, is centred on the ego, the transcendental "I", and related to manifold intentional objectivities, each an intentional unity of manifold modes of consciousness. The great and astonishing thing is that if I refuse myself all natural world-belief, every belief I carry or could carry as beginner, if I cease to take myself as child of the world, a natural human being, I have ready, have prepared for myself precisely thereby, an endless open field of new experience: the field of my transcendental subjectivity. What makes it at once significant for the beginner is the apodictic evidence of "I am", present already at first grasp. The bold radicalism of overthrowing everything that was or could be valid for me opened an apodictically evident validity and being not contained and not containable in that totality. The universe of being of the child of the world is not the universe of being as such. The Cartesian "overthrow" is reinterpreted as the vague first form of the epoché: what Descartes demanded without understanding it was the reduction. The last sentence states the course's ontological result in its sharpest form: the totality of worldly being is not the totality of being, since the constituting life that has the world as correlate is not a part of it. This answers the absurd question of Lecture 37 ("what if nothing were?").

Perhaps giving up everything means gaining everything, and radical renunciation of the world is the necessary way to see ultimately true reality and so to live an ultimately true life. Perhaps in the inconspicuous evidence of the ego cogito, and in the mediately grounded evidence of transcendental intersubjectivity, lies all possible truth and science in absolute justification and in the sought ultimately adequate philosophical sense. Perhaps it is true in the strictest sense that self-knowledge, but only radically pure or transcendental self-knowledge, is the only source of all ultimately genuine and satisfying scientific knowledge, the philosophical, which makes a "philosophical" life possible. Then philosophy would be nothing but the systematic self-unfolding of transcendental subjectivity in the form of systematic transcendental self-theorizing on the ground of transcendental self-experience and its derivatives. The editor refers to Beilage XXVI on two ways into philosophy as universal and absolutely justified science. The evangelical formula of losing and gaining (Matthew 16:25), already echoed in Lecture 29, returns now as the formula of the epoché. The definition of philosophy that gives the lecture its title is advanced under a "perhaps": it is the programme to be verified, not a result. The Cartesian Meditations close on the same thought, with Augustine's "in interiore homine habitat veritas".

A distant presentiment of this significance of the newly seen transcendental subjectivity may come to the soul "winged", in Plato's word, with the pinions of longing transcendental vision, from the astonishing remark that all bracketed validities, all worlds put out of validity, are preserved within the bracket. For me as phenomenological ego, uninterested theoretical spectator of myself and my worlds, these worlds are not naively existing; but since they received all the validity they have for me in my acts, I can watch this conferring of validity, and more specifically what it consists in to become convinced of an existence and being-so, to grasp it itself, to bring it to experiential and other evidence. Every evidence in which, as natural knower, I grasp something real as itself and a truth as truth itself, I have, as transcendental spectator, immediately and originally in my seeing. Nothing has been lost. On the contrary: what I simply had as existing as naive ego, I now have as had in my having, together with the previously hidden consciousness in which it was conscious, in its experiencing and thinking, in the great context of subjective intentional accomplishment from which it springs in sense, mode of appearance, and mode of validity. And if this accomplishment reaches back into hidden depths of transcendental subjectivity, I already know that the horizons of every present and past consciousness can be unravelled according to their latent implications. The winged soul is the Phaedrus image of the soul rising to the ideas. The substantive thesis is the fifth misreading-guard stated positively: the epoché loses nothing, since the bracketed world is kept "as had", together with the evidences in which it was had. Transcendental phenomenology therefore gains the world plus its constitution, not the ego minus the world.

Does this not suggest that a universal study of transcendental subjectivity in all its essential possibilities and factual actualities would finally contain all falsity and all truth, everything merely meant and all true being according to its ultimate sense, which must be a sense of transcendental accomplishment? Seen from the elevated, all-surveying transcendental standpoint, are not reason and unreason titles for transcendental accomplishments, aimings, attainments, and failures, which can be clarified in their hidden structural connection? Must not the complete clarification of the accomplishment by which the natural knower attains true being and theoretical truth, carried out in the transcendental sphere, make visible the true being of this true being and truth itself, as the point of unity of the accomplishment and inseparably contained in its context? The transcendental spectator does nothing but observe without prejudice and, unfolding, become aware that being and beings, truth and true states of affairs, originate in subjective cognitive life, as motivated results of certain motivating factors, as intentional unities in an intentional doing in which alone they are what they are and mean what they mean. This is the thesis of transcendental idealism in its methodological form: truth and being are "unities of accomplishment". Three guards are needed. "Originate" does not mean "are caused by" (constitution is not causation); "in which alone they are what they are" concerns sense and validity, not the manufacture of things; and the thesis includes failure and unreason, so it is not a guarantee that whatever appears is true. The formulation is interrogative, which marks it as leading thought, not demonstration.

It is an irrefutable leading thought that, however much the natural universe with all natural-dogmatic positings of a priori and empirical sciences remains under the epoché, no truth is lost but all are gained in a higher sense. The transcendental method is a way, through excluding all natural truth, through universal overthrow, to realize from itself all truth, but as absolute, absolutely justified, lifting it from the hidden relativity of its validity and placing it on the absolute ground where all relativities stand in the clear thematic gaze and, universally embraced, become the absolute theme of knowledge. But the goal is far, the way laborious and still to be cleared. One cannot search without leading thoughts; but the ways and theories preparing them must be worked out step by step. We must keep away from the basic error of traditional philosophy, as if vague and plausible possibilities were already theories. The editor refers to Beilage XXVII on phenomenological reduction and absolute science. The concluding warning is essential to the tone of the whole lecture: the grand programme is heuristics. Husserl's charge against traditional metaphysics, that it mistakes plausible possibilities for theories, is turned against any premature reading of his own idealism as a finished doctrine.

#### Lecture 53: The Problem of Intersubjectivity

##### a) The Possibility of a Transcendental Naivety of a Pure Phenomenology and the Philosophical Task of an Apodictic Critique of Transcendental Experience

The apodictic evidence of the ego cogito, in which transcendental subjectivity first accrues to me, is only a beginning, not an end. It is apodictic, but it soon awakens a bundle of puzzles about its true sense, reach, and limits. To mention one: the "I experience", "I think" in the momentary now seems apodictically certain, and my transcendental past is certain to me too; but does not memory, to which alone I owe it, often deceive? Could not my whole transcendental past and future be a transcendental semblance? So the great task of an apodictic critique of transcendental experience still lies before us. The editor refers to Beilage XXVIII on immanent adequacy and apodicticity and immanent time as the apodictic form of the self-constituting subjectivity in the streaming present "I am". Transcendental semblance, the notion used against the world in Lecture 34, is now turned against transcendental memory. The question is exactly the one the Cartesian Meditations (§ 9) answer by restricting apodicticity to the living present and to the general structure of the past, not its content. Beilage XXVIII's title points to the answer here too: immanent time as apodictic form.

More: we have ceased to be children of the world and renounced universal natural experience and knowledge in all its dogmatic forms, and implicitly all natural valuations and actions insofar as they yield objects of knowledge. The new, infinite yet self-enclosed transcendental experience opened. With it we have a field of new theoretical knowledge (and of other purely transcendental acts). The natural sciences are given up; transcendental sciences must arise instead, disciplines of a new, self-enclosed universal science (*scientia universalis*), which a priori uses and could use no premise from any dogmatic science. This is in a sense true and self-evident, but only from the standpoint of a certain higher naivety. There can be a transcendental naivety, parallel to natural naivety, with a special sense. The concept of transcendental naivety is the self-critical centre of the final lectures. Having left the natural attitude does not guarantee having left naivety; one can practise phenomenology in the same trusting way positive scientists practise their science.

This matters because it clarifies the theoretical structure of beginning philosophy, as Euclidean geometry is clarified when mathematicians put certain axioms out of play and show which theorems remain untouched, revealing their independence and the deductive structure of the whole. Similarly, the whole doctrine of the transcendental reduction can be detached from the motivation of the beginning philosopher. Were the aim of philosophy (in our sense) indifferent to us and dropped entirely, we could still perform the psychological and transcendental epoché and all the associated analyses of consciousness. Naive and transcendental experience and science would still be opposed, "naive" meaning simply the opposite of "transcendental". But a second concept of naive knowledge can be defined: every knowing not guided by the idea of absolute knowledge from all-sided justification, and similarly for every kind of position-taking and every life of reason. In this second sense not only natural knowing untouched by the epoché is naive but also knowing on the ground of transcendental subjectivity, as long as it undergoes no apodictic critique and omits every question of absolute justification. The analogy is to independence proofs in geometry (non-Euclidean geometries showing the independence of the parallel postulate). The reduction is "independent" of the Cartesian motive of absolute justification: it can be performed without it, which means transcendental phenomenology is possible as a positive science. Two senses of "naive" result: non-transcendental, and non-apodictically-criticized. Transcendental phenomenology practised without apodictic critique is non-naive in the first sense and naive in the second. This distinction justifies the whole structure of the course, which began with the idea of philosophy precisely to make the second criterion operative.

As historical natural science follows the evidence of external experience, extends it and lets it speak in all directions, balancing discordances from one-sided experience and securing future harmony, and trusts logical evidence in the predicative sphere, seeking only to perfect it, so one can proceed in the new sphere of transcendental experience. One can bring transcendental actualities and possibilities to clarity, distinguish and describe systematically the main types of transcendental events, and so sketch a transcendental descriptive phenomenology, as natural history has long had a descriptive, classificatory typology of the intuitive forms of organic species and developmental forms. The editor refers to Beilage XXIX on the difficulties of a deepest grounding of philosophy as universal science by way of the reduction. The model of natural history recurs from Ideen I § 75 ("descriptive science" of morphological essences). The point is that such a phenomenology, however valuable, would be a positive science of the transcendental, with its trust in experience unexamined.

As we can disregard factual reality in external experience, consider possibilities transformed in free phantasy, and found eidetic sciences, like pure geometry of ideally possible spatial shapes, so here, in the transcendental sphere, an a priori science of the possible forms of transcendental events is conceivable. One could distinguish essential types of acts, perception, recollection, expectation, phantasy, liking, disliking, and subtypes like thing-perception, perception of animals, self-perception and perception of others, and ask what essential structures each contains by possibility and necessity. All this could proceed in the same naivety, trusting the evidence of experience, of intuition of possibility, of logical consequence, as in the objective sciences, without properly philosophical pretension. We would have a rational and an empirical phenomenology before all philosophical interest and before philosophy itself. How it would look in execution is not our concern; its possibility is worth noting. One thing, the lowest and most intelligible, is clear and beyond doubt: running through my transcendental life in the phenomenological reduction, or, to mark its habituality, in the phenomenological attitude, I have, as far as intuitive realization succeeds, a homogeneous continuum of transcendental self-experience, as far as the unfolding yields harmonious self-intuitions. Occasional discontinuities, as when discordances appear in clarifying a transcendental self-recollection, are balanced as in natural-objective recollection: memories have slid into or over one another, and on closer clarifying approach the overlaps and confusions separate into a harmonious intuitive continuity. Whether this must be so with apodictic necessity is not settled; it belongs to the familiar style of memory. So de facto I have a firm transcendental universe of transcendental experience, as I have an empirical universe from natural, objective (outer and inner) experience. I have it as existing for me by virtue of the harmony of transcendental experience that keeps restoring itself, and of the experiential belief, documented in the horizon of every present, in this persisting style of harmony. As the real universe is given as the one infinite connection of harmonious external experience, so irreal subjectivity, the infinite totality of my transcendental life, is given as the continuous unity of possible transcendental experience. The parallel is exact and deliberate: the transcendental universe is given, like the world, as the correlate of harmonious experience whose harmony is a matter of style, not apodictic necessity. So everything Section Two said about the presumptive character of world-experience applies, mutatis mutandis, to transcendental experience. This is the reason for the apodictic critique, and it is also the definitive refutation of the reading of transcendental subjectivity as an indubitable Cartesian residue: its being for me rests, beyond the living present, on presumptive harmony. "Phenomenological attitude" is defined as the habituality of the reduction.

##### b) Transcendental Egology ("Solipsistic Phenomenology") and the Transition to the Intersubjective Reduction

Husserl proposes to continue for a stretch, still setting aside the specifically philosophical interest, though this consideration will serve it. The phenomenology of intersubjectivity is to be worked out first within the "naive" transcendental phenomenology just described, before the apodictic question is raised.

The transcendental reduction as systematically built was a reduction to transcendental subjectivity, often named, in Cartesian fashion, the reduction to the ego cogito, and so it seemed self-evidently a reduction to my own transcendental ego, the reducer's, and my own life. How could another transcendental subjectivity make sense? The epoché on my universe includes the exclusion of all bodies as spatial things. The exclusion of my own body does not touch my own life, in which the body is now a phenomenon of spatial experience. But as for other people and animals, it is not apparent how the reduction could yield more than phenomena posited in my empathic positings. If as phenomenologist I may no longer let things, and so others' bodies, count naturally as existing realities, the support for the indication of the other's psychic life, present to me only as appresented through his experienced body, falls away. A transcendental phenomenology seems possible only as transcendental egology. As phenomenologist I am necessarily a solipsist, not in the ordinary ridiculous sense rooted in the natural attitude, but in the transcendental one. The objection is stated in its strongest form: since the other is appresented through his body, and bodies are bracketed, the other's life seems to survive only as my positing. "Transcendental solipsism" is the name Lecture 36 already used; the question is whether it is the last word.

The beginner may think, unless he already has those winged presentiments that make him seek all true exteriority in interiority, that the epoché must be lifted some day, that the time must return to experience and think naturally and do justice to the natural sciences. Then the other's subjectivity will get its due, with phenomenology somehow contributing new methodical aids to natural experience and the dogmatic sciences. The editor refers to Beilage XXX on the supposed difficulty that, remaining in the epoché, "one never gets back to the world". He also reports Husserl's struck continuation: that this is not so at all should soon emerge from a deeper understanding of what the epoché achieves; "for me myself, I confess, the first knowledge of the phenomenological reduction was a limited one in the sense described. For years I saw no possibility of making it intersubjective. But finally a way opened which is of decisive significance for making possible a full transcendental phenomenology and, at a higher level, a transcendental philosophy." The struck confession is historically valuable: Husserl dates his own passage from egological to intersubjective reduction to a discovery after years of difficulty (Beilage XX places it around 1910). The beginner's picture, that phenomenology must be suspended to regain others and world, is the misunderstanding the course has been guarding against; Beilage XXX's title names it.

That this rests entirely on misunderstandings of the true sense of the reduction must be clear to anyone who has appropriated the full content of our demonstrations of the transcendental sense of experience of others, of empathy: what we said about the transcendental reduction in empathic presentification of others' subjectivity, about the phenomenological content of the givenness of one's own body and of others' bodies, and about how others' psychic life is indicated. It follows indeed that my own transcendental ego and life have the privilege of a first, original givenness, since only to myself do I have the direct access of self-experience, self-perception, self-recollection, self-expectation; whereas I can experience others' subjectivity, which itself exists only as directly self-experiencing, only in the mediate way of indication, which makes it conscious to me by presentifying its self-perception, self-recollection, and so on. This is an intentionality of the second level, a mediate one. The asymmetry of access is conceded and turned into the solution. The other's subjectivity is given as a presentification of self-experience that is not mine, an intentionality of the second level; and presentifications, as Lectures 39 and 47 showed, admit a reduction reaching into what they presentify. So the reduction of empathy should yield the other's transcendental life, as the reduction of memory yielded my transcendental past.

Transcendental subjectivity is given, and only is, in levels of relative immediacy and mediacy, levels of intentional implication. Even the immediacy in which I am given to myself as transcendental ego has levels. Quite immediately I am given to myself only in my living present; only of it do I have the most immediate form of experience, perception. Of my past and future I have only recollection and anticipation, with intentional mediacies. Looked at closely, the sphere of the present too has such a structure: we reach the flowing limit-point of the pure now, or the pure self-perception of this momentary primally living now, and a stretch of original retention and protention, whose intentionality is mediate. This does not stop us speaking of a concrete self-perception and a concrete present. The editor points to a critical note. The strategy for intersubjectivity is to relativize the contrast of original and mediate givenness. Even my own ego is given originally only at the flowing now; retention is already mediate. So mediacy cannot disqualify the other, since it already pervades my own self-givenness. This is the source of the later doctrine that the "primal ego" is given only as a limit.

Analogously I say with right: my transcendental ego alone is given to me originally, from original self-experience; others' subjectivity is given to me mediately within my own self-experiencing life, in self-experiencing empathies, not originally, but still given, and experienced. As the past as past can be originally given only through recollection, and the coming as such only through expectation, so the other as other can be originally given only through empathy. Original givenness in this sense and experience are the same. The formula is the crux of Husserl's answer to solipsism: the other's mode of original givenness is empathy, just as the past's is memory. Demanding that the other be given as I am to myself would demand that he not be other, as demanding that the past be perceived would demand that it not be past.

My own present and past are non-independent; the full concretum is the whole unity of my life given in original experience, including my whole past and anticipated future. But whereas my originally experienced present can exist only within the unity of my whole life, this cannot straightforwardly be said of the relation of this whole life to intersubjective life, of my ego to the totality of other egos I am in community with. On the contrary, it seems conceivable that I am alone, that no other bodies ever appear in my field of experience through which I could experience others by empathy. At any rate one can perform a phenomenological abstraction, restricting phenomenological experience and research to the concrete unity of one's own transcendental subjectivity, disregarding all empathy and taking no other subjectivity into account. That means thinking one's own life modified, as in the transcendental exclusion of thing-experience. But it does not mean the reduction yields only one's own ego; it is merely restricted. For important methodological reasons it is even necessary to restrict oneself in long stretches of research and first work out a systematic egology, a solipsistic phenomenology. The asymmetry between the unity of my life (necessary) and my community with others (seemingly contingent) justifies egology as an abstraction, which the Fifth Cartesian Meditation will perform as the reduction to the "sphere of ownness". Husserl insists that the abstraction is methodical: the reduction as such yields more than the ego; egology is a chosen restriction of it.

To see fully how the reduction, in the medium of my own ego experienced first, brings transcendental intersubjectivity to experience, and how bracketing the spatial world and so others' bodies and people does not put out of play the others' pure egos with their cogitations, consider the fiction just mentioned. Suppose I had never met other bodies, so that there were for me only things whose spiritual significance, their cultural predicates, refer only to my own work. For simplicity I assume I have my surroundings in constant, unbroken certainty, without semblance. Performing the reduction, inhibiting as transcendental spectator all naivety in which I have this world as simply existing, I keep it as intentional objectivity of my pure life, first in the form of spatial experience. I see what the naive having of the world, supposedly experienced as lastingly existing, actually looks like. Exhibiting and describing the unity of experience in which a single thing is experienced in naive certainty led to comprehensive analyses covering possible as well as actual experience. The editor points to a critical note covering the pages that follow. The thought-experiment of a world without others isolates what the reduction yields for things, so that the difference in the case of others can stand out.

A thing is perceptually given as lastingly existing in a synthetically harmonious continuity of perception in which it is continuously conscious as one and the same; but in each phase I have it in a particular how of subjective givenness, in changing orientation, near and far, right and left, with a corresponding change of perspectival appearance: the thing appears in ever new aspects. What is properly seen is always distinguished from what is only co-seen, relatively self-grasped from anticipatorily co-grasped. I am active, moving eyes and touching hands in the free "I can", conscious that the visual aspects belong to freely chosen eye positions and the tactile ones to the positions of hands and fingers. The analysis of thing-constitution (orientation, adumbration, kinaesthetic motivation) is the doctrine of Thing and Space, summarized to show that the thing, reduced, is the correlate of a system of appearances tied to my freedom of movement.

These manifolds implied in the experience called continuous perception of the thing have a fixed style of connection; only if they run accordingly does perceiving have the character of continuous harmony, continuous self-confirmation of the positing of existence as positing of the objective sense. Only then are the anticipations reaching beyond the properly perceived confirmed, and the thing appears, according to its always only partly realized sense, with ever new properties and sides, confirming and determining itself. The style and sense of experience include freely setting going the series of appearances and the consciousness of the free capacity to take other paths of perception and bring other unseen sides into view by walking up, looking around, touching. For each freely possible mode of perceiving the associated series of appearances are known in advance and hypothetically constructible in their systematic style. Only as long as this style is confirmed, as long as the appearances actually run accordingly, do the continuously merging intentionalities have the character of fulfilment, or, what is the same, is the intentional object conscious as continuously one and, in this self-confirmation, as actual. The naive ego falls into doubt or gives up what it experienced as existing, in the consciousness of mere semblance, which means, as one convinces oneself by the reduction and reflection on experiencing consciousness, that the appearances no longer run in the familiar and expected series, but appearances conflict with appearances. Section Two's analysis of the world's contingency reappears here in reduced form: existence and semblance are translated into harmony and conflict of appearances. The "hypothetical constructibility" of series of appearances is what makes the system a rule, not a mere collection.

It is not hard then to characterize the discrete synthesis by which interrupted perceptual continua are brought into harmonious unity through recollection, as when I become aware that what I saw before, did not see for a while, and see now is the same, and so become certain that something is while I do not see it. Here too we deal with systems of actual and possible perceptions, "possible perception" being a title for a positionality that confirms itself in its own ways. Finally one reaches the inescapable insight that everything belonging to the lasting and constantly confirmed conviction of a world, a world of things I sometimes experience, sometimes not, partly know and partly not, is transcendentally nothing but a determinately describable system of actual and possible experiences of fixed essential structure, with a continuous transcendental connection I can run through in transcendental experience and constructive intuition. Every piece of natural experience translates into a corresponding transcendental experience and shows its true sense in it. The unperceived existence of things is analysed as positional possibility of perception, not as a hidden substance: "possible perception" is itself a validity, as Lecture 49 showed. The "translation" thesis is methodological: nothing in natural experience is lost; each piece acquires a transcendental reading.

I can see that the existence of the thing itself, the object of experience, is inseparably implied in this system of transcendental connections, and that without such a connection it would be unthinkable and simply nothing. The editor refers to a critical note. "Simply nothing" is the strongest statement of correlation in the course, and it must be read with the sense-constitutive qualification of Lecture 52: the thing is unthinkable as a thing apart from the system of experiences in which it is a thing, not produced by them.

Of course, once the possibility and actuality of other subjects is included, from which we now abstract, it appears that other subjects can carry such systems too, related so that the intentional object of one subject's system is the same as another's. It is clear, then, that as long as I can know nothing of other egos, the existence of the real world, theme of my possible experiences and thoughts, reduces to a closed, though manifoldly endless, system of transcendental experiences of the type "external experience", in which intentional series of experience lie as consistently confirming themselves, as poles immanent to this system, posited and always harmoniously identifiable. The transcendence of the world has no metaphysical mystery: it is of another kind, but most generally of the same genus as the transcendence of numbers and other irreal objects. The "immanent pole" formulation is precise: the thing is immanent to the system as its pole, transcendent to every single experience. Assimilating the world's transcendence to that of ideal objects removes the picture of a world "behind" consciousness: both are identities across manifolds, differing in kind of manifold. The restriction "as long as I can know nothing of other egos" marks this as the egological stage.

In this way the real world reduces to a universe of intentional correlates of actual and possible experiences of my transcendental ego and is inseparable from them as correlate. So far, then, the subject, experiencing the world and devoted to it as child of the world, is, without knowing it, always with itself, within the closed circle of its own transcendental subjectivity. The phrase "always with itself" records the result of the egological abstraction, not the final doctrine; it is the solipsistic appearance about to be dispelled.

If among the things so-called other bodies appear, my surroundings showing other people and animals, their physical bodies, as experienced by me, again reduce to systems of subjective occurrences within my transcendental subjectivity; nothing is taken from their experiential reality for me; it is only clarified in its concrete, full, and true sense. The other's body is treated exactly like any thing: the reduction clarifies it, it does not diminish it.

What of the other's psychic life and its expression in bodies? To ask is to answer. Other bodies as things are realities for me, indubitable certainties, as long as the style of experience prescribes the harmonious courses and their continuation. If so, the analogy with my body indicates something further, the other's psychic life, and what is indicated is no longer transcendentally subjective from my own sphere of life. The other's psychic life in its transcendental content is all I can now claim. It is what is purely transcendentally indicated, and so, together with my universe of life-occurrences covered by the title ego cogito, I co-experience mediately, in the indication of empathy, a second transcendental life, and so many. The crucial asymmetry with things: the other body reduces to my experiences, but what it indicates does not, since it is indicated as not mine. Reducing the empathic indication therefore yields another transcendental life, not a content of my own.

So the transcendental reduction yields directly my ego and mediately this or that alter ego, an open plurality of other subjects indicated, or to be indicated, by experienceable bodies. This indication is itself experiential certainty with its own way of confirming itself harmoniously. The result of Lecture 53b: the reduction is intersubjective in its yield, egological only in its starting point. Empathic indication has its own empirical right, a point Lecture 54 will turn into the refutation of transcendental solipsism.

#### Lecture 54: The Way of the Phenomenological Reduction to Transcendental Idealism and Its Phenomenological Sense as Transcendental Monadology

The view that pure phenomenology is possible only as transcendental egology arises from misinterpreting the sense and achievement of the reduction. Only the deepest exhaustion of this sense, as attempted in these lectures, can protect against such misinterpretations. These clarifications have no equal in philosophy in their reach, since all philosophy depends on them, and with it the general structural form of all admissible world-views. The correctly understood reduction already sketches the route to transcendental idealism, and all phenomenology is nothing but the first strictly scientific form of this idealism, the first precise statement of its genuine sense and the first real proof of it in the most compelling form, execution as strictest science. But we can only present here what can really be seen at the beginning, and regarding transcendental solipsism the following must be stressed. Husserl claims the title "transcendental idealism" and in the same breath qualifies its proof: it is "proved" only by the execution of phenomenology as science, not by an argument. This is the stance of the Cartesian Meditations § 41. The mention of world-views (Weltanschauungen) signals the ambition of the course: transcendental idealism is not one world-view among others but the frame that decides which world-views are admissible.

Already in the natural attitude reflection teaches that the world and every being I can know of or speak of meaningfully is known in my knowing, experienced in my experiencing, thought in my thinking, in short, conscious in my consciousness. Even if it came to me by supernatural revelation, the revelation would again come to me as a consciousness. The starting point of idealism is a platitude available in the natural attitude. The example of revelation shows its universality: no source of knowledge escapes the form "given in consciousness". The platitude becomes a thesis only when its implications are made universal, as the next paragraphs do.

Going further, every cognitive distinction I may make falls within knowing subjectivity: between mere and correct opinion, between opinion and insightfully grounded knowledge in which an opinion proves true and what it means comes to givenness as true being. Truth and true being present themselves as occurrences appearing in, and realizing themselves in, knowing consciousness. The difference between appearance and reality is itself a difference within consciousness, the thesis that disarms the sceptical demand to step outside consciousness to compare it with reality.

Passing into the reduction means taking this subjectivity of all cognitive occurrences more seriously than the natural reflecting mind, and making clear that as long as I have a world existing prior to and in itself and only go back, case by case, from the object to subjective knowing, I have left some cognitions unconsidered: all those from which that world in itself has validity. It has validity for me only insofar as I have conferred validity on it. I must not overlook habitual validity, the implicit horizon-intentionality, which contains implicit validities without my performing an express act. So I inhibit all naivety of validity and objective being, and establish myself as spectator grasping how it really happens that something is there for me as thing, person, art and religion, state and people; that it counts as reality for me, that I am certain of it, believe, experience, know it. As transcendental spectator I have, universally embraced, concrete knowing-living subjectivity, in which all supposed and correct validity or meaning, vague and clear, erroneous and insightful, takes place, and in which every being is contained as meant in meaning and, at best, as experienced in experiencing, conceived in conceiving, seen in seeing, inferred in inferring. The world "in itself" is the unreduced remainder of Lecture 48: the validity of the world as prior to knowledge is itself a habitual and horizonal validity, and the reduction suspends it. The list of objects (art, religion, state, people) marks the scope: the reduction concerns the whole cultural world.

All occurrences of my cognitive life, factual and those conceivable in pure possibility, constructible a priori in their essentially necessary and possible forms, are now in my field of research. Here lies the great task of eidetic phenomenology: to clarify what kinds of cognitive occurrence are conceivable and under what essential laws they stand. It is evident from the start that what I call in the natural attitude an object, a connection of objects, a state of affairs, anything real for me, is a unity of identity intentionally conscious in manifold actual or possible experiences. In all of them it is meant as its identical objective sense, despite very different subjective modes of appearance, in the mode of certainty. That it is the same in all means that these manifold experiences, with their modified modes of appearance, yield in synthetic connection an overarching consciousness of one and the same existing object, conscious only in different subjective modes. Of course it also happens, a familiar type, that the synthetic consciousness cannot hold its unity of identity: one consciousness and another aim at unity but separate in conflict, and then not only does certainty modalize into conjecture and doubt, but the consciousness of non-being, a striking-out of being, arises. The central definition of objectivity: a unity of identity across manifolds of consciousness, established by synthesis. Non-being too is an occurrence of consciousness (the "striking out"), so the correlation thesis covers failure as well as success.

Yet the naive knower always has the practical conviction that being and non-being are not merely contingent and relative, that in every case a true being can be worked out by cognitive activity, as correlate of perfect, or at least progressively perfectible, insight; or, as one says, he knows himself in possession of the faculty of reason and able, in processes of rationally guided, insightful knowing, to work out something final as against the merely subjective and supposed, or at least to approach finality. Transcendental phenomenology must investigate, in the universal context of transcendental subjectivity and after the study of the general structures belonging necessarily to a thoroughly intentional life, the special essential possibilities covered by the title of reason and rational action, and must give ultimate clarity and determinacy of sense, from their transcendental origin, to the concepts "true" or "definitively confirmed being" that arise in rational knowledge. Idealism does not abolish the distinction between true being and mere opinion; it takes the naive conviction of reason as a theme to be clarified. This is the programme of the "phenomenology of reason" in Ideen I, Part IV, restated as the task that transcendental idealism must fulfil.

Considering my situation after passing into the transcendental attitude, in which I embrace with absolute universality all actual and possible objects of knowledge that can ever count or meet me as meant or truly existing, finding them as known in their knowing, as intentional moments of the concrete accomplishments in which they arise as unities of validity, it seems at first that I must say: everything I can ever see as truly existing is nothing but an intentional occurrence of my own life as knower; not attached to a passing single experience, but to the whole connection of motivation running through my life's intentionality, specifically to the sense- and appearance-contents arising in my primal foundings and becoming habitual, and the cognitive possibilities opened from them, all according to fixed essential laws of transcendental life. Owing to these laws, something meant that appears in the mode of evidence of seen true being can, after the contingent act has passed, be confirmed in new cognitive activities as the same true being, in renewed evidence, and no evidence can ever arise conflicting with it and demanding that self-given true being be struck out. This is the solipsistic reading of idealism stated in its strongest form, including the claim that true being is a lawful pole of my own life's motivations. "It seems at first" warns that it is not the conclusion.

Whatever difficult investigations are needed to make truth intelligible in correlation with evidence and its motivations, their style and result seem prescribed in advance. It seems that if the accomplishment of knowing and its attainment of final truth is to become intelligible at all, it could only be in this style and with this result. But then we are in an uncomfortable situation, that of transcendental solipsism. Does all this not say that all true being designates only an ideal polarity forming in the motivations of my own transcendental life and enclosed in it alone? Are my fellow humans and their inner lives also only identity-unities originating in my life, with correlative sense only in relation to my experiences? Yet the other lives his life, can perform the reduction in himself as well as I, find himself as absolute subjectivity, and grasp me as alter ego as I grasp him. As I am for myself, not merely an intentional occurrence in the other's cognitive life, so conversely. The dilemma: the correlation thesis seems to make every being, including others, a pole of my life; yet others are subjects for themselves, as I am. The reciprocity argument (he can reduce as I can) shows that a solipsistic reading contradicts the very structure it derives from.

There must be truth in this, and the question is how to create transcendental clarity for it and reconcile it with what was said earlier. Both claims are held: correlation and the self-standing being of others. The task is reconciliation, not choice.

The decisive thought: the confirming evidence in which an object is given to me and proves itself as existing reality can be immediate or mediate. Immediate is the evidence of perception. Even if, as in all external experience, it is constantly mixed with anticipations, perception includes the evident consciousness of immediate self-grasping; the perceived object is "evidently" conscious as bodily there. In continuously harmonious external perception we have a form of original confirmation that shows the perceived, through steady fulfilment of anticipations, as the bodily same from ever new sides and so realizes it progressively in knowledge, bringing it to ever more perfect self-realization. Here the thing itself in its true being is the idea of something identical and identifiable that would show and confirm itself as the same in its bodily selfhood as my experience continued. As far as new experiences can be set going in my stream of consciousness, in new life-contexts synthetically unifiable with earlier ones, so far reaches the correlative existence of this same thing; it is what it is as the identical of my possible experiential realizations unifiable into harmony and has all properties that would appear in such contexts. The distinction of immediate and mediate confirmation is the key. A thing's true being is the idea of the identical across my possible original experiences. That definition applies to what can be originally given to me; the next paragraphs show that it cannot apply to the other.

Suppose no bodies had appeared in my surroundings, so that I had no notion of other subjectivity. Then every objective reality, the whole world, now lifeless, would be nothing but a connected plurality of intentional poles, correlate unities for systems of my possible and actual experiences, realized or realizable as bodily givens in endless harmonious progressions. "True being" would be the idea of a possible constructibility of the identity-correlate, the substrate identical in the manifold of its presenting appearances, of the corresponding courses of my possible, thoroughly harmoniously confirming experience; and outside the ideal system of my possible experience the thing would be nothing, in the sense in which it is "actually" or "in truth" there for me. In a world without others, idealism would be egological without remainder. The qualifier "in the sense in which it is actually there for me" limits the claim to sense and validity, the guard against reading "nothing" as a metaphysical annihilation.

But admit other bodies, and other people are there for us, experienced by empathy, which has its own continuous harmonious confirmation. In harmonious empathy we remain in the self-confirming certainty of the immediate presence of this person before us. His expressions, his words, what they lead us to expect of his actions, and how the actions run, all fit together into a system of self-confirmation. But this immediacy is only relative, and the confirmation, regarding the other's subjectivity, is a confirmation by merely harmonious and mutually confirming indication, though one distinguished by its motivational rootedness in really self-giving experience, an "appresenting" indication. Original experience, real perception, I have only of myself and of objectivities I likewise perceive and experience directly. The range of my ideally possible perceptions covers only my life-stream and the modifications I can introduce into it by my activities, and further all the intentional unities I constitute and can constitute in my life, in contexts of actual and possible objective perception in which I realize them bodily. The range of my possible original experience is exactly my life plus the things it constitutes. The other's subjectivity lies outside it in principle, which is the fact the solipsistic reading overlooked.

Surveying my whole cognitive life in the transcendental attitude and weighing the achievement of my knowledge of others, it is and remains self-confirming knowledge, even though it is not confirmed for me by the co-indicated subjectivity becoming originally experienceable, perceivable itself, which in principle it cannot. With the same right, in general, by which things are there for me, the right of continuing empirical confirmation, other bodies as things, and then other souls, are there for me. In transcendental clarification all things are unities of possible harmonious perception (as original realization) belonging to and constituted in my life. The other human subject, however, is in transcendental clarification a second transcendental subject: not an object realized or realizable in my life in the original, not merely a unity of my possible perceptions, but something legitimately indicated by certain unities of my experience, indicated as another ego. This indication has its natural right, the right of empirical confirmation. The knowledge that what stands before me bodily as another's body, a thing among things, is in truth an intentional correlate of one of my perceptual systems does not make this body a fiction, as it does not for any thing, and takes nothing from its reality, nor from its function, acquired in my life, of indicating another transcendental subjectivity empirically and with confirmation. The editor refers to Beilage XXXI, "Grounding of transcendental idealism. Radical overcoming of solipsism". This is the resolution. The other is not a pole of my possible perceptions but a subject legitimately indicated through them; his being is not exhausted by my experience of him, because what my experience indicates is precisely a being-for-itself inaccessible to my original experience. Transcendental idealism therefore distinguishes two modes of being for me: constituted unity (things) and indicated co-subject (others). Only the first is "correlate" in the strong sense.

The reduction thus leads to two universal structures of life founded in each other. First, my life, and every ego's transcendental life, constitutes itself for itself originally in experience. It is a universal stream of life in the form of original self-consciousness, "innermost" perception, which is to say self-realizing in consciousness for itself. This perceiving is only exceptionally and in particular attentive, active self-grasping, but it is constant perceiving in the sense of appearing to itself in the original. Every perceptual self-presence has constantly new horizons, freely disclosable through self-recollection and self-anticipation. "Innermost perception" as constant self-appearing, prior to reflective grasping, is the pre-reflective self-consciousness of the time-lectures (the self-appearance of the flow). It is what later authors (Henry above all) would radicalize; here it is the first structure on which the second is founded.

In this stream of life as appearing-for-itself there arise, according to its factual course and universal essential laws, systems of particular life-forms, specifically systematic connections of actual and possible perception of spatio-temporal objectivities, and correlatively these perceptual unities constitute themselves, the objectivities themselves, a universal thing-world, partly realized and partly realizable through progressive "external" perception. Special mention goes to the original perceptual constitution of my own bodily existence, the original perceptual double unity "own body", bearer of my sense-fields, system of my organs of perception and will. The lived body is singled out because it is the hinge between the two structures: constituted as a thing and experienced from within, it is the "primal body" from which empathy starts.

Through experience of others' bodies rooted in my experience of my own body, secondary as regards the psychic side, I co-experience others' subjectivity within my subjectivity. Here the universal reduction yields, in descriptive community with my own life flowing in original self-realization, a second transcendental life, and as many, in open endlessness, as I can experience constituted things as bodies. Precisely because the other's subjectivity does not belong to my range of original perceptual possibilities, it does not dissolve into intentional correlates of my own life and its rule-structures. It gives itself with empirical right, by its own sense, as a being that is in itself and for itself and only for me has the mode of givenness of "the other". Only subjectivity can be for itself in a genuine and absolute sense. Being-for-oneself is appearing to oneself, is being as a transcendental life-process of objectivation, being under the classic title ego cogito. The decisive inversion: the inaccessibility of the other's inner life, which seemed the ground of solipsism, is the ground of his independence. Because he cannot be constituted in my original experience, he is not my correlate. "Being for oneself" is defined as self-appearing, which only subjects have; things are only "for others". This is the ontological core of the monadology.

Taking the other's transcendental life in its community with mine, it follows that through this community the intentional constitution of my thing-world gains community with the constitution the other performs, as constitution of the same thing-world. As within my life I bring a present perception into synthetic unity with a remembered perception of mine, conscious of the same thing present now and formerly, so through empathy I can bring a perception empathized into the other and performed by him into synthetic unity with my own, conscious that it is the same thing perceived by both of us; and vice versa. So too with ideal objects, the number series or a scientific theory I have realized constitutively in myself and that another has produced and seen in his thinking. All true objectivity for me is true for everyone and has its transcendental being as a constitutive unity of knowledge, constituted or constitutable as intentional correlate in everyone's cognitive life. But the only absolute being is being a subject, as being originally constituted for oneself, and the whole of absolute being is that of the universe of transcendental subjects in actual and possible community with one another. So phenomenology leads to the monadology anticipated by Leibniz in a brilliant aperçu. The editor refers to Beilage XXXII on phenomenological reduction and absolute justification. The final paragraph completes the analogy between memory and empathy begun in Lecture 53: as identity across time is constituted by synthesis of present and recollected perception, identity across subjects is constituted by synthesis of my perception and an empathized one. Objectivity is thus defined intersubjectively: "true for everyone". The ontological conclusion distinguishes absolute being (subjects, which are for themselves) from relative being (objects, which are constituted unities). This is transcendental idealism as monadology, not as solipsism: the absolute is plural and communal. Unlike Leibniz's monads, Husserl's have windows, empathy, through which they constitute one common world; the Cartesian Meditations (§§ 55–56, 60) develop this "monadological intersubjectivity", and the absence of pre-established harmony is compensated by the constitutive community of experience. The course ends where the Cartesian way could not reach: at the plurality of transcendental subjects that makes a common world, and hence science, possible.

# Commentary on Husserl, *Zur Phänomenologie der Intersubjektivität*, Erster Teil: 1905–1920 (Husserliana XIII)

## Editor's Introduction (Iso Kern)

Kern opens with Husserl's own note in *Formal and Transcendental Logic* (1929): the main lines for solving the problem of intersubjectivity and overcoming transcendental solipsism were already worked out in the Göttingen lectures of winter semester 1910/11, though carrying them through required detailed investigations that came much later; the *Cartesian Meditations* would give a short account of the theory, and the explicit investigations belonging to it were to follow (XVII). The promise was never kept — Husserl's later years went to other projects — and Kern presents the three volumes of *Zur Phänomenologie der Intersubjektivität* as the posthumous discharge of it. The framing is not merely pious. It fixes two things at once: that Husserl himself dated the decisive methodological breakthrough to 1910/11, and that the manuscript of those very lectures, printed here as Text No. 6, is among the papers he can scarcely have had in mind when he made the promise. Kern's edition therefore both fulfils and exceeds Husserl's plan, reaching forward past 1930 and back to material Husserl had left behind. The footnote's dating matters: Husserl wrote the note while correcting proofs in summer 1929, after drafting the *Cartesian Meditations* in spring of that year, with the main text of *Formal and Transcendental Logic* just behind him in winter 1928/29 — so the retrospective claim about 1910/11 belongs to the same burst of self-assessment as the Paris lectures.

Editing the intersubjectivity manuscripts posed a problem the *Husserliana* had not yet faced in this form. Earlier volumes rested largely on lecture courses; here, apart from a few lecture manuscripts, the material is in the main writing Husserl addressed to no one — neither reader nor auditor — but to himself, "monological meditations" (XVIII). Kern's characterisation of these research manuscripts is precise and consequential: Husserl carried out his philosophical reflection *by* writing, using shorthand as the medium in which he could move with the least friction, so the pages hold reflection *in statu nascendi* rather than results reached elsewhere and then recorded. The editorial difficulty follows directly. Some pieces are brief notes, others run for dozens of pages; the typical unit is three to twelve shorthand sheets, a single day's work, and such day-works cluster into groups that circle the same question over weeks or months — what Kern calls a phase of thinking, whose texts stand to one another as variations. Repetition is thus structural, not accidental: Husserl had to keep re-thinking his way into a problem to hold it in view, and he also used repetition to test whether a thought was original and genuine.

Repetition of a second kind arises across periods. Husserl returned to the same problems at intervals throughout his life, but each time from a new angle, out of new insights, in new contexts, so that even the recurrences are variations rather than duplicates (XIX). This observation does the editorial work of justifying a chronological rather than a systematic arrangement: if returns are always transformations, then the sequence of returns is itself the philosophical content.

What Husserl wrote in this meditating mode was less what he knew than what he did not yet know. He wrote not to record insights but to gain them, thinking-and-writing as one act, often trying out dialectical possibilities without committing himself to them (XIX). Hence the research manuscripts offer paths rather than results — including paths that lead nowhere. The point guards against a misreading that dogs any use of the *Nachlass*: a formulation found in these pages is not thereby Husserl's doctrine, and an apparently un-Husserlian thesis may be a probe rather than a position.

Husserl's genius lay in restricting himself to a single problem and deepening it analytically without systematic side-glances; but the same disposition carried his meditations away from their starting question, back into presuppositions and fundamental problems, so that they often lack outward closure (XIX). Digressions, confrontations, parallelisations, and — increasingly in the later years — the drive to survey several problems together compound the effect. Kern is naming the feature that makes these texts hard to excerpt: their unity is the unity of a movement, not of a completed argument, and cutting them at a topical boundary destroys what they have.

The style of the meditations changed. Until the late 1920s Husserl formulated his thoughts in fully articulated language; in the last decade of his life — whether from the pressure of a death no longer distant or from a weakening command of easy expression — the writing turns fleeting, abrupt, telegraphic, which makes the shorthand of those years exceptionally hard to read (XIX). The remark bears directly on the third volume of the set rather than this one, but it explains why Kern's editorial confidence varies across the three.

How is such meditation to be published? Kern, taking his cue from Husserl's own remark quoted at the outset, approached the manuscripts from the side of subject matter, and at first considered selecting the texts thematically *and* ordering them according to the inner systematics of Husserl's problematic (XIX–XX). He abandoned the second half of that plan. The systematics was never a settled matter for Husserl himself, and the individual texts are too materially comprehensive to be filed under a single problem-aspect. Decisively, the inner character of these meditations excludes systematic ordering altogether: they were not written from the standpoint of a system but deposit the actual course of thoughts in search, so the only ordering principle that answers to them is the one they already have — the chronological. Kern adds that chronology also serves comprehension, since it alone makes visible the original interconnection of the problems as they were pursued, and that it accommodates the "repetitions" (which are seldom mere repetitions) by documenting Husserl's thinking on intersubjectivity across its several periods. He goes so far as to have asked whether a sachlich viewpoint should govern the publication at all, or whether any selection at all does violence to a path of thought recorded in these manuscripts in so singular a way. Practical and economic objections to a purely chronological edition settled the matter, and a thematic grouping is defensible because most of the manuscripts are in fact led by one material problematic.

The selection rules follow (XX–XXI). Only manuscripts came into consideration in which intersubjectivity is the leading aspect, or in which — for Husserl himself — its weight is greatest. This principle has a sharp negative consequence Kern states plainly: material on intersubjectivity embedded in ethical investigations, in systematic introductions to phenomenology, in reflections on time, in the constitution of the "worldview," on the idea of anthropology, on the grounding of the human sciences, remains reserved for volumes under other titles, because there intersubjectivity does not dominate. Nearly every philosophical problematic has an intersubjective dimension; if that sufficed, the criterion would collapse. Likewise excluded are unpublished preliminary drafts and reworkings belonging to Husserl's own published projects — *Logical Investigations*, *Ideen*, *Krisis* — which belong in new editions or supplementary volumes of those works. Kern therefore concedes what the three volumes cannot be: a complete record of everything Husserl wrote on intersubjectivity outside his publications. He balances the concession with a strong claim about what they are: after this three-volume publication little is likely to remain in the unpublished papers that would significantly alter or enrich the now accessible thoughts of Husserl on the theme.

Even within the criterion, not everything qualifying has been printed (XXI–XXII). Texts contributing nothing of their own to the development of Husserl's thought on intersubjectivity — texts that within the same period merely repeat, or say less well, what others say — were set aside. Kern's account of his hesitation is the methodologically interesting part: no two texts say exactly the same thing; divergent formulations of a thought already expressed, or the same thought in different contexts, can throw independent light on the problems and their development; and differences Husserl himself found negligible may weigh heavily for a reader with other interests. Where in doubt he printed rather than omitted, preferring redundancy to the risk of burying an important contribution and consigning it, by exclusion, to permanent neglect. The remark is an implicit invitation to read the volumes as documentation of a genesis, not as an anthology of best statements.

The first volume contains — apart from some later appendices — manuscripts from 1905 to 1920 (XXII). Kern insists the terminal dates are not arbitrary but mark real incisions. From 1905 comes the first manuscript that can be grouped under the heading of intersubjectivity; 1920 closes a productive period, since from 1921 Husserl was preparing the "great systematic work" that was to replace the previously planned continuation of the *Ideen* and that set new demands on the intersubjectivity problematic as well. The second and third books of the *Ideen*, in Edith Stein's and Ludwig Landgrebe's compilation, appeared only in 1952 as *Husserliana* IV and V, the first book in 1913 — a dating that will matter repeatedly below, since it explains why so much of the intersubjectivity material lay in drafts for an unpublished continuation.

Husserl began developing his problems of intersubjectivity only from 1905 on. Kern is careful about the apparent counter-instance (XXII–XXIV). The First Logical Investigation (1901) does touch intersubjective matters through the communicative function of linguistic expression, but Husserl introduces that aspect precisely in order to set it aside and to consider expression only with respect to distinctions that belong to it equally in solitary and in communicative speech. Still, the treatment of the communicative function already commits Husserl to a conception of the experience of others that will survive into his later work: linguistic expression *intimates* the speaker's acts or lived experiences, which the hearer, on the basis of the expression, "reads into" the speaker. Husserl subsumes this intimation, together with non-linguistic expressive movements such as gesture and facial expression, under his concept of indication: for the interpreter these are throughout signs for outwardly manifested experiences. Indication, on the account of the First Investigation, rests not on insight into a logically lawful connection — that would be proof — but on the conviction of the sign's existence non-insightfully motivating the conviction of the indicated thing's existence, and its psychological origin is association, whose characteristic achievement is to bind merely co-present contents into a felt belonging-together. Understanding another's experiences in linguistic expression, like the understanding of expression generally, is thus for the *Logical Investigations* not a judging of the assertive kind but consists merely in this: that the hearer *apperceives* the speaker as a person expressing this or that, or, as one may say, straightforwardly *perceives* him as such — even though the mental phenomena that make him a person do not fall within another's intuition. The apparent paradox marks exactly the point Husserl will spend three decades unfolding. How such an association is possible for the understanding of other persons is a question whose answer determines whether the proposed account of understanding is entitled to stand; Husserl raises it in the *Logical Investigations* without answering it. The difficulty is severe on his own premises, since another person's experiences can never fall within the understander's intuition, so it becomes obscure how the associative belonging-together of intimating indications and intimated foreign experiences can come about at all. Kern notes that an analogical transfer from self-perception seems not yet to be in view: that thought would require the understander to perceive directly, in his own case, the connection of bodily or vocal expressions with expressed experiences, and then, on the strength of similarity, to apperceive an analogous connection of these expressions with foreign experiences — whereas Husserl explicitly denies, at least for mimic and gestural expression, that there is any indicative relation for the expresser himself, and denies too that there is a phenomenal unity between the expressions and the experiences expressed. So the associative unity of indicating expressions and indicated experiences would have no ground in self-experience. These problems lie wholly outside the horizon of the *Logical Investigations*. Two guards are worth registering: the *Logical Investigations*' account of expression is not yet a theory of the experience of others, and the later doctrine of analogical apperception cannot be read back into it.

Husserl took up the problems of the experience of others and of intersubjectivity in exactly the years in which he laid the methodological and systematic foundations of his philosophy: 1905 to 1909, the years in which he worked out the idea of phenomenological reduction as the most fundamental of all methods (XXIV–XXV). Kern makes the connection tight. By Husserl's own judgement the concept and the correct use of "phenomenological reduction" are first found in the manuscript complex from which the first text of the present volume comes — the so-called Seefeld sheets, going back to the stay at Seefeld in summer 1905 — and the method was then developed at length in the lecture courses of winter 1906/07 and of 1907. The position of the reduction forced Husserl straight into the problems of intersubjectivity: how, on a method that goes back to pure and absolute consciousness, can a plurality and mutual separateness of absolute consciousnesses be reached and grounded? Kern adds that Husserl seems in those years always to have held to a monadological multiplicity of consciousness, that is, to the absolute character of foreign consciousness over against one's own, so that he had to face at once the problem of how foreign consciousness can come into its own from the standpoint of the phenomenological reduction. Text No. 1 and its appendices (I–VII) document these problems. The point deserves emphasis because it inverts a common picture: the reduction did not first isolate a solipsistic sphere that intersubjectivity was later called in to repair; the demand for an account of the other is coeval with the reduction itself.

One name belongs immediately to Husserl's engagement with the problem of foreign experience: Theodor Lipps (XXV–XXVI). Especially in the early years, Lipps's theory of empathy seems always to have stood in Husserl's horizon; an index is that Husserl used the term "empathy" for the experience of other persons from the mid-1900s on, though without its implying for him any determinate theory of the experience of others, as it did for Lipps. Husserl used the word regularly for foreign experience even though he never accepted Lipps's theory, and even after he had explicitly stated that empathy was a false expression. The first direct contact with Lipps's theory of empathy *as* a theory of foreign experience probably occurred in 1905 or shortly after: the papers contain a five-page shorthand excerpt from Lipps's essay "Weiteres zur Einfühlung" (1905), of which Husserl owned an offprint. Not only the handwriting of the excerpt points to 1905–1907, but also the fact that Husserl, in his usual manner with excerpts, copied almost verbatim without inserting critical remarks or taking a position — which suggests he had as yet no position of his own in this problematic. Kern's footnote adds that the excerpt covers only the first seven pages of the essay, where Lipps defends his theory against critical objections.

The first use of the word "empathy" in Husserl's own voice appears, so far as Kern knows, in 1905, in the summer-semester lecture course on the theory of judgement (XXVI–XXVII). There the word names the mode of consciousness that the *Ideen* will call the neutrality modification of non-doxic (non-objectivating) acts. Just as one finds the same sense-content in objectivating acts of belief and in mere presentations, so within the non-objectivating acts there is a split between an actual question and empathy into the question, actual joy and empathy into joy — and Husserl glosses it: instead of "empathy" one may also say "thinking oneself into," "phantasying oneself into." He extends the point to the qualitative modification (in the terminology of the *Logical Investigations*) of objectivating acts, calling quasi-judgements "empathies": we can empathise ourselves into the judgement without ourselves judging, so that mere presentation is the empathy-modification of judgement. Kern flags the proximity of this usage to Lipps — more exactly to his concept of aesthetic empathy as the quasi-reality of works of art, which carries no consciousness of empirical actuality — but suggests Husserl may have taken the word not from Lipps but from Alexius Meinong, whose *Über Annahmen* (1902), studied closely by Husserl, explained empathy into artworks as phantasy-feelings and phantasy-desires, concepts roughly corresponding to Husserl's neutrality modification of non-objectivating acts. Husserl dropped the term in this sense after the 1905 course. The episode matters as a guard: the earliest occurrence of *Einfühlung* in Husserl has nothing to do with the experience of others.

Everything indicates that Husserl began to occupy himself with the problem of empathy from about 1905, above all in confrontation with Lipps (XXVII). Kern raises the possibility of an additional stimulus from Alexander Pfänder and Johannes Daubert, both Lipps's students, who spent the summer of 1905 with Husserl at Seefeld; Husserl also had personal relations with Lipps.

How Husserl treated the empathy problematic in those early years (1905–1910) cannot be established with certainty (XXVII–XXVIII). The larger and more important part of the relevant texts survives only in excerpts and transcripts Husserl made in his own hand between 1914 and 1916 — copies undertaken for heavily reworked and thereby obscured manuscripts, made not out of historical fidelity toward older texts but to secure and regain the matters expressed in them. Kern's examples show the contamination: both Text No. 2 (Husserl's own "excerpt from my oldest sheets on empathy before 1909") and Text No. 3 (largely a transcript of sheets from 1909) already contain the idea of the pure ego, which Husserl unambiguously admitted into his phenomenology only around 1912. The passages in question give the impression of later interpolations and supplements, so one must still assume that the excerpt and the transcript rest in their main lines on old sheets from before 1909 and from 1909. But few texts on empathy survive in original form from that early period, and it cannot always be said exactly which formulations, concepts and theses already belong to those years and which were inserted afterwards. The methodological caution is Kern's most important single warning for reading Texts Nos. 2 and 3.

Despite this, a fairly unified picture of Husserl's earliest empathy problematic in 1905–1910 emerges, and it differs clearly from the later treatments (XXVIII). Its characteristic is that it goes primarily to the empathy of sensations and sensation-fields into the foreign body — the empathy of what Husserl calls the aesthesiological stratum. On these early texts, grasping the foreign body as sentient is the lowest and foundational level, constituting the foreign body as body prior to the understanding of any kind of expression. Kern quotes the point: expression of the psychic is not spoken of with respect to sensations; that the other, when he has directed his eyes at a thing, has a sensation of pressure is not grasped through expression, since expression refers to a higher sphere, and a body must already be there for certain bodily processes of a determinate kind — thoughts, feelings, resolves, tensions, expectations, attentiveness — to be marked out in it and "express" themselves. The inversion of the *Logical Investigations* is complete: expression, there the entry point, is here derivative.

This earliest Husserlian approach puts him at once at odds with Lipps (XXVIII–XXIX). Husserl states the difference directly: his investigations of empathy did not make the expression of psychic movements, of foreign life-utterances, their main theme; rather, what stands first for him is the unity given in one's own self-perception of lived-bodiliness and fields of sensation and movement — the peculiar fact of the localisation of sensations in the lived body, and with it the fact of grasping the body as endowed with sensations. It is upside down to attach the whole problem of empathy to mere expressive movements, to bodily expressions and utterances of the psychic, as is customary and as Lipps did in his own meritorious accounts; the grasping of "utterance," of the "expression" of psychic acts and states, is already mediated by the grasping of the body as body. Kern ties this to a reproach directed against Lipps, which brings out the peculiarity of Husserl's whole problematic and the aims he pursued in it: he rejects Lipps's appeal to wonderful, further inexplicable instincts of life-utterance and of imitation of foreign expressive movements as an explanation of empathy, calling it a refuge of phenomenological ignorance, and demands in its place a phenomenological clarification guided by the exhibiting of the phenomena themselves. Because Husserl makes this demand, he is so intent on the first presuppositions of empathy. The methodological point is what drives the aesthesiological turn: a genuine phenomenology of the other cannot begin where an unanalysed instinct is asked to do the work.

There are many further divergences between Husserl's earliest empathy problematic and Lipps's theory (XXIX–XXX). Above all Husserl never interpreted foreign experience, as Lipps did, as a projecting-in of what is immediately lived into outwardly perceived bodies. One may say straightforwardly that he began to develop his thoughts on foreign experience *in opposition to* Lipps's theory of empathy, which he had studied thoroughly — the evidence being not only the underlinings and marginalia in Husserl's copies of Lipps's works but also the roughly twenty-five pages of shorthand excerpts he made between 1905 and 1915 from Lipps's various presentations. But all the other divergences appear secondary in comparison with the difference in aim and in the initial framing of the problem. Yet there are not only divergences. The commonality that was probably most important and most fruitful for Husserl lies in the parallelisation of empathy with recollection carried out by both. Already hinted at in Husserl's earliest empathy texts, this parallel plays no essential role there; it gains fundamental significance in the lectures "Basic Problems of Phenomenology" of winter 1910/11 and in the deepened treatment of the empathy problematic preserved from 1914 or 1915. Kern's flag is worth keeping: the recollection-parallel is the hinge on which the later theory of appresentation turns.

Husserl's earliest position on empathy was characterised above through its primary problem-framing, and it must essentially remain so characterised, because no worked-out solution to these problems exists from that period (1905–1910) (XXX–XXXI). Only in 1914 or 1915 did Husserl develop thoughts one may really call a theory of foreign experience. From 1905 to 1910, and indeed beyond, he got hardly further in this problematic than problem-formulations and conceptually very vague intimations of possible solutions. What remained decisive for him throughout was an establishment already pronounced in the *Logical Investigations*, which also grounded the whole empathy problematic of his time: another's experiences are not properly and originarily perceivable. Yet he nonetheless takes foreign experience to be a kind of perception, and he tries in his earliest texts first of all to determine this peculiar kind of perception more precisely by contrast with mere thing-perception, using the concepts of *Mitwahrnehmung* and *Kompräsentation*, later replaced by *Appräsentation*, which include a re-presentation (*Vergegenwärtigung*). He thereby grants foreign perception a certain mediateness. How this mediating something "more original" is at play and how it is possible, these oldest texts give scarcely any information. Husserl also turns away from the thought of the analogical inference, but he made the central argument of Lipps against that theory his own only briefly, since he was probably already then trying to think empathy, in its lowest stage, in the direction of an experiential apperception resting on analogy and association, and to think that apperception as having its ultimate origin in self-perception. The guard here is against reading the mature Fifth Meditation backwards: appresentation is at this stage a name for a problem, not a solution.

Of all these earliest texts on empathy one may well say what Husserl himself noted on a sheet from 1909 in this connection: that the problem of empathy as the constitution of the foreign I in my consciousness is not solved by any of the preceding analyses (XXXI–XXXII). Kern couples this with another self-assessment, in a text probably from 1910, occurring among the modes of time and of re-presentation: how do matters stand with the analogising consciousness, with the pictorial, and with the symbolic; further, with the empathy I have rather neglected? One must therefore say, with Husserl himself, that in this first period he did indeed pose and develop the problem of foreign experience in a determinate direction, but did not work it out in a way answering to the demands of his philosophical standpoint or permitting a halfway satisfactory solution. As regards the constitutive analysis of empathy, this holds even for the *Ideen*: although in the only book of that work published in his lifetime Husserl announced a closer investigation of empathy for the following book, the accounts of empathy both in the first draft of the second book, written immediately after *Ideen* I (November–December 1912), and in the somewhat later related texts that Edith Stein took up into her elaboration in 1916–1918, do not go essentially beyond the position of 1905–1910. What the 1912 draft does bring to the fore is the thought of the manifold of appearance of foreign consciousness — the "world-aspect" the other has — a thought that appears earlier in a secondary place; but it too was not developed then into a systematic analysis of empathy. Kern cites Edith Stein's recollection of Husserl's 1913 summer-semester course "Nature and Spirit," in which she says he expounded the phenomenological fundamental consideration from *Ideen* I largely verbatim from the manuscript of the second book, and that empathy was mentioned again and again there without Husserl saying what it consisted in — a recollection that, though it needs nuancing, roughly marks the state of his empathy problematic at that time.

For this earliest period (1905–1910) a quite different aspect of Husserl's engagement with intersubjectivity must also be noted: the question of the specific experience of the spiritual world of society and of history in its own context, of "world" as a context of motivation (XXXII–XXXIII). This is the aspect that connects Husserl less to Lipps than to Wilhelm Dilthey. Dilthey's idea of a descriptive and analytic psychology, meant as a pure analysis of the context of life without natural-scientific causal explanation, to serve as the foundation of the human sciences, probably furthered Husserl's thoughts of a purely subjective — that is, non-natural — context of different subjects of consciousness very greatly, and thereby prepared the drawing of intersubjectivity into the "field" of pure phenomenology. Kern adduces a letter from Husserl to Georg Misch of 27 June 1929 confirming the importance of the years around 1905 for his philosophical development: few conversations with Dilthey in Berlin in 1905 — not his writings — meant an impulse that led Husserl from the *Logical Investigations* to the *Ideen*, and that the phenomenology only incompletely presented in the *Ideen* and really completed only between 1913 and 1925 was carried through in innermost community with Dilthey, though with an essentially differently shaped method.

Of greatest importance for the formation of Husserl's intersubjectivity problematic, and for his philosophy generally, were the lectures of winter 1910/11, "Basic Problems of Phenomenology," published here for the first time, together with the associated "Preparation" of October 1910 (XXXIII–XXXIV). In two of his own publications and in his papers Husserl referred to hardly any other lecture course as often as to this one. He kept the manuscript together with the "Five Lectures" of 1907, which he regarded as foundational for his theory of phenomenological reduction, in the same bundle; when he planned the great systematic work in 1921 that was to replace the conception of the *Ideen*, he went back to the 1910/11 lectures, and in 1924/25 had them transcribed together with the October 1910 "Preparation" by his then assistant Ludwig Landgrebe — at which time an important piece of the original manuscript had gone missing (since recovered). The bibliographical detail carries argumentative weight: Husserl's own repeated returns are the evidence for the claim that this course is the decisive one.

The manuscript published here as Text No. 6 does not reproduce the entire two-hours-a-week course but only its first part, up to Christmas 1910 (XXXIV). By Husserl's own report, discussions in which he spoke freely attached themselves to that part, and the end of the present manuscript points to them. The absence of a manuscript for the second part is probably owed not only to its disputational character but also to the fact that from Christmas 1910 and through the whole second half of the winter semester Husserl was occupied with drafting his great article "Philosophy as a Rigorous Science" for *Logos*. During that semester he also held a second, four-hour course, "Logic as Theory of Knowledge," so he scarcely had time left to prepare the "Basic Problems" further in manuscript. No closer indications could be found of how the free second part proceeded.

Although the published manuscript of this course does not speak much of intersubjectivity, its greatest significance lies precisely with respect to it (XXXIV–XXXVI). Husserl refers to the course not by its official title but as "lectures on intersubjectivity," "lecture on empathy and extended reduction," "lecture on phenomenological reduction as universal intersubjective reduction," "phenomenological reduction and transcendental theory of empathy," or simply by the keyword "empathy." Its real achievement lay in the extension of the phenomenological reduction to intersubjectivity carried out in it — the methodical drawing of intersubjectivity into the thematic "field" of pure phenomenology. Kern quotes the retrospective assessment from the 1923/24 lectures "First Philosophy": for Husserl himself, as he confesses, the first cognition of phenomenological reduction was a restricted one; for years he saw no possibility of shaping it intersubjectively; finally a way opened that is of decisive significance for the possibility of a full transcendental phenomenology and, at a higher level, of a transcendental philosophy. That way opened with the 1910/11 course, or in its beginnings already in the October 1910 "Preparation," and consists above all in the thought of the so-called double reduction of re-presentation: in re-presenting, not only the re-presenting (actual) experience but also the re-presented experiences intentionally contained in it are reflectively grasped as phenomenologically pure consciousness. By this route Husserl could extend phenomenological experience to the foreign experiences re-presented in empathy, and could regard nature as an index for the phenomenological regulation and coordination that comprises all the consciousness-streams or "I-monads" standing in a context of empathy with one another. This extension of the phenomenological field of research to foreign consciousness does not arrive unexpectedly in the lectures but is prepared step by step. It is prepared above all in principle by detaching the idea of phenomenology from the Cartesian thought of absolute, indubitable givenness — that is, by recognising re-presentation as a mode of experience that is not merely legitimate but necessary within phenomenology itself. This does not set phenomenology back into the "natural attitude." Although in the "Preparation" (Text No. 5) the reduction to intersubjectivity is projected under the guiding idea of a "pure psychology," in the lectures Husserl grasps it as a transcendental method excluding all apperceptions of the natural attitude or experience. One of the first sentences of the course states that phenomenology is by no means psychology, that it lies in another dimension and requires an essentially different attitude from psychology. For phenomenology the name "transcendental psychology" also drops away. Under this bare title emerges what will become an ever greater problem for Husserl right up to his last work, the *Krisis*: the problem of the difference and the identity of pure psychology and transcendental phenomenology. Not only does the whole idea of phenomenology appear in new shape in the 1910/11 course through its extension to intersubjectivity; the course also opens far-reaching material problems, especially the principle of the unity of *one* consciousness (of "one stream of consciousness") over against other, foreign consciousness — a problem that will essentially determine Husserl's new position on the idea of the pure ego. Two guards are in play here: the extension of the reduction is not a relaxation of it, and the "double reduction" is a methodological device for making foreign experience phenomenologically accessible, not yet a constitutive account of how the other is experienced.

Husserl designated the 1910/11 lectures not only by reference to intersubjectivity but also, in two places known to Kern, as "lectures on the natural concept of the world," naming them by their beginning (XXXVI–XXXVIII). At first sight the beginning with the natural world-concept looks accidental relative to the extension of phenomenology to intersubjectivity carried out later in the course. Perhaps it is not. Husserl introduces the natural world-concept with explicit appeal to Richard Avenarius, whose works he knew and valued, and above all to *Der menschliche Weltbegriff* (1891), which he had certainly studied thoroughly by 1902. In that work, which sets itself the task of the "restitution" of the natural world-concept against its alteration by dualistic metaphysics, foreign experience plays a central role. The basic error of that metaphysics consists for Avenarius in the introjection or localisation of foreign experience in the body or brain of the fellow human, and in the transfer of the doubling of the world thereby arising to the ego itself and its own experience, whereby introjection becomes self-insertion. Avenarius's demanded restitution therefore consists primarily in the abolition of this introjection and self-insertion. The question whose answering Avenarius designates as the philosophical task *par excellence* runs: how do foreign experiences relate to the foreign individual? These thoughts are indicated but not expounded in the only fragmentarily preserved lecture manuscript of 1910/11. It is nonetheless informative to have them present, since an inner connection between the beginning of the lectures with the natural world-concept and their extension of the phenomenological reduction to intersubjectivity thereby suggests itself. It is also to be assumed that Husserl already had this extension of the reduction in view at the beginning of the course, since he had prepared it in advance (Text No. 5, October 1910) and since empathy is spoken of right at the beginning of the lectures. On closer analysis a significant kinship between Avenarius's restitution of the natural world-concept and Husserl's reduction to pure intersubjective consciousness would probably show itself, despite many differences and oppositions — indicated here only in outline: in both cases what is at issue is the abolition of a localisation of experiences in nature, the exclusion of "introjection" or of the insertion of "soul-life" into external bodies. For the rest, "introjection" in the context of psychophysical experience of self and other, as experience of the human being, remains for Husserl a problem thrown up anew again and again and treated differently each time, as the partly contradictory texts assembled by Edith Stein as *Ideen* II attest at length, and as Text No. 7 in the present volume also shows.

In the "Basic Problems" of 1910/11 Husserl expressed in germ thoughts he brought to full effect only much later (XXXVIII–XXXIX). He writes to Roman Ingarden on 10 December 1925 that the extension of phenomenological reduction to intersubjectivity, which he had worked out in a two-hour winter course of 1910/11 at an unfavourable but in many ways productive time, thus remained wholly unused. Kern reads this against the *Ideen*, which occupied Husserl from 1912: the thought of intersubjective reduction is not contained in them in fruitful shape. In the 1930 "Afterword" to the *Ideen* Husserl himself explains that the presentation of phenomenological reduction in the "fundamental consideration" of that work suffers from "imperfections": although in everything essential unassailable, it lacks the grounding of this idealism as regards the explicit position on the problem of transcendental solipsism or of transcendental intersubjectivity, on the essence-relatedness of the objective world valid for me to the others valid for me; the supplements were to be brought by the second volume, drafted at the same time as the first, which he then hoped soon to supply. In fact, in the first volume of the *Ideen*, the only one published in his lifetime, the phenomenological reduction, and thus the phenomenological field of experience, remains restricted to the pure consciousness of a single ego. But the second volume too — treating the ontology and constitution of the natural and spiritual world, and originally comprising also the epistemological part now published as the third — does not methodically carry through the extension of the reduction to intersubjectivity. Husserl there discusses empathy and communication, but not with respect to their reduction to pure consciousness (to the "transcendental others"), rather as constitutive achievements for the experience of the psychic and spiritual world. This is probably one reason why in 1921 Husserl let the draft of the continuation of the *Ideen* drop and planned a new "great systematic work," for which he immediately drew on the "Basic Problems" of 1910/11. In his two great lecture courses "Introduction to Philosophy" (winter 1922/23) and "First Philosophy" (winter 1923/24), in which he again systematically drafted the shape of his philosophy, he expressly went back to the double, transcendental-intersubjectivity-establishing reduction of 1910/11.

Husserl had extended the phenomenological reduction to intersubjectivity in 1910/11 without thereby developing a theory of foreign experience (XXXIX–XL). The passages on empathy in those lectures are very short; for drawing foreign consciousness into the field of phenomenology he contents himself with the quite general determination of empathy as a kind of re-presentation. Only some years after the 1910/11 lectures did he occupy himself intensively with the analysis of foreign experience and shape this problematic in a way that remained fundamental for his later reflections on the theme during the 1920s and 1930s. The sentence marks the volume's central claim about periodisation: the methodological breakthrough of 1910/11 and the constitutive theory of foreign experience are two different achievements, separated by several years.

Texts Nos. 8 to 13, with their appendices, document that fundamental engagement with the empathy problematic (XL–XLI). Alongside the 1910/11 lectures (Text No. 6) this group forms the central piece of the present volume. These extraordinarily thought-rich texts, which illustrate the style of Husserl's research monologues at their finest, belong closely together both materially and in the external appearance of the manuscripts (paper, script), and were probably written within one train of thought over a few weeks. Against this stands the circumstance that the manuscripts lie scattered today in several bundles in the papers: not only are the various manuscripts torn from one another, but even the sheets of the individual continuous manuscripts lie in different places, so that their restitution sometimes required a genuine work of detection and puzzle-solving. This is not a peculiarity of Husserl's earlier manuscripts on intersubjectivity; many later manuscripts are dissolved in the same way — for example the manuscript "Zur Lehre von der Einfühlung" of September 1918 (Text No. 15 of the present volume), for which Husserl had made a precise table of contents in his own hand on the original manuscript cover, and which is today scattered over no fewer than five entirely different bundles. The dissolution is above all owed to the fact that Husserl later reached back to these texts again and took out of the original bundles single sheets or whole texts that particularly interested him at the time, placing them in other contexts. Demonstrably he used, in early 1927, at least parts of the large manuscript group published here as Texts Nos. 8 to 13 in preparing his lecture course "Empathy in Phenomenology." On such reviewings he also destroyed single sheets he had repeatedly considered, so that the reconstructions undertaken here remained partly lacunose and sometimes only text-fragments could be presented. Nevertheless the group shows a strong unity and closure and gives a rounded picture of Husserl's conception of foreign experience at that time.

Exactly when Husserl wrote this group could not be established unambiguously (XLI–XLIII). Only one of the texts is dated in his hand: the manuscript of Text No. 11 carries the pencil note "August 1915." But in Text No. 10 reference is already made to this same Text No. 11, with the date "August 1914." The years 1914 or 1915 are for many reasons the only ones that come into question as the time of origin. The group was certainly not written before late summer 1914, since Husserl was occupied at least until spring 1914 with a large, never completed reworking of the Sixth Logical Investigation, and since Text No. 10 refers to the war situation. During the summer semester of 1914 Husserl held a course on "Basic Questions of Ethics and Value Theory" and occupied himself above all with axiological problems. The group in question could therefore have been written in August or September 1914. But against this speaks, besides the reference in Text No. 10, possibly also the circumstance that Edith Stein from winter semester 1913/14, under Husserl's direction, wrote a state examination thesis on empathy, which she submitted to him only in November 1914. Conversations with Edith Stein might have stimulated Husserl to his own investigations. Yet the group in question shows no particular relation to such conversations or to Lipps's theory of empathy, which stood at the centre of her thesis. It is rather improbable that Husserl found in the first weeks of the war the inner strength and calm for such subtle meditations; he himself reports that he was paralysed in the first period of the war. In spring 1915 Husserl returned to his draft of November–December 1912 of the second volume of the *Ideen* and busied himself with elaborating that draft again. But the group (Nos. 8 to 13) seems not to stand in the context of that elaboration. Not only is there in the manuscripts no reference at all to that second volume of the *Ideen* (just as, conversely, no reference at all is made to the group in the spring 1915 elaboration), but Edith Stein, who prepared the manuscripts for *Ideen* II and III for publication in 1916 to 1918, evidently did not know this group of texts at all. These fundamental texts on empathy seem not to have been written in the context of the work on *Ideen* II at all, although that work would have needed a deepened discussion of the problematic very much.

It is also improbable that Husserl engaged intensively with empathy during the summer semester (May to July) 1915 (XLIII). Alongside a customary historical-philosophical course he then held a two-hour class, "Selected Phenomenological Problems (as Introduction to Phenomenology)," in which arguments for transcendental idealism were also discussed. In a note added afterwards to that course Husserl remarks that he had forgotten the whole doctrine of intersubjectivity there, in the entire course, and asks whether that was war psychosis. Kern uses the self-reproach as negative evidence for the dating.

Most probably Husserl composed the group of texts on foreign experience in August or September 1915, as the date on the manuscript of Text No. 11 indicates (XLIII–XLIV). The necessity of occupying himself with this problem may have made itself felt precisely through the lack of it in the summer 1915 class. Husserl's capacity for work also seems to have improved in the course of that summer: on 20 September 1915 he writes that a better time has now come, that he has moreover promised a work for the end of October for Eucken's seventieth birthday, and that he is at present "on top" and has cherished thoughts he can no longer let go of. The Eucken *Festschrift* was never published because of the war, and it cannot be ascertained on what theme Husserl meant to write his planned contribution. But it is very possible that those cherished thoughts he could no longer let go of are the ones held fast in the group Nos. 8 to 13. The period from September 1915 comes no longer into question for their origin: at the end of September and the beginning of October Husserl was in a clinic with nicotine poisoning, and from early 1916 he was preparing his lectures for Freiburg im Breisgau, to which he had been called in the summer of that year. Those lectures were introductory in character and contained no detailed treatment of empathy. On grounds of content and of external circumstance it is excluded that the group first arose in Freiburg.

The problematic of foreign experience developed in this group has, despite a certain continuity, quite different dimensions from the earliest empathy texts of 1905 to 1910 (XLIV–XLV). The basic problem is no longer the empathy of sensation-fields into an external body but is expressed rather in questions such as: how does an external thing come to have the form of the null-appearance? or: how is the translation of the external appearance of the foreign body into the systems of appearance of interiority possible? The question is: how is an external appearance interpreted as an internal system of appearance, that is, as a new view of the world centred in a proper null-point of orientation (a point of view) — how, then, is the experience of foreign points of view possible? This framing arose for Husserl from the insight that into a merely externally appearing body no sensation-fields can be empathised at all immediately, but that only the null-appearance-mode of a body can demand the apprehension as lived body — there being the crucial point. Within this problem-horizon he undertakes quite novel considerations about the phantasy-like displacement of one's own lived body into an external appearance, about the mirror image, about the possibilities of I-doubling, in which throughout phantasy-consciousness plays a wholly central role. He thereby develops the thought that the *possibility* of the experience of the other is given prior to its actual experience, so the idea of an in a certain sense a priori character of the other, though from quite different motives and in a quite different sense from what Max Scheler in the second instalment of his *Formalismus in der Ethik* (1916) and in *Wesen und Formen der Sympathie* (1923) represented as "Robinson's" a priori evidence of the existence of some "you" at all.

In this problem-context a substantially new concept of "self-experience" emerges in Husserl probably for the first time (XLV): the word no longer means, as in older texts, merely psychophysical self-experience, but encompasses a whole "world" grasped in the intersubjective context as an individual "world-appearance" ("world-aspect") — and for it Husserl will use, from about 1916 to the beginning of the 1920s, the expression "solipsistic sphere," and later "sphere of originality," "primordial sphere," "sphere of ownness." The lexical history is a doctrinal history: the terminology of the Fifth *Cartesian Meditation* is here in formation.

The idea of analogy remains of great importance for Husserl's discussion of foreign experience (XLV–XLVI). On one side he explains that the foreign I is posited as analogue of the "I there," of the I displaced in thought into the there, in a presentation through analogy that presupposes for its part the apprehension of one's own lived body by analogy with any external body. On the other side he writes that the "apperception" of the foreign soul-life takes place without further ado, that the understanding apprehension is thus an immediate one, so that not only is there no analogical inference but there is also no analogising, no transfer through analogy. In this opposition, occurring in texts written at the same time, one must keep in view the different senses of "analogising" — an expression that in Husserl can also mean a proper image-consciousness — and further note that Husserl underlays this opposition with the distinction between the non-intuitive, dark apperceiving of the other and the clarifying intuitive filling-out of that apperception, in which analogy first comes forward. This distinction, only hinted at in the group of 1914 or 1915 but later of great importance as the difference between improper and proper foreign perception, does not uproot analogy from Husserl's idea of foreign experience, if only because on his general theory the intuitive shape of an experience-act over against its original shape is what is really in question. Out of the idea of analogy in the conception of foreign experience there arise for Husserl many recurrently discussed problems, above all the problem of the constitution of one's own lived body as a full spatial body coming to givenness in external appearances. The guard here is against taking the denial of analogical *inference* as a denial of analogy: what Husserl rejects is a mediate reasoning, not the analogical structure of the apperception.

Three further texts with appendices (Nos. 14 to 16) follow the weighty group of 1914 or (more probably) 1915 (XLVI–XLVII). The first of them, which by Husserl's own indications arose between 1915 and 1917 — on the basis of a genetic mode of consideration expressed in it, most probably 1917 — and stands thematically close to the third chapter of the first section of *Ideen* II, treats the relativism of normality of experience conditioned by psychophysical conditionality and discusses conditions of the possibility of an intersubjectively identical world. In this connection Husserl expresses a thought he would take up and discuss repeatedly afterwards: that intersubjectivity ("objectivity") belongs not only to reality but also to appearances ("aspects"), which in the first draft of *Ideen* II of 1912 he still assigned to the individual subject (the individual monad).

The only fragmentarily preserved three-part Text No. 15 comes from Husserl's stay in Bernau (Black Forest) during September 1918 (XLVI–XLVII). There are few manuscripts from those months. Husserl suffered severely under the political collapse of Germany and could concentrate only with difficulty on philosophical work; in that situation he seems rather to have sought refuge in reading other philosophers. So he read several writings of the still highly revered Paul Natorp from the beginning of September 1918, among them the *Allgemeine Psychologie* (1912). Husserl wrung the text published here as No. 15 from himself in that bleak stretch, which shows through even in its lines. Its problematic belongs in the vicinity of *Ideen* II ("Nature and Spirit"), on which Husserl's private assistant Edith Stein was then working and which likewise stood again at the centre of his own interests. He develops the distinction between "straight" and "oblique" (reflective) empathy — already hinted at in a text of 1910 — and investigates above all the function of empathy for the constitution of the psychophysical unity of the human being, for the constitution of "interiority" and "exteriority," and for the constitution of the empirical person. Over against the accounts of 1914 or 1915 (Texts Nos. 8 to 13), held to be "too constructive," these show rather an "empirical" character and call the former into question through the thesis of the non-intuitability of one's own lived body as such in external space.

In the last text (No. 16), which arose as an independent reflection in the context of an excursus on the natural and human sciences within the ethics lectures of summer semester 1920, Husserl gives a fundamental and comprehensive survey of his problematic of foreign experience and reaches back to the foundational lectures of 1910/11 (Texts Nos. 5 and 6 of this volume) (XLVII–XLVIII). At its centre stands the difference between improper empathy as psychophysical experience of nature and proper empathy as absolutely empathetic taking-cognisance. Kern's closing choice of text is thus also a closing argument: the volume ends where it structurally began, with the 1910/11 breakthrough, now surveyed from the far side of the constitutive analyses.

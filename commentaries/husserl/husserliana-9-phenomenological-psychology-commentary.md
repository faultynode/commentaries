# ***Phenomenological Psychology: Lectures, Summer Semester 1925*** **(Husserliana IX)**

## **Introduction**

### **§ 1\. The Development of Modern Psychology; Dilthey's Decisive Critique and His Proposals for Reform (Explanatory and Descriptive Psychology)**

Husserl opens the lecture course with a historical placement of psychology. Psychology, he writes, belongs to the very oldest of the sciences — alongside logic, ethics, political theory, and metaphysics. Already with Plato it was founded; with Aristotle it received its first systematic elaboration. There has never been any lack of zealous cultivation of psychology since then, and especially not since the beginning of the scientific modern age. The point of this opening is not merely to give a chronological reminder; Husserl is establishing that psychology is *not* a young discipline whose troubles can be ascribed to immaturity. On the contrary, its long pedigree heightens the question: why, in spite of all this cultivation, has it failed to develop in the way the other rigorous sciences have? Already with the first attempts at a new founding of natural science — Kepler, Galilei, Descartes at the threshold of the seventeenth century — corresponding attempts at a new founding of psychology paired themselves. The exact natural science that emerged then, methodically novel and flowering with unheard-of successes, became at once a model that retroactively shaped psychology and the concrete humanities (*Geisteswissenschaften*). There arose immediately a passionate striving to carry out a similar methodical reform in psychology — to reshape it, too, into a science exactly explanatory from elementary laws. The motif of *imitation of natural science* is here installed already as the first historical fact: from the seventeenth century onward, modern psychology was conceived in the shadow of mathematical-experimental physics.

However — and this is the decisive turn — *none* of the great early modern attempts produced what was hoped for. Husserl names them: the psychology of Cartesian dualism; the psychology of Hobbesian materialism; the metaphysical monism of Spinoza; and even Locke's great impulse toward an empirical psychology on the basis of inner experience. None of these led to the desired success: none brought psychology into the shape and force of a continuously ascending development; none produced a unity of firmly grounded, steadily expanding theories and methods. The list is significant. By including Locke alongside Descartes, Hobbes, and Spinoza, Husserl is signaling that even the path that *seemed* most promising — the appeal to inner experience — failed to deliver a cumulative science. The point is not that these thinkers were not great. The point is that, in spite of their greatness, psychology never reached an analogue of the steady progressive form that physics achieved over the same period. Husserl is preparing the diagnosis: something more than effort or genius is needed; a structural problem in the very conception of the discipline must be at fault.

Husserl contrasts this failure with the manifest success of the mathematical and physical sciences in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. When we think of their theoretical acquisitions, we are overwhelmed by admiration. At every stage of their development they were compelling, because of the *evidence* of their ever-more-refined method and the magnitude of useful applications. Nothing analogous can be reported from psychology — at least not when we consider the period from the seventeenth to the nineteenth century. And the deficiency cannot be ascribed to a lack of intellectual power in the psychologists; they were certainly not behind the great natural scientists in mental capacity. Husserl is sharpening the diagnostic question: if it is not for want of talent, what was the structural problem?

The structural problem, he says, lies in deep-seated difficulties that remained hidden even into our own time. Psychology could not, from the beginning, resist the *temptation of naturalism* — the temptation to imitate, externally, the model of natural science. While the new natural science, once set on its proper methodical track, could no longer come to a standstill and grew almost miraculously in heightening progression, psychology, for all its great willing, could never — through sheer succession of hopeful beginnings — come to a *definitive* beginning that would have allowed a definitive progression to follow. The contrast is sharp and revealing: physics had *one* productive beginning, and from there a continuous growth; psychology had a series of beginnings, each promising, none cumulative. The metaphor of "never coming to a final beginning" is the lecturer's compressed verdict on three centuries of modern psychology.

In the nineteenth century, psychology took on a new and indeed magnificent upswing. The impulse came from leading German physiologists and physicists: Johannes Müller, E. H. Weber, Volkmann, Helmholtz, Hering, G. Th. Fechner — and was further promoted by the organizational power of Wundt. There arose a psychology intimately bound up with natural science and especially with physiology, faithfully fitted to it in methodical manner. The naturalistic psychology that had already been intended in the times of Descartes and Hobbes now realized itself in an essentially new form: by approaching, with the highly developed experimental art of physiology, the psychophysical problems of sense-psychology — or rather of sense-*psycho*physics. This new psychophysical, physiological, experimental psychology achieved international validity, internationally homogeneous institutes and working methods, and a unanimity of conviction that for a time scarcely seemed to lag behind that of the less exact biological-natural-scientific disciplines. Husserl is here giving a fair, almost generous account: the new experimental psychology achieved real things, including a kind of international scientific community, and one should not deny it that achievement.

Undoubtedly this psychology brought to light an abundance of very remarkable facts that had previously been hidden, and these are *really* psychological facts, even if physiologists count many large groups of them within their own science. The unanimity in theoretical *interpretation* of these facts lags behind that of the exact natural-scientific disciplines, but in another respect — namely with regard to the methodical style of the theories sought — it is once again complete. In any case, the conviction reigned in international research circles of the new psychology, a conviction unbroken until very recently, that now finally the only true and genuine psychology had been set in motion as a rigorous science, on whose paths the totality of all psychological problems — all those belonging to individual and cultural spirituality — would have to lie. All that was required was, as in every experience-science aimed at elementary structure and explanation from elementary laws, patient restraint and a thoroughly cautious advance; one should only not reach prematurely for problems not yet ripe for scientific treatment, for which the factual basis is not yet supplied and the necessary experiential concepts are not yet created. Husserl is reporting the *self-understanding* of the experimental psychologists: the slow-and-steady model, the conviction that they were on the right track, and that any unresolved questions would be reached eventually by elementary upward construction.

A not inconsiderable increment of inner security was gained by the new psychology through the successful creation of a *psychotechnics*. Now this psychology really seemed to stand on a par with exact physics. It had reached the point, indeed, of making its psychological knowledge — exactly as physical and chemical knowledge — technically utilizable. The mention of psychotechnics is important: applicability is the ultimate proof of seriousness in the natural-scientific worldview, and Husserl is reporting that experimental psychology had reached that threshold and felt itself confirmed by it.

And yet — Husserl now turns — it came to pass that against this in a certain way assuredly successful psychology, a very radical skepticism arose, of a kind that could never have arisen against exact natural science. This skepticism has gained ground from year to year and taken various forms. Among them, the one that should interest us here is the most radical skeptical reaction — the one going back to Dilthey on the one side and to the new phenomenology on the other. This skeptical critique turns against nothing less than the whole methodology of this psychology, insofar as it claims to explain the facts of spiritual life *really spiritually*, that is, *psychologically*. Husserl flags here, as a preview of his own program, that the two main lines of rejection — Dilthey's and his own phenomenology — converge on a single point: that the dominant psychology is methodically inadequate to what it ostensibly studies.

In its later, more matured form, this skepticism turns also against the methodical *manner* of establishing psychic facts *as psychological*. This means: it is not enough to make experiential facts simply visible as facts; what is required is to unfold them in explicit *inner* experience, and to bring them under concepts that express the structural essential character of these facts as psychic. And here springs the most radical reproach: this psychology is reproached with being *outright blind* to the proper essential character of psychic life — blind to all essential forms of the specifically spiritual, i.e. of a subjectivity that is intentionally active and constitutes spiritual formations as well as spiritual community. For this reason it is wholly incapable of accomplishing what every so-called exact and explanatory science is supposed to accomplish; for all explanation is knowledge from essential concepts and along the guideline of the essential necessities grounded in them. The accusation, then, has two parts: experimental psychology *misses its object* (it is blind to the intentional life of consciousness), and consequently it *cannot fulfill its explanatory promise* (since explanation requires essential concepts, which require essential intuition into the very kind of being it has missed).

In 1894, in the midst of a time filled with the most extravagant hopes for the new psychology — a time that simultaneously expected from it an exact grounding of the *Geisteswissenschaften* and a reform of logic, of epistemology, and of all specifically philosophical sciences — Dilthey's "Ideas Concerning a Descriptive and Analytic Psychology" appeared in the proceedings of the Berlin Academy: the first attack against this naturalistic psychology. Husserl calls it a work of genius, even if not perfectly matured, and says that it will certainly remain unforgotten in the history of psychology. He then characterizes Dilthey: thoroughly rooted in his studies in the humanities, undoubtedly one of the truly great humanists of the nineteenth century, he was far more a man of inspired *overall intuitions* than of analyses and abstract theorizings. The capacity for elementary experiential analysis, for logical precision, for thinking in precision-concepts as practiced in mathematical natural science — that was not his particular strength. But incomparable was his capacity to survey concrete spiritual life — individual and socio-historical — in its living concretions, to grasp intuitively its typical configurations, its forms of transformation, its motivational connections, and on the basis of these to carry out great overarching interpretations that make intelligible to us the proper being and becoming of historical spirituality in its concrete necessity. The portrait is sympathetic but pointed: Dilthey's greatness lay in synoptic intuitive grasp, not in analytic-conceptual rigor.

It was Dilthey, first of all, who *felt acutely* how little modern philosophy did justice to the *Geisteswissenschaften*, and felt how all the reformist efforts of modern logic and critique of reason, whether they reached back to Kant or to English empiricism, were all too one-sidedly determined by natural science. Early on he labored himself for a *critique* of reason as it is active in the humanities — for an epistemological clarification of the essence and possibility of those magnificent achievements which lay before us as the new *Geisteswissenschaften*. And there he soon stood before the question of how far the new physiological and experimental psychology could ever do justice to these sciences of spirit, how far its pretension — namely, to be the theoretical foundational science for explaining concrete spirituality — was justified. He came to a decidedly negative result. Husserl is here reconstructing Dilthey's central motivation: an unease, born from the actual practice of *Geisteswissenschaft*, with the dominant epistemology and psychology, both of which were modeled on physics.

In extensive expositions Dilthey seeks to show that, over against this "explanatory" or "constructive" psychology (as he calls it), a "descriptive and analytic" psychology is needed; its Idea he attempts to sketch in detail in continuous critique of the now-dominant experimental psychology. This experimental psychology, he says, follows the ideal of exact natural science, in particular that of modern *atomistic* physics. Just as physics does for physical appearances, it wants to subordinate the appearances of psychic life to a causal nexus by means of a limited number of unambiguously determined elements. It proceeds, like physics, hypothetically and constructively, by means of inferences that *transcend* experiencing intuition. So, on the basis of experience, it projects hypothetical substructions of *unexperienced* causal connections and law-hypotheses about them. But — and Husserl here italicizes Dilthey's verdict — *this whole procedure is wholly inadequate to the essence of the psychic*. It has sprung from an unwarranted extension of natural-scientific concepts over the domain of psychic life and history. Husserl is presenting Dilthey's argument carefully: the critique is not that experimental psychology lacks rigor, but that the *type* of rigor it borrows is foreign to the nature of its subject matter.

In natural science this procedure has sense and necessity. For natural science rests on *external* experience, which gives us nature as merely spatial juxtaposition; meanwhile what gives this mere juxtaposition its lawful unity — the objective connections of pervasive causality — is not immediately intuitively given. Psychology, however, and in further consequence all *Geisteswissenschaften*, relate to the one *spiritual nexus given throughout in inner experience*. Inner experience gives no mere juxtaposition; it knows no separation of pieces, of independent elements. It knows only states *internally* interwoven, interwoven in the unity of a universal nexus, which itself is necessarily co-given as nexus in inner intuition. Whether or not we look at it and its individually emerging moments, whether or not we direct our noticing particular gaze at individual perceptions, memories, feelings, willings, whether or not we direct it at their interweavings, their flowing into one another and their springing apart — all of that, and *as one* nexus, is *Erlebnis* (lived-experience). Living life streams ever on, and it is not merely *is*, it is *lived*, and at any time a noticing, a considering, an evaluating can be directed to it. But these themselves are only new pulses of this life, not external to it but ordered into it as moments, occurring as living-through within the living, directed at individually emerging life in the unbroken unity of one life and lived-experience. The paragraph performs, in Dilthey's voice (and prepared by Husserl), the decisive turn: the inner is not a juxtaposition of elements but a connected, streaming life.

It is in this — that psychic life is not given merely externally and as a juxtaposition, but is given *in its nexus*, given through self-knowledge, through inner experience — that the *fundamental difference* of psychological knowledge from knowledge of nature consists. And it is in this also that the fundamental peculiarity of the *Geisteswissenschaften* over against the natural sciences is grounded. While the latter can only *explain* hypothetically-substructively and constructively, it is the essence of the *Geisteswissenschaft* to *understand*, to render intelligible. More precisely, its proper task is — through the establishment of unified intuition, in returning to the inwardly experiencable spiritual nexuses (one's own and others', the individually-subjective and the community-subjective) — to render intelligible the unity of spiritual life, of spiritual working, performing, creating. In short formula: *natural-scientific explaining stands in contrast to humanistic understanding*. The classic Diltheyan formula is here cited and adopted as the structuring contrast.

But — Husserl adds an important nuance — this *understanding* is not already accomplished by the mere establishing of unified intuition in which the concrete nexuses are *re-lived*. Every poetic depiction does that much. The much-celebrated psychology of the great poets and writers is, in truth, no psychology, no science. Rather, a scientific, intuitively grounded analysis, concept-formation, and systematic description is required. The task concerns, as will be elaborated, not only the descriptive exhibition of the *types* of individual psychic data, but also the types of the *connections*. For in the psychic, the individual is mere abstraction. A feeling, a mood, a thought emerging, a hope stirring — none of these is ever an *isolated* experience; it is what it is in the psychic milieu, in its interweavings, its motivations, indications, and so on; and these are inseparable, co-lived moments of the connection, of the psychic function. So the great task is to systematically dissect the manifold interweavings that unify themselves into the unity of a structural connection, and to describe them in their typology. *Structure* designates the complex interweaving belonging to every concrete phase of streaming psychic life. The succession of phases, which gives unity successively to the stream of life, also has its typology. The psychic nexus is a nexus of effect, a nexus of development, and is thoroughly ruled by an analytically demonstrable *immanent teleology*. Through all of life runs a being-directed at values, a unified striving for happiness, for satisfaction — an instinctive or consciously purposive being-directed. The paragraph thus reaches the topic of *structure*, of *teleology*, and of the impossibility of taking the elementary psychic moment in isolation; it also distinguishes scientific descriptive psychology from the merely artistic re-creation of inner life.

So at one time instinctively, at another consciously purposively, new spiritual formations issue from spiritual formations; the subjective psychic activities and the accomplishments of individual subjects connect themselves into common activities and accomplishments. The theme thereby includes creative spirituality of all levels, up to the high formations of art, science, religion. These must be made intelligible by understanding, brought to descriptive understanding by means of elementary and systematically advanced analysis. Husserl is here completing the upward sweep of Dilthey's program: from elementary psychic states, through structural complexes, to the highest cultural forms — all are to be understood by descriptive-analytic, intuitive psychology.

The great significance of Dilthey's expositions lay above all in what he said positively about the *unity of psychic life as a unity of lived-experience*, and in the requirement he drew from this for a purely intuitively drawing, descriptive psychology — a psychology that, despite "mere" description, was to accomplish a kind of highest explanatory work, namely that which Dilthey expressed with the word *Verstehen*, understanding. For can one not say in a very good sense, as historians do: to understand a historical nexus *is* to explain it in the only sense that makes sense? Dilthey had pressed through to the recognition that scientific description on the basis of *external* experience and scientific description on the basis of *inner* experience — experience of the purely spiritual — designate a fundamentally essentially different accomplishment. He saw that in the domain of spirituality the latter already carries in itself a complete explanatory achievement, by virtue of the *re-livability* of all spiritual interweavings, including all interweaving of motivation. Pure analysis and description, sufficiently far carried out, even *fully satisfactorily* explains what the concrete, historical *Geisteswissenschaft* asks about — because here explanation has no other sense than to make visible the inner necessities of spiritual becoming, of the spiritual emerging from spiritual grounds. Spirituality bears in itself, and *purely* in itself, a kind of causality: the *causality of motivation*. And this belongs to the very content of lived-experience and is thus directly accessible to plain intuition and description. To understand the genesis of a work of art humanistically is *not* to do psychophysics — i.e. to ask after the causalities running between the artist's psychic life and physical nature, the psychophysical causalities — but rather: to transpose oneself into the life and striving of the artist, to bring it to a corresponding and fully living intuition, and from his motives to make intelligible the system of purpose-settings and realizing actions. Once this is fully accomplished, no meaningful art-historical question remains. The paragraph completes Husserl's reconstruction of Dilthey's positive doctrine: *motivation* is the proper kind of "causality" in the spiritual realm, and re-living motivation is the proper kind of "explanation" in the *Geisteswissenschaften*.

### **§ 2\. The Reasons for the Limited Effect of Dilthey on His Contemporaries: Inadequacy of Their Understanding and the Limits of His Approach**

---

The time, at first, was not ripe for the reception of such thoughts. Those who stood under the spell of the naturalistic attitude of the dominant psychology were *unable* to immerse themselves in the problems Dilthey raised. To be sure, Dilthey did exert a strong influence — not only through the Academy treatise we are considering, but already through his older works — in one respect: through his ever-new efforts to demonstrate, and to characterize in principle, the methodically unbridgeable peculiarity of the natural sciences over against the *Geisteswissenschaften*. The previously dominant naïveté of the naturalistic interpretation of the *Geisteswissenschaften* was at any rate broken through. One can say that the whole great literature of the recent decades on the manner and method of history and of the historical *Geisteswissenschaften* over against those of the natural sciences goes back to Dilthey — though Husserl adds in a footnote that this is perhaps to say too much. All in any way valuable thought-motifs had already been struck by him. But precisely the most significant thoughts — those of a purely intuitive, descriptive-analytic psychology in opposition to the dominant natural-scientific psychology — found at first no resonance whatsoever. The shape of the situation, then, is paradoxical: Dilthey's *general* program of distinguishing the *Geisteswissenschaften* from the natural sciences did get through; but his *positive proposal* for what should replace naturalistic psychology did not.

Granted, Husserl says, one must admit that Dilthey's critique lacks a sufficiently *principled* sharpness. Stimulating as he is in what he says against the humanistic pretensions of naturalistic psychology, he does not press through to a principled clarity about its proper sense and the limits of its possible accomplishments. In fact, such accomplishments did exist in the great groups of psychological facts and factual regularities that experimental psychology had first brought out, which can only be produced in its inductive-experimental method. On the other side, the persuasive force of even Dilthey's most valuable positive expositions on the method and problem-circle of a purely descriptive inner psychology was, for various reasons, inadequate and unconvincing. Above all, the exemplary illustrations were insufficient, insofar as they were unable to awaken the conviction that from intuitive particulars and a description tying onto them, a psychology — an explanatory elementary and foundational science — should come forth. The point: Dilthey was so good at the historical-concrete that he never showed how the *psychological* would emerge as a *general*, *law-bearing* science out of the intuitive descriptive material.

In the historical *Geisteswissenschaften* — which deal with historically given individual spirituality, with states and laws in their respective individual typology, with the conditions and historical becoming of peoples, with the historically factually given artistic currents, artists and art-works, scientific formations, and so on — what is at stake everywhere is the *individual*, and the *individual* explanatory accomplishment of intuitive and descriptive analysis of which we have spoken. The creative becoming of a work of art becomes individually intelligible, finds a purely *individual* "explanation," when we are able to transpose ourselves into the artist, into his spiritual life, into the milieu that motivates him spiritually — naturally on the basis of interpretation of the historical data. By going after the motives that determined the artist, we bring ourselves *from within* to the understanding of what he strove for, willed, realized, and what original sense the work itself creatively received from him. The art of historical description and explanation, then, holds itself purely to the individual. So in every *Geisteswissenschaft*. But psychology is not supposed to be the science that depicts individual and historical facticities in their individual nexus and makes them individually intelligible from their individual nexus-motivation.

Rather, psychology is supposed to be a science that *recognizes the laws* of psychic life and the laws by which communal spirituality and culture in general grow. Through this we want to be enabled to explain from laws — that is, to bring about, for the *Geisteswissenschaften* as well, a lawful explanatory accomplishment beyond their individual explanation. But in this respect Dilthey did not offer any satisfying answer. How is it supposed, on the basis of mere inner experience or mere intuitive making-vivid of foreign spiritual life and of a community life, to accomplish a description that is more than individual understanding? How is it supposed to lead to general psychological laws? How is it ever to get beyond vague empirical generalizations? Should we have to make do with an inductive morphological typology, with a mere natural history of historical spiritual formations? That may have its value. But is that everything? Does not every morphology, every natural history, demand a rational explanation from laws? You will later learn to understand the radical deficiency of Dilthey's idea of a descriptive psychology. Husserl says here only briefly, in advance: Dilthey had not yet seen that there is such a thing as a *general essential description* on the basis of intuition — but now an *essential intuition*; just as he had not yet seen that the relation to consciousness-objectivities, which constitutes the radical essence of psychic life, is the proper and infinitely fruitful theme of systematic soul-analyses *as essential analyses*. The decisive omission, then, in Dilthey is the absence of *essential* (eidetic) intuition and the absence of the *intentional* analysis of consciousness.

Husserl begins a new lecture and pauses to recapitulate, since the gap between sessions may have loosened the spiritual continuity with the first two lectures. He wants not merely to repeat but also to let some additional thoughts flow in that are suited to make the manner of Dilthey's psychological intentions even clearer, and to make the critical words with which he had closed more intelligible. Against the "explanatory" psychology led by the natural-scientific model, Dilthey demands a "descriptive-analytic" one. The former seeks, in a hypothetically-constructive procedure, to construct from assumed psychic elements — say sensation-data and feeling-data — a causal nexus that is itself unexperienceable, and, like physical natural science (and, in psychophysics, intertwining with it), to accomplish "explanation" through causal laws. Against this psychology, the modern naturalistic experimental psychology, Dilthey is in a certain respect *skeptical*. It is incapable of serving as a foundation for the *Geisteswissenschaften*. Rather, what is required is a *purely intuitive* (and not hypothetically-constructive) descriptive, analytic psychology. This is — Husserl quotes almost verbatim — the depiction of the constituents and connections that occur uniformly in every developed human psychic life.

The naturalistic psychology, Dilthey insists, overlooks that psychic connections are *lived* and that they are all bound together in a *single* connection — the total connection of the streaming psychic life. *Connection* (Zusammenhang), then, is not something *hypothetically added in thought*, not something *inferred*, but, as *lived* in purely inner experience, is accessible to direct articulating analysis and description. Psychology is nothing other than description and analysis of this *one* connection given inwardly as life itself — and indeed, as Dilthey thinks, throughout it is a matter of life-connections of the developed "typical human." Nature we explain; psychic and spiritual life we *understand*. To accomplish understanding is the task of all historical and systematic *Geisteswissenschaften*, which are for this reason referred back to descriptive and analytic psychology as the foundational science of understanding. The hypothetical substructions of experimental natural-scientific psychology can only confuse them and burden them with useless hypotheses. A psychology that renounces the grounding of what happens in spirit out of the understandable connection of spiritual life is necessarily *unfruitful*. Husserl notes in a footnote that in the lectures themselves he gave fuller exposition and used the Aristotelian expression "apodeictic" for "explanatory" knowledge: explanation from laws is (a) pure a priori, (b) the apodictic form of the empirical regularity of mathematical physics.

Husserl illustrates Dilthey's claim with several examples. Religion is a realm of spiritual culture; when we speak of it, we necessarily come upon general concepts like feeling, will, dependence, freedom, motive, and the like. It is clear that such concepts can only be clarified within the generally regular psychic connection as it is purely intuitively given in inner experiences. From there we must create an understanding, e.g., of how God-consciousness arises and gains power. Likewise when in jurisprudence the universal spiritual fact *law* becomes the theme: we are led back to fundamental concepts such as norm, statute, accountability, and so on. How could we clarify these concepts otherwise — how could we make intelligible the purposes effective in law and how individual wills are subjected to the law — without returning to the regular connections in the inwardness of human psychic life? Similarly in the sciences of state, where concepts of community, rule, dependence, and so on, lead us back again to inner psychic facts; and likewise of course in the *Geisteswissenschaften* that seek to give a theory of literature and art. It is clear that this holds of all *Geisteswissenschaften*, whatever cultural systems or external organizations of human society they relate to — family and community, church and state. Having sprung from the living connection of the human soul, they can be understood only out of *that* itself. Only because uniformity and regularity exist in psychic life can they arise as overarching powers over the individual and make possible a common order for all individuals. Husserl is here giving the program of Dilthey in extension: every domain of culture is grounded in the regularities of psychic life and is therefore the proper domain of an intuitively-descriptive psychology.

Notable, Husserl adds, is the parallel with epistemology. As much as epistemology claims an independent position over against the *Geisteswissenschaften*, the same holds for it, as Dilthey extensively shows. The title *knowledge*, with all its special configurations, finally leads back to the unity of the psychic connection as the substratum of all knowledge-processes. So epistemology, too — if it is not to operate with unclarified concepts of a randomly grown psychological tradition — is referred back to a descriptive, analytic psychology as its underlay. Husserl thereby has Dilthey's whole architectonic before us: the *Geisteswissenschaften* and *epistemology alike* both presuppose, as their foundational science, descriptive psychology.

Critically, Husserl now turns to indicate again that, however significant the impulses and however inspired the intuitions in Dilthey's expositions, great defects nonetheless made themselves felt. His contrasting of the naturalistically outwardly-turned psychology with the descriptively inwardly-turned one was not yet led back to a final clarity, to the ultimate principled sources. Husserl reminds the audience of one main point that, though it played no explicit role in the contemporary counter-critique, nonetheless worked in the hidden background. It is in any case decisive for why, with all admiration for Dilthey's intuition, we cannot stop at his ideas and cannot recommend the imitation of the methodology that hovered before him.

The return to inner experience and to the descriptive analysis to be accomplished in pure inner intuition makes possible an *individual understanding*: an understanding of the occurrence of an individually spiritual act or formation in the connection of lived-experience — an understanding from individually motivating grounds. The reconstruction of the concrete motivation makes visible the *necessity* — e.g., the necessity of why precisely this decision of a politician had to grow as it did, or why a researcher had to come precisely to this theoretical train of thought. This is a kind of *individual* explanatory accomplishment. But psychology is supposed to explain from *general insights*; it is supposed to be a science of *laws*, to gain a generally theoretical fund of laws that does for the *Geisteswissenschaften* (and, if Dilthey sees rightly, also for epistemology) something analogous to what mathematical theory and theoretical physics do for the natural sciences. But how can we, in a psychology that purely rests on inner experience of psychic life and on description, arrive at *law-generalities*? Or are such not possible here, and is a lawfully explanatory psychology only possible as *psychophysical*? It seems almost that, according to Dilthey, a descriptive-analytic psychology should be and could be nothing other and nothing more than a *descriptive natural history* of human psychic life — of the "developed typical human," as Dilthey expressly says.

But, Husserl asks: is the typology of psychic life — which we trace back by elementary analysis to sensations, sensuous feelings, perceptions, memories, expectations, manifold types of judging knowing, of valuing, desiring, willing behavior — is this typology something like a *mere natural-historical* typology? And when we then go after the typical personal forms, do we not come merely to a comparative empirical psychology of the oldest style, which delivers us a manifold of typical forms of personalities, characters, temperaments, of associations, but never anything like a *general necessity*, an explaining that enables knowledge from laws? All natural-scientific explanatory accomplishment that comes about under the title *physics*, and which, normally understood, is taken as an *unconditional* task of natural knowledge, rests on the fact that nature — the realm of so-called external experience — has a so-called "a priori" structure. More precisely, over all the empirical typology of given nature, as systematically pursued in the natural-historical disciplines and depicted in descriptive type-concepts, there stands a realm of *necessary and exact generalities*. Before all questions about particular empirical configurations of a physical thing, there stands the fact that it *is* in general a thing, that in all its changes it preserves an *ideal norm*, a *form* — what we call its *mathematical essence*. In all empirical apprehension, insofar as it is to be identifiable in unanimity and intersubjectively, as the *same* thing, it is *temporally enduring*, *spatially extended material*. But what is taken as such in factual subjective experience is in principle nothing fixed; in the play of "intuitions" it carries, however, a *mathematical* as ideal in itself — an ideal toward which the empirical is *approximation*.

Already in saying "spatio-temporally extended," one has indicated a mighty title for *over-empirical* necessities, which the mathematics of spatiotemporality seeks to develop according to axioms and theorems: an infinite realm of ideal laws to which every natural object as such, and every intuitive apprehension as approximation, is absolutely bound. Not that *quantitative* concepts are the essential thing — as if only such concepts brought necessities with them — but rather that nature has a *universal form*, a universal lawful framework that binds everything natural in unconditional necessity. It is not the place here to show how the reference back to this a priori makes a theoretically *explanatory* experiential science of the type of physics possible — if you only see, what is in any case quite manifest, that if we leave out of consideration this a priori framework, this referring back of the empirically actually given in each case to the *idea* lying in the infinite, then only a general *natural history* becomes possible, but nothing more of *law-explanation*, of an explanation from necessities. Husserl illustrates by means of physics what the *Geisteswissenschaft* lacks: the bridge from natural history to *law*, mediated by the *a priori essential framework*.

Should now the realm of "inner" experiences, should the lived-experiential nexus that constitutes our inwardly streaming life — or, taken purely concretely, the *person* — also not bear in itself a *formal lawful framework*, a universal form of absolutely unbreakable necessities or lawfulnesses, to which an explanatory accomplishment of *Geisteswissenschaft*\-work could be methodically referred back? And should not the same also hold with regard to the most universal structural forms for the union of individual personal spiritualities into communal spiritualities? If the critical thought just stirred up had no support, then we would stand before a remarkable paradox that Dilthey has not noticed. To make individual spiritual acts and formations intelligible — this is nothing other than to make visible their *individual necessity*. By intuitive reconstruction of the concrete persons, of their individual lived-experiential and faculty-connections in which these acts are embedded — but in which they have also sprung forth from the proper motivation of this intersubjective experiential connection in that motivated manner — we see their individual necessity, and nothing else is called *Verstehen*. Husserl is pressing: if "understanding" already involves *necessity*, then we are already operating with something law-like, even at the individual level — and the question becomes irresistible whether there is not also a *general* psychic apriori beneath it.

It may be that we deceive ourselves in the reconstruction — e.g., in the historical reconstruction of the personality of a Bismarck and of the motives that guided him in a certain decision. But if the reconstruction is historically correct and has come to a really unified and complete intuition, then it becomes evident in it why Bismarck, *as he was* and *so motivated*, *necessarily* decided thus. And even if the reconstruction were false, if it is fully intuitive, then individual necessity lies in it: *if* Bismarck had been so-and-so and *so* motivated, *then* he would have to act in such-and-such way. Understanding reaches exactly as far as motivational analysis and reconstruction reach. If the reconstruction is incomplete, then the necessity, too, is incompletely clarified. The paradox now lies in this: that there is supposed to be necessity *in the individual*, and yet no *pure laws* of necessity — laws that, like all pure laws, present *hypothetical* connections, connections of pure generalities that refer to *pure possibilities*. Dilthey seems not to have clearly distinguished, even in the individual case, between *strict necessity* (or *explanatory understanding*) and an *empirical generality* and *empirical induction*; otherwise this paradox would have struck him and led him further.

Here, too, Dilthey's parallelization of the grounding of the *Geisteswissenschaften* and the grounding of epistemological critique by means of a descriptive psychology is illuminating. The parallel paradox indeed arises and has even stronger weight. Briefly: epistemology wants to make intelligible *in general and in principle* how cognitive doing in its psychic inwardness can bring about the accomplishment of objective validity. But how could it ever solve such a task if it rested on a psychological empirical knowledge that would deliver only natural-historical generalities instead of unbreakable and intuitively evident necessities? Knowledge principles cannot possibly be clarified from vague biological type-generalities. So in every respect: a *psychology that yields necessities* is a *desideratum*. The verdict is sharp: Dilthey's descriptive psychology, however valuable, cannot ground either the *Geisteswissenschaften* or epistemology, *because it lacks an apriori*.

The imperfect maturation of Dilthey's ideas explains the at first victorious reaction on the side of experimental psychology. The October issue of the *Zeitschrift für Psychologie und Physiologie der Sinnesorgane* of 1895 brought a brilliantly written reply on the side of Hermann Ebbinghaus, the founder of experimental psychology of memory. The tenor of the counter-critique is best expressed in some sentences of a private letter that Ebbinghaus had already sent the year before to Dilthey (cited from Misch, *Dilthey's Gesammelte Schriften*, vol. V, p. 423). Ebbinghaus declares that he holds the whole thing fundamentally misguided and misleading, and says further: he was not really prepared for "so much unfairness toward the present psychology and so little clarity as to the fact that what you are recommending to people is just what people have long been doing." Now, the latter is, with regard to the essential, certainly not correct — though it is correct that many of Dilthey's expositions could be interpreted that way. The lack of a clean, principled clarity also makes it understandable that Dilthey's many attempts to draft a reply did not succeed, and that a detailed counter-publication subsequently failed to materialize. At any rate, the success was, at first, entirely on the side of experimental psychology — also in the eyes of the outside-standing philosophers, to the greatest mortification of Dilthey, who almost despaired of being able to lay out clearly to his time what he had grasped, in his inspired intuition, as overwhelmingly significant insights. The closing of §2 thus delivers the historical verdict: Dilthey was *right in spirit* but, lacking principled clarity, could not press through against the experimental psychologists. Husserl is preparing the ground: phenomenology is to supply what Dilthey lacked.

### **§ 3\. Task and Significance of the "Logical Investigations"**

---

A complete change of the inner and soon also outer situation of Dilthey took place at the turn of the century. In 1900/01 Husserl's *Logical Investigations* appeared, as the result of ten years of efforts at a clarification of the pure Idea of logic, accomplished by reaching back to the *sense-giving* or *cognitive accomplishment* taking place in logical consciousness, in the lived-experiential nexus of logical thinking. More precisely, what was at stake in the individual investigations of the second volume was a turning back of the intuition to the logical lived-experiences which take place in us when we think, but which we precisely do *not* see, do not have in attentive gaze, when we carry out the thinking-activity in natural-originary fashion. The thinker knows nothing of his thought-lived-experiences, but only of the *thoughts* which his thinking continuously produces. It was a matter of getting hold of this hidden-running thought-life through subsequent reflection and fixing it in faithful descriptive concepts. It was further a matter of solving the newly-arising problem: making intelligible *how* there takes place, in the accomplishment of this inner logical lived-experience, the formation of all those spiritual configurations that emerge in assertive-judgmental thinking as manifold-shaping concepts, judgments, inferences, and so on — and which find in the fundamental concepts and fundamental principles of logic their general expression, their general objectively-spiritual imprint. The opening paragraph of §3 announces *the* turn: phenomenology arrives as a method of recovering the hidden life of thinking by reflection, in order to make intelligible the *correlation* between logical thought-formation and the subjective accomplishments that produce it.

---

#### **a) Critique of Psychologism; The Essence of Irreal (Ideal) Objects and Irreal (Ideal) Truths**

---

Preceding all this in the *Logical Investigations* was an important preparatory work. It concerned a *pure grasp* of these imprinted sense-configurations themselves and the combatting of all empiristic or psychologistic mingling of psychological contents of thinking activity into the logical concepts and propositions themselves. For example, the expressed proposition — that which is gained as result in logical thinking — contains, as sense-formation, *in its sense itself*, nothing of the thinking, just as little as the *number* counted in the counting-experience contains in its sense-content anything of the psychic activity of counting. Numbers, propositions, truths, proofs, theories form, in their ideal objectivity, a self-enclosed realm of *objects* — not things, not realities, like stones or horses, but objects nonetheless. In its main core, the formal logic systematically founded by Aristotle is a theory of propositions, a discipline that brings out the pure forms of possible judgmental propositions and the formal *laws* such propositions must satisfy if they are to be capable of being true. To this belongs the whole "syllogistic." Let us now consider the highest fundamental concept of this logical theory, that of the *proposition*, and hold ourselves to a clarifying example. The opening of subsection (a) thus enacts the central anti-psychologistic move: distinguishing the *content* (the proposition) from the *act* (the judging).

The Pythagorean theorem is, in the realm of propositions, *one and unique*, whenever I or anyone else thinks or proves it — just as the number 4 is one and unique, whenever and by whomever it is thought, brought to conscious givenness through counting. A proposition, a number, is no real occurrence in the universe — not occurring here or there in individual oneness, moving or resting and exercising real causality. That holds only of the *written* number and the *spoken* proposition, here or there. But the written expression of a number or proposition, the utterance, is *not* the Pythagorean theorem itself, nor the number 4 itself. As Leibniz already saw — indeed already the Scholastics, and as Bolzano pressed to most consistent effect — such *irreal*, or, as one also says, *ideal* objects are, in their numerical-identical uniqueness, substrates of true and false judgments *just as* real objects are. Conversely, *object* in the most general logical sense means nothing other than *anything whatever about which something meaningful and true can be predicated*. The classical anti-psychologistic point is here stated with its full generality: ideality is a form of objectivity, and is no less rigorously a substrate of predication than reality.

It is useful for our purposes, Husserl notes, if I emphasize that actually *two* things had to be fought through in the systematic-critical preliminary considerations of vol. I of the *Logical Investigations*: (1) against logical psychologism, the just-discussed *irreality* and yet *objecthood*, the ideal identical *being* of such objects as concepts, propositions, inferences, truths, true proofs, etc. This entailed that these objects are *meaning-unities*, in such a way that *in the sense-content itself* nothing occurs of the psychic acts and other subjective lived-experiences that constitute the *changing* consciousness of these objects; and that they are in no way bound to real human beings or other subjects. Psychic acts, as their subjects, themselves belong to the real world. (2) And in close connection: there belongs to the ideal objectivities a *pure ideal* truth, which says nothing about the world, nothing about anything real. Thus pure logic in the syllogistic speaks about *pure concepts* and *pure propositions*; likewise pure arithmetic speaks about pure numbers of the number series, expressing law-truths and theories in whose sense not the slightest is said about the spatiotemporal factual world. The point: the anti-psychologistic battle was at the same time a discovery of *two* domains of irreality — irreal objects and irreal truths.

The truth that 2 \+ 3 \= 5 stands by itself as a pure truth, whether there is a world, and this world with these actual things, or not. It conceals not the slightest of real factualities in its sense. Likewise the logical principle of contradiction, etc. Purely ideal truths are "a priori" and are insightful as truths in the unconditional necessity of their generality. This generality requires, however, a characterization. Ideal objects are not related to factual states-of-affairs, but they are, *implicit in their sense*, related to *possible* states-of-affairs — to ideally possible, ideally conceivable ones. In pure arithmetic no counted things or processes of the factual world are presupposed; but every number has a *general extension*. Under the idea 3 fall all conceivable sets, all that can be fictively imagined, insofar as they are countable as 3\. Accordingly, every arithmetical proposition and the whole of arithmetic is a priori applicable to every conceivable, and in further consequence to every actually occurring, multitude in facticity. Likewise the logic of propositions (the Aristotelian syllogistic) is applicable to all conceivable cases in which conceivable humans or other rational beings would judge and, in their judging, utter the corresponding propositions as expressed sense. So to every ideal object inseparably belongs an *ideal extension*, the idea of an *all-totality of conceivable singular cases*, and with it a *holding-in-general*, an unconditional general validity not bound by the presupposition of factual particulars, factual facts. That is precisely what *unconditional general validity* means. "Before" the givenness of a fact and a fact-world (a priori) we can be unconditionally certain that what we, as logicians, as arithmeticians, etc., assert must be able to find application for everything and anything that could come to us as corresponding factual reality. Husserl is here unfolding the structure of ideality: every ideal object has its own kind of *extension* — its range of conceivable possible singular cases — and this is what underwrites its a priori applicability.

Yet the generality reaches still further insofar as ideal objects, with their extension of possible corresponding singularities, can themselves refer to ideal objects. Thus numbers count not only possible realities; one can also count *pure numbers* themselves. Likewise the logical ideal truths speak about *propositions in general*. But propositions are not only propositions about the earth, about the Black Forest, about the factual nature in which we live; there are also propositions about *idealities*; at any time it is possible to make statements about statements, just as the logical laws themselves are propositions about propositions. If pure logic states that every contradicting proposition is false, every non-contradicting one possible, then this also holds for the propositions that are uttered *in logic itself*, in which nothing is said about the factual world. Let us summarize: there are doubtless *irreal* objectivities and corresponding *irreal* truths, with an ideal extension by which they are possibly referable to ideal, but always also to *possible real*, beings — but, well-noted, to *ideally possible* real beings, whose factual existence is not presupposed. Correspondingly, there are *sciences of irrealities*, "*a priori sciences*," whose Idea theoretically dominates a self-enclosed realm of ideal objects. The subsection thus closes with the basic Husserlian conception: a *region* of irrealities, an *a priori science* of them — paradigmatically logic and mathematics.

---

#### **b) Investigation of the Correlation: Ideal Object – Psychic Lived-Experience (Sense-Forming) by Means of Essential Description in the Reflective Attitude**

---

But the greatest theme of the *Logical Investigations* was not such determinations of the "Prolegomena," always with restriction to pure logic, but *descriptive investigations of psychic lived-experiences* to which all sorts of ideal objects of logic and mathematics are in a certain way *inseparably related*. Namely, apart from the *extension*\-relation lying in the sense of ideal objects — by which, for instance, every "number" has a relation to possible groups of singular objects and especially to possible singular realities — ideal objects necessarily have *another* relation to possible reality: namely, to *psychic subjects and activities*. This is precisely the relation that played the main role in the battle against psychologism in logic. What in this battle had to be excluded as a ruinous *contamination of ideality* — in order to gain a pure formal logic as parallel to pure mathematics — that *should and must* now become the chief theme: namely, the *epistemological-psychological*. The dialectical structure of the move is here striking: what had to be *separated out* in vol. I now has to be *thematized* in vol. II — the relation of the ideal object to the act-life that makes it conscious.

Let us consider more closely: a proposition can predicate about stones, heavenly bodies, and the like — therefore about real beings. It does this through its *sense*. But about whatever it may predicate, it has *infinitely many possibilities* of being factually asserted by us or by some conceivable someone, in possible judging-acts on the basis of possible subjective lived-experiences of perceiving, remembering, picturing. Similarly, to the sense or content of a pure number nothing belongs of a counter and his counting-experiences. But ideally we can conceive any arbitrary psychic subject, a human being of given reality, or a fictive centaur, who counts and in counting becomes conscious of the relevant number. Indeed, we see ourselves pressed to say: the numbers are *produced* in the counting, the judgment-propositions are *produced* in the judging-doing. The remarkable status of ideal objects, then, is that they exhibit a double relation: they have their *being-in-themselves*, and yet they come to consciousness only as *produced* in subjective acts.

A remarkable state of affairs thus confronts us with the ideal objectivities with which the logician has to do. On the one hand, they have, as objectivities, their own kind of being-for-themselves, being-in-themselves, and relative to this we have the *a priori truths* purely related to their being-for-themselves and in-themselves — those of arithmetic and pure logic. This *in-itself* means: they are *what they are*, whether they are counted or thought, judged, known — or not. Thus every mathematical proposition or every number. On the other hand, it is *unthinkable* that such ideal objects should not be capable of being conscious and knowable in their correspondingly subjective psychic lived-experiences and activities. And indeed in such a way that we see ourselves driven to say: in the appropriate case, the number is *produced in counting*, the corresponding proposition, the corresponding truth, is *subjectively formed* in the judging-doing. Husserl is here describing the *paradox of ideality*: the object is independent of the act, and yet is given only through the act — and *just this* duality is what needs investigation.

This very circumstance — that ideal objects confront us in given cases as *subjective formations* in forming lived-experience and doing — was the source of the at that time almost universal *psychologization* of ideal objects. Now even when it had been made evident that ideal objects, in spite of coming to formation in consciousness, have their own being, being-in-itself, there nonetheless existed a great task that had never been seriously seen and taken up: namely, to make this peculiar *correlation* between *ideal objects* of the pure-logical sphere and *subjective psychic lived-experience as forming doing* into a *theme of research*. When a psychic subject — myself, say, this thinking human being — accomplishes in my psychic life certain (and certainly not arbitrary but quite specifically structured) psychic activities, then there is accomplished in them a successive *sense-forming and producing*, whereby as the successively becoming product the corresponding number-formation, the corresponding truth, the corresponding inference and proof, the corresponding theory, and so on, emerges. If I "repeat" an equivalent producing — if I once again carry out the same counting, predicating, inferring actions — they are indeed psychically a *new fact*, but I can evidently recognize that what has come to be is identically the *same* pure number, identically the *same* truth, etc. To speak of this in vague and empty generality, however, gives no *scientific* knowledge. How do the hidden psychic lived-experiences look that stand in correlation with the respective idealities — and that must run their course in the manner of quite specifically belonging *productions* — so that the subject can have consciousness and evidently knowing consciousness of these idealities as objects? The question is sharply posed: the *correlation* is not just to be acknowledged, but to be *concretely described*.

Therewith the proper theme of the *Logical Investigations* — and, in corresponding extension, of the *whole* of phenomenology — is named. You see that the primary interest does not lie in the idealities themselves and does not want to rest and be satisfied in their own sphere. Arithmetic, the a priori science of numbers and other purely mathematical formations, is the matter of the mathematician. With regard to traditional logic, it is no different — only that, before, a purification had to be carried out and it had to be shown that, regarding the formal-logical, there is an exactly parallel, a purely logical and ideal objectivity. Indeed it turned out that this discipline of pure propositions and truths in general is *inseparable* from arithmetic and the whole formal mathematical analysis, so that under the title *mathesis universalis* a single science had to be marked off, as Leibniz had already recognized — only now made comprehensible. So now in general: one can do mathematics as a mathematician, and the theme is then the mathematical idealities purely for themselves and in their ideal relations to one another. As mathematician one constantly lives through the correlative mathematical activities, but knows nothing of them; that one can only by *reflection*. In any case, they are *not the scientific theme* for the mathematician. But if one turns the theoretical interest precisely to the *manifold* of subjective doing — to the whole nexus of subjective life in which the mathematical "begets itself" in the mathematician — then a *correlative* direction of research is taken. It is obviously a direction in which the mathematical *also* occurs — namely, as a respective ideal formation, as it "appears" in forming and otherwise consciousness-producing lived-experience and has a kind of share in temporality and reality, without itself being a real and thereby temporally individuated object.

What was here taken up in concretely-to-be-carried-out individual investigations was, as can now also be said, *a theory of logical-mathematical knowing*, a scientific investigation of mathematical knowing in one with the mathematically known — but quite concretely, exhibiting step by step in psychological reflection the correlative inner occurrences which, in logical-mathematical knowing, so to speak make it the case that mathematical objectivity can occur: occur as concept, as proposition, as propositional connection, as theory and whole science, but also as number, set, multiplicity, as substrate of a theory of manifolds, etc. Husserl is making explicit the meta-task: not the mathematics, but the *production-conditions* of mathematical objectivity in conscious life.

You now grasp why the *Logical Investigations* designated this work directed at the psychic *also* as *descriptive psychology*. In fact, what they alone intended and had to intend was the production of an *unveiling inner intuition* of the thought-lived-experiences hidden from the thinker, and an *essential description* moving in pure inner intuition and related to the pure experiential givenness. On the other hand, however, to characterize the new-kind peculiarity of the method, the name *Phenomenology* was chosen. In fact, a new kind of method of dealing with the psychic broke through here. On the one hand, the task or attempt was new: to ask back radically and consistently *from* the relevant categories of objectivities *to* the modes of consciousness specifically belonging to them — to the subjective acts, act-structures, lived-experiential underlying — in which such objectivities become conscious and at the highest come to evident self-givenness. The new task is the *back-questioning* (Rückfrage) from the object to its mode of consciousness — the move that becomes characteristic of all subsequent phenomenology.

Already at the first stroke a new world opened up. With astonishment it was to be observed what immeasurable manifold of differences emerges purely intuitively in the innermost life, and becomes accessible to strictly scientific work, when one only has the courage to interrogate *consciousness itself* in a consistently carried-out inner intuition — and, so to speak, watch *it* perform within itself the becoming-conscious of such objectivities, see *how it looks from within* as objectivity-accomplishing consciousness. The "new world" is the world of intentional life *grasped from within*, and the *Logical Investigations* discovered its scope by sustained inner attention to the doing of consciousness.

I must add, however, that an extension of the problematic had to obtrude itself at once. The same problems that here had grown from the logical and mathematical idealities had obviously to be posed for *all* objectivities — including the cognitive *real* ones. And they could have been posed *from them* directly, too — and were already in part taken up in the *Logical Investigations*. The horizon thus expands: the phenomenological task is not limited to logic and mathematics but is general — every category of objectivity demands its correlate-investigation.

#### **c) Closer Characterization of the Reflection Decisive for Phenomenology (Stage-Wise Carrying-Out of Reflection)**

---

For the further characterization of the peculiarity of task-setting and method of logical clarifications, the following is to be noted. The *back-questioning* from the respective objectivities to the subjective lived-experience and active forming of a subject who is conscious of such objectivities was, from the start, *determined by certain guiding intentions* — which, however (so far I had not yet come in reflective clarity), did not yet come to expression in the form of clarified thoughts and demands. For the highest level of reflective clarity about the sense of the methodical procedure was only gained much later, precisely through reflection on what had been deepest need and enabling accomplishment in those "Investigations." Husserl is performing here a methodologically important self-correction: the method that *operated* in the *Logical Investigations* was not yet *understood* methodologically; what is now (1925) being taught is the result of years of subsequent reflection.

First of all, it was aimed at a *pure intuition* and *not* at a theoretical-hypothetical construction in the manner of natural-scientific psychology. The logical thinking itself, and the thoughts thought in it, the conceptual contents, the objects as their substrate-objects — exactly as they are conscious in thinking, purely according to the subjective and changing modes in which they "appear" therein — were to be *brought into view* and *described* in faithful descriptions. Description meant not only pure expression of what is intuited in concepts originally drawn from this intuited itself, but, before that, also as far-reaching as possible *analysis* of what is intuited into its intuitively unfoldable moments. The *Logical Investigations*, then, did *not* hypothetically reconstruct logical life; they intuitively brought it forth and described it.

Husserl gives an illustration: if I render a judgment — say I establish that through two points a straight line is determined — I *live through* this establishing experience or doing; in a certain way I am conscious of it, but in judging I am not *directed* at it. But quite obviously, I can in retrospective looking *especially attend* to it and just for that reason also make the *reflective* assertion. So instead of "through two points a line is determined" I then assert: "I judge, I am convinced, I am just now thinking judgmentally that..." Obviously, I can then ask: what does this judging experience look like — what does the passively pre-given look like in *how*, the judgment-act look like more closely — does it let itself unfold further, what can I purely intuitively establish in it? So in every case. It had to be possible to pursue here the general typology of thinking experience — in general and yet purely intuitive descriptions. This obviously belonged into a psychology on the ground of "inner experience" that had been demanded already since the beginning of the eighteenth century, since Locke's *Essay on Human Understanding* (a psychological epistemology), and that had been attempted in execution. But for *deeper-lying reasons* it had never come to *systematic* and *pure* descriptions, neither for the experiences of experiencing and thinking nor for those of valuing, willing, acting — and to these, for their *imaginative* counterparts. The shift to a methodologically self-conscious inner-experience-based description was, in the eighteenth century, *announced* in Locke but not actually carried out — and Husserl will say why.

In part, this was because one was all too hastily striving for an *explanatory* accomplishment in psychology — and indeed according to the model of natural-scientific explanation — and consequently slid quickly past the domain of pure intuition, of pure givenness in inner experience. The diagnostic continues the pattern: the temptation of *explanation by causal hypothesis*, even from the side of those advocating *inner experience*, kept psychology from staying within description long enough to discover the real shapes of its theme.

Of particular importance — what one noticed only very late — is that *reflective experience*, the so-called "inner," has *very many stages and depth-dimensions* and is exceedingly difficult to set in motion, if one is to strive *beyond the most superficial*. Indeed, of the depths and mediacies one had at first no idea at all. One did not see that inner experience is not a *plain reflection* that without further ado leads to the concretion of the respective interiorities, but rather that the concretion lets itself be grasped as theme only in *many stages of reflection* — that inner experiencing is a *process of unveiling, to be accomplished in ever-new reflection*. The need for pure and systematically progressing description, and the conviction that it designates a great field of difficult — philosophically as well as psychologically foundational — work, awoke soon after the flowering of an experimental psychology. The motives lay in logic, ethics, aesthetics, that is, in the normative philosophical sciences and in the critique of reason intertwined with them. *One must first come to know the knowledge-life itself, the valuing, the willing-life itself, in inner experience, and create clear concepts from this, in order to gain a theory of knowledge, of valuation, and of will*. In other words, one had to gain, from within, a clear understanding of how "truth" arises as the accomplishment of "rational" knowing, how true value arises as the accomplishment of "rational" valuing, how ethical goodness arises in ethically right willing as subjective accomplishment. Reflection, that is, is *layered*: it has stages of depth, and only multi-stage reflection reveals the structures of the act-life.

#### **d) Brentano as Pioneer for the Investigation in Inner Experience — Discovery of Intentionality as Fundamental Character of the Psychic**

---

Attempts at reform — above all of logic and epistemology, and likewise of ethics and aesthetics — were the order of the day. Brentano took part in them in his way, and energetically advocated, especially in his Vienna lectures, that all healthy reform here leads back to a *purely descriptive analysis of the logical and all other conscious givennesses in inner experience*, ultimately inner perception. And from this he demanded for psychology in general — into which all such analyses are to be ordered — a *purely descriptive psychology* to be made the basis of all explanatory theories, which he later also called *Psychognosie*. Brentano's place in the lineage is here installed: he too saw, like Dilthey, that the new psychology had to be *descriptive* and rest on *inner experience*; but he reached this from the side of logic/epistemology/ethics, not from the side of historical-cultural *Geisteswissenschaften*.

However heatedly Brentano was opposed in his time, he gave in this respect — and not only for Germany, but also for England, and thus internationally — extraordinarily strong impulses. Particularly significant was a *general standpoint, itself already drawn from inner intuition*, for all psychological descriptions: we today designate it with the word *intentionality*. For the first time, the *fundamental essence of all psychic life* — *consciousness* as *having-consciousness of something* — was put in the focal point, and indeed brought out descriptively as the most general essential character of psychic life, to be drawn directly from the evidence of inner experience. Husserl thus crowns Brentano with the historical achievement of *intentionality as the fundamental character of consciousness*.

One could not escape the recognition that intentionality is a *fundamental property* of psychic life — given quite immediately and evidently *prior to all theories*. If I perceive a house, I shall first perhaps say: what is present here is the *house outside*, and in me a psychic lived-experience of perceiving, perhaps a *perceptual image*, as a distant effect of the house itself on my psychophysical subjectivity. But however it may stand with this causal relation, and whether anything can be said against it, it can in any case be made evident that *in the perceptual experience itself there lies a consciousness-relation*, and indeed to the house perceived *in it itself*. The illustration sharpens: the apparent "image-theory" is set aside for an *intentional* analysis of the perception as itself a consciousness-of.

It may be that I later legitimately come to the conviction that I have fallen victim to an illusion. But before that I had purely the consciousness *"there-being house"* — descriptively nothing at all is distinguished from an ordinary perceiving. Of an externally-internally psychophysical causality there is naturally no talk if the house is a mere hallucination. But it is clear: the momentary lived-experience is in itself not only just a subjective lived-experience in general, but precisely *a perceiving of this house*. So descriptively, an *object-relation* belongs to the experience — *whether or not the object actually exists*. Likewise, if I imagine to myself a centaur, the lived-experience of the fiction is itself *phantasy of* such-and-such a centaur; in the experience that we call *memory* lies likewise itself the *relation to the past*, in *loving* the relation to the *loved*, in *hating* to the *hated*, in *willing* to the *willed*. The classical structure of intentional life is here run through: *every act has its object-correlate*, where "object" need not exist outside the act.

Already in Scholasticism intentionality had been seen; there had been distinction between *truly real objects*, as in themselves and for themselves being, and *merely intentional* objects, as meant *within* the meaning, experienced *within* the experiencing, judged *within* the judging. Therein lay that psychic life as consciousness has the peculiarity of relating itself to objectivity. But it is *fundamentally mistaken* to say that Brentano had only *rediscovered* an old scholastic doctrine. His great discovery and his true originality consist in this: that — guided by the idea of a *descriptive psychology*, which he, still naturalistically attuned, thought of as the underlay of a natural-scientific style of *explanatory* psychology — he sought a *descriptive principle of distinction between physical and psychic*, and was the first to establish that *intentionality* is the *descriptively graspable essential character of the specifically psychic*. Apparently this is a small twist on the Scholastic doctrine of intentionality. But such apparently small twists *make history* and *decide the fate of science*. It was the consequence-rich and now self-evident consequence of this descriptive foundational establishment that psychology *first* had to commence as a pure *descriptive science of psychic life* — and, so delimited, was nothing other than the descriptive science of *intentionality*, of the manifold configurations of consciousness *as consciousness of something*, with all the contents distinguishable in it in inner intuition.

To be sure, this doctrine, despite its evident descriptive rootedness in psychic life, was burdened with the greatest difficulties and obscurities: how the conscious-as-such was to be treated in concrete inner-experiential research, how *intentional* "analysis" was to be carried out, what kind of generality was possible and to be aimed at in this sphere, and how from here psychology was to be grounded as factual science of a psychophysically real psychic life — all of this was *in the dark*. So the dominant psychology reacted on the whole with rejection. Nonetheless, one could not entirely escape the Brentanian impulses. There were unprejudiced researchers who, after Brentano's exhibition, could no longer overlook the fact that intentionality is the fundamental property of psychic life, given in quite immediate inner-experiential evidence prior to all theories. The historical situation, then, was: Brentano *opened* the path but did not work out the methodology of intentional analysis; what remained was for Husserl to do this.

Once clearly seen, intentionality had to be a *psychological* theme — indeed *the central* psychological theme. So we find already since the 1870s in Germany, and later outside it, significant psychologists who wanted to take *descriptive* account of intentionality. At the same time the more general demand for a *descriptive psychology as psychological foundational discipline* found various advocates. Dilthey does not appear to have been determined in this by Brentano. Rather, he must have come quite on his own — and indeed, led by his humanistic interest-sphere — to the demand for pure description. The *central significance of intentionality* plays, characteristically, no role with him. Husserl marks the disjunction in the path: Dilthey understood the *descriptive* moment but not the *intentional* moment; Brentano understood the *intentional* but not the *eidetic*.

By contrast, the *Logical Investigations* are a full unfolding of Brentanian impulses — as is self-evident, since I was an immediate pupil of Brentano. (Husserl marks the beginning of a new lecture in a footnote — May 19.) And yet, in these investigations, a new turn and an essential reshaping of the Idea of descriptive psychology took place through an essentially new method — so much so that Brentano himself was never willing to acknowledge them as a mature execution of his Idea. As he, closing himself up in his circle of thoughts, refused to go along in the new direction — quite differently from Dilthey, who took up the *Logical Investigations*, although they had arisen outside all relation to his writings, with great joy and saw in them a *first concrete execution* of his "Ideas Concerning a Descriptive and Analytic Psychology." The disjunction between teacher and student is here stated openly: Brentano did not recognize Husserl's work as the maturation of his program; Dilthey, by contrast, recognized in it precisely what *his* program needed.

Dilthey himself produced this connection, since unfortunately I had, under the influence of Ebbinghaus' brilliant counter-critique, not held it necessary to read Dilthey's great work; all the more so since in those years I had little receptivity for the significance of Dilthey's writings in general. In my inner struggle for a *principled overcoming of positivism*, the strong leaning toward positivism that had emerged in Dilthey's earlier work, the *Introduction to the Geisteswissenschaften*, had to repel me. I was at first not a little surprised to hear from Dilthey personally that phenomenology, and indeed the descriptive analyses of the specifically phenomenological vol. II of the *Logical Investigations*, stood in essential harmony with his "Ideas" and were to be regarded as a first basic piece of a *real execution* of the psychology he had in view as ideal, in matured method. Dilthey has always placed the greatest weight on this convergence of our researches from fundamentally different starting points, and in his old age, with downright youthful enthusiasm, took up again his abandoned investigations on the theory of the *Geisteswissenschaften*. Their result was the last and most beautiful of his writings in this regard, over which he sadly died: "The Construction of the Historical World" (of 1910\) in the *Abhandlungen der Berliner Akademie*. Husserl is here owning his own initial blindness to Dilthey, and explicitly affirming Dilthey's later judgment: the *Logical Investigations* are indeed a concrete maturation of what Dilthey was after.

The further I myself have advanced in the formation of the phenomenological method and in the phenomenological analysis of spiritual life, the more I have had to recognize that Dilthey was *in fact right* in his — to me at first so foreign — judgment about the inner unity of phenomenology and descriptive-analytic psychology. His writings contain an inspired *pre-view* and *pre-stage* of phenomenology. They are by no means antiquated; even today they remain overrich in most valuable concrete stimuli for work in the methodologically advanced and, in its whole problematic, differently shaped phenomenology. I therefore most warmly recommend to you the study of all the writings of this extraordinary spirit. They bring true treasures of intuitive exhibitions and descriptions in historical connection; and everywhere where really pure description in most faithful adjustment to the givenness of actual intuition rules, the phenomenologist has a prepared and fruitful working soil. But a *training* in that purely inwardly-turned psychology, which Dilthey actually had in view but which he himself did not yet bring into the form of strict science — with conceptually rigorous method and decisively-carried-out establishments — one cannot gain from them. As pleasing as Dilthey's late great influence is, it can, in external imitation of his manner, work *unfavorably*, as is very well to be observed. Husserl is here delivering the balanced verdict: Dilthey is rich in intuitive material but lacks methodical-conceptual maturity, and *external* imitation of him is dangerous.

So, the psychology into which I want to introduce you in some main pieces will not, without further ado, be of Diltheyan stamp, but rather *phenomenological* psychology, as I have already announced. The bridge to the rest of the course is built: the psychology of these lectures is *phenomenological* psychology, drawing on Dilthey's insight but disciplined by Husserl's method.

#### **e) The Further Development of the Thought of Intentionality in the "Logical Investigations." The Performance-Character of Consciousness. Transition from a Purely Descriptive Psychology to an A Priori (Eidetic-Intuitive) Psychology and Its Significance for Epistemology**

---

It cannot be possible here in the Introduction to characterize in every respect what the *Logical Investigations* newly opened up methodically. Only a few things I can make clear here, and at first the following. Brentano had not gone beyond an externally classificatory-descriptive consideration of intentional experiences — or, what is the same, of *modes of consciousness*. He had never seen and taken up the great task — *to ask back, from the fundamental categories of objects as possible objects of consciousness* (specifically of *knowing* consciousness), to the entire manifold of possible modes of consciousness through which such objectivities come to be conscious for us and can in principle become conscious — and, from there, to clarify *researchingly further* the *teleological function* of these modes of consciousness for the synthetic *truth-accomplishment of reason*. Only through this *posing of problems* (in however imperfect a form it itself still had in the *Logical Investigations*) did a *deep-penetrating insight* into consciousness and its accomplishment become possible at all. Indeed, only then did it emerge that consciousness is not merely an *empty though many-colored having-of-consciousness*, but a *performing in manifold demonstrable forms and corresponding syntheses* — everywhere intentional, goal-directed, directed at *ideas of truth*. I can also say: only so did a *transcendental* doctrine of consciousness become possible; and in one with that, an innermost *understanding* became possible of the apperceiving — taking place in psychological inwardness — of objects of every category, and further an innermost understanding of all *truth-accomplishing performances* as logical, axiological, and practical. The decisive widening, then, in the *Logical Investigations* (and beyond), was to see consciousness not as a *static taxonomy of modes* but as a *teleologically structured performance-life* directed at truth.

Consciousness, intentionality, revealed its true essential character only in such directed work. Before, in psychology — and *still* now in the whole natural-scientifically oriented psychology — a conception of psychic life dominated which, as a matter of course, saw it as an *analogue of physical natural-happening*, as a complexion of elements always renewing itself in transformation. One held it as the task to lead complexes back to elements and to their elementary forms of combination, and to corresponding causal laws. As against this, it now turned out that this whole conception is *senseless*: that the *synthesis of consciousness* is totally different from the *external connection of natural elements*. To the essence of conscious life belongs — *instead* of spatial juxtaposition, mutual containment, mixedness, and spatial wholeness — an *intentional interwovenness, motivated-ness, being-enclosed-in-one-another in a meaning-relation* — and in a manner that has, in form and in principle, *no analogue in the physical at all*. Even Brentano was, in the indicated sense, a naturalist: something like *intentional implication* and *intentional analysis* as the analysis of possibly continuously-into-one-another-wound sense-givings he had not yet seen. Therewith is also designated what makes the radically foreign character of every intentional inner psychology, of every truly descriptive-analytic psychology — foreign over against all the experimental and psychophysical and other *outer*\-psychology, which only spans the inductive *outer* sphere of objectified psychic being. The contrast is at its sharpest here: intentional implication is *not* a network of elements in juxtaposition but a *layered meaning-fold*, structurally unique to consciousness.

Yet there is one more thing — and perhaps the *most* important, and fundamentally essentially new of the psychological-phenomenological method — that I have not yet designated, which also first emerged in the *Logical Investigations* and entirely determined their way of working. Namely, it soon turned out that a descriptive investigation of the new-kind goal-setting of the *Logical Investigations* by no means has the character of a *merely empirical-psychological* investigation, neither a psychophysical one nor a descriptive one in the *natural* sense. The exclusive theme of the *Logical Investigations* was the *psychic correlative to the respective meant objectivities* (and especially to the logically ideal ones), thus the manifold of psychic modes in which, *purely in the immanence of psychic life*, concepts, judgments, theories shape themselves as *ideally identical sense-unities*, with respective modes of *meant or evidently true* being. The crucial shift is announced: phenomenology is *not* empirical psychology — it is something else.

The psychology of the millennia wanted to be, and was in all its historically changing configurations, an *experiential science* of human and animal psychic life. But if one goes back from ideal objectivities to the consciousness that subjectively forms them, then one can soon convince oneself that the *inner passivities and activities* in which they subjectively shape themselves and come to evident givenness, and which can come to intuitive unveiling through *methodical reflection* and phenomenological analysis, are not *empirical contingencies of human act-life* — not chance facticities that *could be thought otherwise*. Rather, it is evident that *if* anything at all like *numbers, mathematical manifolds, propositions, theories*, etc., are to come to subjective givenness, to become conscious in subjective experiences, *these necessary experiences must have their essentially-necessary, everywhere-identical structure*. In other words: whether we take ourselves as thinking subjects to be humans, or whether we fictionalize as angels or devils or gods, any beings that count, calculate, mathematize — the counting, mathematizing inner doing and life is, *if logical-mathematical is to come out*, in a priori necessity *everywhere essentially the same*. To the a priori of pure logic and pure mathematics itself — this realm of unconditionally necessary and general truths — *correlatively* there corresponds an a priori *of psychic kind*: a realm of unconditionally necessary and general truths relating to the *mathematical lived-experience*, the mathematical imagining, thinking, connecting, etc. — namely as a manifold psychic life of a *subject in general*, insofar as it is to be thought in pure ideality as one that knows mathematical contents within itself. If we take the logical-mathematical objectivity as one that *must be intersubjectively insightful*, then for the single subject we would have to posit *communicative subjectivity* and its *communalized life*. The decisive shift is here: descriptive psychology turns into *eidetic* science — the structure of any *possible* subject that does mathematics, not just contingent humans.

The same holds for *all* psychic correlation-investigations referring to objects of every region and category, and it holds not only in empty generality, distant from the matter, in opinion and assertion, but in a *determinate*, particular form corresponding to the determinate objects, and in relation to the *unveiling intuitions* belonging to them. Just thereby a new-kind *Idea of psychology* opens up — new-kind not only through its concretely and universally grasped theme *object-consciousness*, but also through this: that it would be a *non-empirical, an a priori psychology*. It would be a priori in the same sober sense in which *mathematics* is called an a priori, a non-empirical science, or, as we also say, an *essential science* (Wesenswissenschaft), and so shows a peculiarity that has its evidence *prior to all "theory" of a priori knowing*, that is, *prior to all epistemological interpretation*. Instead of treating of the *fact* of human subjects of this earth and world, this psychology accordingly treats of the *ideal essence* of a mathematizing, and more generally a knowing subjectivity in general — to be unveiled phenomenologically *exemplarily*, but *ideally possible*, *conceivable* in unconditional generality. Husserl is fully laying out the shift: the new psychology is *eidetic*, taking up not the fact-bound psychic life of humans but the *essential structures* that any cognitive subjectivity must have.

Against this it counts for nothing that Lotze had already noticed the a priori in the framework of immanent sensation-qualities, and that logic, in the obscurity of its noetic-noematic interpretation, passed as an a priori discipline. With that, one had no single actual beginning of a phenomenological a priori psychology, and no representation even of its *possibility*. The same therefore holds also of Brentano. Lotze and Brentano are gently set aside: insights touching on a priori elements were there, but no *systematic a priori psychology* was yet projected.

Of an a priori psychology there had been much talk in older times, namely in the Leibniz-Wolffian school of the eighteenth century. To this Kant's critique put an end. But this psychology was ontological-metaphysical. It was not a psychology that, like this new one, was *purely intuitive and descriptive* and *yet at the same time a priori* — that thus rose from intuitive concretions to intuitive necessities and generalities. This new peculiarity will receive in the systematic and concrete expositions to which we shall soon pass yet a precise clarification, up to full self-evidence. This a priori (eidetic-intuitive) psychology was first possible after the peculiarity of *intentional analysis*, of intentional implications, was concretely brought out and in pieces carried out — and that at first in naïve intuitivity, *prior to* any question about its empirical or a priori meaning. But you already notice that it is precisely this — eidetic intentional analysis — that gave to the descriptive-psychological analysis of the *Logical Investigations* an essentially different character than that of the descriptive-analytic psychology demanded by Dilthey in the interest of the historical and systematic *Geisteswissenschaften*, and also another than that of Brentano's *Psychognosie*. Husserl thus places his own work in the lineage: Leibniz-Wolff thought a priori psychology, but did it *metaphysically*; Dilthey and Brentano thought *descriptive* psychology, but did it *empirically*. The *Logical Investigations* did *eidetic descriptive intentional* psychology — combining what no one before had combined.

Even the *Logical Investigations* gave a *descriptive-analytic psychology in the interest of psychology itself* — but also, as on Dilthey's side, *for the grounding of a theory of reason*. But this psychology had an essentially different countenance, in keeping with the new-kind starting points and goal-settings that led to it. From the start, of course, the aim was not *a new founding of psychology*. The aim was a purely *epistemological* one. As against formal logic as an a priori science of the essential forms and essential laws of certain ideal objectivities, what was demanded was a science whose business is the systematic *clarification of the subjective thought-activities* in which these thoughts come to subjective formation — and especially of the particular *rational* activities in which such thoughts adopt the *norm-form of evident truth as super-subjective validity*. The shift is registered: from grounding logic, one ends up with the need for a *transcendental* doctrine of subjectivity that *also* grounds psychology.

It is only another way of putting it if I say: what was at stake was a *first concretely carried-out attempt at an epistemology* — though one still restricted by its theme, restricted to the formal-logical and naturally extended formal-mathematical sphere of reason. For the first time it became evident here that a *theory of reason*, as a *system of rational accomplishments* taking place in the thinking subjectivity, can never be brought about by *vague, distant-from-the-matter* a priori and empirical discussions, as had hitherto been usual — nor by a *merely empirically descriptive* psychology on the ground of inner experience. Rather, it became evident that in place of empirical psychology there must enter a new-kind, *purely a priori and yet at the same time descriptive* science of the psychic — namely a science that, in unconditional and seen (directly intuited) necessity, makes intelligible *how* psychic life, and especially knowing life, accomplishes within itself, and according to its a priori essential nature, *intentional accomplishments* — and in particular the accomplishment of the type *ideal validity*, which is called *true being and truth*. So a *new psychology* is forced on Husserl precisely *by the demands of epistemology*: a purely eidetic descriptive intentional science of the psychic.

So, although a reform of existing psychology was not at first considered, it became unambiguously visible to me afterwards that here lay the *beginning of a new-kind psychology* — and indeed *a re-shaping of the old idea of an a priori psychology*. Just as in the *Prolegomena* and in the III. and IV. *Investigations* the old idea of an a priori *ontology* had experienced, in a new non-metaphysical form, a revival. The historical-systematic claim is now made: the *Logical Investigations* effected the resurrection in *non-metaphysical* form of *two* classical a priori projects — *ontology* and *psychology*.

A remarkable change of the historical relation between psychology and epistemology thus announced itself. On the one side, there had been for centuries in very changing forms an *empirical* psychology (most recently in the so successful form of an *experimental* psychology), a psychology that at all times raised the claim to *include epistemology in itself* or at least to *furnish its foundation*. On the other side, since Kant, a *transcendental* epistemology had come to powerful effect and validity, which sharply rejected all empirical-psychological foundationing and combatted it as *psychologism* — but on the other hand, alongside psychophysical psychology, knew of *no other and a priori one* that could count as a necessary foundation. In all this, psychology, like every objective science, was to retain the right of *independent existence*. The standoff between psychology and epistemology, then, was shaped by a *lack*: there had been no *a priori* psychology to *mediate* between empirical psychology and transcendental epistemology.

What was historically *new* was now this: that an epistemological investigation of the logical-mathematical, *entirely independently* commencing and carried out from traditional empirical psychology, *nonetheless* pressed toward a *methodical reform of psychology* and *re-shaped* the problem of the relation of epistemology and psychology. These concretely carried-out epistemological-logical investigations had to make use, *unavoidably*, of a psychological analysis — but of a *new kind*, *running a priori*. An a priori science of the psychic, drawing from pure inwardly-turned intuition, as it here appears in *actual beginning pieces*, could not possibly be insignificant for a rigorous scientific empirical psychology, *just as* an a priori science of *space* and of the *formal Idea of a nature in general* had not been insignificant for an *experiential knowledge of nature*. The systematic place of the new science is here named: it stands *to empirical psychology* as geometry stands to empirical physics.

Already Brentano and Dilthey had seen that traditional empiricism and the epistemological-critical *psychologism* carry in themselves a *core of unbreakable right*: namely, that an epistemology must indeed ground itself in an intuitive analysis of cognition, and an ethical theory of reason must likewise ground itself in an intuitive analysis of ethical conscious-life — that is, in a *descriptive psychology*. But so long as this analysis was thought of as *empirical*, the objection of the anti-psychologists could not be met — namely, how an epistemology, a science of the *a priori principles* that are to make intelligible the possibility of objective rational accomplishment, could be founded on psychology, on an empirical science. To this is added: the underpinning *descriptive or psychogenetic* investigations were *necessarily without result* — they were unable to lead to an understanding of the logical and ethical, because they could not press through to *genuine intentional analyses*. Had they done so, the *genuine psychological a priori* would also have easily impressed itself, since the then-self-evident reference-back to the *constitutive* gave the stimulus. The diagnosis is now decisive: the historical impasse was not from lack of empirical *content* but from the absence of *intentional* analysis — and *with* intentional analysis (and the eidetic move), psychology and epistemology can each find their proper relation.

#### **f) The Consistent Extension and Deepening of the Problem-Setting of the "Logical Investigations." Demonstration of the Necessity of an Epistemological Grounding of the A Priori Sciences by Transcendental Phenomenology — the Science of Transcendental Subjectivity**

---

To be sure, the opened horizons for a new epistemology and theory of all other kinds of reason, on the one side — and for an a priori psychology on the other — had first to be further traversed before complete clarity for the future tasks of rational-theoretical and psychological research was possible. In the *Logical Investigations* the highest level of principled clarity had by no means already been reached. Only in the *progress* of the researches beyond the *limited problem-sphere* of the *Logical Investigations*, only in the *consistent extension* of them to a problematic that *spanned the all-totality of possible objects in general* and the *all-totality of possible consciousness in general*, or *possible subjectivity in general*, could the *final principled clarifications* grow. Husserl is opening the subsection that names the final widening: from the *Logical Investigations* (restricted) to *transcendental phenomenology* (universal).

What had to present itself, in the pressing need — already awakened by the formal universality of the *mathesis universalis* — for a *universal extension* outward from the *Logical Investigations*, was the *extension* of the a priori and formal logic and mathematics to the Idea of a *total system of a priori sciences for every conceivable category of objective objects*: that is, at its highest, the *demand for a universal a priori of possible worlds in general* alongside that of formal mathematics. On the other side, and *correlatively*, the *extension* of the purely a priori consideration of *only-formal-generalities-considering* knowing consciousness to the *content-wise more determined* knowing consciousness, related to *every particular category of objectivities*. And finally, from this had to grow an *a priori pure doctrine of consciousness* in full universality, which also spanned *every kind* of valuing, striving, willing — and thus consciousness of every type in general — and thus grasped the *whole concrete subjective life* in all the configurations of its intentionality. With that the *totality-problems* of the *constitution of the world* and the *unity of consciousness-subjectivity*, the *individual-personal and the communalized*, were opened. The widening is fully sketched: from a regional logic and mathematics, *to* a universal ontology of possible worlds and a universal doctrine of intentional life.

Yet in further consequence the consideration of the sense of a *radical and final-principled science of reason* — as the transcendental philosophy developed in the modern age actually wanted to be and, as philosophy, had to be — *forced* an essential *differentiation* of the new Idea of an a priori and descriptive psychology into *two parallel sciences*, distinguished only by *principled attitude*. The one was the exact *analogue of pure geometry* and, more concretely, of *a priori natural science*: an a priori psychology in the *natural* word-sense. The decisive distinction is announced: phenomenology splits into *two* a priori sciences — a *natural-attitude* a priori psychology, parallel to mathematical natural science; and a *transcendental* phenomenology proper.

*A priori* natural science — to this we count disciplines like pure geometry, pure mechanics, and so on. Abstracting from all the factually given nature, these establish, according to concepts and laws, *nothing other* than that without which a nature in general would be unthinkable. In parallel, then, *a priori psychology* would seek to establish what is essentially necessary to the psychic *as such* — without which soul and psychic life are in general *unthinkable*, *non-sensical*. The structural parallel between geometry/mechanics for nature and the a priori psychology for soul is made explicit: in each case the a priori names the *essential framework* of all factual data of the corresponding region.

But all such a priori sciences require an *epistemology*, a *critical-rational clarification* and *subjective grounding* — quite as the formal logic and mathematics that span them in their generality require it. And it turns out that *none* — *not even the a priori psychology* — is so independent that it could pose and solve the *epistemological* problems *for itself at the same time*. From it, rather, a specifically *transcendental* psychology (for which, however, the historical word *psychology* no longer well fits) must be distinguished — as that a priori *science of the subjective* to which finally all rational-theoretical research leads back. In other words, between a so-called *transcendental subjectivity* and a *psychological subjectivity* had to be distinguished — and correspondingly between a psychology that stands in the *bond of objective sciences* and a *transcendental phenomenology* that stands *entirely outside* this bond. In its whole research-attitude — that is, *from the most radical ground* — it separates itself from all the sciences that, since Aristotle, have come to development in our European culture. Over against it, all these sciences are called *dogmatic* (positive) or *naturally naïve*. To all the truths so exactly established in them refer the new-kind problems which are traditionally called *rational-theoretical* — and these are, as is to be seen, *transcendental-phenomenological*. The fundamental partition is here put with full sharpness: there is *the totality of natural-dogmatic sciences*, and there is *the unique science of transcendental subjectivity*. The latter is presupposed by all rational-theoretical questioning.

(A footnote marks the beginning of a new lecture.) The methodical grounding of a *universal science of transcendental subjectivity* — and indeed in the *phenomenological method*, that is, in pure description on the ground of pure essential intuition and intentional analysis — is sketched and in main pieces already carried out in the *Ideas Toward a Pure Phenomenology and Phenomenological Philosophy* of 1913\. Since in those Ideas it was a matter precisely of *philosophy*, of a radical grounding of philosophy as *strictest science*, it was sharply emphasized that this phenomenology *is by no means identical with a naturally understood a priori psychology*. Husserl is here marking the architectonic relation between the 1913 *Ideen I* and the present lectures: the *Ideen* sought transcendental philosophy directly; these lectures, by contrast, will treat the *natural-attitude* a priori psychology.

This was now often — and particularly by those who found it more convenient to remain in the well-worn ruts of dogmatic thinking and shied from the toilsome exercise of the foreign phenomenological reflection and essential intuition — understood in such a way *as if* the psychologist as a *world-related researcher* did not need to concern himself with such philosophical principle-research. All the more so since I myself had said that dogmatic science *could* very well continue to be possible as hitherto, *without* recourse to *transcendental* questionings. Yet *transcendental phenomenology* has the peculiarity that *every one* of its propositions can be transformed into an *a priori-psychological* one *in the natural sense*. What was likewise *not* seen is this: that an essentially *similar* phenomenological method can also from the start *ground a naturally-objective a priori psychology* — *provided* only that one omits the *radical change of attitude* in which only the *transcendental* becomes theme, and *provided* that one does not let work along *methodical intentions* and *ultimate philosophical questionings* that lead entirely outside normal natural science. Husserl is here making *the* key methodological point: there is a *single* phenomenological method that yields *two* sciences depending on the attitude. In the natural attitude it yields a priori psychology; in the transcendental attitude it yields transcendental phenomenology.

Only very slowly, and at first in few people, does the recognition break ground that the *theoretical core-content* in scientific psychology (individual psychology and community psychology, and in further consequence every explanatory *Geisteswissenschaft*) consists in a *phenomenological and a priori essential science of the psychic*. One learns to see that the psychophysical, experimental, and aesthetical *outer-psychology*, however much it simultaneously pretends to rest on inner experience, can *never* lead to an actual and proper psychology — to a psychology that delivers *insight*, that is, *understanding* for the *psychic and spiritual life itself*. Such inner insight is, however, *impossible* so long as the founding *inner experience* is not the inwardly-turned intuition opened up in *phenomenological* method, and the inner-experiential analysis has not taken on the form of *unfolding intentional implications*. As soon as that is seen, however, it follows that the *enormous wealth* of systematically interconnected exhibitions of those analyses of consciousness — gained from *transcendental* interest and the *transcendental* attitude — must be *taken over into psychology* in a *corresponding change of attitude*. Then, however, it becomes evident that there *delimits itself* a strictly self-enclosed and *infinite* science from *the a priori of phenomenological interiorities*, which is *the central science for all Geisteswissenschaften* — but also the *fundamental science* for the psychophysical experimental psychology. Husserl is here giving the final formulation of the architectonic relation: *transcendental phenomenology* and *a priori phenomenological psychology* both contain the same content, separated only by *attitude*; and the *phenomenological-psychological* content, transferred into the natural attitude, is the *foundational* science of all *Geisteswissenschaften* and of empirical psychology alike.

### **§ 4\. Summarizing Characterization of the New Psychology**

---

(Husserl marks the beginning of a new lecture in a footnote.) In essentials we have now said what was to be said in an *Introduction*. You now know, in the most general respect — though only out of *historically oriented preliminary indications* (thus as out of a wide distance) — what the new, as necessarily demanded, psychology *wants to be and must be*. The opening of §4 declares that the historical work of the Introduction is essentially done; what remains is a *summarizing characterization* of the new psychology's defining features.

(Only a few items are to be added — nearly self-evident complements — in order not to lead expectations astray.) The parenthesis announces that what follows is a series of clarifying complements to ensure that the project is not misunderstood.

If we survey the *basic characters* of the new psychology, then they were, in catchwords: *apriority*, *eidetics*, *intuition or pure description*, *intentionality* — and, to name this also: *remaining in the natural dogmatic attitude* instead of the specifically philosophical, indeed *transcendental* attitude. Husserl is delivering the list: the five fundamental characters of phenomenological psychology. The first four are positive (a priori, eidetic, intuitive-descriptive, intentional); the fifth — staying in the natural dogmatic attitude — is *negative* in form (a *not* doing the transcendental reduction), but is just as essential because it is what distinguishes phenomenological psychology *as such* from transcendental phenomenology *as such*.

*1\.* The title *apriority* signified: this psychology aims *in the first place* at essential generalities and necessities — at everything *without which* psychological being and life are simply unthinkable. Only *in the second place* does it aim at *explanation* of psychological facticity, at *theory*, that is, *eidetic explanation* — which *for us* is naturally the first interest. The point is sharp: phenomenological psychology is *primarily* a science of *essential structures*, and only secondarily a science of *facts*; but in pedagogical or practical terms, the move from essence to fact (eidetic explanation of facticity) is what makes the science *useful* in the broader scientific economy.

*2\.* The title *intuition* or *description* designates to us the *source* of this a priori. The second basic character is named: the a priori is *not* derived from speculation, not gained by inference; it is *intuitively* drawn from inner experience, and *purely descriptively* expressed in concepts.

Purely from *inner intuition* and analysis of the *intuited*, and in an *intuitive ascent* to generic *necessities*, the establishments are gained as *essential establishments*. These are *not surmised, supposed essential necessities*, but the necessity and unconditional generality of validity *itself* becomes the *intuited*. (Husserl marks the beginning of a new lecture in a footnote.) Husserl is here distinguishing the eidetic from the speculative: the necessity *itself* is *seen*, not posited. The ascent to general necessities is an *intuitive* ascent — a *Wesensschau* — and not a hypothetical inference. This is precisely what the *Logical Investigations* added to the program of inner-experiential descriptive psychology: that the *necessity itself* can be brought to evidence.

*3\.* Further, through such a procedure, the *most general essential character* of psychic being and life is brought out: *intentionality*. Psychic life is *consciousness-life*; consciousness is *consciousness of something*. This general title *consciousness*, with the inseparably belonging titles *I*, *personality in general*, and *objectivity as objectivity of consciousness* — under which everything psychic stands — this, so taken as it presents itself on the basis of inner intuition according to its own essence: this *double centering* of conscious life gives the investigations of every inner psychology, in their progress, a peculiar, *teleological centering*. There necessarily grows the task of *descriptively pursuing* the systematically belonging consciousness-manifolds that essentially belong to the cognitive becoming-conscious or possible-becoming-conscious of objectivities of every category. Every category of possible objectivities designates an *index for a methodical lawfulness of possible psychic life*; every possible real world designates a lawfulness of a possible *intersubjective* psychic life. The intentional character is here unfolded structurally: consciousness has *two* poles (the I-side and the object-side), and these *poles* generate a *correlative* lawfulness — to every category of object there corresponds a determinate structure of consciousness in which it can be given. This is the essential program of *correlation research* (Korrelationsforschung), of which the eidetic intentional analyses provide the systematic execution.

*4\.* In the *transcendental* attitude, all such investigations take on the character of *philosophical foundational investigations* and lead to a radically grounded, and finally a *universal philosophy*. The corresponding transcendental import of the same investigations is registered: in the transcendental attitude, the *same* correlation-investigations rise to the dignity of *first philosophy*. The continuity between phenomenological psychology and transcendental phenomenology is at the same time the continuity between *psychology* and *philosophy*.

But we *as psychologists* do not want to be philosophers — that is, at least at first, we do not want to be — just as little as the mathematicians, the physicists, the philologists, etc., as was already said. I indicate here only further: *as philosopher*, *no one can be born by essential grounds*. Everyone can only *begin* as a natural, unphilosophical human being. A priori, however, a radical, systematic grounding of philosophy demands a *subjective* and likewise a *historical* ascent from the natural standpoint to the philosophical. Husserl is here pointing to the *pedagogical* and *systematic* necessity of beginning naturally: even though transcendental phenomenology is the ultimate philosophical science, no one *starts* there; everyone *must* ascend from the natural-dogmatic attitude. The point bears on the present lectures: they are *naturally-attituded* a priori psychology, and *just for that reason* they prepare the ascent to transcendental philosophy.

Perhaps our psychology now offers an *a priori possible*, *natural starting point* for the ascent into a *transcendental phenomenology and philosophy in general*. To this extent, such an inner-psychology would be of a particular *philosophical interest as an educational, motivating preliminary stage* of philosophy (though by no means as its *foundational* discipline). But however much this interest is to be effective and however much *philosophical spirit* is to be prepared through this lecture, neither philosophy is to be *presupposed* nor philosophy systematically pursued here. We want to remain in the *natural attitude*; we really want to be nothing other than *psychologists*, directed in *natural human manner* at the objective world as actuality and striving — *insofar as it is a world of spirit* — *to investigate it*. The pedagogical-architectonic status of the lectures is here made fully explicit: phenomenological psychology serves as a *propaedeutic* to transcendental philosophy, but is itself *not* philosophy. It is a *natural-attitude* science of the essential structures of psychic life — and its presentation must itself remain in the natural attitude.

Yet here above all some supplementary words are needed with reference to the sense of the talk of *natural attitude* and of *dogmatic sciences* as sciences *of this attitude*. Husserl is signaling that the term *natural attitude* needs clarification, lest it be misunderstood.

The *world*, posited in the self-evidence of its existence and constantly presupposed (which it draws from the *unanimity* of our constant experience), is the universal theme of all sciences that we, in contrast to philosophy, call *"sciences of the natural attitude."* The natural attitude, then, is *that attitude* in which the world is *always already* posited as existing actuality, *prior to* any reflective question about it. Every "natural" science presupposes the existence of the world as the framework of its inquiry.

Precisely this *presupposition of experienced world-actuality*, and the *referring-back* of all scientific cognitive striving and of all otherwise-human praxis to the world presupposed as experienced actuality, *characterizes* the natural attitude. The definition is here finalized: what defines the natural attitude is *both* the positing of world-actuality *and* the constant referring-back of all activity to that posited world.

That another attitude is possible, *by excluding this presupposition*, is thereby indicated; but we need not enter into it. Even the a priori sciences as they have been historically formed — e.g. geometry and other purely mathematical sciences — do not actually *lose* this natural attitude, although in their particular themes and in their groundings they contain and presuppose *nothing of a world-fact*. They deal with their idealities, with ideally possible spatial and temporal configurations, ideally possible sets, ideally possible things in general, etc.; likewise with ideally possible configurations of the *psychic and spiritual* — thus our a priori-directed psychology. Husserl is making a subtle but important point: the natural attitude is not exclusively about *facts*. The *a priori* sciences as historically practiced also belong to the natural attitude — even though they thematize *idealities* — because they remain *related to a world*, even if to a *possible* world, and presuppose the world-frame.

*World-related* are these a priori sciences in this sense: they are formed *with a view to* a world-knowledge and *as its means*. A priori research is *mediately also world-research*. Thus to the spatial a priori — or, what is the same, the geometrical — there correspond in factual nature the manifold *spatial facts*. The given nature *as spatial* must obviously satisfy the essential forms and essential laws of every conceivable space, of all conceivable space-configurations. Accordingly, *geometry* in the infinity of its a priori laws is *at the same time and eo ipso* a knowledge of nature, a knowledge of the *experiential world* — though a one-sided, incomplete one, related only to the *space-form*. The continuity of a priori sciences with empirical sciences is here registered through the example of geometry: pure geometry, while ideal, *applies* by essential necessity to all factual spatial nature, and so contributes — eo ipso — to nature-knowledge.

*5\.* In the same sense, the *pure essential doctrine of the spiritual* — of the singular-psychic as of the psychic of the *community* and of communal accomplishments — is *eo ipso* at the same time a knowledge of the world, with regard to the spirituality factually pervading it. Husserl is now placing his a priori phenomenological psychology in the same systematic position as pure geometry: as a *pure* essential doctrine, it nonetheless contributes by essential necessity to the knowledge of the *factual* spiritual life that pervades the actual world.

And it is, just as the mathematics of nature, *even a knowledge of the highest cognitive dignity*, insofar as it everywhere produces in the factual space the *apodictically necessary lawful structure*. The empirical-inductive natural science rose to an incomparably higher level of knowledge in the moment in which it adopted to itself the *mathematics of nature* and recognized that the *systematic development of the a priori inseparably belonging to nature* delivered to the factual nature *without further ado* an *infinity of absolutely necessary laws*. Husserl is reminding us of the historical achievement: physics became *high* science precisely when geometry-mathematics was wed to empirical observation. The analogous step is now demanded for psychology: it will become *real* science precisely when eidetic-phenomenological essential laws are recognized as *the* a priori framework of empirical psychic life.

The same must hold of *every* experiential science. Beyond the stage of vague, inductive empiricism it must rise. Its first task, if it is to become a *strict* science, must be to establish the *essential laws* that govern its domain *a priori* — *prior to* all consideration of the contingently factual. So too for psychology. The recognition that here, *a priori*, an *infinity of essential laws systematically to be investigated* precedes all contingent factuality is the most important recognition for the development of psychology into a *strict science*. The promethean program for psychology is here distilled: become like physics by acquiring your own eidetic-a priori framework.

Naturally, however, there will then remain for a psychology that wants — as it always wanted — to know the *spirituality experientially occurring in the given world*, *beyond* the a priori, also *those* knowledges to be gained that *determine and regulate the factual as such*. The *a priori* is everywhere, in psychology as in natural science, only an *unbreakable*, *formal frame*, within which empirical factuality must hold itself if it is to be in any way thinkable, a priori *possible*. Husserl is clarifying: the eidetic frame does not *replace* empirical psychology; it provides the *frame* within which alone empirical psychology can be rigorous.

*How now* — after the recognition of the a priori and in reference back to it — *the method of empiricism*, specifically of the *search for empirical "laws"*, is to be shaped, is then a *new question*. The matter is postponed: the lectures will not concern themselves with the *empirical* methodology, only with the *eidetic* foundation.

In any case, we do not want to see our task as building the *Idea of an empirical psychology* itself, but rather — *through the bare construction of the Idea of a purely a priori inner-psychology* as a *self-enclosed science* — to prepare (so as actually to gain it in future explicit execution) *that general and principled essential understanding of spirituality* which, in and for itself, is *one of the highest goals of scientific striving*. The lectures' task is here positively stated: to construct the *Idea* of a pure a priori inner-psychology, so that the *essential understanding of spirituality* becomes available — even though the explicit execution of this Idea will require further work beyond the present lectures.

*6\.* In conclusion, one more thing, to exclude misunderstandings. Since we have all formed the concept of *a priori science* from *mathematics* — as the oldest and until recently the only fully developed a priori science — we understandably tend to see *a priori science in general* as something like a *mathematics*: thus the a priori psychology as a *mathematics of spirit*. But here we must be on guard. The domain or the domains of mathematics demand a *method* and a *type of science* fitted to them, and this type does *by no means* belong to every a priori. The danger to be excluded: false expectations that phenomenological psychology will look like mathematics. It will be a priori, yes — but not *mathematical* in form.

What I mean here is illustrated by *geometry*, which — as its domain demands — *builds up a deductive theory*; a theory that leads back the infinity of deductive knowledges to a small number of *immediately evident self-evidences*, to *axioms*, or deduces them from these. But the *psychic domain* is of a *totally different essential structure*: it has, with analysis, an ever-growing and never-to-be-limited manifold of *immediate* essential insights, and *mere*, immediate intuition delivers here already an *entirely endless science* — an *intuitive and descriptive a priori* — and *this above all* does *not* stand in question for us. *Mediate*, inferring and deducing procedure is, to be sure, not absent in higher stages, and leads to a *higher-stage* a priori; but the *whole science* is by no means of the *type of a mathematics*. The closing of the Introduction, then, is a sharp warning: phenomenological psychology will be *descriptive-intuitive-eidetic*, not *axiomatic-deductive*. Its richness lies precisely in the *immediate essential insights* that intuitive analysis can ever-newly unfold; deduction plays only a derivative role. With this caveat, the Introduction ends; the *Systematic Part* will commence by undertaking the actual delimitation and development of phenomenological psychology.

## **Systematic Part**

### **§ 5\. Delimitation of Phenomenological Psychology: Distinction from the Other Humanities and from the Natural Sciences. Calling into Question the Concepts of Nature and Spirit**

---

After the conclusion of the *historical Introduction* to phenomenological psychology, Husserl declares, we now begin today with its *systematic development itself*. Our first task must be to give the scientific title *psychology* an *originarily clear sense* — a very necessary task in the present case. The opening installs the methodological pivot: the systematic exposition will begin not with a theory, but with a *delimitation* of what psychology even is — a delimitation that, as the §-title indicates, will turn out to require that the very concepts of *nature* and *spirit* be put into question. (Husserl notes in a footnote that this section is to be compared with Beilage V.)

Science in general and as such represents the ideal of *systematic knowledge* in the form of *finality* — or in the form of a *method* that justifies once and for all and for everyone in absolute evidence. The standard against which any science is to be measured is here given: not the contingent success of a discipline at a given moment, but the *ideal* of definitive method.

However imperfectly the historically grown sciences may satisfy this ideal, some of them realize it in the form of an *evident approximation*. So, the "exact" sciences. No one will hold a total upheaval possible here, by which, say, physics could *completely change* its entire methodical configuration, its theoretical countenance. With *psychology*, however — by what we have already learned — it stands differently. The psychology of the ancients, e.g., that of Aristotle, has a *totally different countenance* from that of the moderns; but it is not as though the new one has reached, even approximately, a methodical configuration of *rigorous science*. How mighty are the differences between the empiristic psychology of the Lockean school, the materialistic psychology of Hobbes, the metaphysical psychology of Herbart, and so on, down to modern experimental psychology. But even this one — despite its international recognition, as we heard — does not have its finality of goal-setting and methods acknowledged by Dilthey or by the phenomenological school. The diagnosis: in psychology, unlike in physics, there is no shared definitive methodical face, no stable approximation to the scientific ideal — and so the need for a *radical reflection* (Besinnung) on its very ground.

Where sciences are in such a situation, *only* a *radical reflection* can clearly help. It requires returning to the *concrete total context* out of which their *domain* as a theme of knowledge is set apart and thought out. Clarity must be created concerning what gives the relevant scientific domain its *essential unity*, and how it essentially distinguishes itself — outwardly as well as inwardly — *from its first originary sources of sense*. So for psychology we have, as a starting question: with *what* does it deal? What is its thematic sphere? Self-evidently one will answer: not material nature — that is the domain of the natural sciences. It deals with *spiritual facts*, with humans and animals insofar as they are *spiritual beings* and sites of spiritual, psychic happening; as *general* psychology with what is everywhere commonly valid, as psychological anthropology and animal psychology correspondingly delimited for the realms of humans and of animals respectively. The first naïve characterization of psychology's domain is given — and is about to be problematized.

With *spiritual facts* the manifold sciences of the social-historical world likewise have to deal — the sciences that treat of nations, states, laws, religions, etc., within the historical unity of any given cultural humanity, and likewise treat of the various configurations of *objectified spirituality* like languages, literature, art, and of the manifold forms of *work-formations* as they appear in continual transformation in the unity of history. The concept of a *psychological anthropology* could span all these disciplines bearing on the human. But when one speaks of *psychology*, the meaning is evidently that it is to be a *science of the most general forms and laws of spiritual facts*, over against the sciences of the *individual concretions* in historical actuality. This is similar to how, regarding the nature-sphere, *physics* as abstract law-science stands over against the *concrete natural sciences*, which deal with the individual configurations of things — earth, sun, stars — and with the concretely typical configurations exhibitable in this individual nature. The law-science is then called to perform *explanatory accomplishments* within the changing concretion. Husserl is here proposing the *naïvely natural* schema: psychology is to spirit what physics is to nature.

On the other hand, we know that *vividly felt unclarities* exist about the carrying out of these intentions — unclarities ultimately about *how* nature and spirit, and all the sciences belonging to these two titles, *stand to one another*. The natural and the spiritual do not confront us clearly and separately, such that a mere pointing-out would suffice: *here* is nature, and *there*, as the totally other, is spirit. Rather, what at first seems self-evidently separated turns out, on closer reflection, to be *unclearly interwoven*, mutually penetrating in a way that is difficult to understand. The simple schema breaks down on inspection: nature and spirit are not given as two ready-made domains.

Modern natural science sprang from a *one-sided direction of interest and method*, which under the title *nature* did not merely pick out, from originary experience, a sphere of plainly exhibitable experiential givennesses, but had in view already an *artifact of method* — it was a nature that it did not have *in advance* as experienced, but rather an *Idea* whose realization through theory it undertook. The decisive point is here installed: the "nature" of mathematical natural science is not the *experienced* nature of pre-scientific life but a *constructed idealization*.

To its method essentially belonged a consistent *separating off* of all "merely subjective" peculiarities belonging to the things of *immediate* experience — all characteristics stemming from subjectivity. This excluding of the subjective, that is, of the spiritual, was however not in the least interested in the spiritual itself, but only in what remained as *residuum*, as the purely physical or material, in such a method. This mere physical was the *theme*, and not the spiritual, which here was to be removed merely as a *contamination*. And so the subjective itself was not at all scientifically grasped or delimited. The classical Husserlian diagnostic of natural science's *abstraction*: in defining nature as pure object, it leaves subjectivity untreated, a mere "remainder."

On the other side, the *Geisteswissenschaft* — and above all psychology, which is after all directed toward the *radical and general* of the spiritual — was unable to come to the corresponding *correlative* accomplishment. Namely: now that the spiritual *had* to be the universal theme, it was unable to create clarity about the *unity-connection of the spiritual in the concrete world*, about its various *originary configurations* and those *derived* configurations in which the experienced concrete world undergoes *spiritualization* — and had undergone such in ever-new configurations.

Its *disjecta membra* of spirituality, which the natural-scientific method had cut away and thrown out in order to win its Idea of nature, would have to be brought back into their *originary connection*; in other words, the natural-scientific abstractive procedure would have to be *re-thought* and the abstraction *undone*. But trained in the habits of abstractly symbolic natural-scientific thinking, one no longer reflected on the originary sense of the natural-scientific concept of nature, and treated this nature as if it were *immediately the concretely intuited given*. The metaphor of *disjecta membra* (scattered limbs) is precise: the *Geisteswissenschaft* inherits scattered fragments from nature-science's abstraction, but it lacks the methodological self-awareness to put them back together as members of a concrete whole.

So both — *spiritual world and nature* — remained equally *unintelligible*, even though within the practice of natural-scientific method it was perfectly intelligible *what one wanted to investigate*. The verdict closes §5: the apparent clarity of natural science is an *internal* clarity that does *not* extend to the relation between nature and spirit. Both poles, taken together, are *philosophically* unclarified — and this unclarity is what phenomenological psychology must address through a return to the *originary experiential world*.

### **§ 6\. Necessity of Return to the Pre-Scientific Experiential World and to the Experiencing in Which It Is Given (Unanimity of Experience)**

---

Beneath all so-called *exact* nature and all spiritual life lies the *self-evidence of an experienced world* — pre-given to us in *natural experiencing*. To make psychology a rigorous science, the necessary first step is the *return* to this pre-scientific experiential world, prior to all the abstractions of natural science and prior to all the conceptualizations of historical *Geisteswissenschaft*. The opening of §6 carries through what §5 prepared: the radical reflection demanded for the unclear sciences requires us to *step back* from the constructed objects of physics and the abstract concepts of cultural science alike, and find the *common ground* on which both rest.

This experiential world is, for each of us, the world in which we *live*, in which we are bodily present, in which we deal with things and other persons. It is not a theoretical construction; it is the *self-evident horizon* within which all theoretical questioning, all natural-scientific inquiry, and all humanistic interpretation alike unfold. Husserl is here laying out the structure of what he calls the *pre-scientific experiential world* — anticipating the *Lebenswelt* of the *Krisis* but in the more specific function of grounding psychology.

*Experiencing* (Erfahren), in turn, is the *act* through which this world is given to us. And it is essential to note: experiencing is itself *unitary* — it is not a sum of disparate sensations, but is structured by *unanimity* (Einstimmigkeit). The experiences I have unfold continuously, each new experience confirming, modifying, or rarely conflicting with previous experiences, and the running stream of experience accomplishes, on the whole, the *positing of a single, coherent world*. The category of *Einstimmigkeit* is here put forward as the structural feature of experience that underwrites the *unity* of the experienced world.

When an experience is *disturbed* — when something I expected to find turns out to be otherwise, when a perception contradicts a prior perception — the unanimity is not annulled but rather *re-established* through correction. We correct, we doubt, we look again; and through this corrective work, the unanimous world reasserts itself. Indeed, even *illusions* are recognized as illusions only against the backdrop of an underlying unanimous experiential world. The point is: the world's unity is not a posit added to experience but an *immanent character* of the experiential stream itself.

And this experiential world is *not* merely *mine*. We constantly take it for granted that other persons experience the *same* world — that the table I see, you also see; that the river I cross is the same river you cross. *Intersubjective* unanimity is built into the very structure of natural experiencing: we treat each other as fellow experiencers of one and the same world. The opening of the experiential world's structure to *intersubjective* unanimity is here already announced — and will become a major theme.

Husserl emphasizes: this experiential world is not the *theoretical* object of physics or of any other science. It is what *precedes* all these. It is the *pre-given soil* on which both natural science and the humanities erect their theoretical superstructures. Both physics and history alike *presuppose* that their researchers experience a world, that the world they experience is one and the same, that there are facts to discover and other minds to communicate with. None of this is constructed by the sciences; it is *taken from* the pre-scientific experiential world.

What follows is methodologically momentous: the *first task* of any reflective science of psychic life is to make explicit the *structure* of this pre-given experiential world and the *unanimous experiencing* through which it is given. This is not a return to the naïve attitude — it is a *reflective recovery* of what the naïve attitude takes for granted. Phenomenological psychology has, as its propaedeutic, the *description of the experiential world and of experiencing* — for only by clarifying these can it then proceed to the description of the *psychic life* within which both the world-experience and the world-experiencer are constituted.

Husserl thus closes the section by emphasizing that the project of phenomenological psychology, far from being a turning-away from the world (toward a private inner sphere), is on the contrary essentially *world-related*: it begins by clarifying the *experiential world* and the *intersubjective experiencing* in which alone any psychic life takes shape. The pre-scientific experiential world is the *necessary point of departure*.

### **§ 7\. Articulation of the Sciences in the Return to the Experiential World. The Systemic Connection of the Sciences Is Grounded in the Structural Connection of the Experiential World; Idea of a Universal Science as Science of the Universal World-Structure, and of the Concrete Sciences That Have the Individual Configurations of Experiential Objects as Theme. Significance of the Empty Horizons**

---

Once the experiential world is recognized as the pre-given soil of all theoretical work, the architectonic of the sciences must be re-thought. The sciences are no longer to be classified by some external typology, but by their *manner of access to the experiential world*. Each science has its *thematic region* — a region carved out within the unified experiential world. The systemic connection of the sciences, that is, must mirror the structural connection of the experiential world itself. Husserl is here laying out a methodological principle: the *unity of science* is grounded in the *unity of world*.

Already in pre-scientific life we operate with broad *regional* distinctions: things, persons, plants and animals, artifacts, communities, institutions. These are not arbitrary partitions but reflect the *structural articulation* of the experiential world. Each region offers a different mode of accessibility and a different network of essential features. The natural scientist takes one regional articulation as his thematic field; the historian, the linguist, the psychologist take others.

Husserl now distinguishes between *universal* science — the science of the universal *world-structure* — and *concrete* sciences whose theme is the *individual configurations* of experiential objects. The universal science seeks essential structures that hold for *any* world whatever (or for the experiential world *as such*); the concrete sciences seek to describe and explain the actual contingencies of *this* world's particular content. Both are needed, and both find their place in a comprehensive architectonic.

A novel methodological tool is here introduced: the significance of the *empty horizons* (Leerhorizonte). Every experiential object is given in a *halo* of what it is *not*\-yet-given as — co-meant, anticipated, presupposed. The chair I see has a back I do not see now but anticipate; the city I am in has neighborhoods I have not visited but co-mean as belonging to the same city; the historical event I read about is set against vast empty horizons of prior and subsequent events. Empty horizons are *not* nothings — they are *positively meant* though indeterminately given.

The systematic relevance of empty horizons is twofold. *First*, they are the methodological clue to the unity of the experiential world: the world is one *because* every experience opens onto empty horizons that connect it to all other experiences. Without empty horizons, experience would be a series of disconnected moments. *Second*, they ground the *progress of knowledge*: scientific inquiry can be understood as the *unfolding* of empty horizons into determinately given ones, as ever-new investigations make explicit what was previously only implicitly co-meant.

The significance of empty horizons is also constitutive for *intersubjectivity*: the others I encounter are given with empty horizons of their own subjective lives, which I cannot directly experience but which I co-mean as belonging to them. Likewise the *historical past* is co-meant in its empty horizons as the past of *the same world* in which we now live. The empty horizon, then, is the structural condition for the unity of the experiential world across regions, across persons, and across times. Phenomenological psychology will be especially concerned with empty horizons because they constitute the structural fabric of intentional life.

### **§ 8\. The Science of the Universal World-Structure as A Priori Science**

---

The science of the universal *world-structure* — that is, the science of those features that hold for *any possible* experiential world as such — is, by its very nature, an *a priori* science. It investigates not what *happens to be* the case in our world, but what *must* be the case in *any* world whatever in which experiencing subjects experience things, persons, communities. The §-title announces the basic methodological claim: the *Idea* of a universal world-structure is intrinsically *a priori*.

The status of this a priori is the eidetic-intuitive a priori unfolded in the Introduction. It is not the Kantian transcendental a priori, nor the Leibniz-Wolffian metaphysical a priori. It is gained by *Wesensschau* — by intuitive grasping of the *essence* of *world-experience*, varying examples imaginatively until the *invariant* (the *Eidos*) is brought to evidence. The a priori of the universal world-structure is therefore the *eidetic structure of any conceivable experiential world*.

This a priori science of world-structure stands in a definite relation to the empirical sciences of the actual world. Just as pure geometry stands to factual physics — providing the necessary structural framework within which factual nature must hold itself — so the a priori science of world-structure provides the necessary framework within which all actual descriptive sciences of the world (natural-scientific or humanistic) must hold themselves. The parallel with geometry/physics is here installed as the systematic model for the relation between the a priori and the empirical.

The a priori science is thus *fundamental* for all empirical sciences of the experiential world — not in the sense of *deducing* their content, but in the sense of *delimiting* the structural framework that any conceivable empirical content must satisfy. Empirical psychology, for example, can only describe psychic facts if those facts *are* possible facts within an experiential world; and what is possible is structurally pre-determined by the eidetic structure of *experiential world as such*. The a priori, here as with physics, is the *invisible binding rule* of all empirical inquiry.

### **§ 9\. Essential Intuition as the Genuine Method of Apprehending the A Priori**

---

The reader should note that §9 has sub-paragraphs (a)–(f) corresponding to the moments of the method of *Wesensschau*. Each is to be discussed in turn.

---

#### **a) Variation as the Decisive Step of Detachment from the Factual through Phantasy — the Eidos as the Invariant**

---

The method of *essential intuition* (Wesensschau) begins with the *factually given*: a single example, taken from experience or memory. This example serves as the *starting point* (Vorbild, exemplar). But essential intuition is not a generalization from an inductive collection of cases — it is an entirely different procedure. Its key act is *variation in phantasy*: from the given example, we generate, in *free imaginative variation*, an *open infinity* of imaginary variants — variants that differ in this respect or that, while preserving certain structural features.

The crucial methodological insight is: it is *not* empirical observation, but *imaginative variation*, that releases us from the factuality of the example. In phantasy I am free to vary the example *arbitrarily*; the example loses its grip on me as *this* fact, and what remains in view is only what *cannot be varied away* without the variants ceasing to be variants of *this* kind of thing.

What *cannot* be varied away — what remains constant across the open infinity of variants — *is* the *Eidos*, the essence. The Eidos is thus the *invariant* under free imaginative variation. The Eidos is not "extracted" from the examples by some empirical comparison; it is *seen* (in a peculiar essential intuition) as that which any conceivable variant must satisfy in order to be a variant of *this* kind. The method, then, is not inductive abstraction but *eidetic intuition* of the invariant.

---

#### **b) Variation and Alteration**

---

Husserl carefully distinguishes *variation* (Variation) from *alteration* (Veränderung). *Alteration* is what happens to an actual object as it changes through time: this leaf was green, now it is brown — the same leaf has altered. *Variation*, by contrast, is the *imaginative production* of *different* possible objects, not the change of one and the same object. The leaf I imagine as red, the leaf I imagine as larger, the leaf I imagine with seven veins instead of three — these are *variants*, not *alterations* of the original leaf.

This distinction is essential because the *Eidos* is not what remains *the same* through alteration of *one* object (which would still bind us to the factual), but what is invariant across an *open infinity of possible* objects. The Eidos *leaf* — what makes anything a leaf at all — is grasped not by tracking a particular leaf's history but by varying *what could count as a leaf* in any imaginable case.

---

#### **c) The Moments of Ideation: Starting from the Exemplar (Vorbild), Opening Up an Open Infinity of Variants (Arbitrariness of the Process of Variant-Formation); Overlapping Coincidence of the Variant-Formation into a Synthetic Unity; Grasping the Congruent as the Eidos**

---

Husserl now articulates the act of *ideation* into its component moments. The *first* moment is the starting from the *exemplar*: a single concrete example must be in view; ideation does not begin from an abstract concept but from a definite case. The *second* moment is the *opening up* of an *open infinity* of imaginary variants. This must be a *truly open* infinity: there is no closed list of variants but an indefinite horizon in which ever-new variants can be imagined.

The *third* moment is the *arbitrariness* of the variant-forming process — *Beliebigkeit*. This arbitrariness is essential: only because I can vary *arbitrarily*, without confining myself to "realistic" possibilities, can I sweep out the full range of conceivable variants and so isolate what is *invariant under any conceivable variation*. Arbitrariness is the methodological correlate of *necessary universality*.

The *fourth* moment is the *overlapping coincidence* (überschiebende Deckung) of the variants into a *synthetic unity*. As variants are produced, they pass *through* one another in phantasy, and a *common* feature — something that all variants exhibit, something that *runs through* all of them — comes synthetically to evidence. The variants do not stand alongside one another like a list; they *coincide* in a structural feature that *all* of them present.

The *fifth* moment is the *grasping* (Erfassung) of this *congruent* moment as the *Eidos*. This is the act of *eidetic intuition* proper: I now see, *intuitively*, that this is what makes any of these a variant of *this* kind — and that there is no conceivable variant that lacks this feature. The Eidos is thus *grasped as the invariant in the synthetic unity of the variants*.

---

#### **d) Separation of Empirical Generalization and Ideation**

---

Husserl now sharply distinguishes *eidetic ideation* from *empirical generalization*. Empirical generalization works by surveying actual cases — actual leaves, actual perceptions, actual minds — and abstracting features that they *factually* share. Its generality is the generality of an *induction* from a sample: it is *fallible*, *contingent*, *empirical*. The "generality" of an empirical concept is the generality of *what has been observed so far*.

*Eidetic ideation*, by contrast, has nothing to do with factual cases as such. The exemplar from which it starts is a *mere instance* — its function is only to *set in motion* the imaginative variation. The other variants are *phantasy-products*, not observations. What is grasped, the Eidos, is not what is *common* to a factual sample but what is *necessary* for *any conceivable case whatever*. Its generality is the generality of *unconditional* necessity, not of inductive surveyance.

This distinction is methodologically decisive for phenomenological psychology. If phenomenological psychology were merely empirical-generalizing, it would be just an extension of inductive empirical psychology. What makes it a *new* science is precisely that its generalities are *eidetic*: they are *necessities* of psychic life as such, valid for any conceivable conscious subject, not merely for the humans encountered so far.

---

#### **e) Bringing Out the Hierarchy of Genera and Gaining the Highest Genera through Variation of Ideas. — Idea-Intuition without Starting from Experience**

---

Eidetic intuition does not yield only a single eidos at a single level. *Eidos* themselves come in *hierarchies* of generality: the eidos *animal* is more general than *vertebrate*, which is more general than *mammal*. Husserl notes that the procedure of *variation* can itself be iterated on the level of *ideas*: I can take the eidos *mammal* and vary it imaginatively (to invent imaginary mammal-like animals not encountered in actual zoology) to bring out the higher eidos *vertebrate*; and so on upward toward ever more general genera.

Hence we obtain the *hierarchy of genera* (Stufenfolge der Gattungen): a layered articulation of essences from the most specific to the most general. At the very top stand the *highest genera*, those that admit of no further generalization. For example, *object in general* (any *Etwas*) is the highest formal genus; *physical thing in general* is a high material genus; *psychic experience in general* is another. Each highest genus delimits a *region* of being.

Note that at the higher levels of variation, one may *no longer begin from experience*: the variation can move *purely within the realm of ideas*, with no need to anchor in any factual exemplar. The methodological consequence is that *eidetic science is autonomous*: it does not, at higher stages, depend on empirical occasions but can proceed in *pure ideal* movement among essences. This is what makes eidetic phenomenology a fully *a priori* science.

---

#### **f) Summarizing Characterization of Essential Intuition**

---

To summarize: the method of *essential intuition* consists of (1) the starting from an exemplar, (2) the free imaginative variation generating an open infinity of variants, (3) the overlapping coincidence of variants in synthetic unity, (4) the eidetic grasping of the invariant, and (5) the possibility of iterative variation at higher levels yielding the hierarchy of genera up to the highest genera, including the possibility of variation purely within ideas. This method is *the* genuine method of apprehending the a priori — for it alone gives the necessary, unconditional generality that defines the a priori.

The method is in principle applicable to *any* domain of inquiry — to nature, to mathematical idealities, to psychic life, to social formations, to history. Wherever there is something whose *essence* one wants to grasp — what *it is* and what it *must be* to be that kind of thing at all — there the method of Wesensschau is the proper instrument. Phenomenological psychology will deploy it on its proper domain: the eidetic structure of psychic life.

---

### **§ 10\. The Method of Intuitive Generalization and Ideation as Instruments for Gaining the General Structural Concepts of a World as Such, Starting from the Experiential World ("Natural World-Concept"). Possibility of an Articulation of the World-Sciences and Bringing Out the Significance of the Science of Spirit**

---

Husserl now clarifies the rhetorical and methodological function of the long discussion of eidetic method. The detailed description of the method of ideation has been introduced not as an *example* of a phenomenological-psychological analysis (although it is *also* one), but rather as the *means* by which we can come to a *radical division of the world-sciences*, indeed to a *radical conceptual articulation of the world* into the *essential world-regions*. By this means the *spiritual*, in all its fundamental configurations in which it is a general and necessary structural element of the world, must be brought out — and by this means a *secure guideline* would be gained for systematically sketching out all the problem-directions that open up, in keeping with these various fundamental configurations, for a *fully adequate science of spirit*.

Only by proceeding so radically can we free ourselves from all traditional prejudices, one-sidednesses, and unclarities that have led to ever-new psychologies but never to *the* one psychology — the one *genuine* psychology that draws from the *necessities* of its clearly grasped thematic field the necessary method and the necessary connected system of problems to be solved, and that for just this reason can only *be* one.

Husserl is careful to clarify: the fact that the description of the method of ideation is *itself* a psychological description is, for now, beside the point. The description was introduced for our present purpose, and we could not have made do with merely pointing to the mathematical procedure. Mathematics, as a purely eidetic-historical discipline that has grown up around magnitudes and numbers and been exclusively practiced on them for millennia, has given rise to the *firmly rooted prejudice* that an a priori method of this sort is to be practiced *only* on the mathematical (and the closely interwoven formally logical). Our description, by contrast, makes it evident that *wherever* and *at whatever stage* objectivities come to original intuitive givenness, the method of ideation can be practiced and essential generalities can be purely intuitively brought to view.

Next comes the methodological move that will guide the rest of the systematic part. *First*, we must unveil what has been experienced as world only narrowly and with unclarified horizons, by setting *possible experience* in play, progressing from possibilities to ever-new possibilities, and forming for ourselves an *overall picture*, a really explicated, even if openly progressing, *total intuition of the world* — namely, as it would and would have to look, all in all, if we, either really experiencing or thinking ourselves into some experiencing, filled out the open, indeterminate horizons with *unanimously fitting experiential possibilities*. And likewise, of course, with regard to the open horizons that belong to every stretch of memory-revived past experience.

We would then also see clearly that these horizons are *infinitely indeterminate*; that what we represent to ourselves as the unknown or unexperienced world — namely the world outside the frame of experiential familiarity — at any moment represents only *one* possibility, to which we could *at will* set quite different possibilities alongside. On the other hand, however, it is also clear that *if* we bring several such possibilities, in synthesis with the actuality partially fixed by experience, to full clarity (as a process to be carried in infinitum), then immediately the *typically common* must assert itself. We can now consciously *set in play* the method of *originarily intuitive generalization*; and so, in the evident consciousness of arbitrariness, progressing comparatively and freely varying, we would gain a *necessarily common* and, in all variation of the possibilities, *simply invariant* element.

So the horizon of open indeterminacy, surrounding the realm of actual experience, is *itself* a realm of *variation-capable possibilities* — bound, of course, by their being *real* possibilities. These are continuations of the experientially familiar into the unfamiliar, which through experience has a piece of *pre-sketching*, and in given cases is an incompletely determined *pre-expectation*. The methodological insight: the *horizon* is not a featureless void but is *positively structured* by the experientially-rooted styles that any continuation must satisfy.

Thus the method of intuitive generalization leads, even before it is fully formed, to a *pure ideation*, to *structural generalities* as absolutely insightful necessities — necessities that say: I do not know how things look or how the whole world looks where my actual seeing does not reach, *but it must, somehow, look*. And from what is seen, the *general style* of what is *not* seen is necessarily pre-sketched. Every continuation of the seen, in real possible experience — and indeed in unanimously continuable experience — is, to be sure, indeterminate; but it can be made evident through intuitive generalization that the realm of possible experience, and thus the whole world, has such-and-such *necessary structural forms*. Among these obviously belong *time and space*. However unknown to me the not-yet-experienced world-region may be, however arbitrarily I might populate it with possible experiential objects, I am bound: necessarily — *this is an absolutely evident demand* — they are, in general, enduring objects, spatially distributed, etc.

If we practice this method concretely and systematically, we gain *determinate generic generalities* as *invariant structures*; but with that, we also gain *originally drawn structural concepts* — not word-concepts, but concepts whose corresponding ideal essence is self-actively, insightfully drawn. So it is for all structures, and thus for *nature* and *spirit*. All vague word-concepts must really disappear, demanding replacement by originally evidently formed ideas and the essential concepts expressing them. After all of this you will now understand that in this method we gain *the secure guideline* for articulating in advance the *possible world-sciences*, for grounding them on the essential structure of the experiential world, for equipping them with most-originally-drawn essential concepts.

For our purpose, what must be held: *within the universal and intuitively-rooted articulation of sciences*, we gain the *essential place* of the *science of spirit* — and the possibility, *within the spiritual itself*, of creating the essential articulation: following the fundamental configurations in which the spiritual has to function *for* the world. What a fully adequate science of spirit has to accomplish would be thematically sketched in advance. A genuine science must indeed have a *clear and determinate thematic field* in view: its universal goal and goal-system must guide it, not as a vague and empty concept, but as an essence-intuited generality.

Husserl reiterates: only by proceeding so radically can we be free of the traditional prejudices, one-sidednesses, unclarities that have led across the centuries to ever-new psychologies but never to *the* psychology — the *one* psychology that draws from the proper-essential necessities of its clear thematic field the *necessity* of an adapted method and the necessary articulations of the problems to be solved (and so also of the disciplines). For this, however, we need not only the method of *originarily intuitive generalization* but also the method of *pure induction*, which is in general the method of all *principled* insights.

Husserl adds a closing methodological reflection: the fact that our whole procedure — including the description of the method of intuitive generalization and the inductive method itself — is *itself a preliminary exercise in this very psychology* whose insightful possession we steer toward, is a piece of the *referring-back* of every conceivable kind of knowledge *to itself*. This characteristic self-reference of phenomenology will be returned to.

Husserl now responds to an anticipated objection. Could not, in our context, the mere reference to *the experience of the universally known a priori sciences*, namely the mathematical, have sufficed? Could the difficult description of the inductive procedure as the method of the a priori not have been spared? Husserl notes that this question presupposes the description of ideation was introduced as a *first example of an inner-psychological analysis*. But that was not the leading intention. Rather, the method should serve as a *means* for sketching out the *systematic path* on which alone a conceptually sharply outlined and necessity-illuminated *leading Idea of psychology* as a universal science of the spiritual — and, equally, every narrower concept of psychology — is to be gained.

This path leads through a *radical articulation of the world-sciences* — or, through a radical and at the same time all-comprehensive *analysis and description of the experiential world* according to the most general and essentially-fundamental structures of it: meaning structures that, in spite of all variability of *content* in real being and happening in different times, spaces, particular contexts, *remain invariant*. Indeed, suppose it can be shown in compelling evidence by a fit methodical procedure that through the *whole* of the experiential world — purely as experienceable — an *all-spanning invariant typology* runs through. More precisely: suppose there exists an evidently demonstrable invariant typology under which all realities to be attributed to the world in possible and actual experience *must* stand, both as regards their *fundamental kinds* and as regards the *forms of their combination* into wholes, and likewise for the all-comprehensive *orders and relations or combinations* that constitute the *all-unity of the world*. Then obviously a *systematic theme for a universal world-science* lies in this universal typology — namely, as the *universal structural science of the experiential world*.

An all-spanning, ever-deeper-to-be-pursued *framework* would express itself in it, which obviously prescribes an *unbreakable norm* for all possible particular sciences: prescribing for them their essential *demarcations*, those of their domains and of their problems, and of their problem-interweavings.

So we can say, in retrospect on the general feature of the ideative method: *every object has its Eidos*, or stands *under essential concepts*. And — what specially interests us here — every *possible experiential object*, every *individually being thing*, has its *essence*, its *whole Idea*, which unites within itself the *Ideas of all its qualities*. An *ideal object* is itself an essence, an *ideal*, an *Eidos*; it may, in addition, stand under higher *eide*, higher pure genera. But what is itself a *"this here"* and not an essence, *has* an essence — and we can set out to seek it. The general description of the method of ideation is obviously *itself* a description in essential generality; in free variation we can let any exemplary object on which it is practiced become any arbitrary object whatever, a *variable* in the sense of mathematics. So it is itself an essential insight that the ideative method is everywhere applicable.

But "applicable" does not yet mean "applied." The a priori knowledge that we have in advance about any given object — that it has an essence and, explicitly, a manifold of essential generalities belonging to it — is only a *task*, only goal-giving, for practical striving. The task can be enormous in some cases and quite easy and immediately discharged in others. Pure essential concepts like *color* or *tone*, like *brightness*, *timbre as moment of a tone*, we shall easily grasp. But when we speak of a *thing in general* in pure generality, of a *body in general*, an *animal being*, a *human in general*, it may be that the pure generality comes about only in the *word-meaning*, empty, non-intuitive.

If we want to actualize the empty meaning, to solve the task here to be posed — to actually produce the corresponding Eidos, which without doubt is producible — then it turns out that we make only difficult headway. Indeed, penetrating deeper: each of these concepts demands *many dimensions* of intuitions to be performed, and the progressive production of the Eidos, e.g., of the *natural thing in general*, of the *human in general*, and the unwinding of its *eidetic elementary necessities*, demands in each case an entire *infinite science*. At the head, so to speak, of every such science stands the *leading prefigured concept* and the most general — and only according to the most general essential structures articulated — *essence to be produced through intuition*. *That alone* will matter for our task.

### **§ 11\. Characterization of the Science of the Natural World-Concept. Demarcation of the Concept of Experience from the Kantian Concept of Experience. Space and Time as the Most General Structures of the World**

---

(Husserl begins a new lecture, 18.VI.25. The §-title points to Beilage VI.) In our last expositions we sketched the *Idea of the science of the "natural world-concept"* — that is, the *universal descriptive science of the invariant essence* of the pre-given as of any possible experienceable world. With that we sketched the *universal frame* that must contain the a priori of every possible *mundane science*. All possible *goals* that such sciences set themselves externally, all *problems* posable in them, have an a priori type — they stand under essence-general problems of which the relevant sciences must be aware in order to work in ultimate goal-clarity and methodical clarity, that is, in order to be sciences in the strictest sense.

It cannot be our task here to explicitly project this great science itself. But by further following this Idea in some fundamental strokes, we can gain a great deal — and enough to win a *preliminary, essential Idea* of the possible universal science of *spirit*, an Idea that necessarily inscribes itself into the full universal Idea of that world-science.

Our beginning must, according to what has been laid out, be the *unveiling of the experiential world*; and at first it is the unveiling of the *fact* of this actually experienced world. As we have presupposed, this has already been done — we have busied ourselves self-actively with this unveiling. We know what this says. The world is experienced; in every moment of our waking life it is, *bodily*, our actuality. But "proper" bodily there, namely *directly perceived*, is only a *narrow perceptual field* of actually perceived realities and connections. This *near-field* is surrounded by a *ring* of unperceived familiarities, mixed with unfamiliarities, and finally we have the *far-field* of open *unfamiliarities*; similarly for every point of the past.

In the progress of actual experience, much of the unexperienced unveils itself of itself; but *possible experience*, which we, motivated by the actual, at any time set in play, also reveals to us, *in the picture of possibilities*, the consequent *style* belonging to our experiential world; and the *necessity* of this already hints at the possibility of the *discovery of eidetic necessities*. To all this belongs, if we are to gain generally descriptive knowledge about the style of the experiential world (and possibly of every possible experiential world), *intuitively carried out empirical and then eidetic generalization*.

Here an important methodical (but also substantively immediately further-leading) remark is to be inserted, and best by way of an objection that will press itself upon those coming from Kant's *transcendental philosophy*. On the close relation of our problematic to that of Kant in the *Critique of Pure Reason* I cannot enter. We are moved by no philosophical interests in the sense of a transcendental philosophy. But however much our goals and even paths diverge from Kant's, an *essential kinship* of the problem of the evident structure of the world of possible experience with the problems of Kant's *transcendental aesthetic and analytic* — even *dialectic* — is unmistakable.

Now Kant *emphatically teaches*: *the world is no object of possible experience*. Whereas we have constantly, in all seriousness, spoken of the world precisely as the *universal object of a universally-spanning and to-be-spanned experience*. I cannot acknowledge the Kantian proposition, however the concept of *experience* is formed, if it is to remain useful. For *us*, real *singulars* are experienced, *but also the world is experienced*, and the two are *inseparable*. We experience, say, a thing; we perceive it; we come back to it in remembrances; we have in the connection of these memories a *synthesis of many individual experiences into the unity of one experience as experience of the same*. But also: what *I* experience can equally well be experienced by *another*; and not only my experiences connect into the unity of one experience, but also *my and others' experiences* again connect into experience of the same.

*As far as* unanimous synthesis into the unity of an *unanimous experience* is possible, *that far* extends our concept of experience. As experiential actuality, as *being real*, what we afterward grasp as a *member of the real and actually-being world*, is what is experienced — and the *world itself* is what is experienced, namely as the all-embracing horizon of unanimous experience. Husserl is here distinguishing his concept of *experience* sharply from Kant's: experience is *unanimous synthesis*, and it reaches *as far as* such synthesis reaches; the world is *not* (as for Kant) a regulative idea but the *open horizon* of unanimous experiential synthesis.

*Space* and *time* are now identified as the *most general structures of the world*. They are the *forms* within which all experiential realities and connections of realities are necessarily ordered. The space and time at issue here are *not* the abstract space and time of mathematics, but the *experienced* space and *experienced* time of the pre-scientific world — the world of perceived things at distances and durations. The eidetic essence of *experiential space and time as such* is, however, capable of being brought out by ideative variation, and what is so brought out is the *necessary frame* within which any experienceable world must hold itself.

---

### **§ 12\. Necessary Starting from the Singular Experience, in Which Passive Synthesis Lets the Unity Emerge**

---

The all-encompassing structures (space, time) of the experiential world are themselves accessed only through *singular* experience: I begin with this particular perceived thing, this particular remembered event. The structural a priori of the world is not graspable except by starting from *individual experiential cases*, and varying them imaginatively to bring out the invariant.

What makes the singular experience already a unity of experiential meaning is *passive synthesis*. The flow of sensory data is not, in the first place, an unordered stream that I must consciously gather; on the contrary, *prior to* any deliberate activity of mine, sensory data already arrange themselves into unities — *this* color belongs to *this* surface, *this* shape and *this* color belong to *this* thing, the side now seen and the side anticipated belong to the same enduring object. This unifying work is *passive* — not done by me but happening *for* me.

Passive synthesis, in its various configurations, belongs to *experience itself* and is in principle *its unity*. It is *the underlay* for the setting-in-play of the *activities* of the relating, logically generalizing, generalizing-concept-and-proposition-constituting *doing*. And this is so in such a way that the concepts, the general states-of-affairs and state-of-affairs-connections, *gain their logical intuition* and become *insightful knowledge*. Husserl is here installing the structural priority of *passivity* over *activity* in conscious life — a doctrine that has been a constant in his work since the *Investigations on Passive Synthesis* (Hua XI). The unity of the experiential object is a *passive* accomplishment of consciousness; conceptual articulation is the *active* accomplishment that builds on it.

### **§ 13\. Distinction between Independent and Non-Independent Realities. Determination of Real Unity by Means of Causality**

---

Surveying the real singulars within a structurally not-yet-known world, we seek the most general and even a priori necessary distinctions. As a first — and the most fundamental — distinction, there presents itself: (1) between *real "concrete" individuals*, things in a certain widest sense, and (2) the *modifications, modes of individual reality*, which are mere *thingly* modifications, *not themselves things*.

Within the experienceable real singulars, certain ones confront us that have, so to speak, the form of *primal realities* (Urrealitäten). These are characterized by being, according to their own sense, *perceivable, experienceable* without their experience presupposing the experience of other real singulars. E.g., a house, a human, a mountain — to experience them does not require prior experience of other realities. Their experience is *self-standing*. Consider, however, the experience of the *color of a house*, the *shape of a mountain*, the *facial expression of a person* — these experiences are *non-independent*, and indeed *as such* of these objectivities color, shape, etc. I can win the color only after having, however fleetingly, *noticed-grasped* the concrete individual whose color it is. Such real singulars presuppose, for their experience, the others.

To this realm Husserl would also count *multitudes*, *configurations* of every kind. Granted, to grasp an *avenue of trees* we do not have to perceive tree by tree in successive singular grasping. But even if we grasp the group, the avenue, at the first glance, in this collective grasping lies an enclosed *co-grasping* of each individual tree for itself. Still, here there is a difference compared with the earlier examples, insofar as those presuppose a *for-itself-grasping* of the founding singulars in the sense of an *exclusively-directed* and not a mere *co-grasping*. Taking both together, the real singulars in fact divide into *"things"* and *thingly modes*, which finally lead back to singular things: collections of things, configurations, moments of things, possibly of thing-groupings, etc. For this reason I called the things, the realities in the pregnant sense, *primal realities*.

(Husserl begins a new lecture.) We distinguished in the last lecture the concepts of *independent or concrete reality* and *non-independent reality* (mere non-independent moments at concrete realities) and then sought to work out the *concept of the thing*, of the real individual, in the pregnant word-sense. Subsequently it seems to me that older determinations, as I gave them in earlier years and that place the *concept of causality* at the focal point, are *not to be dispensed with* for the concept of the thing. The leading thought I hold: a mere configuration, a mere multitude of concrete singulars that gives itself unitarily intuitively is *not yet a thing*, even though, taken together, it falls under the concept of the *independently experienceable*. However concrete objects stand in the unity of an overlooking experience, they form *eo ipso* an intuitive unity, they form a *sensuous* configuration. With that, however, they are not *really unitary*.

If we ask what makes specifically *real unity* and what makes anything experienceable in mundane experience a *thing*, a real in the specific sense, then it is best answered: *real unity* is such a unity whose experienceable inner qualities *not only preserve unity in their unchanging and changing, but which in the change of these qualities preserves a fixed causality*. Or, as we can also say: in their temporally changing states there express themselves, *in the causal states*, fixed *causal properties*. Things — e.g. material things — have, e.g., changing states of motion, but in these *express themselves determinate mechanical properties*; they have changing states of warmth, of electricity, of magnetism, in which fixed *thermal, electric, magnetic* properties express themselves, belonging to the relevant things firmly in the change of these states. The immediately experienceable qualities of things, of material things, of sensuously perceived things, are *dependent in their courses of change on their thingly environment* (or on the sensuous qualities of these surrounding things), and likewise conversely.

These dependencies are experienced *in their own way*. And every thing itself is from the outset *apperceived as such a lasting individual* that has its *familiar style* of causal behavior: its familiar style of behaving under its respective thingly circumstances — e.g., in lighting, warming and cooling, in pressure and push, etc., to comport itself such-and-such; to modify itself such-and-such with the modifications of the sensuously experienceable qualities of the circumstance-realities. That every thing has its *thingly environment-horizon* says more precisely: it has its *causal* horizon — it is, in its qualities, in *constant dependence* on its environment, but in such a way that this dependence has its *fixed style*, depending on the kind of environment and its respective qualities. Just this expresses itself in general scientific experiential consideration in *causal laws*. *Every* thing is not only the unity of changeable qualities but stands under causal laws as *rules* by which things of each category, in all their possible surroundings, comport themselves in a determinate, foreseeable way.

The law is, to be sure, not itself directly experienced or experienceable, but only knowable through *thinking* on the basis of experiences. But to the *experiential sense itself* it belongs that what is experienced has its *causal style and horizon*. To know a thing means to *foresee* how it will *causally* behave: e.g., to know a glass plate is to know that it will break under lively pushing or being thrown; an elastic spring, pushed, will oscillate in known manner; a rolling ball striking another will, depending on the direction of impact, exercise the corresponding movement-effects on the struck ball.

Where many things are given together, they are not yet *one* thing by mere unity of sensuous configuration. They are united into *one* thing only when they form a *whole* that is an *identical substrate of causal properties* and has its *fixed style* — discoverable through unveiling experience — of behaving causally under the respective circumstances of its existence.

In this sense a *stone* is a real thing, or the sun, or even our whole *solar system* — also the latter, for however much each individual member of this system is itself a real for itself, the *system* is also a *real causal unity*. Similarly, a *human* is a real thing, but also a *club* and a *people*. A club within its real social environment is a *unity of causality*, as a people in its peoples-environment.

---

### **§ 14\. Order of the Realities in the World**

---

In the frame of the world of pure experience, *things* divide into those of *higher* and *lower order*. We come ultimately to *things of the lowest order*, *singular* things, which no longer give themselves as a *thing-system*, as multitudes of single things connected into the unity of a thing — e.g., a celestial body, a human, etc. This leaves open, however, that in the causal process a *fragmentation* of a thing into several single things may occur, and that in principle a singular thing might be conceivable as *divisible*, divisible again into things.

Carrying out an overview over the whole *world of plain experience*, it is clear that it articulates itself thoroughly into *singular thing-realities*, which can then in turn be *united into real thing-complexes* as things of *higher order*. Each such reality in the specific sense is a *causal unity*, causally related to its real environment, and in this play of changing causal alterations is a *substrate of fixed causal properties* — thus preserving its causal *habitus* firmly.

Within this pervasive structure, which belongs to the form of *this* world and of *any* world, a great distinction now confronts us. The thing-realities fall apart into *living* ones, in everyday word-sense — into *psychically, soulfully alive and active* — and on the other side, *psychically lifeless* things. Humans and animals offer examples of the first. *Plants* we also call living beings. But only if we *seriously* experienced them as psychically living, as sensing, perceiving, feeling, striving, psychically affected or psychically active in the manner of psychic subjects, only then would we count them among the *psychic beings*, among those things that *are subjects and live as subjects*. Otherwise they count among the *non-subjects*, the psychically lifeless — like stones, mountains, houses, architectures, artworks of every kind, machines, books, etc.

One of the most immediate distinctions — one that imposes itself even on the most superficial consideration — thus presents *spirituality as structural moment in the experiential world*. From there we must press forward to the *pure distinction of physical and psychic* and then in general *spiritual*; and thus to the *fundamental insight* that in the concrete world of pure experience *physical nature and spirituality* are everywhere *inseparably interwoven*, while yet *each* of them is to be pursued purely in itself as a self-enclosed connection.

### **§ 15\. Characterization of the Psychophysical Realities of the Experiential World. Higher Independence of Corporeality Over Against Soul**

---

What presses itself upon us as a difference between *subjects* and *non-subjects* was a difference *in the kind of things*. By a *positive* mark only subjects were defined — namely as *psychically living* — while the other things were defined only as *non-subjects*, only *negatively* through the lack of psychic life. What *psychic life* means, we have for now sufficiently clarified through examples (which we can multiply manifoldly): say sensory sensing, perceiving and other experiencing; performing thought-activities like collecting, comparing, distinguishing, practicing generalization and ideation, inferring and proving; also passively feeling pleasure and pain or actively taking pleasure or displeasure in something; instinctive striving, or actively setting-goals-for-oneself and being oriented to them desirously or willingly; and actively realizing this goal in action.

Psychic in the sense of such examples is grasped directly, through correspondingly directed experience (called *psychological*) at psychic subjects — as *their* psychic life. It presents itself as an unceasingly and necessarily *changing* stream, as a stream with fleetingly emerging and again away-streaming waves.

We experience subjects, however, also with *lasting* psychic qualities — namely, qualities that, in the manifold change of psychic doing and lived-experience, express themselves as *enduring*. So from experience we know some humans as short-sighted, others as far-sighted or normal-sighted; some as deaf, others as quite hard-of-hearing... in such enduring qualities a *psychic habitus* manifests itself.

Subjects are not only psychic in nature. The distinction of psychic realities cannot mean that subjects have *no other* properties than psychic ones. *Concrete thing-realities are subjects*, are animals and humans, only when we take the psychic qualities together with their underlying *non-psychic* (physical) qualities. Humans and animals are experienced as *leiblich-seelisch* (body-and-soul) beings; we *distinguish* their soul-life from their *corporeal embodiment* (Leiblichkeit), which, purely taken, contains nothing of the psychic but yet really belongs to the human or animal.

(Husserl begins a new lecture.) In the analysis of the experience of concrete thing-realities like *human or animal*, we easily distinguish their *soul-life* (and the experientially manifesting habitual properties of the psychic subjects) and on the other side their *corporeal embodiment*. In this opposition, *corporeality* contains nothing further of the psychic, while it really belongs to the human or animal in question.

General experience shows us a *general possibility of change* for such realities: from psychic subjects they can change into psychically lifeless things — in a word, they can *die*. Every animate being can change into a *mere body*, which in such mode of origin is called *corpse*. With this even foreseeable change every animate being is *apperceived* in experience.

Accordingly experiential analysis shows: such realities are *two-layered* — they are *psycho-physical* beings. Their *Physis* and their *Psyche* are abstractively to be considered for themselves at the concrete whole. Each has its own course of change, but the changes are *not indifferent* to one another. Human corporeality, and so every animal corporeality, is bound to its *specific style*.

But whichever particular kind of change may be sketched in the concrete experience of a human or a horse, *one thing is certain*: when the always-open possibility of *bodily decay* enters — when the unitary form of an organism passes over into a non-unitary mass or into mere inorganic matter — *then the psychic life is annihilated*. Nullity here means *non-being in the real world*, that is, within the unanimously experienced and experienceable manifold of things and thing-modalities, occurrences, etc.

Compared to the experience of the *corporeal*, the experience of the *psychic* stands quite differently. The corporeal *can* be experienced as *unanimated* — in the form of the inorganic, or at least of the organically non-unitary, that it can take on at any time, it lacks animation. Conversely, however, where psychic is experienced — and is to be experienceable — an *organic embodiment* must be the underlay. In possible experience, the animated body presupposes a corporeal-embodiment-substrate.

The matter is illuminated more deeply when we note that every *worldly experiencer*, who in originary perceptual manner experiences a world of experience as his *environment* (Umwelt), finds in it his *own body* as the *central member* of his corporeal environment — and specifically as his *foremost perception-organ*, by means of which alone he can experience things of every kind, including foreign humans and animals.

The *own body* (eigener Leib) functions here also, so to speak, as the *primal body* (Urleib), insofar as the perception of one's own body is distinguished as the *most originary body-experience* and as the *apperceptive presupposition* for every possible experience of the type *foreign body*. In that the own body is perceived *as* body, it is perceived *as animated*, and indeed in *originarily-own* and *self-perceived* animation. My body is given to *me*, and to *me alone*, *originarily*, as that in which my soul-life *rules*. My soul-life is, for me, *directly* — in the strictest word-sense — *perceived*, and directly perceived not *alongside* the body but *as animating it*. Here alone I experience originarily in perception this *unity of body and soul*, this *interleaving* of bodily and animating happening. So here, for me, is the most originary source of the sense of body and soul and animation. It is also clear that *only* so long as my body has its typical form, its articulation into organs in which I correspondingly can *rule* — perceivingly, movingly, pushingly, etc. — is it possible for an experienceable thing-world to be there *for me*. It is perceivable only in activities of perceiving, which everywhere are also *bodily* activities: seeing, touching, etc.

As for *foreign* bodies and the foreign soul-life belonging to them, it is — as is easy to see — experienceable for me only in *this* manner: that I can *interpret* the foreign corporeal-embodiment which I sensuously experience, by means of *analogy* with my own (as the alone-originarily-perceived embodiment) and with my life (which alone perceivingly animates it).

As soon as this analogy fails — or, where it was effective, is broken — as when the foreign corporeal body loses the *style of an organism* that mine does show — then that body there *loses the capacity* to indicate experientially a belonging animation. Foreign soul-life then in principle ceases to be experienceable. What for me is in principle not experienceable does not belong to my experiential world; in it it is nothing; it is not worldly being.

An *objective* world is one *intersubjectively experienced and experienceable*. It thus presupposes that *each subject*, *first for himself*, has his own experiential world — with things, animals, humans — and that there exist *experiential possibilities*, through *mutual understanding*, of *exchanging our experiences*, and in this exchange of producing an *intersubjective unanimity* of mutual experiences. Accordingly the presupposition of an *objective* world (on which we stand both as natural humans and as representatives of the objective sciences) already says this much: that we each have given our own corporeality (as the *primal site* of an own animating life) and likewise have given foreign corporeality, in *analogizing foreign-experience*, as a *site of foreign psychic life*. However we may further conceive the presupposed experiential world to be altered, with whatever changing determinate content in the progress of actual and possible experience, this much is clear: in the *apperceptive sense* of an animation that functions *as one's own and as another's*, *corporeality* is constantly presupposed.

### **§ 16\. The Configurations in Which the Spiritual Appears in the Experiential World. The Peculiarity of the Cultural Object, Which Is Determined in Its Being by Subject-Relatedness**

---

With the distinction between physis (body) and psyche in place, it would be natural to follow descriptively the structural distinctions made, by studying purely what makes up, on the one side, the essence of *mere corporeality* — which has reality partly as psychically lifeless for itself and partly as the underlay (Leiblichkeit) of a psyche. (Husserl notes Beilagen IX and X are relevant.)

It already presses itself on us here that *all corporeality of the world stands in a single corporeal connection* — one to be considered *for itself*, governed by the closed unity of a *universal physical causality*. And likewise that *spatiality* and *spatio-temporality* belong *in distinguished manner* to corporeality, and that all the psychic only *mediately* — through its corporeality and its spatiotemporality — has share in objective extension.

On the other (soul) side, corresponding descriptive problems would arise: questions after the *descriptive fundamental peculiarities of all the psychic*, after its elementary types, the connecting unity-forms, what makes the unity of *one* psyche necessary purely as psyche, and how moreover several souls — for all concrete separation — can win *unity* through purely psychic relation and connection, and how between humans and animals a *spiritual nexus*, a *personal unity of higher order*, becomes possible.

But (Husserl begins a new lecture, 26.VI) a closer consideration soon shows that our structural division between *physis* and *psyche* is *not yet a fully pure one*, and that a deeper analysis is required to prepare the *Idea of a pure and mere nature* as it became the foundation of modern natural science. And correspondingly on the correlative side, the Idea of pure soulful spirituality is to be determined in parallel manner, through a corresponding purification. This purification is an *exclusion of a certain "merely subjective"*.

It is for our purpose of great importance to consider in *what different significations* there can be talk of *the subjective* in the consideration of the experiential world — or: *in what configurations* the subjective and spirituality can play a role in the experienceable world at all. *Spirituality in the form of psychic life and psychic habituality of animal subjects* is one fundamental form — indeed, the *primal form* — in which spirituality has *worldly existence*, whether in the form of singularity or community.

But now we must also note that *things* — even *lifeless* things, when we take them as experienced by us — can bear a *spiritualization* in themselves: can, so to speak, have spiritualization incorporated in them, while yet remaining lifeless things and not bodies in which spirit has been embodied in the form of proper animation. The vast majority of things of our *life-world* — the world that constantly surrounds us as our experiential world — can serve as example: bookshelves, furniture, houses, fields, gardens, tools, pictures, etc. These are experienced quite *immediately* as *spiritually meaningful things*; they are not seen as merely physical, but in their sensuously experienced shape — in their spatial form, in every winding of that form, in their other sensuous traits — a *spiritual sense* expresses itself.

This sense, often in a many-membered spiritual structure, has its *counterpart* in the structure of the formations and articulations of the sensuously experienced corporeality, in which it *embodies itself expressively*. The expressed sense does not lie *alongside* the expressing corporeal; both *as one* are concretely experienced. Before us stands a *twofold corporeal-spiritual objectivity*. So there is an *analogy* between the experiential kind of this kind of cultural object and the experience of animal beings: in both cases sense is not posited alongside the body, but the corporeality is taken as *expressing* the sense in its own peculiar way.

The cultural object refers back to *subjective producing*. A pen, a chair, a tool, a work of art — these are experienced not merely as physically present things but as the products of intention, purpose-setting, the realizing action of one or more persons in their historical and social context. Following this back-reference, we again stand within *psychic life*; for what makes the human a *personal subject* is not the physical but what *animates* it.

Looking more closely at this unveiling clarification: we gain clarity about what kind of "spirituality" we ascribe to the *cultural objects* under the title *spiritual sense*, and in what *relation* this spirituality stands to the *soul-life* (which we designated as the *primal-spiritual* — as the originary site of all spirituality).

*Cultural sense*, objectified spirituality of every kind, is on the one hand *objectively experienced* — namely as a sense *belonging to certain things*, *dwelling expressively in them*, *spiritualizing them*. On the other hand, every *expressed sense* and every object equipped with sense in its self-unveiling experiential content *refers back to a psychic subjectivity* — to a human or human community from whose *personal accomplishment* it has sprung. It is a *formation of a subjective forming*, sprung from intention, purpose- and means-setting, realizing action — and finally, as total accomplishment, in the widest sense, *work-formation*.

As originarily creatively become — as accomplishment in the accomplishing doing — the cultural object has for the accomplishing subjectivity, *and only for it*, the *original form*, the *actual originality*. It develops in or out of its intention and realization in a *stage-sequence of configurations*, in each of which its respective *purpose-sense* and *work-sense* dwells perceptually; and so finally in the completed work itself — which is not only *something* for the maker in general, but is *originarily there in the work-form*, in the intended and purposively shaped form, intuitively.

This work-character — the purpose-sense — is naturally *not* something *sensuously perceived and perceivable* like, e.g., the arrow-shape when an arrow is whittled. The physical shape is sensuously experienced, seen with eyes, felt with fingers. But the *purpose-form* expressed by the word "arrow" is *unsensuously sprung* from intention and will; but where it becomes actual in *purposive activity*, the sensuous shape produced according to the guiding purpose is seen *at the same time* as *intended and effected*. Here the work *as work* has its most originary mode of givenness — perceptual in the good wide sense.

What has thus come to be can be *repeatedly experienced*, and by *any number* of subjects — but not in the same sense *originarily perceptually*. *Individually the same* cannot be produced twice; only a similar or equivalent can. But individually the same is repeatedly experienced as *this* work-formation — e.g., as *this arrow* — and by any number of subjects. And this not only in such a way that *spectators*, watching the maker and empathically understanding into his purposive activity, have their *co-experience* of the work as such — but also, *after* the work has come to be, it is *generally experienceable*. The originally arisen purpose- and work-sense is something *permanently appropriated* to the corporeal object. The corresponding experience has the *fundamental form of interpretation*: the arrow is physically-sensuously seen and, as one with that, *understood as arrow*, according to its purpose-sense.

In this way an object endowed with purpose-sense — a *cultural object* — has, on the one hand, an *originary realization* in *personal* life and has, for the personal subject, the mode of givenness of *proper perception* through originary production. On the other hand, it has, for *every other subject* within the community of mutual understanding, *manifold experienceability* in the form of intuitive and self-corroborating *interpretation*.

The cultural object — or its cultural sense — is *not* a *psychically real* in the manner of real psychic states or real psychic properties expressed in them. Generally speaking, a *thing-real* is a unity of change, a unity through a duration that extends changeable states standing under causal regulation. In this sense a material thing, but also an animal, a human, is a thing-real unity — but *not* a speech, a writing, a literary work, a work of plastic art, etc. The *cultural sense*, insofar as it belongs to the corporeal body, so to speak, of the *work*, and belongs to it *enduringly*, is not in the manner of a *reality-layer* belonging to this corporeal body. This corporeal body, considered for itself, has its unity of corporeal reality.

The sense that makes it a *cultural object* is, to be sure, time-related, and in a certain way we also speak of a duration for it. But here we find no states extending themselves over the duration and changing — *no proper temporal extension* with extending time-fullness — and nothing of a *bald-altering-or-not-altering* with identity of the cultural object. And as no proper alteration, so also no proper causality of alterations. The unity of the work, its identity, determines its *invariant purpose-sense*.

If a picture, through darkening or decay of colors, *physically* alters, and is *consequently* experienced differently in its sense, then it is not as though *the artwork as such* — *this aesthetic picture* — has altered; rather, *a different picture is now experienced*. And indeed the experience is a *false* one, one not to be unanimously sustained under appropriate unveiling.

Also the general historical *change of interpretation* of past cultural formations is not an alteration of them in the reality-sense in which we speak of the alteration of a physical thing or human; but rather a sequence of *historical modifications* according to which a *primal work* with its primal sense passes over into a *manifold not of actual but immanent works* — a manifold that shows in itself a special type of unification: as a manifold of competing interpretations *of the same*.

(Husserl begins a new lecture, 28.VI.25.) It is also to be noted that *different things* can never have *identically the same* states or real properties; each has *its own*, which are *individual*. But *different cultural formations* can very well, with really separated individual corporeality, have *identically the same sense*. Thus a mathematical proposition that is objectively embodied in the German language — and so is a *German cultural formation* — can with fully identical mathematical sense come to *embodiment* in other languages; and anyone can grasp this identity.

The sense is thus a *peculiar irreal layer* on a corporeal reality. Through this it receives a *place* in the real world — a kind of temporal duration, spatial binding, indeed even something like spatial extension — and again something like *effect* in the world, insofar as, sprung as it is from subjective accomplishment, it can in turn *act into* the post-understanding subjectivity, motivating it to new personal accomplishments.

We must take these peculiar kinds of experienceable objectivities as they are *experienced* and *experienceable*, as they give themselves in unveiling and experience and in the frame of possible unanimous experience, possibly verifying themselves as *intra-worldly actualities*. We may not push them aside merely because they are not *thing-realities* like nature-realities; and we may not, on account of their "subjectivity," without further ado regard them as *psychic* objects. Going back to their *origin-site* we find them as *personal productions* — and find that, according to the sense appropriated to them, they are *not* psychically real, but *intersubjectively identifiable actualities*, manifold experienceable in the manner of interpreting *after-understanding* (Nachverstehen), and possibly experienceable in a *general purposive validity* — as purposiveness in the sense of a generally-valid purpose-system.

While *realities* are what they are *without question of subjects* that refer to them, *cultural objects* are in determinate manner *subjective*, springing from subjective doing — and on the other hand *addressing themselves to subjects* as personal subjects, presenting themselves as useful, as instruments usable by anyone under fit circumstances, as destined for aesthetic enjoyment, etc. They have *objectivity*, an objectivity *for subjects and between subjects*. The *subject-relation* belongs to their proper essential content with which they are meant and experienced. A *physical thing* or a *human* is, to be sure, *a priori* relatable to a subjectivity, as experienceable and knowable *through* it; but a *thing* contains in its experiential content *nothing* of a subjectivity related to it. A thing-analysis, however far we drive the unveiling, leads only to thingly marks, never to a subjectivity creating according to purpose-sense, or referring to the created as purposive, useful, beautiful. *The relatedness to a personal community belongs, however, to the proper sense of all cultural objectivity.*

And precisely for this reason objective research here must turn, partly on the *cultural sense itself* and its work-shape, and partly and *correlatively* on the *manifold real personality* that the cultural sense co-presupposes — to which it itself constantly refers.

### **§ 17\. Reduction to the Pure Realities as Substrates of Exclusively Real Properties; Exclusion of the Irreal Cultural Senses**

---

(a) After making this clear, we recognize that the concrete experiential world *permits a purifying reduction* through which we gain, as a universal structure running through it, a *self-enclosed realm of pure reality*. However richly the experiential world may be equipped with cultural sense — however much such sense may be continuously appended through the culture-creating accomplishment or sense-giving that we humans have performed singly or as wider or narrower communities — *nothing stands in the way* of our *abstracting* from these predicates of the experiential world (these subject-related irreal characters) and directing the gaze exclusively at the universe of pure *thingly* realities that unite with them. As pure thingly realities we then obtain *humans and animals* in their concretely real *two-layeredness*, and the *psychically lifeless* realities in their exclusively real properties. Obviously this *thing-world* is *prior in itself* to the *cultural world*: culture *presupposes* humans and animals, who in their turn presuppose corporeality.

(b) Having so gained the pure thing-world, we can abstractively take *one further step back*. If we abstract, at the *animal* realities, from their *psychic* — so that also the *primal-spiritual* is set out of consideration — then everywhere only *pure corporeality* remains, as the *absolutely spiritless*. So proceeding with regard to the whole experiential world, we gain, as a *universal and obviously purely-self-enclosed core-structure* of the world, *nature in the pregnant sense* — *physical nature*. It is built exclusively out of corporeal realities, from whose objective sense our abstraction has excluded all the psychic and likewise all cultural significance; each purely grasped as a *unity of physical states and pure physical causality*, while all together they are interwoven in the universal unity of a *purely physical-causal nexus*. Here is the realm of *originarily spatiotemporally extended*. Not without reason Descartes defined the physical thing as *res extensa*, even if he later got into deviations.

And here the spatial and temporal place — that is, space and time as *place-system*, as the *individuating* form, or the form of all physical individual realities — is to be distinguished from the *what* concretely localizing itself within it, the thingly *what*, the *qualitative*. The qualitative, as identically repeatable, is a *general* down to its last specific differences. Only in *localization* does it become the *individual one-time this*. Note well: we do not thereby say that pure *physis* exists or can exist for itself, as if a world as pure nature were thinkable. We here need take no position on this; we claim only to have carried out an *abstraction* by which an own and specifically-to-be-researched nexus — as a universal, even if perhaps abstractly non-independent, *world-structure as core-structure* — emerges. Just as we can stay experiencingly in the *physis*\-layer (noticing, unveiling, confirming, holding our gaze far from all that is spiritual), so we can also stay similarly in research itself in this layer.

### **§ 18\. Juxtaposition of the Subjective and the Objective in the Attitude of the Natural Scientist**

---

The natural scientist, in carrying out his abstraction of *pure physis*, methodically excludes everything *merely subjective*. But what does this exclusion look like, and what does the *subjective* mean in this context? Husserl now opens up the meaning of *subjective* in the natural-scientific attitude.

The *experienced* qualities of things — their colors, sounds, warmth or coolness, smells, tastes — are precisely *subjective*: relative to the perceiving subject, to its position, lighting, mood, sense-organs. The natural scientist sets these aside as belonging to the *appearance of the thing for a subject*, not to the thing *as it is in itself*. The "real" nature is the nature *abstracted* from these subjective qualities — the geometrical-mathematical residue.

This abstraction is the foundation of modern mathematical natural science. It is the methodological correlate of the conviction that what is *objectively true* about nature is what is *invariant* across the changing subjective appearances. The objective is what *all* possible perceivers, suitably positioned, would agree upon; the subjective is what varies with the perceiver.

Husserl emphasizes that this distinction *presupposes a particular pre-given experiential world*: a world in which experiencing subjects perceive things and can communicate their perceptions; a world in which subjective experiences can be *corrected*, *confirmed*, *contested* by other subjective experiences. The natural-scientific abstraction is *not* a starting point — it is a *secondary operation* performed on the pre-given experiential world.

Consequently, the *objective* nature of natural science is the *purified residue* obtained by abstracting from the subjective. But the *subjective* that is so abstracted is *itself* an experienceable structure of the world. And as such, *it too* admits of scientific research — but a research *of a different kind* than that of natural science. Phenomenological psychology will be precisely the science that takes the *subjective itself* — the realm of perceiving, appearing, experiencing — as its theme.

The natural scientist *uses* subjective experiences (his own perceptions, instrument readings) as *methodological steps* but does not make them *his theme*. The psychologist who would understand psychic life cannot follow this method — for to him, the *subjective* itself, the experiencing, is the very topic.

### **§ 19\. The True World in Itself a Necessary Presumption**

---

Although what is given to us in experience as being-actuality always leaves open possibilities of *correction*, and although in fact the *surrender* of an experienced actuality is often necessary, in natural life we are nevertheless dominated by the *certainty* that despite all this an *objective, finally true* world *is* — *the* true world, as we simply say. This counts for us as an *unquestioned self-evidence*. *In this lies, more precisely seen, a presumption that reaches beyond every actual experience.* (Husserl adds in a footnote: better — in natural life this is the *practical certainty* of a *practical truth*; the *presumption* belongs to the scientist, the *truth-in-itself*.)

For it lies in the fact that the unanimity establishing itself in our whole experience will not, in infinitum and in all presenting actualities, turn out to be *illusion* — and *can not*; and that endless experience cannot keep advancing forever and everywhere in the form of *corrections*. Rather, however many corrections may be needed in the particular, certain is for us that this *via dolorosa* of corrections at least leads, in progressive *approximation*, to the knowledge of a *no-longer-correctable* actuality. There *must* be a *definitive*, true actuality — the *world in its actual truth* — that establishes itself in the endless process of experience, or at least establishes itself ever-more purely, ever-more perfectly. As definitively true, it must then be *theoretically determinable*. Every break in the certainty of experienced actuality, every transformation into discord, doubt, into invalid illusion, all corrections — these count for us as *mere bridges of knowledge* into the *realm of definitive actuality*. Behind all these doubts and illusions lies the *only subjectively hidden and veiled, in itself unquestionable and indelible true actuality*.

This presumption — belonging to the natural style of universal experience — in its never-questioned certainty dominates also the *objective sciences*. To work out the *true actuality* through observation, experiment, and theoretical thinking — that is, to project *definitively true predications and theories for it* — is their *goal*. The objective natural sciences, especially, take their scientific striving for *pure objectivity* as a *struggle against the merely subjective* of an experienced and yet merely supposed truth, against all "merely subjective" illusion, against all merely subjectively experienced disputes, doubts, merely preliminary likelihoods.

Discord, illusion, etc., are in fact *subjective occurrences*: they occur in the *consciousness-domain* of experiencing subjects, both singly and in working communities. Likewise, of course, the *unanimity* itself — the *experienced actuality itself* — as it is conscious in the experiencer as certainty of these or those appearance-unities. Naturally the natural scientist has to do also with all this subjective. But, as before: *the subjective is not his theme*. It is not his business to research descriptively how unanimity, discord, illusion etc. *look* and what they are theoretically determined as such. His theme is rather *straight-on* experienced nature itself in its true actuality; while the traversal through illusion, doubt, etc., is the *operated method* that runs in this subjective and yet does not *itself* have it as theme. So it is to be distinguished at all sciences: when the mathematician speaks of counting, calculating, of his designating, writing-down, mathematics is not for that reason a science of such subjective doings but of magnitudes, numbers, etc.

Still one thing useful to note. *Experiencing simpliciter* — that is, experiencing in normal attitude — is to *live unreflectedly* in the carrying out of continuously continuing experiential synthesis, or in syntheses that connect discretely broken-off experiences to newly setting-in ones. I say "unreflected" because it is a *change of attitude* if the experiencer turns back his gaze from the experienced object to the *experiencing* and the *experiencing subject*; if he does this in the unity of a synthetic regress, he finds on the one side the object (and possibly so the whole world) as the straight-on unanimously experienced and continuously experienceable, and finds on the other side at the same time that this object — unnoticed — was in his experiential field as conscious, and afterward, in particular manner, conscious enters his field of noticing. Here *two actualities* are for the reflector related to one another and both given in the unity of one validity: the *objective, worldly* actuality and the actuality of *experiencing* as "the having or gaining the objective in its subjective field."

But take the case of an *illusion*: in the straight gaze direction — in which the experiencer is given over to the objective and *is not aware of himself and of his experiencing* — at first something is experienced as actuality which turns out afterwards to be invalid illusion; thus instead of a *being* a *non-being*. This illusion shows itself as embedded in the rest of the experiential environment whose actuality is not contested. It, in its sustained certainty, is what *vetoes* against the illusion and assigns it nullity. So in the straight gaze direction. But now if a *turning back* of the gaze upon the *subjective experiencing* takes place, then on this side again *something actual* is given: namely the actual subject and his actual *grasping* of a real, and his actual *crossing out* of this real as invalid illusion. Now the illusion gives itself, instead of as objectively being, as a *subjective occurrence in the consciousness-field of the actual subject*, and *in relation* to the rest of the not-contested experiential environment as the *subject* that *falsifies the environment at that objective place* — a subject that first has its kind of validity in the *form of crossed-out-ness or nullity*.

The care with which we trace the *subjective* that the natural scientist methodically excludes — in order to gain the *pure nature* freed from all and any *merely subjective* — has for us a *twofold purpose*. On the one hand, to bring the Idea of *the natural scientist's nature* to clarity and recognize it as a *mere abstraction-product*; on the other hand, gradually to oversee the *whole subjective* in all its experiential configurations. Only so can we gain an originary understanding of what a *universal science of the subjective* can intend, and which mutually interconnected — yet again to be distinguished — disciplines come into question.

---

### **§ 20\. Objectivity Is Demonstrable in Intersubjective Unanimity. Normality and Anomaly**

---

That something is objectively the case — that this thing has this true property, that this is a real cultural sense, that this event actually occurred — is demonstrable in *intersubjective unanimity*. My individual perception alone proves nothing about the objective; only when *other* perceivers, suitably positioned, confirm or modify my perception, and when in iterated mutual exchange a *stable unanimity* among us establishes itself, do we have *objectivity*. Objectivity, then, is not a property of single perceptions but the *intersubjective validity* of perceptions across the community of perceivers.

Yet not every perceiver is equally a witness to the objective. Some perceivers are *anomalous* — color-blind, intoxicated, mentally disturbed, infant, dreaming, hallucinating. Their experiences cannot serve as direct evidence of the objective. *Normality* of the perceiver is therefore a *normative* condition on the role he plays in establishing objective truth. The structure of objectivity thus rests on a community of *normal* perceivers — a community that, on the basis of unanimous experience, demonstrates the objective character of the world.

The distinction between normality and anomaly is itself rooted in the experiential world. We *experience* certain ways of perceiving as normal — sober, fully awake, well-functioning sense-organs — and others as anomalous. The normal way of perceiving is one that produces *unanimity* with the community of co-perceivers; the anomalous way is one that *fails* to fit into intersubjective unanimity. *Anomalous experience is integrated into the normal world precisely as anomalous* — as a *case* to be corrected or set aside.

The presumption of an *objective world in itself*, as discussed in §19, is therefore the presumption of a world demonstrable in intersubjective unanimity *across normal perceivers*. The natural scientist's *objectivity* is the limit-form of this presumption: in physics, the objectivity is *all that any normal perceiver under any normal conditions could be brought to confirm*. The structure of objectivity is here decisively *intersubjective*, normative, and presumptive.

---

### **§ 21\. Stratified Structure of the Psychic**

---

Returning now from the side of the objective world to the *psychic* itself, we find that it too possesses a *stratified structure* (Stufenbau). The psychic is not a homogeneous mass but is articulated into layers — of different elementary types and different degrees of complexity — that build upon one another in a definite order.

At the lowest layer are the *passive* moments: the *hyletic* data — sensory contents (colors, sounds, kinesthetic sensations) — and the *passive syntheses* that unify them into unities of object-appearance. These passive layers run *prior to* and *underlying* all active accomplishments of consciousness. Without them, no object would be given for thinking, valuing, willing.

Above this comes the layer of *objectivating intentionality*: the perceiving, imagining, remembering, expecting consciousness, in which something is given as *something*. This layer is the structural pivot of all conscious life — *consciousness of something* as the fundamental form.

Above this come the *higher* layers of *valuing*, *desiring*, *willing*, *acting* — the *axiological* and *practical* spheres. These build upon the objectivating layer: I cannot value or desire or will except in relation to *what* is given to me as object. Thus valuing and willing presuppose objectivating consciousness.

The stratified structure is essential to the psychic. *Any* psychic life — however different in concrete content from human life — must exhibit this layering. It is part of the eidetic structure of the psyche as such that the higher layers presuppose the lower. *Phenomenological psychology must investigate this layering systematically* — both the eidetic necessities governing it and the concrete configurations it takes in actual psychic life.

---

### **§ 22\. Concept of Physical Reality as Enduring Substance of Causal Determinations**

---

Husserl returns to the concept of *physical reality* — but now with the question whether the concepts of *causality* and *reality*, in the broadened sphere (psychic, psycho-physical), do not undergo *differentiation*. The question is sharply posed: when we move from pure physical realities to animate ones, does the concept of *reality* still mean the same thing? Husserl will argue that it differentiates significantly.

Recall the most general point already made: *causality* means *experiential dependency of individual alterations* — that is, alterations of individual objects whose mutual modifications are regulated. And this dependency is sense-determining: it lies in the experiential content of the relevant objects themselves. In the realm of the *res extensae* — the abstractively simplified spatio-temporal world of pure natural science — every such *res* has an *enduring being*: its fixed property-character, despite its constant alterability in a constantly altering thingly environment.

All alterations of a thing hold, in their relation to the alterations of surrounding things, to a *fixed alteration-style*. And indeed, *in the sense of experience as thingly experience*, this mutual attunement of things in their alterations lies from the start: this more-or-less determinate *foreseeing* how things will behave under given circumstances; but also the *presumption* that one can investigate and become more closely acquainted with this *own kind* of the thing.

Grasping objectively and conceptually what lies everywhere in the experiential sense, we say: *every thing as reality has its fixed causal properties*, each related to its generally possible thingly environments. Each such property is an *index* of a fixed *causal regulation* in the dependency of alterations. We transfer this self-evidently — and earlier did transfer it — to *animate* and *human* realities. They are objectively knowable experiential unities in the spatial world and are incorporated into this world through their *physical bodies*, which considered for themselves are already realities.

But these bodies have in *animation* not merely a psychic *hovering* above them, but a psychic *interwoven, connected* with them — not externally attached but interwoven in the special manner of *doubly-unitary realities*: as animals, as humans. The *psychophysical causality* is real causality, and the unity is real-causal unity — but, as we shall see, this real-causal unity differentiates the concept of *reality* from the pure physical case.

---

### **§ 23\. Physical Causality as Inductive. The Peculiarity of Psychic Interweavedness**

---

Husserl now focuses on a crucial point: *physical* causality is *inductive*. That is, the lawful regularities by which physical things behave are discovered and known through *induction* from observed cases. The physical law is not directly intuited but is *inferred* from empirical correlations of the type "whenever this configuration of conditions, then this kind of behavior."

This inductive character of physical causality is *characteristic* of pure physical nature. The objects of physics are objects whose connections with one another can be known only through observation, experiment, and inductive law-formulation. There is no insight into *why*, from the inner essence of the physical thing, this specific lawful behavior must follow; we know only *that*, empirically, it does.

What now of *psychic* interweavings? Are they likewise to be conceived inductively? Husserl argues that *psychic interweaving has a fundamentally different character*. The interwovenness of psychic states with one another is not, in the first place, an inductively-discovered regularity, but an *intrinsic* unity given in inner experience itself. The succession of my thoughts, feelings, perceivings is not for me an inductive regularity but an *intrinsically motivated* unity — *motivation* in the sense of the spirit-realm's proper kind of "causality."

Husserl warns against a dangerous error that has played a fateful role in modern thought. The fact that each of the two reality-layers inductively connected to the unity of a spatio-objective reality — the physical-corporeal and the psychic — for itself considered has the style of an inductive unity, and even of a certain *parallel structural form* (which alone makes a total unity of parallelistic body-soul construction possible at all): this *by no means* means that what here has come to the real unity of an animate being under the titles *corporeal body and soul* is *really pervasively parallel* — really of pervasively parallel structure of being. More precisely: the physical thing in which a soul *embodies itself* as corporeal body is, as physical thing, nothing other than a unity from inductive belonging-together — for itself, and in its connection in universal physical nature, a *merely real unity from inductive causality*. *In no way* does the *parallelism* of the corresponding psychic mean that the soul too is a *merely real unity from inductive causality*.

Such a view can be held only if, instead of immersing oneself in the proper essence of psychic being itself, one allows oneself — as psychology in its first beginnings did, with Descartes and Hobbes — to be led by the *prejudice* that the new natural science is the prototype of every genuine objective science, and that therefore every objective science must have the same style and, correlatively, every objective being (every objectively spatial reality) must have exactly the properties of the physical reality. This conception determined the fateful *parallelistic doctrine of Spinoza*: according to it, physical world and psychic world would be two exactly parallel worlds of *absolutely identical structural form, being-form and law-form*, built only out of fundamentally different material.

This is, however, looked at closely, *downright absurd*. Not just because the souls do not have an own-soulful space with its own *soulful geometry* — that already would suffice — but also because, according to this conception, it would equally well have to be said that bodies *spatialize themselves* in the parallel soulful space, must present themselves in the souls through expression, etc., just as souls embody themselves in physical space and physically corporealize and express themselves. All of this is absurd, just as is the whole metaphysical ontology of Spinoza that still lies behind it.

We will be able to understand the *true* relations more deeply when we are further along, simply because we do *not* construct metaphysically but *do nothing further than* unveil the sense enclosed in the natural world-experience itself of its experiential objectivities, including the souls there appearing as animation. What must count for us as the *decisive* point — and was already sharply characterized in advance — was: that the soul must, to be sure, *also* have an *inductive* structure in experience, but that it *is nothing less than* — and *cannot be* — a unity that is, in itself, purely inductively-causally constructed.

### **§ 24\. The Unity of the Psychic**

---

(Husserl begins a new lecture. The §-title points to Beilagen XI and XII.) On the basis of the foregoing, we can grant psychological naturalism only this much, and only this much is a priori certain: in a manner of consideration of the animal that starts from its *pure physical embodiment*, from its physical analogy and physico-chemical explanation, and that thereby naturally lives in the purely *inductive* — and that then ascends to the consideration of the psychic and psycho-physical — there must be possible a rich field of inductive investigations and the bringing-out of *inductive properties* of the spiritual being. Only, one must not become blind to this: that *at the higher level the inductive-causal does not make up the real essence of spirituality*, indeed that inductive research itself must press beyond the merely inductively graspable. For without *description of the psychic in itself* it does not get far.

But to take seriously the description of the spiritual in its proper essentiality, then one must soon become aware that the psychic — leaving aside what gives it unity through its connection to a physical embodiment, namely as its *unitary animation* — is a *self-enclosed unity*, standing under *totally distinctive essential concepts*.

Let us reflect: every soul is the *unity of a streaming psychic life*; and the streaming therein as psychic state or *act* stands under wholly distinctive principles of this *stream-unity*. To this belongs already the *wondrous temporal structure* of the streaming alteration in the forms "ever-new now" with the streaming-form "just past" and on the other hand the anticipation-form "just coming."

But the soul is not only streaming life, but a life in which *peculiar new unities*, *habitualities*, are unfailingly constituted — namely the *passive* and *active capacities*: capacities of perception, of feeling, of memory, of intellect, etc. With these constantly changeable capacities we are already referred to the unity, essentially belonging to each soul, of an *identical I as substrate for specifically I-relative capacity-properties* — for changeable, in fact never-resting, possibly through breaks totally altering *personal properties*, including the pregnant so-called *character-properties*; through all this running the unity of the *spiritual individuality* that endures through character-alterations.

Mere indication of such peculiarities and deeper immersion in their sense leads to the recognition that in pure regard to the psychic in its proper essentiality, *new-kind principles of unification* of the manifold appear. The concepts: *real flowing state* (here away-streaming experience), *real properties*, *real process*, *enduring reality in the causality of real alterations* — these are concepts that here gain a *totally new-kind sense*, and one that at first has *nothing to do with* empirical-inductive unity-formation. In particular, then, *psychic and specifically personal causality as causality of spiritual motivation* is *totally different* from inductive causality.

To be sure, as we said from the outset, the inductive also plays its role in the psychic sphere. And it is what first makes possible the *embodiment* of the psychic sphere, its *embodiment in the unity of the inductive spatial world*. Since psychic lived-experiences are in their way also temporally formed — namely, are experiential data running in the time-like unity of a stream — and have here their regularities of coexistence and succession, they naturally also form a field of empirically associative unification, a field of expectational, inductive nexuses. But this inductive causality need not first establish the in-its-way quite external expectational unity. What unifies itself here inductively is already *inwardly previously* united from peculiar *essential principles*, and has its inner manners of producing ever-new and higher *synthetic unity*. The *externality* of the inductive — I mean the inductive linkage of purely psychic contents (so an unspatially understood externality) — appears here as something *secondary in inwardness*.

Indeed, perhaps it will later turn out that *all* externality, including that of the whole inductive nature, the physical and psycho-physical, is *only an externality that constitutes itself in the unity of communicative personal experience* — and so is only a *secondary*, requiring *reduction* to a *truly essential inwardness*. But to speak of that is not the place here. Husserl is here hinting at the move that will be made in the closing §§ of the systematic part: the move from psychological to transcendental.

Our consideration is of significance insofar as it removes a *historically intelligible natural-scientific prejudice* that dominates the recent psychology, led by the development of natural science. The methodical thinking-habits of the natural sciences transfer themselves into psychology; what in the former was *appropriate method* had finally to become a *damaging hindrance* here. Quite one-sidedly, one tried again and again to proceed exclusively in natural-scientific fashion and to reduce all reality-research to inductive research. *Inductive science* and *empirical factual science* counted, and still count for many, as equivalent expressions. With this is connected the unclear — indeed as a rule, in principle false — *transfer of the Idea of natural science* onto the science of spiritual beings and the science of the soul itself.

### **§ 25\. The Idea of a Universal Natural Science. Dangers of the Naturalistic Prejudice**

---

(Husserl notes Beilage XIII.) There is a *legitimate* extension of the Idea of natural science — namely the one that conceives it as the *universal science of all and every spatio-worldly reality*, in its all-spanning unity as a single world that connects all realities *real-causally* in the frame of the universal spatio-temporal world-form. In universal experience, and in the universal presumption proper to it, *one* world is posited as ever-enduring and ever-validly being-actuality, in unified spatio-temporal form and spanned by a unified causality — such that *all* the singulars of this spatial world are unities of *fixed causality*.

Then *nature*, in extension, expresses the full *natural external world*, whose mere *structure* is physical nature. The extension then already indicates the natural progress-direction of research from physical nature to the *concrete spatial world*. Whether this progress-direction is in fact the only possible — and so necessary — to make personal subjectivity into theme, we shall still have to consider seriously. But here, in this starting point and habituation, lies a *compulsion* to conceive the concept of natural science such that — on the ground of physical natural science and proceeding beyond its inductive-causal sphere of being — it researches *all* the with-it inductively-real-interwoven being in *all* inductive-causal peculiarities.

Here just inserts itself the *prejudice* that the whole spatial world is in fact, in the same sense, *nature* as is the physical nature; and closely connected with this is *sensualism* in the interpretation of psychic life and the interpretation of psychic life as an *own realm of elements* that, under exclusively inductive regulation of their alterations, form a *psychic-real nexus* which in turn with physical nature (or physical embodiment) is once more interwoven into a real-causal unity *in the manner of merely inductive natural causality*.

We do not deny that here many things — even many things very closely connected with physical nature — are to be purely inductively researched. This is shown by *physiology*, which is in large part *psycho-physical somatology*. There we in fact grasp a closed *natural side* of the *somatic soul*, insofar as throughout inductive nexuses connect this *somatological-psychic* of sensation and feeling-sensoriness with physical embodiment. *But the concrete and full world does not have the mere style of a nature.*

*The naturalistic prejudice must fall.* Only from this prejudice springs that *mechanizing determinism of the natural world* that makes the world into a *fundamentally unintelligible and senselessly running machine*. A prejudice that also makes the naturalist *blind* to the proper accomplishment of *all the historical* and *generalizing* sciences of *personal spirituality and culture*.

---

### **§ 26\. The Subjective in the World as Objective Theme**

---

(Husserl begins a new lecture.) What we have just laid out in anticipatory traits and in principled leading-doctrines will gain its grounding content and concrete clarification when we direct our gaze on the *subjective occurrences* — not yet considered under the aspect of a *Geisteswissenschaft* — that are excluded in the natural-scientific methodical access to nature.

I must call attention to this: the method of excluding the *merely subjective*, *mutatis mutandis*, proves itself once again necessary if we want to investigate the animal-human world, beyond physical nature, in pure objectivity — even if as a *nature in the extended sense*, that is, in the one-sided direction of interest on the *inductive*.

A priori it is evident that *whatever* is objectively given to us — and is to be capable of being so given — *must* give itself in *subjectively changing experiences in changing subjective modes*. Possibly the talk of *objectivity* and its *knowledge* has good sense also where a *spatial world* is not in question. Even then the general would hold. In any case it holds of everything in the *naturally given experiential world* as *objectively experienceable*, and so also of humans and animals — and not merely with regard to their physical embodiment.

A human can be given in many modes through experience, in differences of orientation and of perspectival modes of appearance. Taking these words in the usual sense, they apply exclusively to the *physical embodiment* of the human, and to him *insofar as he has embodiment*. But also his *psychic side* has, with different content, such differences — both with regard to the *somatological* and with regard to the *higher* and so the whole psychic. Thus *my body* is for me differently experienced and experienceable than for anyone else; *objectively* it is the same, and knowable as the same against such subjective modes of givenness. Likewise as with the specifically psycho-physical (Leib-Seelisches), so also with the *higher psychic*, given in changing *modes of appearance* — according as it is originally given by me as mine, or to another as foreign.

Wherever the Idea of objective knowledge is in play — even of the psyche and whatever otherwise stands under the title of the subjective is to become theme of objective knowledge — a methodical *distinction* and *separation* must be made between the respective *objective itself* and the *manifold "merely subjective"* belonging to it but not entering into it itself. This must, where any danger of intrusion into the objective content threatens, be *methodically consciously thematically excluded*.

For physical natural science, no particular difficulties attach to this exclusion — precisely because it excludes all and every subjective (already what embodies itself as *soul*). But difficulties arise as soon as some kind of subjective or subject-laden becomes the *objective theme*, and especially when the theme is in generality not only universal physical nature but the *whole concrete spatio-temporal world*, with animals and humans, and further with human communities and their cultures. (Husserl adds a footnote: with a "pure psychology" we would have the reverse situation to that of natural science: all the "objectively physical" would have to be excluded. But isn't *that* still in the psychic, in the *intersubjectively psychic nexus of corroboration as Idea*?)

Here arise difficulties: the merely-subjective to be excluded in each case — all the subjective modes of orientation, the perspectival changes of appearance, modes of doubt, nullity, correction, etc. — are *not foreign to psychic life*. They necessarily belong to psychic or personal spheres, namely those in which they appear. They do not so simply fall *out* of the thematic sphere as they do from the *in-principle subject-foreign* thematic sphere of natural science.

All the subjective modes that the physical natural scientist excludes are nonetheless *his* (and his human and animal fellows') *appearances* — appearances *of* or *at* the objective, appearing in *his* or others' psychic sphere. Within the *physis* they are nowhere; but they nevertheless *are*, and are in the realm of animal or human *consciousness*. Whoever has them can in *reflective turning of gaze* find them in himself and possibly describe them in their changing descriptively-graspable peculiarity.

If we want to be not merely physicists but *soul-researchers*, we cannot naturally avoid drawing all such *subjective* into our theme — and not only the subjective that immediately belongs to the *mode of givenness of the physical*, but also that *other* subjective, including what we ourselves as psychologists *exclude* in order to obtain a human being in *pure objectivity*. To be sure, if I have as a particular theme the soul of an ape with which I am just experimenting as animal psychologist, then every subjective apprehension-mode by which it is given to me belongs to *my* psychic sphere and not into the sphere of *this ape-soul*. But if I have a universal psychology in view — and a *universal world-consideration* into which all particular consideration and particular science of objective fit themselves — then I cannot let any of such subjective entirely fall.

Every subjective belongs in *some* psychic sphere, and possibly in the psychic spheres of many at the same time. So a psychology can be possible that does not co-treat *all and every* subjective; and is it not foreseeable that this must hold for all conceivable groups of subjective that can be made visible to us in the experiential world? But this is only a guiding line, to enter into the *proper, deeper* difficulties.

Reflecting on the perspectival appearances and the sensuous illusions: *In plainly outward-directed experiencing we know nothing of subjective modes of appearance*. The things stand for us simply as bodily actualities — incompletely, to be sure; in the progress of experience ever-new marks of them enter into the circle of bodily actual grasping. All the subjectively significant words used here ("the things are there *for us*, in the progress of *our* experience," etc.) only *indirectly indicate* what is experienced. In the *plain* thing-experiencing itself, all subjective *as such* is missing. *Our whole subjectivity remains, so to speak, anonymous for itself.* It loses this anonymity through *reflection* — through a possibly manifold turning-aside of the gaze from the experienced thing and its thingly determinations onto the subjective modes-of-appearance of the same, and then possibly onto myself, onto the I that finds this subjective there: onto the I that is in general engaged there, e.g., as experiencing this thing, considering it more closely, relating it to other things, valuing it, desiring its enjoyment, etc.

---

### **§ 27\. The Difficulty That the Objective World Is Constituted Through Exclusion of the Subjective, But That All the Subjective Itself Belongs to the World**

---

(Husserl begins a new lecture.) We were in the last lecture caught in a *transitional consideration*. A turn is preparing itself. Within the *natural experiential world* we all live; on it all our doing and suffering relate; on it relates every thematic attitude in which we set up some objectivity, some domain of intersubjectively-unanimously experiential givennesses, to busy ourselves in theoretical work — and finally to create sciences of whose intersubjectively corroborable truth we can convince ourselves. The world-universe itself spans all such objectivities — all and every that is intersubjectively unanimously experienceable and knowable as actuality. Each is, however, *subjectively changingly given* for each of us. This subjective changing is *excluded* in order to experience-out and determine the objective identical and true.

This already brings with it, quite generally, a difficulty I should have emphasized in advance in the last lecture in this generality. Namely: if the world-universe, as just said, spans *all and every* that *is* in *objective truth*, then how does it stand with the *set-out-of-play subjective* of the manifold subjects? The world-universe must indeed contain these as belonging objects within itself. *The subjects and all their subjective we can experience-out and determine as objective under exclusion of these or those subjective modes of givenness.* The objective world-universe — is it anything other than *the all of what is brought to unanimity of experience in together-experiencing subjects*, standing under the presumption (and an empirically self-corroborating one) common to us, that it will, possibly with needed corrections, let itself ever be experienced-out as a fitting unity (and further then theoretically unanimously be knowable)? *So in a milieu of the subjective the world-sphere seems to swim, indeed itself to be in it — and to be possible only as a subject-related and self-constituting-in-the-connected-subjects unity-formation.*

On the other side, do we not have an *objective* science of the subjective? And is not all the subjective — including the respective acts of experiencing — itself an objective theme? The paradox is now sharply on the table: *the objective world is "constituted" by excluding the subjective, yet the subjective itself belongs to the world.* This is the structural difficulty that drives the move from psychology toward phenomenological reduction: only by a *radical change of attitude* — a *bracketing* of the natural positing — can we treat the *subjective* without falling into the loop of subjectivity and objectivity in the natural attitude.

Phenomenological psychology, then, faces a methodological turn: to treat the subjective as such, we must not merely thematize it within the natural attitude (where it is always *part* of the objective world); we must execute a *reflective turning* that frees the subjective from its incorporation in the objective world and lets us study it in its own structures. This turning will be carried out in §§28 ff.

---

### **§ 28\. Carrying Out the Reflective Turn of the Gaze onto the Subjective. The Perception of Physical Things in the Reflective Attitude**

---

Husserl now executes the long-prepared methodical turn. We tie onto the *spatial-worldly experience* with which every natural scientist begins — both in the narrow sense (the physicist concerned with mere physical nature) and in the wider sense (the researcher of all spatio-temporally-causally interwoven realities). Here the progress-direction from narrow to extended nature is sketched: all the spiritual as real in the all-nature leads back to *singular souls as animations of physical bodies*. Reality-research must accordingly be founded in physical research. So let us begin as physicists (in the widest word-sense). How the physicist gains his theme *nature* by an access-method of excluding the merely subjective in order to have nature fully consciously as *pure* nature was discussed. Obviously *straight-on* experiencing precedes — the *reflectionless* experiencing — and above all the straight-on *perceiving* of the *res extensae*. The reflective doing has no function other than to *habitually fix the gaze* on what already stands before our eyes in the straight experiential direction, the *purely extensive*.

First it comes down to *one's own* perceiving, and only afterwards to *communication* with others. The experience and research is at first carried out in the *first form of immediacy*; the communicative research presupposes this immediate one. We act as if no other co-experiencer were in our field. The purely-on-objective-nature-directed thus, perceiving straight-on, grasps the natural *directly as itself* in its natural properties and relations. There are at first no motives to distinguish between merely *specific* sensory qualities and *truly* objective ones. We do not need here to consider this distinction, as it appears only at a higher level.

In any case, if we straight-on noticingly experience and purely look at the *being-in-space* and *so-being*, then everything that comes to grasp here presents itself as *spatio-thingly*: shape as *the shape of the thing*, qualities belonging to it in motion and rest, in alteration and non-alteration; color likewise as spreading over the spatial shape and qualifying the thing. *Nothing whatsoever of the subjective falls into our spiritual field of view* — none of the subjective of modes-of-givenness that we earlier spoke of, which without doubt in a wealth of differences affects this pure perceptual thing and its pure intuitive objectivity.

(Husserl begins a new lecture.) In our earlier lectures, in the context of our general reflections on the structural properties of the experiential world and especially on the *access-methods* by which the natural scientist abstracts his pure nature, we had several times touched on the same themes that have now begun to occupy us. This should not confuse you. Note the *new viewpoint* of our investigations. Now — after we had circumscribed the Idea of natural science from various reflections and feelings-about on objectivity and subjectivity, and after the task arose for us, over against it, to make *the in each case excluded-by-the-natural-scientific-attitude subjective* into theme — it is no longer a matter of merely indicatively speaking of the subjective as a *means* by which to make a different main theme (say physical nature) recognizable in its purity. Rather, now this *subjective itself* is to be the theme, and the tasks of *strictly scientific* treatment of it are in question.

The *new starting point* is this: we are no longer *in* nature and natural science, but take, so to speak, a *gaze-direction that turns away* from it. We do this by turning our *theoretical interest* (and turning it in pure isolation) to what natural science, in its lower and fundamental level of physical natural research, has *excluded as merely subjective*: as not belonging to its aim or damaging to it. What sort of self-enclosed domain — what sort of corresponding universal science — will accrue to us, we do not yet know; nor how it may stand to natural science. *That* subjectivity as such embodies itself into nature, we know from outer-worldly experiential consideration. But how that subjective, which we take up as the *discarded refuse* of the natural scientist into theoretical interest, may stand to the *psychic* — to that which has embodiment in nature — can be known to us nothing in our present attitude. *Where our research leads* (how we will be carried from our first directly-grasped subjective to ever-new subjective, and how the circle closes and what its counter-circles may have) must first show itself.

Nonetheless, the *first grasps* of the last lecture — however much they take up themes already otherwise touched — are intended as the *beginning of a serious, systematically progressing science* that establishes itself as *independent*. It begins, in a kind of marking-off from natural-scientific research. Natural science begins with the purely natural experience and proceeds to description of real-causal determination of the natural. *We* begin — without any interest in the natural-scientific *results*, and so also without using the slightest of these — by *stepping back* to consider what *lies before* the natural-scientific beginning. Or: what gives the natural scientist, at any time, his beginnings — gives him his nature-things, *subjectively*.

So we said: his first is *perceiving*. And what lies in his *own* perceiving (apart from the communicative co-experiencing with his fellows) can be set apart. As first theme of scientific subjectivity-research arose simply: "*I perceive spatio-thingly*"; *I have therein my perceived*, singular and many, a perceptually-filled space with things, etc. Our gaze-direction is not for now on the *I and the I's activity*, but on the *perceived*, namely according to the *subjective how*, exactly as it gives itself in perception. It is not a matter of *contingent* subjective occurrences but generally of the *subjective* that is to be exhibited *everywhere and necessarily*, wherever original perceptual experience takes place.

From the straight-on perceived thing — which we had not detailed-described — we went *directly* to the possible reflective turnings of the gaze and to the subjective they revealed. From the ongoing-perceived thing and what is describable in it as its properties, were distinguished, in different senses, the *manifold of modes-of-appearance* of it, its *aspects*: the constant change of its modes-of-appearance in ever-new *sides*. Then in a new reflection-direction, but inseparably connected with the foregoing, the *manifold modes-of-appearance of orientation*: the whole perceptual space, as each individual thing and each thing-group, is given only *oriented* — as right and left, in front and behind, etc. All orientation is referred to a *zero-point of orientation*, a *zero-thing*, which is my *own body* (as perceiver).

And yet another novelty: the *perspectival mode of givenness* of each perceptual thing and each of its perceptual determinations, but also of the whole unified perceptual field, the spatial total perception. The differences of perspective hang inseparably together with the subjective differences of orientation and of side-givenness. Suppose the orientation changes: we then say (going somewhat beyond our attitude) that we bodily perform a locomotion in space, or stand still and the things move — or both. But whatever experiential ground these talkings may have, attending purely to the *subjective appearances* of the subjective relations of right and left, in front of me or behind me, etc., it is clear that the things show themselves from changing sides, and that the *perspectives* alter. How very differently a cube-face appears — and ever differently again — when the cube rotates in oriented space. To rotate *in oriented space* is the *properly phenomenal rotation*, which by no means signifies, already in experience, an *objective* rotation. Objectively, perhaps, I see an unmoved cube *just when* it appears in the rotating change of orientation (i.e., when I go around it or know myself bodily pushed or driven around it).

This subjective rotation as orientation-phenomenon brings with it a great and continuous *change of aspects* of the cube-face. These are *perspectival modifications*. And paying attention to the *color*, it has, in functional dependence on this, its own *color-perspectives* in corresponding change. The face as straight-on-perceived gives itself, say, in the objective coloring that I describe as evenly red. But if I attend to the parallel-with-the-change-of-orientation modification-of-givenness of the coloring, I have analogously to the case of shape ever-new *shape-shadings* — ever-new *color-shadings*. The same uniform red appears with each subjective rotation as *ever otherwise being*. In the perspectival presentation, I have, as *perspectival red*, by no means a uniform red but a more-or-less *uneven* red, gradating in certain ways.

It is to be noted carefully what stands distinguished in the change of attitude: as the *identical spatial object* and the *manifold of perspectival modes of appearance*. It must further be attended to that *both* — *manifolds in their continuous change* and the *appearing-in-them, presenting-in-them unity* — are inseparably *one*; that they belong to perception, even when the perceiver, lost in his object, *notices nothing* of the manifold (i.e., performs no reflection). However easy it is to begin here and to execute and distinguish such gaze-turnings, *one must first learn to see in the right way* — in order to see, in all essential moments, what lies in the various directions and yet has the *unity-relation to the same objective*. It is necessary at no point to go *beyond* the perceived as such, but to describe it purely as such — purely in its *subjective how* of givenness; on the other hand, to overlook nothing constitutive for it.

Here we must establish, and for all time keep in mind, that the *objective shape*, the objective extension, presents itself *perspectivally* in *shading-shapes*, in a correlative *shading-fashion co-extensive* — but such that these shading-shapes are *not themselves shapes in space*. They have an extending — their *kind of extension* — but the *spreading of perspectives* of spatial things is *not* a spreading in space. Naming what presents itself *the objective* of the perception (though in other contexts it can rightly again be called subjective), then in relation to it its *subjective shadings* are not in the same space; not even in a second, picture-like space. The perspectives belonging to perception itself are *not silhouettes of the object on a tablet*. A tablet is itself objective in space and is itself perceivable only by subjective perspectives. So even its drawn-in silhouettes are perceivable only by perspectives that are not themselves silhouettes. Silhouettes are *objective* images *of* the objective. By contrast, *perspectives of an objective-spatial present this subjectively, un-spatially*.

The manifold of subjective perspectives *presents* one and the same spatial thing — the same objective shape with similar qualifications. Every division in the perspective itself, in its own spreading, is itself again *presenting* for a corresponding part of the objective shape. And conversely, every part has its endless manifold of perspectival presentations belonging proper to it. Always the relation is: *subjective presentation* and *presented*, *appearance* and *appearing*. And always it is to be distinguished: we may not confuse what is a matter of the presentations in the manifold with the *unity* presented in the manifold. Only a *limit case* stands out — I recall the *one privileged mode of appearance* of the cube-square (namely the parallel-to-frontal-plane case).

Soon it will turn out that here lies a particular starting point for *newly-directed exhibitions and descriptions*. But first that may be saved up. In the case of perceiving the spatial thing straight-on and describing it as a whole or in qualifications, there separates as fundamental structure the *extension itself* — as extensive spatial shape — from what *qualifies* it: color, roughness or smoothness, and so on. If we perform a reflection on the corresponding and necessarily belonging perspectival presentations, there results for each perspective a *parallel structure*: to objective shape corresponds *shape-perspective*, to objective color *color-perspective*, etc. And the unified coloring of the whole extension of each thing-perspective distributes itself over the part-extensions to be set apart in it. And this distribution in turn *presents*, namely, a corresponding distribution of the thing-coloring according to the parts of the spatial thing-extension.

### **§ 29\. Perceptual Field — Perceptual Space**

---

Going beyond the *singularly perceived single thing*, we take up this fact: what offers itself as a single thing to noticing has its *perceptual spatial background*. We notice — again beginning with straight perception and description — that *one whole perceptual field* objectively *spans one whole thing-field*. The field, as perceptual field, is a *perceptual space* that contains in itself all the things singularized in it with all their spatial shapes. The single shapes remain in every perceptual change and motion belonging to *their things*, but they are shapes *in this space*, occupying a piece of space from it. Or: every thing of perception gives itself *in the perceptual space* as filling it at its place — through its shape, as *qualitatively filled*.

That the perceptual space is experienced as *endlessly open* — a space into which we could press forward progressively to new perceptions — is certain. But holding ourselves to what is actually perceptually given, we have the *respective thing-manifold* in the unity of an *actually perceived space*. To be sure, this is not perceived as a *separate thing for itself*, but carries with it a *leading-outward empty horizon*. This — the whole question of horizons — will be a theme for new descriptions.

---

### **§ 30\. Spatial Primal Presence**

---

What is at any moment *actually perceptually given* in the perceptual space — what is, in the sharp sense, *here* and *now* — has the privileged status of *spatial primal presence* (räumliche Urpräsenz). It is the bodily-given core of perception, as opposed to the merely co-meant, the anticipated, the past-related. Spatial primal presence is the *originary mode of givenness* of the spatial — the most concrete way in which spatial reality is *itself there*, *bodily*.

This concept of *primal presence* is methodologically crucial. The perceptual space is filled out by primal-present things; the empty horizons surrounding the primal-present are *co-meant* but *not primally present*. Husserl is here installing a structural distinction within perception itself: between what is *given as itself*, in primal presence, and what is *meant-along* in horizon-structures. This will be deepened in subsequent sections into the full structure of perception.

---

### **§ 31\. Hyle — Hyletic Data as Matter for Intentional Functions**

---

Husserl now introduces a crucial technical distinction: *hyle* and *intentional function*. Within tactile perception (which Husserl illustrates), I have a unified perceptual field that has its unity from *tactile presentations*; this is the *tactile field* — a sensation-field totally different from the visual one, that *never* can mix with the visual to make *one* field. Unification of visual and tactile occurs only by virtue of *apprehending functions* that make *appearances of* from sensation-data. There a visual datum and a tactile datum can have unity by the one being given in the character of objective shading (as visual shading), the other (as tactile shading) of the same object.

We can clearly distinguish: purely the sensation-data — visual or tactile — for themselves, and we can *abstract* from the *appearance-function*, from the consciousness-character that makes them shading of this or that. Precisely seen, we cannot even say that such data *must* necessarily stand in such shading function. The possibility of data *without* shading function is suggested already by the fact that cases can be found — e.g., in perceptual doubts and unveiling illusions — where the same or equivalent sensation-data alter their shading-function and then objectively present something different.

Only the *reverse* is evident: if *spatially-objective* perceptually appears, it can appear *only* through sensation-data — namely, in that such data have the *subjective functional character of shadings*. If we attend purely to the data — to the contents *of* such shading-functions, but in abstraction *from* these functions — we find that they belong to *fundamentally different genera*. All such data-genera suited to the shading function we *take together* in order to gain the concept of *sensation-datum*.

Yet we must take one step further. Already note: *what* as a subjective character *in* a visual datum (or in data of other genera) makes it the case that not only this datum is given to us but also *with it and so to speak in it presenting itself an objective is experienced* — this is only a *special case* of a general wondrous occurrence. A wider class of subjective occurrences will confront us in many sub-shapings, designated by the words: *a mere subjective datum, or a complex of subjective data, is perceptually given which, however (whether in the manner of an objective shading-function or in another manner) makes objectively conscious something different from it*. E.g., if a datum (visual or acoustic) reminds of, or refers to, another, the other is not in it *presented* — it is not in it brought to perception through something like shading — but is nonetheless *through the subjective function of reminding or referring* brought to consciousness.

Certain *merely subjective* data — namely those that are not themselves objectively perceived (through subjective shading-function of other data) — and that *in their own essence are free of all consciousness-characters* — have the *distinguished position* that they can in manifold ways become *core-contents* of remarkable functional characters, which all have in common that *by means of these core-contents*, as it were *spiritualizing them*, they make *other* objectivities conscious. All these functional characters are called *intentional characters* — characters of *consciousness of something*. But the perceptual data — which as *purely-subjectively-given core-contents* are *matter* for the *modes of consciousness leading beyond themselves*, and which in their own essential content contain nothing of such consciousness-characters — are generally called *hyletic data*.

Hyletic data are *color-data*, *tone-data*, *smell-data*, *pain-data*, etc., considered *purely subjectively* — without thinking about the *body-organs* or *psycho-physical*. The expression *Hyle* (matter) indicates this *being-core* — being matter for consciousness-functions. A special case is then the *function-mode* in which the subjectively-perceptual datum is *shading* and so becomes the *perceptual appearance of* an objective. The general concept *Hyle* offers the *broadest extension* for the concept of *sensation-datum* to be drawn from the *purely subjective sphere*, and removes all confusions that come with the blurred, ambiguous word *sensation*.

With this consideration we have prefigured a *fundamental structure* in the pure subjectivity at whose purely-immanent unveiling we work, and have anticipatorily brought it out. For in the perception-sphere itself we have so far only become acquainted with *one* shape of *intentionality* — that which has *objective perception* as its essence in itself. *Hyle* and *intentional function* are the two structurally distinguished moments of perception: the *core-content* (sensory matter) and the *meaning-giving form* (consciousness-of-something).

Limiting ourselves once more to this sphere, it serves us — through the work of the previous lectures — to bring to evidence a *fundamental knowledge* that will likewise prove valid for the *whole pure subjectivity*: namely, the *fundamental distinction between immanence and transcendence* documented in pure subjectivity itself.

### **§ 32\. Noticing Givenness as the I-Relative Mode of Givenness of the Object**

---

What we call *perception of the spatial and in this sense the objective* contains, as our previous analysis shows, *a manifold of possible perceptual directions*: as direction of *noticing*, *grasping*. Indeed, we must say *several perceptions are interwoven in one unity* if we distinguish *noticing-grasping* and *perceiving*; if we count to perceiving merely that *something is given as direct and itself*, whether or not the priority of the *noticed-grasped* is present.

Husserl notes: the talk of *noticing*, *attending to* is an *I-relative* talk — but the I and its noticing is *not itself findable* in the description-directions we pursue. Our descriptions concern the *object* and its modes-of-givenness. We notice immediately that the *difference of the noticed-given object* and the *not-noticed* is also such a *mode of givenness* that is exhibitable in the object itself as its *standing out*, its peculiar *priority*.

Setting this aside, *several* things are perceived in our sphere (i.e., grasped as bodily actuality, as immediately existing): on the one hand the *object itself* — and it alone, when we straight-on objectively perceive (e.g. this table here); on the other hand the *subjective modes* in which, relative to the object, this is given as object from a side, as oriented in oriented space, and as so-and-so shading. The object itself gives itself *as enduring being*, extending through a *duration*. Its duration is the form of its whole determining content, to which spatiality and qualification of spatiality belong. The duration — the *temporal extension* — is given, becomes conscious, in perception (in continuous perception).

From this, we grasp in our reflection-directions the *continuous streaming on* of the familiar givenness-modes — as the continuously-subjectively succeeding perspectives. Here the noticing-grasping can either fix one such momentary mode, or pursue the unified streaming sequence of these modes — the away-streaming sequence of sides, of momentary orientation-modes, of perspectives, and the manner of their unification. And everything that thereby offers itself is *perceived*, given as being. I can take various attitudes here, whereby in some cases the perceived of the different perceptual directions shows itself as *interwoven*, and in some — in a certain important manner — *separated*.

Namely (1) if I move in reflection from the straight object-perception in the first and natural way to the modes-of-givenness, they are perceived — they are noticed in their subjective being. *But the object itself remains also noticingly perceived* — although not, as in straight-on directedness, *exclusively* noticed. What I now grasp is the *object as appearing in the so-and-so how of mode-of-appearance*; or conversely, the mode-of-appearance is preferred as main theme, *but yet as mode-of-appearance of the object*, which so to speak remains further in grip and remains in its previously posited actuality.

Now we see (2) that we *could* attend out of the perspectives to the *hyletic data* and make them *subjective objects for themselves*, in abstraction from what makes them shadings — also without thematic interest for the object presenting itself. Why should I not also be able to pursue *the concrete perspectives of the purely subjective appearance-series* without being thematically directed onto the appearing object — without holding it in grip as existing, having it in my actuality-field, having it in my reckoning as object among my objects? It naturally *appears* continually. But does it have to *count along as being* for me, when it stands outside my experiencing interest? So even in the *straight-on object perceiving*, its modes-of-appearance are constantly also there, but in this *purely objective attitude* they *do not come into account*.

### **§ 33\. Objective Temporality and Stream-Temporality**

---

Here, enclosed in the unity of my perception, there is a *well-distinguishable multi-layeredness*, and I can in any case let my perceiving alone *rule in each layer*, remaining purely in its connection. Each can be taken, *as an own perception-sphere*, also as an *own actuality-sphere*. On the one side, the *sphere of the being object* (or of the perceived spatial field) — the sphere of *objective temporality*. On the other side, the sphere of the *merely subjective appearances* with their temporality — *to be sharply distinguished* from the objective temporality.

This *fundamental distinction* will extend itself *beyond the perception-sphere*. Even in restriction to spatial objectivity, it is clear from the outset that it can be not only *perceived* but also *remembered*, *expected*, *thought about*, *valued*, etc. So we still have many subjective ways in which objects can be conscious. The *double temporality* thus reaches much further.

We have the *infinite objective time* as the *form of the objective world* — under which objective space itself stands — and the *subjective time* as the form of *all subjective being in the manner of streaming*, *streaming as lived-experience*. It is a special problem of pure phenomenological analysis to make clear how, in the subjective time-form of streaming (and specifically in the streaming of *appearances of the objective*), there constitutes itself not only the *objective content* (according to space, spatial shape, quantitative fullness) but also the *objective form of duration* itself appearance-fashion.

One must not externally say: the subjective sequence of appearances is *appearance* — is something like shading-presentation of the objective sequence of contents of the objective duration. The matter is not that simple. Let it suffice here that we have sharply distinguished these two different time-forms and accordingly the corresponding, essentially different time-objectivities within the frame of perception itself. With this we have also determined a certain — purely-in-this-frame exhibitable — distinction between *outer* perception as objective spatio-temporally directed perception and *inner* perception as perception of the *purely subjective*.

Now we can also say: inner perception is *perception of the immanent*; outer perception is *perception of the transcendent*. The correlative distinction of *immanence* and *transcendence* is clarified, insofar as it already shows itself in our frame, in the following way.

---

### **§ 34\. Distinction of Immanent and Transcendent, Reell and Irreell in Perception. The Object as Irreal Pole**

---

Holding ourselves purely to the *inner* or *immanent* perception — that is, in the pure perception-direction onto the *subjective in its own immanent time* — what we here singly exhibit and claim as perceived is *individual*, having its *individuality, its one-time-ness* in its time-determination, and as individual or temporally-being has its *real* (reell) connections, or as a *reell time-whole* has its *reell parts and moments*. The stream of the subjective — in our sphere the stream of subjective sensation-data, perspectives, appearances — is called, as the stream of immanent-temporal objects (themselves uniting into the unity of a time-objectivity), the *Erlebnisstrom* (lived-experience stream). Everything that we take from this immanent time-sphere as singly-immanent time-object — as one such time-object's reell parts or moments — is *immanent*.

Sharply distinguished from the *reell* content of the lived-experiences is the *object itself*, as it appears *in* them: it is *not reell* part or moment of any lived-experience. The perceived table is given in the perceptions, but it is *not* a reell content of these perceptions. Rather, the table appears *in* them, is presented *through* them — but as something other than them, as the *transcendent pole* that the manifold perceptions all aim at. The *transcendent* object is the *irreal pole* of perception — it does not belong reell to any lived-experience, but is inseparably *meant-in-them* as the *one* that they all *present*.

The *object*, then, *transcends the reell content of the experience-stream*. To this stream are *immanent* only its reell moments. Yet this concept of the transcendent — as the object conscious *in* immanence — carries with it many great difficulties and shimmering ambiguities, and demands further consideration. These will at the same time give an intimation of the *wondrous implications* (hidden from all traditional psychology and epistemology) that are concealed in the intentional experiences, even in something so relatively simple as an outer perception. Without unwinding and exact description of these implications, there is no serious psychology — and no serious theory of experience and reason.

The *ideal-pole* (the object as transcendent) is *not* something like a *rigid piece common to all the intentional experiences in which it ideally dwells*. It is not common to them as a *reell piece*. It is *constituted in* the flow of perspectives. In the flow of shading perspectives this one and the same — even objectively rigid — table appears; but while it appears as identical, it appears with *ever new marks*, in *ever new sides*. What constantly appears in the unity of each momentary perception-phase is what we call the *specially appearing table-side*. But more closely seen, it is not in each phase this mere table-side that appears in it — that is rather the *perceived table as specially seen from this side*.

Likewise the *whole continuity* of shading-appearances is not the sequence of *specially perceived table-sides*; rather, *in the whole continuity*, the *table itself*, which presents itself now specially from this and then from that side, is the *ideal* that dwells in the *reell* continuity of shadings — and that means here: the *perception-object builds itself up* in the experience-flow, yet *not* in the manner of a piecing-together, as the picture of "build" suggests.

Crucial caveat about *adequate* and *inadequate* perception: we cannot simply say that the spatial object is *adequately given*, since in every phase of perception it is given with more than is actually perceived. We must distinguish: (1) the *perceived object simpliciter* — what I as perceiver experience as actual and bodily — and its description as *this object simpliciter*; (2) the *perceived purely as correlate of perceiving* — namely, what perceiving inseparably bears within itself as *its* ideal, its *appearing-to-it* and *meant*, exactly as it is meant in *this* perceiving.

When in perceiving an *ideal and ongoing identical* is meant, and when in each phase the *same* is meant in pre-grasp with not-yet-actually-seen-but-to-be-seen marks — with a *horizon of unfamiliarity* — then the *horizon itself*, the pre-grasping with a pre-grasp-sense, belongs *to the adequate sphere*. But it is overstepped if we want to determine what is *only meant in pre-grasp* in a perceptual stretch *as object*, and treat it as something actually given.

Since now the spatial object itself is *not* what perceiving actually has — *not* what actually appears of it — but as spatial object is necessarily *more* — *the spatial object itself is never adequately given*, and is itself never *bare correlate of the perception of the moment*. It itself is *transcendent* to perception in *yet another sense* than that of the polar irreality — namely in that of a *perceptually-given* — and therein lies, *in belief bodily grasped* — which, however unanimously the perception may progress and the belief in this unanimity be corroborated, *cannot through any synthetically-extended perception be completed* to give the object adequately as completed in all that it is, *reckoning with no further pre-grasp toward possible new perception and corresponding corroboration*.

### **§ 35\. Substrate-Pole and Property-Pole. The Positive Meaning of the Empty Horizon**

---

Within the perceptual object two structural poles can be distinguished: the *substrate-pole* (the *thing*\-as-such) and the *property-pole* (the qualities — color, shape, weight, etc. — that are *of* the substrate). The substrate is the *bearer* of the properties; the properties are the *determinations* of the substrate. This is not an external division of the object but a structural articulation belonging to the object itself as constituted in perception.

The structure of the perception thus essentially involves the *empty horizon*: the substrate is always grasped *with a halo of more determinations to come*. I see the table; it is colored, shaped, located; but it has many further determinations — its underside, its weight, its history, its connection to the room. These are *co-meant* in the empty horizon of the perception, as further determinations that *could* be brought to fuller intuition through further perceiving.

The *positive significance* of the empty horizon is twofold. (1) It is what makes the perceived object a *real object* — an object that is *more than* its momentary mode of appearance, an object that has *further determinations* awaiting unveiling. Without empty horizons, the "object" would just be the momentary appearance-content. (2) It is the *structural condition* for *progressive* perceiving: I can move from this perception to the next, ever-newly unveiling further determinations, *because* the object has been all along meant as *including* these as-yet-undetermined determinations. The empty horizon is, then, the constitutive structure that makes possible the *progress* of perceptual unveiling.

The empty horizon, despite being "empty," is *positively structured*. It is not a featureless void; it is a horizon of *anticipations* — what the further perceptions *could* show. These anticipations have a *style* drawn from prior experience: I anticipate, of this table, that it has a back, a weight, a determinate solidity — even before I have explicitly probed any of these. The *positivity* of the empty horizon is thus the *prescriptive style* of further perceptual possibility.

---

### **§ 36\. The Intentional Object of Perception**

---

In all these structural distinctions, what we have come to recognize is *the intentional object of perception*. The intentional object is not the *physical* object of natural science, abstracted from all subjective relations. It is the object *as it is meant* in the perceptual act, *purely as correlate of the perceiving*. It is, in Husserl's terminology, the *noematic correlate* of the *noesis* (the perceiving act).

The intentional object is *transcendent* to the lived-experience (it is not *reell* in it) but *immanent* in the strict sense of belonging *inseparably* to the lived-experience as its noematic correlate. It is the *meant-as-such*, the *appearing-as-such* — the *object in the how* of its givenness. As such it provides the proper *thematic field* for phenomenological psychology: it is what is to be described in its own essential structure, prior to and independently of the question whether it actually exists in the objective world.

The intentional object has its own complex structure: substrate and properties, modes of appearance, modes of orientation, modes of attention, modes of doubt and certainty, modes of empty and filled intentionality, of pre-grasping horizons and confirmed determinations. All these structural features belong to the intentional object as such, and are accessible to phenomenological description independently of the metaphysical question whether the corresponding "real" object exists.

This concept of *intentional object* is essential for understanding how phenomenology overcomes both naïve realism (which conflates the perceived object with the physical-thing-in-itself) and naïve psychologism (which dissolves the perceived into mere subjective images). The intentional object is *neither* the physical-thing-in-itself *nor* a subjective image; it is the *meant-as-meant*, with its own structural fidelity to be respected by intentional analysis.

### **§ 37\. The Phenomenological Reduction as Method of Unveiling the Immanent**

---

With the distinctions of immanence/transcendence and reell/irreell in hand, Husserl now formulates the *method of phenomenological reduction* as the method by which the *immanent* — pure subjectivity — is unveiled as a field of scientific investigation. The reduction is a *change of attitude* whereby the natural positing of the world is *bracketed* and what remains is the *purely immanent* domain of consciousness and its correlates.

Crucially, the *phenomenological-psychological* reduction is to be distinguished from the *transcendental* reduction. The transcendental reduction brackets the *being* of the world (the natural positing of an objective world-existence) and turns to *transcendental subjectivity* as the absolute ground of all positing. The phenomenological-psychological reduction, by contrast, *remains within the natural attitude* — it remains a science of *psychic* subjectivity within the world — but *brackets the world-positing* in order to attend purely to the *intentional structures* of psychic life. This difference of attitude separates two parallel sciences with overlapping content.

The reduction is carried out by *suspending* (not denying, not doubting) the natural positing of the world's existence and turning attention to consciousness and its objects *as meant*. The perceiver remains a perceiver, and his perceptions remain perceptions; but the *interest* now lies not in the *being* of the perceived but in the *structure of perceiving* and *the perceived as meant*. This bracketing is methodological — a *change of theoretical interest*, not a metaphysical conversion.

Through the reduction, the field of *pure subjectivity* opens up: the stream of lived-experience, the hyletic data, the intentional acts of various kinds, the noematic correlates with their structural articulations. This field is *infinitely rich*: it is no merely thin domain of "consciousness" set off against the world, but a vast structured field of *intentional life* — including the constitution of the world itself as conscious correlate. Phenomenological psychology takes this field as its theme and unfolds it in its essential structures by means of *Wesensschau*.

The reduction is the *methodological hinge* of phenomenological psychology. Without it, psychic life is studied always within and against the worldly attitude — as part of the world, conditioned by physical body and social circumstance, etc. With it, psychic life is studied in its own intentional structure — as *consciousness of* the world (and of all that is given in the world), with its own essential laws of constitution. The reduction is what frees phenomenological psychology to become *the* foundational science of intentional life that it must be.

### **§ 38\. The Access to Pure Subjectivity from Outer Perception**

---

Husserl now articulates how the *transition* into pure subjectivity is accomplished. We began with *outer perception* — the perceptual experience of spatial-thingly objects in the natural attitude. The world is in the natural attitude of perception *our experiential theme*. Experiencing being interested in the objective world is, according to our analyses of outer perception, *strivingly directed* at this in perceptual belief constantly posited and *as Idea presupposed* world — namely, presupposed as a *belief-goal* to be realized in systematic processes of possible perceptive realization. Being-interested is thus practically a strivingly and possibly actingly directed-at-actual-realization-of-the-believed, in progressive experience, which is accordingly *goal-directed*. This attitude of naturally interested outer perception and experience we can *arbitrarily change*.

Perception can run on, the appearance-continuities can stream forth fittingly together with all that intentionally unites itself in them — *without our being interested in the being object and its so-being*. (When the perception has streamed out, no such interest needs to be further at work in the form of the still-holding-in-grip of the once thematically grasped — through which it continues to be a being-theme.) This change of interest — this *not-having-as-theme*, or releasing-from-the-thematic-domain, the setting-out-of-account — is an *essential alteration* of the manner in which the object-consciousness, the perception, is *in its accomplishment*; in particular, of how its *belief is in accomplishment*, of how its *object counts for us*.

The *radical* change of interest of the *phenomenological reduction* makes the *own-being of perception* — with all that is reell and ideell inseparable from it — *the sole theme*; or, in extension, the *sole theme of subjectivity*. The radicalism of the phenomenological reduction consists in this: that *consistently every interest in objective being is suspended*. When perception in itself makes its object conscious as substrate for actualized and not-yet-actualized determinations — conscious as being of a transcendent sense — then this consciousness-making of the transcendent is *itself a peculiarity of perception belonging proper to it*, belonging to its own essence. Thus this peculiarity is, in the interest-attitude on perception according to its purely subjective being, according to its proper essence, *theme*.

Two thematic spheres of objects (or domains of establishments) are sharply separated through the radicalism of the phenomenological reduction. Stated more fully, in reverse order: (1) In the *one* attitude we live exclusively in the *interest for the subjective* — for the outer perceiving as lived-experience and for its inseparable, reell-or-ideell-inhering contents. To this belongs the *outer object*, but purely as the *meant in the outer perception itself* — as its *transcendent meaning*. In phenomenology we say: the *object in brackets* or *in quotation marks*, not the object *simpliciter*. Here, then, thematic establishments concern exclusively the *subjective*; and if subjective contains in itself transcendent intentionality as its meaning, then *that* — and the analysis of this meaning as such — is co-thematic. In the interest for the purely subjective life — that is, meaning and striving exclusively in *reflective* consciousness, and ultimately in reflective perception, in which the subjective realizes itself subjectively according to being and so-being. (2) In the *other* attitude — the earlier in natural life and in itself the necessarily preceding — the interest goes onto the *objectively being*. We live in the *belief in objectivity*: quite usually and constantly, where for us an objective world is quite self-evidently and in-advance there. Living in this belief, or being "interested" in the world in it certainly being, is to be strivingly and actively directed at *doxic realization* of the objective. *This very striving directedness is forbidden in phenomenology.*

While here in the natural attitude the outer perception and its belief is in *actual accomplishment*, and is *medium* for the realizing striving in the direction of the belief-goal, in the *counter-attitude* — the phenomenological — *the reflective perception, the immanent perception of, e.g., the outer perception, is itself in such accomplishment*. The natural attitude opens the ways of every objective knowledge, every objective science, as also of every objective practice acting into the world. In the direction toward objective-worldly establishments, modalizations of the being-belief appear as *hindrances*, transforming its certainty into doubt, into mere supposition, into negation. This leads to the necessity of secondary establishments of *possibility* or *impossibility*, of *likelihood* or *probability*, of *non-being* and of *truly-being*\-in-its-place — but always *in relation to the objective world*. In the phenomenological attitude, which is *kept pure of all objective worldly interest*, all such objectively-directed establishments fall away.

The phenomenological attitude opens the way to *a quite different science*: the science of *pure subjectivity*, in which we *thematically speak* exclusively of *lived-experiences*, of *modes of consciousness*, and of *what is meant in their objectivity* — but exclusively *as meant*. It is a main point ever again to note: *that* a perception perceives a transcendent and that it thereby in its belief-certainty *posits its object as being* (and posits with a transcendent sense of this or that structure) — this is a *type of truths* that, phenomenologically purely grasped, contains in itself *not the slightest meaning about the transcendent world simpliciter*, the being world.

It is also to be noted: every process of determination on the natural experiential ground — e.g., objectively-considering analysis, conceptual fashioning, description, theorization, as every other consciousness-accomplishment performed in the natural attitude — can *afterwards undergo that reductive methodical transformation* into its purely subjective, into the phenomenological. Instead of further placing myself on the ground of the world (and in general on the ground of objects and object-worlds I have naïvely acquired, grasped, accepted), or instead of *remaining on this ground*, I can subsequently set out of play the *accomplishment of the experiential certainty in which and through which I have this ground*. I alter, then, my thematic interest after the fact, and now look at this whole subjective.

### **§ 39\. The Analysis of Perception with Regard to the Perceiver Himself**

---

Once the reduction has been carried out, the analysis of perception can be deepened in a new direction: it can be referred to *the perceiver himself*, who has been until now systematically left out of account in the abstracted perceiver-of-objects. The perceiver is not merely a transcendental ego in the abstract; he is *the one in whose bodily life perceiving takes place* — and his bodily life is constitutive for the *style* of perception. Husserl now opens up the *bodily*, kinesthetic, and orientation dimensions of the perceiver.

What we normally experience straight-on as *the world* signifies a *world-sense* that hangs on the *presupposition of normal corporeality in its normal functioning*. This presupposition is not formulated, but as little as we — perceiving things — think of our necessarily co-functioning perception-organs, as little do we think of how these can sometimes become *anomalous*, although we know this from experience. As soon as such experiences are made, the world-sense constituted in the normal functioning of corporeality — until-then-simply-the-being-world — receives a *new sense-layer*. The world is now no longer the *normally-experienced* but the *normally-experienced world in its relation to normal corporeality* — entering into a *series* with manifold anomalously-appearing worlds, related to bodily modifications of normality. All these worlds, however differently they may look, are from now on *different lookings of the same* world — the world that looks differently "according to corporeal disposition."

This is obviously *the path to physical nature in its ultimate sense-shape* — as the natural scientist tries to determine it thinkingly: that nature which is not itself sensuous, thus free of all sensuous marks, but which in *sensuous marks* and in the *full sensuous worlds* of normal intuitions has its *modes of appearance*. With this grows a *new concept of appearance* as a *new concept of objectivity*.

Through the *phenomenology of corporeality* in its interweaving with the phenomenology of physical thinghood — here including biophysically considered organisms — the *foundational pieces* of a *phenomenology of nature* are accomplished: that is, the phenomenological clarification of *subjectivity as in itself constituting nature experientially* (as experiential sense and experiential proposition, or as system of *free accessibility*). But only foundational pieces.

### **§ 40\. The Problematic of Temporality: Presentation — Retention and Protention (The Positional and Quasi-Positional Modifications of Perception and Their Significance for Practical Life)**

---

(Husserl notes Beilagen XII and XXIII.) Many great themes open up as soon as one focuses, even only on perception as merely spatially-objectively-directed. To phenomenologically study outer perception in its reell and ideell stocks means to be at once led *beyond* mere outer perception. We are so led even through the simple regress to *reflection*. We immediately come upon a new perception-type: the *reflective, immanently directed* perception, in its doubleness as phenomenologically reduced and as natural reflection. Perception of corporeality was a new perception-type, and this also does not close the series. With it a general theme — *perception in general* — stands out from the particular perceptions as particular themes.

One sees soon that perception in general (of whatever particular kind, of whatever kind of objectivity) has its *modifications* in *other kinds of experience*: first in *memory* as *retention* and as *recollection*, and on the other hand in *expectation*, likewise in changing configurations. But one comes upon this not merely looking beyond the domains of perceptions, but also through the obviously very necessary study of the *immanent structure of each concrete perception itself*. The concrete unity of a perception, as a continuously streaming and only in form-of-streaming-being lived-experience, is *a synthetic construction* — or, considered in an ideal-analytic consideration, *to be regarded as such*.

The streaming is a *process* that has an *immanent time-stretch* as form, a stretch we can think as built up from *part-stretches* and *time-phases*, in a sense *time-points* (though we should not think of mathematical ideal points). Here we encounter the difficult problems of *originary time-consciousness*. If we, in reflective attitude, attend to a streaming perception, we can fix our attention on a *momentary phase* that stands out by content — e.g., a phase of a violin-tone distinguished by the setting-in of a scraping sound — and make remarkable observations.

If we hold fast this phase, we do not actually hold it as *perception*. For it is *immediately already past* — a *new* tone-phase is there in its place, characterized as a *living perception-phase*, as new tone-*now*. But *how* is the past phase conscious? And likewise the still-further-back-lying phases? They must indeed be *co-conscious* for a stretch in each momentary now. For how should we otherwise have *more given* than the respective momentary tone-now? How could we have consciousness of the *enduring* tone lasting through a time-stretch? The momentary now is no stretch: obviously we have, in one with the momentary consciousness of the *originally emerging tone-phase*, a *consciousness of the just-past*, as of the *"sinking" into the past*.

With every tone-phase newly emerging in the form *now*, in one, the phase just-now still given as *now* is given in a *new mode* — in the mode of *past, retentionally*. Not merely that it is, instead of perceived, *retentionally* conscious; it is conscious in *ever-new manner* — as ever more and more past, in the progress of the a-priori-never-still-standing process of the *emerging of an ever-new now*. Each *past* is altered into a *more-past*, this again into a still-more-past, and so on. The intentional clarification of this process — the clarification of the manner in which *a time-stretch, a streaming process is conscious, can only be conscious* — is one of the most important themes in the doctrine of pure subjectivity. Pure subjectivity is everywhere and always only in the form of a *streaming life*.

We come upon the *manifold of givenness-modes of the temporal as temporal*: on the givenness-modes of *now*, of *past*, of *past of past*, and so on; differently on the *coming* in its own modalities. And we come thereby on the description of *temporal orientation* and of *temporal perspectives* — generally, on the peculiarity of *temporal appearances as appearances of*: on the particular *kinds and layers of intentionality* through which in immanence temporal as such *appears for us*.

It shows itself: *concrete perception as originary consciousness (originary givenness) of temporally extended objects* is inwardly built up as *a streaming system of momentary perceptions* (the so-called *Urimpressionen*, primal impressions). But every such momentary perception is *core-phase of a continuity*: a continuity of *self-grading momentary retentions* on the one side, and a *horizon of the coming* on the other — a horizon of *protention* — which in unveiling characterizes itself as *continuously coming-graded*. This momentary continuity of retention and protention, belonging to each primal impression, undergoes — in the flowing-off of the primal impression — a difficult-to-describe modification.

Each concrete perception (e.g., of an ongoing tone) often has the form, or can have, of an *ending perception*; and after its end *it is not nothing*. It has *changed into a concrete retention*, into the lived-experience of the concrete *just-having-faded-away tone*. This is *again awakable*: the retention can pass over into an *intuitive recollection*. Recollection is not an actually repeated perception, but a *peculiar consciousness* in which we have it *as if* the tone were once again *hearingly constituting itself as enduring and persisting*. *"The"* tone: it is not a tone of mere phantasy, but a tone given in *belief-certainty as reproduced*, as *re-awakened*, as having-been-for-us, as having-been-perceived.

The intentional analysis of recollection is again a new great problem. Of its hidden depths, traditional psychology and epistemology have had no inkling; only phenomenology has made us seeing for them. Recollection is *re-presentation* (Vergegenwärtigung). Perception, by contrast, is *presentation* (Gegenwärtigung), and indeed *originary* presentation — insofar as it makes a *present* conscious as originarily running. (Husserl begins a new lecture.)

Apparently in one series with retention, protention, and recollection belong *reproductive phantasy* and *perceptual picture-consciousness*. As when we phantasize ourselves into a fairy-tale land or when something similar is presented to us perceptively in a picture by a painter. On closer analysis, *phantasy as pure phantasy* draws us into a *new realm of modifications*. The perceived, the remembered, the expected is characterized as *being*: present-being, past-being, etc. This being can be *modalized* — instead of certain-being it can be possible, probable, doubtful, void. But always it remains *being*: from the subject's side it is always actually believed, in some belief-modalities.

Over against all this stands a *modification*: instead of an actual belief, a *phantasizing-into-a-belief* — whereby what is here perceptively or reproductively presented merely *hovers*, *as if* it were, *as if* it were perceived (or were given as existing), *as if* it were past or coming. The belief is not actual but *quasi-belief*. As every lived-experience in pure subjectivity is only as perceptually conscious (though not noticed), there belongs ideally to each one a possible *corresponding phantasy of like content*. And each phantasy is itself again modifiable into a *phantasy in a phantasy*, and so *in infinitum*.

These *two series of modifications* — emanating from perception — are the *positional* (with actual belief and belief-modalities) and the *quasi-positional* modifications (in the wondrous character of the *belief-as-if*). They all have *universal significance* in pure subjectivity. They all play a constant role already in *experiential life*. We have a constant experiential world not merely through streaming perception. Aside from the fact that this perception in its concretion is itself a *mix* of abstract moments of perception, retention, and protention, it should be noted: *to every streamed-away perception is attached a concrete retention* and *a concrete protention*, which let us *expect* something new.

Further: what is already *sunken into the depths of un-living past* — no longer livingly retentionally in grip — surfaces again, becomes again intuitive *as recollection*. Through *recollection-syntheses* there is constituted a *past* to be pursued in various directions, which — thanks to the at-any-time-producible synthesis with perception and through future-directed protentions — constitutes the *unity of filled time*. Only through recollection and pre-expectation, then, is a *world* there for us as an acquired and permanently-acquired knowledge-possession: a *unified manifold of objects* to which one can return, to which one constantly has access (or can yet gain it), and which one can — *as they were* and *as they perhaps in future perceptions are persisting* — represent, count on, and operate with.

While the *positional* modifications of perception in their synthetic connections accomplish that we get into consciousness *enduring actualities* in pure subjectivity (and have them as exhibitable experiential unities), the *phantasies* create for us *possibilities* — in connection with the actualities of the positional sphere, the *imaginings of being-otherwise*: as when we fictively re-think a gray object as red. This is *presupposition* for a *transforming praxis*: the re-thought red is preferred to the gray, and I now perhaps find practical ways to *realize* the imagined possibility.

When one in this way proceeds, always in the frame of phenomenological reduction, from the various kinds of perception over to their positional and quasi-positional modifications, one researchingly *widens one's field of view* in a *purely subjective nexus* — in which every new shape has the *basic character of all the merely subjective*: *intentionality*. Where we at first had *externally perceived as such* (purely as appearing in the perception-progress), we now have an indefinite manifold of modifications all *intentionally* connected, all intertwined in the streaming life. The structure of pure subjectivity is thus much richer than the analysis of perception alone could reveal.

### **§ 41\. Reflection on the Object-Pole in the Noematic Attitude and Reflection on the I-Pole as Its Underlying. Universal Synthesis of the I-Pole. The I as Pole of Activities and Habitualities**

---

Husserl now turns from the analysis of conscious *appearances* and their modifications to the structural pole *underlying* all these — the *I-pole*. The double centering of consciousness is here brought into focus: every consciousness *of something* has, structurally, both an *object-pole* (toward which the consciousness is directed) and an *I-pole* (from which the consciousness emanates). Reflection on the object-pole gives us the *noematic* analysis; reflection on the I-pole gives us the *egological* analysis.

The I-pole is *numerically and identically the same*: it is the *one single center* of the whole pure subjectivity that we progressively come to know, from our starting point. Each individual outer perception (and experience) goes from the I-pole and refers back to it. Just as an object-pole, in the progression from its appearances to ever-new unanimously synthetically connecting appearances, is *numerically identical the same* (while determining itself ever-newly through ever-new object-contents and bearing precipitates of these determinations within itself henceforward), so similarly for the I.

But the *pure I* is not a mere *thing-object* that constitutes itself as *unity of appearances* and as *substrate of factual mark-determinations*. The I *performs activities*. But it is not a mere *empty ideal pole-point*, merely determined as the point-of-intersection of the activities streaming from it (and then pre-given). Rather, in one with that, *pole of corresponding habitualities*. These are *not*, as for a thing-object, *factually-given and exhibitable factual properties* perceptually given on it, but *qualities* that *accrue* to it *out of genesis* — from the *fact* that it has performed the respective actus, and that belong to it *only in recourse to this, historically*.

With the *originary decision*, the I originarily becomes the *so-decided*. It can immediately afterwards behold itself as that, and later behold itself as *the same* — as the *same one still so decided*. The I *has a history*, and from its history it creates an *enduring* I that remains habitually itself. E.g., I learn a spatial object through individual consideration and first-time gain of it a *"unified representation,"* a *"concept,"* gain it as substrate of marks attributed to it — this is an *active accomplishment beyond passivity*, and yields just this *concept*.

If I see this object again later, it is now for me no longer like a new one, but is *eo ipso the conceptualized*. Going back to the I of the originary act and of the later re-cognizing act, the following is clear. In the originary act I acquire a *knowing* — but not the *momentary* one of the act, but the *knowing as conviction remaining for me*. The later accomplishment is the accomplishment of a *similar* act insofar as the content may be the same. But not only is this act *altered* in that it *awakens the earlier act* and takes on the character of *familiarity* with respect to the object-content; and not only that I, restoring the recollection, can now say: I now believe the same and see the same I earlier found — but I must say: I *took knowledge* of it earlier, and *this knowledge as conviction has remained mine*. So my conviction, and the renewed seeing *confirms* this conviction, which meanwhile *was* ever mine.

Take a proof I have for the first time performed in implicit activities. If later I come back to it in my thoughts, this is not merely a remembrance of an earlier having-proven and a new from-the-present-newly-accomplishing co-belief in the earlier-proven — but my conviction, I say, *has not changed*; I have it still as the conviction then founded in proving. And I say: *I am still the same* — I as the one who has formed this conviction *am the same* — insofar as I still have this conviction. The fact that I perform an activity and have gained a certain conviction (a certain positional accomplishment-result) has a *future-prospect* for the I. To myself, with the founding of this conviction, *a so-being has accrued, as something enduring* — not an *empirical expectation* of how I shall behave later, but a true accretion to my being.

### **§ 42\. The I of Originary Foundings and Subsequent Foundings. Identity of the I in the Holding-On of Convictions. The Individuality of the I Manifests Itself in Its Decisions, Which Are Grounded in the Convictions**

---

*The I has no factual properties*; it has *exclusively so-being as subject of self-founded convictions*. This is the key thesis of §42 — and a striking departure from any naturalistic conception of the self. The I is *not* a thing with properties; it is a *bearer of self-founded convictions*, of *positional accomplishments* that *remain* for it as its own.

The I that performs *originary foundings* (Urstiftungen) — and so founds itself in correlative sense as I of self-founded originary convictions — becomes also, in *community*, the I that *post-understands* foreign I in their originarily-founding doing; and through *assent* (i.e., the fitting self-adaptation of foreign motivation and foreign conviction in one's own life-unity), performs *post-founding* (Nachstiftung), *following*, *co-belief*, *co-valuation*, etc. The convictions so founded in the I are *not born out of the I itself*: it does not itself form them; it does not found itself in its own I-originariness. It follows after, it orients itself by *another*, by his convictions and motivations.

So we have, on both sides, two kinds of taking-position: (1) Taking-position, decision, conviction *purely from grounds of reason* — in which what is *seen*, insightfully, sightedly achieved. (2) From *blind* motives, which through unveiling can indeed be understood, and with this understandability have an indirect rationality, but no sightful one — no one carrying necessity in itself. Their rationality would first have to be delimited. There is a *field of associative-apperceptive transfer* from the *sphere of seeing* into the *unseen anticipated* — what is on the basis of *analogy* with the *earlier-seen* posited and supported by no sightful corroboration.

A further distinction: between *rational motivation* and the *motivation* (the *psychic causality*, the passive and the I-causality) that motivates me *to follow doctrines of reason at all* — that *brings me into the situation* in which I *can see* and *must see*, following passive affections. If I follow the *other*, it may be that I can *re-produce his insight in myself* and do so. In this I *rationally follow him* and decide myself by his reason and at the same time by my own (since his, in the imitation, becomes mine). My decision is *imitated decision*, and yet I have decided myself in free reason. I can justify it before myself. Yet it remains that the decision *does not stem purely from me*: I am not the author; I follow foreign authority — but from own reason at the same time. Possibly I have insight, but the other does not have it at all; then I do not follow re-shapingly his insight.

We must also distinguish: the *passive going-along-with* and the *freely-considered deciding-myself-for-the-decision-of-the-other*, with the difference: *from reason* (perhaps purely from reason) or *from unjustified motives*. So I have: (1) suggested convictions, and only these; (2) freely-following-the-other convictions of active assent; (3) convictions of assent from own reason (perhaps following his insight-path and his insight itself). I am originarily *self-founding from myself*; I am the *freely-motivated subject* of convictions in *post-founding*.

(Husserl notes this is the closing of the lecture, 30.VII.1925.) Every *change of conviction is a change of the I*: I do not have convictions as fleeting lived-experiences but as *I-qualities*, which I have from my own originarily-founding activities, or from my own freely-following-the-decisions-of-other-I decisions — but which I possibly *lose* through other activities and necessarily motivated through them. So long as I *do not give up* the conviction, it is in validity for me. I, as I *now am*, as I *am*, am the so-and-so-convinced one from originarily-own decision. *The I has no other own-qualities at all.*

With this fundamental peculiarity of the I, much is connected. For example, what we call *self-preservation* — purely with reference to the I. Through the I-life runs a *striving* to come to a *unity and harmony in the manifold of its convictions* — such that the I *wants to become one that remains true to itself*, or can remain true to itself (insofar as it is never more inclined to give up its convictions and, what essentially hangs together with this, become *unhappy*). This is, of course, an *Idea*, but it designates the *sense of the self-preservation-striving in its ideality*.

Likewise the concepts *personality*, *character*, and *individuality* — in every purely I-relative sense that strongly determines our speech — relate to this field of *convictions* forming themselves from the I and *I-determinatively* determining it. The I has its kind of *time-extension* as enduring I, in the change of its acts and so of its convictions, its decisions. But it is not only enduring in the manner of an empty stage of such change, or a mere *substrate* of this change. What we in the proper sense call *I* — apart from the communicative relation to *Thou* or *We* — means a *personal individuality*. In this concept lies a *unity constituting itself in the change of decisions and identically enduring*.

It is a bit analogous with the unity of the *real thing* over against the change of thingly states. In the *manner* in which the I lets itself be motivated, with relation to its consciousness-related environment, to changing decisions — and so in the manner of the particularity of its decidednesses themselves and their connections — the I preserves an *individual and exhibitable style*. The I-pole has not only its changing precipitates but, through their change, a *unity constituted in this style*. The I has its *individuality*, its *individual total character*, that goes identically through all decisions and decidednesses; as individual character it has *particularities*, *particular properties* — called *character-properties*.

In the decisions — as they grow in the grasped nexus of already-known decisions of *this* I — apperceptively and knowingly *these or those character-properties* are *manifested*, and so the *individuality in general*. Yet here is to be added: in *associative-inductive* experience, *expectations* form themselves of how, according to previous behavior, the relevant persons (or my I for myself — in which the experience is my own about myself) will behave. But the forming empirical-inductive unity — the substrate of expectation-directions — is here an *index* of an inward, post-understandably, *with-understanding-to-be-unveiled unity*. In the decisions of an I itself lies a *prescribed consequence* for further decisions; and this is a *sphere of understandability* — though in many respects still needing clarification.

### **§ 43\. The Unity of the Subject as Monad — Static and Genetic Investigation of the Monad. Transition from the Isolated Monad to the All-of-Monads**

---

(Husserl notes Beilage XXVII.) What we have laid out suffices to give a representation of *the self-enclosed, everywhere inseparably connected pure subjectivity*. Taken in its concretely complete connection, this subjectivity constitutes what we can call *the concrete pure subjectivity or the monad*. Here *monad* is *no metaphysical concept*, but the *unity of the subjective in phenomenological reduction* — to be researched in *direct intuition* through laborious analysis. Husserl is here reclaiming the Leibnizian term *monad* not as a metaphysical posit but as a phenomenological description: the *concrete unity of pure subjectivity in itself*.

The analytical investigation is here first *static*: dissecting the respective relatively-closed, exhibitable connections, and tracing all the *immanently reell and ideell* according to all essential correlations. But necessary then is also the *genetic* investigation: the research of *geneses* — both *passive* and *active* — in which the monad *unfolds and develops*, in which within the monad the *monadic I* gains its *personal unity* and becomes the *subject of an environment* (an environment given to it partly passively and partly actively-shaped by it itself), and finally the *subject of a history*.

A great step is to be mentioned (and here, alas, only introduced with a few words): in the phenomenological reduction, the investigation is first and naturally led, as we have done, as investigation of *that* pure subjectivity which *the researcher* finds in *first originality* — namely as the pure subjectivity of *his I*, of *his* life, of *his* appearing environment. Most simply abstractively as *solus ipse*. But it has come about for me that the phenomenological reduction is to *extend beyond* this realm of the *solus ipse* — or to *extend into* the foreign subjects that appear to *me*, the researching I, in *outer experience* (and appear in the natural attitude, so to speak as "world-children"). So there results a *pure phenomenology of intersubjectivity*. The transition from the isolated monad to the *all of monads* (Monadenall) is the move into the *intersubjective* domain.

---

### **§ 44\. Phenomenological Psychology as Fundamental Both for the Natural Research of the Psyche and for the Personal Research and the Corresponding Sciences**

---

Husserl now draws together the architectonic conclusion of the systematic part. As soon as the gaze penetrates more deeply from the inductive externality into the psychic inwardness, there shows itself a *proper-essentially self-determining being of the soul* — which is *presupposed* by all psychological induction and so cannot be merely inductively grasped.

If one wants here seriously to come to know the *proper essence of the soul* — and at first come to know it in pure *self-experience*, *as* it is in abstraction from all physis, and is intuitable — then one must of course carry through this *abstraction* in the *right way* and in *strict consistency*. In other words: if the *inductive outer psychology* (the psychophysical and the experimental) is to win *scientific concepts of inwardness* instead of operating with the rough representations of pre-scientific life about psychic inwardness (and thereon grounding the experimental inductions), it must adopt the *method of phenomenological reduction*. It needs an *own science of pure subjectivity*, as we have built up. This proves itself immediately as the *truly central science* — and as the science furnishing all the *properly* psychological knowledge — indeed also the most *extensive*.

(Husserl in a footnote remarks: the misguided attempts of psychologists to form descriptive concepts of the psychic itself rest on this — that they are unable to *see intentionality in its proper kind* and to *treat it accordingly*. The consciousness-nexus is an *intentional* nexus; the unity of consciousness is the *presupposition* of all inductive apperceptions in logical inductions, and must be studied — *before* these — in *its* unity and its unity-creating *lawfulness*, the *inner* one.)

The *inductive* is then of course not to be despised. It gives knowledges by which we, from outside, win rules of *practical judgment* of the psychic nexuses indicated through corporeality. It also teaches us to know the rules of the *empirical coordination* of psychic and physical-corporeal. *Another research-direction* — in a sense the *reverse* of the one that starts from physical nature and natural science — would have required *particular execution*; it was hinted at in the procedure of our lecture but could unfortunately no longer come to execution, in order not to shorten the *central psychology of pure inwardness*.

The progress-direction from physical nature to *further* led to an *extension of the Idea of natural science*. But over against all natural-scientific disciplines in the narrower and wider sense stand the *Geisteswissenschaften* — the sciences of *personalities and their personal formations*. What we said in the last two lectures in phenomenological reduction about I, personality, and personal formations as products of specific I-acts makes it easy to understand that already on the ground of *natural experience* a *purely personal* research is possible — and with this it is also easy to come to an understanding of *what gives the modern Geisteswissenschaften their proper character* over against the natural sciences.

Suppose the *interest-direction onto mere nature* is taken: in one-sided, unbroken consequence, the interest directs itself on what gives the *extensive realities purely for themselves own-being*, and on a truth that *abstracts* from all relativity of binding to individual persons, person-groups, the factually experiencing and thinking communities or humanities (in any contingent particularity). All *"merely subjective"* of various senses is thereby *excluded* as muddying the *consequence of the theme*. That is the path of natural science. If the *spiritual* itself then becomes theme — while holding fast physical nature as founding theme — then a natural science directed at *animality* and *humanity* in the *extended sense* grows.

But one *could* take the *thematic direction onto spirituality* quite differently. And one *has* done so, following the (even if inconsistent) thematic directions of pre-theoretical life. As well as a pre-theoretical interest (e.g., curiosity) can be directed at *mere physical things* and their properties, just as well — indeed much more frequently — at *personal*. And this can become a *consistent theoretical interest*. Naturally: we live in the universality of streaming experience; what appears in its unanimous progress and in the mode of actuality remains, of course, as it stands. It remains — even if momentarily not in accomplishment — yet *in validity*. No phenomenological reduction takes place. Yet the *personal* interest can be so consistent and, in its consistency, drive me on, that I *never meet anything other than personal*. I direct my interest purely on personal: that is, on how persons *as persons* behave and behave to one another, how they *self-determine* and determine others, how they form friendships, marriages, clubs, etc. — how they, with the things surrounding them, shape them into *works* and how they shape the *spiritual sense usefully*: the sense of *signs*, of *signals*, of *imprinted terms*, of *trophies*, *treatises*, or of *picture-artworks*, of *religious symbols*, of *administrative buildings*, of *purposeful institutions*, etc.

If I do so, then *nowhere* is *nature as nature* — neither physical nor psychophysical — *my theme*. Naturally, the things are constantly there, the physical world is there, the animals and humans with their physical embodiment and as somatological unities. But in the *personal interest-direction*, just the *person* — or the *manifold of connected persons* — is the *theme*; and the spatial things therein are only theme as *belonging to the personal environment*. *Umwelt* (environment) is a *word of personal meaning*. *Nature*, by contrast, is a word that has *excluded all personal meanings*. Both concepts are concepts of a *thematic content* that the *theoretical attitude* has delimited.

In the personal gaze-direction onto myself, *I* am the I that *makes experiences*, that has these and those *appearance-courses* in which spatial things, my whole thingly world, *appears* — as it appears *for me* and is in validity *for me*. If I reflect on myself (e.g., for the purposes of a reflection on the life-path I am to take, or even perform an ethical reflection), then *nature in the sense of the natural scientist* is there for me only insofar as I *could*, from my appearance-correlates, set in a *nature-researching* gaze-direction — *which in this attitude cannot occur to me at all*. Similarly when I am directed at *other persons as such* or at *personal communities*: then my interest belongs to the *purely appearing and validly-given environment*, to the in pleasure-and-pain physically-or-otherwise-determining *Umwelt*. The personal direction is *consistent* and *self-sufficient*; it requires no natural-scientific supplement.

With this, the foundational status of phenomenological psychology becomes manifest. It is the *central science* both *for the natural research of the psyche* (which without phenomenological-eidetic concepts can only build inductive somatology, never penetrating to the proper essence of the psychic) and *for the personal-Geisteswissenschaftliche research* (which deals throughout with persons and their accomplishments as constituted in pure subjectivity). Both the natural-scientific and the humanistic study of the psychic ultimately rest on the phenomenological-essential analysis of pure subjectivity.

---

### **§ 45\. Retrospective Self-Reflection**

---

Husserl now closes the lecture course with a retrospective self-reflection. Inseparable from a person is *her environment* (Umwelt), even if it contains ghosts, fetishes, and the like. This is a striking final example: the *Umwelt* — the world-as-experienced-for-the-person — is *constitutively connected* to the person, *whatever* she takes to be in it. Phenomenology does not separate the person from a *true world* set against her *illusory world*; it takes the *meant world* as belonging *to* her, as her Umwelt.

Two thematic directions thereby arise: (1) the description of the *personal environment* — namely, of the persons and personal communities at issue, purely as how the environment intentionally is *theirs*. (2) The *researcher's* taking of position toward the *true being* of these environmental objects — the researcher who is *himself* a person in a personal connection with those persons (and necessarily a member of a personal total-humanity with them). The taking-of-position has, however, the same form as in the *community*: persons agree or disagree, sometimes alternate in detail, *contest* with each other, become *convinced* of truth and untruth. Sometimes they find others as subjects of *meant* objects that, for those others, belong to *their* environment but "in truth do not exist," but are *illusory*, etc.

In all this, however, the *persons themselves* and their *personal life*, working, and creating in relation to their *personal environment*, are the *theme*. And even when *natural researchers* are themes (as persons interested in nature and researching it), nature for them is *belonging to their environment* — as their *work-goal*, as their theme. They themselves are the *primary objects*. The personal researcher takes the natural researcher's nature *as itself a personal-thematic correlate*.

A person *stands bodily there*, and *I myself*, when I take myself as person — when I simply say *I* and call the other *Thou* — *have my body and have bodily existence for myself*. But in the personal attitude, I am *not posited for myself as nature-object*, as the other is not — *not as physical natural-thing* and *not as in-space-being psycho-physical real*. Rather, *my body is now, for me, the environmentally given center of the rest of the environment*, as environmental spatial-thing with somatic qualities in which *I rule* — and so as that through which I rule into the rest of the environment, etc. And likewise the other. *The personal attitude makes the I itself the theme* — as I of its having, as I that acts, and that above all has, as constantly pre-given having, *just its environment*.

A *single experiential world* unites the personal Umwelten. Persons live in the unity of a personal life (whether or not they reflect on it), and they are constantly *experiencing*. But experiencing they can be set in to *nature*: nature-experience is then a *milieu* for a thematic, practically to be carried out intentionality, theme. But the *gaze-direction onto personality and personal* can also be taken; *personal* experience can take the place of *spatial-thingly* experience as *milieu*. Different kinds of experience are *jointly executable*, because they all belong — though *not co-executable in their full thematic clarity* — to *concrete life*.

*The ultimate clarification, however, comes from the purely phenomenological attitude*, as a *purely personal*, *purely historical* one. A difficulty will be found here: as *Geisteswissenschaftler* I am already "purely" personally attuned. Were I thematically directed at nature, I would leave my theme, which is a self-enclosed theme. *Why, then, the phenomenological reduction at all*?

The historian extends his thematic interest onto a *unitary experiential horizon* that draws all the personalities and communities in question into the unity of a *humanity* to which he himself belongs. And this humanity is a humanity in *world-space* and the *spatio-temporal world itself*, which — to be sure — for him comes into account only *as personal environment*. The *Geisteswissenschaftler* in general — even if not a researcher of human personalities and their personal formations, but rather of *animal life* (animals as personal subjects in their animal environment) — is *related to the environment common to him and to us all (and to the animals)*. Every researcher knows himself as belonging to *his scientific research-community* (as a historical one self-propagating through times); and with this special community, he knows himself as woven into the community of *researchers as such* and of the *"earthly" humanity as such* in the unity of its history. In his horizon, then, lies *the all-common spatial world* into which all the personal is ordered.

That all-nature can be pursued and isolated as theoretical theme purely for itself, *natural science* shows — and in its possibility *phenomenology*. But also the *personal* research is, as *purely personal* research, *executable*, as is shown in the genuine *Geisteswissenschaften* — and as is again to be made evident through *epoche of all natural theory*. But — precisely seen — *nature in general is not in brackets*; as experienced and experientially-being, and as in its experiential character pre-theoretically self-exhibiting, *it is constantly presupposed*. It is only not *consistent experiential theme* — namely, in the consistency that wants to end in *experience-theory*. In every personal relation to one's environment lies the *world-belief*, the existence-positing of physical nature.

Thus the *one experiential world* is constantly in *validity* and *remains* so from experience; and accordingly it is the *worldly universum* for natural sciences and *Geisteswissenschaften*. The *natural researcher* does not perform a reduction with regard to *spirit*, although he excludes the spiritual from his theme. That it is constantly there *for him* remains — if he understands himself rightly — in *existence*. Likewise the *Geisteswissenschaftler* performs *no phenomenological reduction* with regard to *nature*: the thematic *exclusion* does not say the *putting-out-of-validity* of experienced nature in the sense of the *phenomenological epoche*.

The *one experiential world* — always *constituted*, pre-given in the constant universal validity of natural experience — is the underlay, the *ground* for all sciences. Although every science has its *thematic limitation*, and in our case only this is of particular significance: that natural research and spirit-research let themselves be *separated*, as in each case "pure"; on the other hand also, as I must extensively present, let themselves be *connected*, insofar as a connected inductive consideration is possible — which researches personal contents and bodily-organic constitution in inductive parallel belonging.

The things are things of nature, the persons are persons *again "in"* nature insofar as they *"have" bodies* — actual nature-bodies that *are nature* — and insofar as they themselves have parallel natural properties. Or: things, nature-objects, naturally-considered souls and persons — purely-personally-considered persons and purely-self-enclosedly-considered spirit-world — *all lies in the one experiential world*. And *this whole* (also personal spirituality in its naturally-worldly existence) — *the phenomenological epoche "switches out."*

The epoche *reduces* all this to its *phenomenality* and takes its own position *not in the world*, but *in the subjectivity for which the world is experienced* — *not* in the *personal* subjectivity (which is experienced *as in the world being*, but at the same time as *having the world environmentally*), but in *that* subjectivity which is *constantly experiencing the personality itself*. And it takes its position in the life that is *not objective personal life* but *the life subjectively encompassing this personal*, *subjectively presenting it in itself*.

*The unveiling of pure subjectivity*: the actual carrying-out of the indications begun in the lecture would include the whole *doctrine of constitution*, but, leaning on it and gradually detaching itself from it, *a general structural doctrine of singular subjectivity and intersubjectivity*. That would be a *great work*. I could thus only sketch its *plan* — and that would already be much. Conceived was this as a kind of *naïve research* that *at first asks for nothing other than what is directly exhibitable* — under exclusion of the by-"me"-experienced and posited *objective world*. But in the background, a *purpose-setting* can be driving:

(1) *I am an inductive psychologist, but I would like to know how the soul looks "inside."* I want, to be sure, *parallel* research and in general *inductive* research. But I then arrive at this: that here a great *non-inductive* — non-outwardly-related — but *immanent* psychic science grows up, to which *induction itself*, as something *inward*, belongs.

(2) Likewise I can be a *personal* researcher and interested in the *personal inwardness*. Here I would have especially to reflect on how *personality* and *personal doing*, *the creating of personal formations*, enters into the *naturally experiencing gaze*. In any case, the *personal I has an "inner" life* to be unveiled and a *self-developing personally*. And here I come again on the demand of a *deeper investigation of pure subjectivity*, and this leads to the same as above.

I could give, then, the *mere sketch*, the *presentation of a pure inner-psychology as fundamental science for the Geisteswissenschaften* — but also for a *natural psychological research* — and thereby the purely subjective consideration of *intersubjectivity* (Vorlesung 10.II.) and *the transition from the psychological reduction to the transcendental-phenomenological*. The closing acknowledgment of §45, then, is at once an acknowledgment of *what the lectures have accomplished* (the sketch of a phenomenological psychology as fundamental science) and of *what remains to be done* — the *full execution of the constitutive analyses*, the *general structural doctrine of singular and intersubjective subjectivity*, and the *systematic transition* from the *psychological* reduction to the *transcendental* one. With this, the lecture course of summer semester 1925 ends.

## **The Encyclopaedia Britannica Article — First Draft**

Husserl's encyclopedia article on "Phenomenology" — drafted in 1927 for the 14th edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica — was produced in several versions, of which the first three were drafted in collaboration with Heidegger. Hua IX preserves the First Draft, the Second Drafting Attempt, and the Fourth, Final Version, along with the closely related Amsterdam Lectures of 1928\. These texts mark a major moment in Husserl's working out of the relation between psychological and transcendental phenomenology — a relation that had been the implicit guiding theme of the 1925 lectures themselves.

---

### **Introductory Definition of Phenomenology**

Husserl opens with a brief working definition. Under *phenomenology* one understands a philosophical movement arisen at the turn of our century, which has set its sights on a *radical re-grounding* of a *scientific philosophy* and, through it, of all the sciences. But *phenomenology* also designates a *new fundamental science* serving these purposes — wherein *psychological* and *transcendental* phenomenology are to be distinguished. The double scope is established at the outset: phenomenology is *both* a movement *and* the fundamental science animating that movement; *and* this fundamental science is itself doubly determined as *psychological* and *transcendental*. The whole article will be an unfolding of this duality and its inner unity.

---

### **I. Psychological Phenomenology as "Pure" Psychology**

---

#### **§1. The Phenomenological Turn**

Every experience, and every other way in which we are consciously occupied with objects, evidently *admits a "phenomenological turn"* — a transition into a process of *phenomenological experience*. In plain perceiving, we are directed at the *perceived things*; in remembering, at the *remembered*; in thinking, at the *thoughts*; in valuing, at the *values*; in willing, at the *goals and ways*. Every such occupation has its *theme*. But *at any time* we can perform an *alteration of attitude* that turns our thematic gaze from the respective things, thoughts, values, purposes, etc., onto the *manifoldly changing "subjective ways"* in which they "*appear*," in which they are *consciously meant*. The first move is announced: the *phenomenological turn* is the redirecting of the thematic gaze from the *objective* (what is meant) to the *subjective how* (how-it-is-meant).

Take a *rigid unchanged brass cube*: perceiving means going through its cube-shape, its individual faces, edges, corners — likewise its color, gloss, and other spatio-thingly determinations — bringing the cube to notice. But instead of so going forth, we can phenomenologically attend to *how*, in what *manifoldly changing perspectives*, the *unchangedly experienced* cube *presents itself*: how, as the same, it appears differently as *near-thing* than as *far-thing*; what modes of appearance it offers in the change of orientation; how each individual determination, in the course of perceiving, presents itself as the one — in the manifold modes of appearance specially belonging to it. *No progressively-perceived thing*, no moment perceived in it as determination, fails — so this regress into reflective experience teaches — to *appear* in manifolds of *different appearances* during the perceiving; even though it is given and grasped as *the same one going through*.

In *unreflected* progressive perception, however, *only* this unity — only the thing itself — is in the *grasping gaze*; the *functioning experiential courses* remain *non-thematic, ungrasped, latent*. *Perceiving is not an empty having of perceived things*, but a *streaming experiencing of subjective appearances*, synthetically uniting in the consciousness of *the same, so-and-so being*. "Mode of appearance" is here to be taken in a *widest sense*: the appearance-modes in recollection of the cube, or in phantasy of a fully like one, are "the same" as in perception — but all are, in certain manner, *modified* (recollection-fashion or phantasy-fashion). Likewise the differences between clear and dim recollection, the gradations of clarity, of relative determinateness and indeterminateness, the differences of *time-perspective*, of *attention*, etc. — all are differences of *modes of appearance*.

Quite analogously, *thoughts, values, decisions*, etc., are in the corresponding lived-experiences (thinking, valuing, willing) *unities of hiddenly functioning appearance-modes*. The same judgment with the same subject and predicate is, in thinking, consciously in alternating *modi*: now as *evident*, now as *non-evident*; in the latter case now as in stepwise action *explicitly judged*, now *not explicitly* — as *vague occurrence*. In the transition from one to the other modus, the *identifying consciousness of the same judgment* (as the one once meant in this and then in that modus) enters in. What holds for the whole of a judgment, or even of a proof, of a whole theory, holds also of every *thematic element*: every concept, every judgment-form, etc. *Thematic unity* here too constitutes itself in *synthesis of manifolds of hidden phenomena* — which, however, can *at any time* be brought to light through *phenomenological reflection, analysis, description*.

Here arises the *Idea of a universal task*: instead of, in the *natural attitude*, *straight-on living into "the" world* as *world-children* — i.e., instead of living in the *latently functioning consciousness-life* and so having the world (and only it) as our being-field, as for-us-now-existing (from perception), as past (from recollection), as coming (from expectation) — instead of *judging, valuing, making this experiential world the field of theoretical or practical projects* — we attempt a *universal phenomenological reflection on this whole pre-theoretical, theoretical, and otherwise life*. We attempt to *systematically unveil it* and through this to understand the *how* of its *unity-accomplishments* — to understand in which manifold typical forms this life is *consciousness-of*; how it *synthetically constitutes conscious unities*; how, in what forms, these syntheses (as syntheses of *passivity* and *spontaneous activity*) run on, and thereby in particular how their unities constitute themselves as *objectively-being* or *not-being*, etc.; in short, *how a unitary experiential and knowledge-world is there for us in all-familiar ontic typology as valid and validating*.

(Husserl notes — and Heidegger marks at this point: "*Why? At first just lay out, purely in the ontological clarification, the field lying as it were behind it*.") If something experienced is given and possible only *in experiencing*, a thought *in thinking*, an insightful truth *in insightful seeing*, then the *concretely all-sided investigation* of the world being for us and scientifically insightful as valid demands the *universal phenomenological investigation of the consciousness-manifolds* in whose synthetic change it subjectively shapes itself as the world-valid-for-us and (in case) the insightful one. The task extends over the *whole* — including aesthetic and otherwise valuing and practical life — through which, for us, the concrete *life-world* is shaped (with changing content) also as a *value-world* and a *practical world*.

---

#### **§2. A New Science?**

Does such a task-setting lead to a *new science*? Corresponding to the Idea of a *universal experience directed exclusively at "subjective phenomena"*, is there an experiential field self-enclosed over against the universal *world-experience* — and with it a ground for a self-enclosed science? A *new* science, one will at first say, is *not* required, since into *psychology* as the science of the psychic belong of course all merely-subjective phenomena, all appearance-modes of the appearing. Husserl's response begins from this objection and develops the proper meaning of *pure psychology* as the discipline answering to the Idea of the phenomenological turn.

Insofar as the *intentional analysis* — as the consistently pursued analysis of *how* objectivities are *meant* in conscious life — is the proper task, this task does indeed find its first home as *psychology*. But not as *empirical inductive* psychology (which deals with humans as natural creatures, in their psychophysical reality), nor as *somatology* or as *psycho-physical experimental* psychology. Rather, as *pure* (phenomenological) psychology: a *descriptive science of the essential structures of psychic life as such*, won by *reflective analysis* under the *phenomenological reduction*. This pure psychology is *parallel to* but methodologically *distinct from* the empirical psychology that takes the psyche as natural part of the world.

---

#### **§3. Constitutive Manifolds and the Field of Pure Psychology**

The task is everywhere to pursue the *at-first-impossible-to-survey manifold of modi* in which the respective *intentional objectivities* (perceived as such, remembered as such, thought, valued as such, etc.) *constitute themselves* stage-wise *as synthetic unities of consciousness-manifolds* — to unveil the *manifold forms of syntheses* through which consciousness in general comes into the unity of one consciousness. Anything other than *"consciousness of"* — always centered on the same unity-pole *ego* — is *not to be found* here. Every psychic datum is itself only exhibitable as a *unity referring back to constituting manifolds*. Pure psychology (and so psychology in general) must begin with the *givennesses of actual experience* — that is, with *my pure I-experiences* as perceptions of, recollections of, etc. — *not* with substructions and abstractions such as *sense-data* and the like. This last point is crucial: pure psychology is to be a descriptive science of *intentional life as it actually unfolds*, not a constructive science of hypothesized "elements."

---

#### **§4. Pure Psychology as A Priori Eidetic Science**

The phenomenological or pure psychology — *as the in-itself first and in itself fully self-enclosed psychological discipline, sharply set off even from natural science* — is, for deep-lying reasons, *not* to be founded as a *factual* science but as a *purely rational ("a priori," "eidetic") science*. As such it is the *necessary foundation of every strict empirical law-science of the psychic*, exactly as the purely rational disciplines of nature (pure geometry, phoronomy, chronology, mechanics) are the foundations for every possible "exact" empirical natural science.

Just as for the founding of these, a systematic unveiling of the *essential forms of a nature as such* was needed (without which nature — more particularly, the spatial and temporal shape, motion, alteration, physical substantiality and causality — would be unthinkable), so for a scientifically *exact* psychology there is needed an *unveiling of the a priori typology* without which I (or we), consciousness, consciousness-objectivity, and so a psychic life *as such* would be unthinkable — with all the distinctions and essence-possible forms of *syntheses* inseparable from the Idea of a *singular-psychic and community-psychic whole*.

With the method of *phenomenological reduction* there combines, then, the method of *psychological essence-research as eidetic*: that is, *exclusion* not only of all judgments overstepping the pure consciousness-life (and so all natural positive sciences), but also exclusion of all *purely psychological facticity*. The latter serves only *exemplarily* as basis of *free variation of possibilities*, while the goal of the establishments is the *Invariant* emerging in the variation — the *necessary form-style* to which conceivability is bound. So, e.g., the phenomenology of spatio-thingly perception is *not* a doctrine of factually-occurring or empirically-expectable outer perceptions, but the *exhibition of the necessary system of structures* without which a *synthesis of manifold perceptions as perceptions of one and the same thing* would be unthinkable.

Among the most important *phenomenological syntheses* to be investigated are the *syntheses of corroboration* (Bewährung) — e.g., the manner in which in outer perception consciousness, in the form of unanimity and fulfillment of anticipating expectations, *appropriates evident being-belief* as consciousness of the *thing itself coming forth*. Correlatively: the investigation of the *modalizations* — of *dubitability*, of mere *probability*, and possibly *evident nullity* — as counter-forms of unanimity-syntheses. And so in *all act-types* (a *pure psychology of reason*). (Heidegger here annotates: "*Transcendental questions\!*")

---

#### **§5. The Reduction to Pure Intersubjectivity**

The *first* phenomenological reduction, the one above described, is *egological*. So phenomenology is first phenomenology of the essence-possibilities of *my alone-originarily-intuitive ego* (*egological phenomenology*). But a *phenomenology of empathy* and of the manner in which empathy *as synthesis of phenomena of my soul* can *unanimously corroboratingly* run on, and so can *indicate "foreign subjectivity" in consistent corroboration*, leads to the *extension of the phenomenological reduction* as *reduction to pure intersubjectivity*. There grows, as the *full purely-psychological phenomenology*, the *essential doctrine of a purely-psychologically-constituted community* — in whose *intersubjectively interwoven acts* (acts of *community-life*) the "objective" world (the world for everyone) *constitutes itself as "objective" nature, as cultural world*, and as world of "objectively" being communities.

---

#### **§6. Historical Sources — Locke, Brentano**

The Idea of a *pure* (not psycho-physical but purely-from-psychological-experience-drawing) psychology leads back historically to Locke's noteworthy foundational work. The development and elaboration of Locke's beginnings takes place in the *empiricist movement* proceeding from him. It culminates in the *brilliant Treatise of D. Hume*. He can be regarded as the *first sketch of a pure psychology* carried through in almost pure consistency (though only an *egological* one); but no less as the *first attempt at a phenomenological transcendental philosophy*. For *two distinguishable tendencies* mix already in Locke — the *positive-psychological* and the *transcendental-philosophical*.

Despite many deep premonitions, the so-consequential movement *founders*, and on both sides. There is lacking in it *radical reflections* on the *goal* of a pure psychology and its possibilities; it lacks the *fundamental method of phenomenological reduction*. Blindness to *consciousness as consciousness-of* (to *intentionality*) means also blindness for the tasks and special methods springing from it. Finally, empiricism lacks the *insight into the necessity of a rational doctrine of essence* of the purely-psychic sphere. All this made impossible — in subsequent times also — a radical founding of a pure psychology and so of a strict scientific psychology in general.

A *decisive impulse* was first given by *F. Brentano* (*Psychology* I, 1874\) through the *great discovery* that lay in his *revaluation of the scholastic concept of intentionality* into an *essential mark* of "psychic phenomena." But he too — still hindered by *naturalistic prejudices* — does *not* see the *problems of synthesis* and of *intentional constitution*, and finds not the way to the founding of a *pure and even eidetic* psychology in our phenomenological sense. Yet *only through his discovery* has the phenomenological movement starting at the turn of our century become possible.

The *parallelizing* of this pure and a priori psychology with the pure and a priori *natural science* (e.g., geometry) makes clear that what is at stake is not hollow "speculations a priori" but a *strictly scientific work*, performed in the frame of *concrete psychological intuition*, of *systematic shaping of purely psychological concepts and corresponding essential laws in their necessary validity*, in *infinite but systematic stage-sequence*. On the other hand, the *scientific character* of the long-known a priori sciences must not here be *presupposed*: the fundamentally different character of the psychic corresponds to a fundamentally different character of the system of its a priori and of the whole method.

---

### **II. Transcendental Phenomenology Over Against Psychological Phenomenology**

---

#### **§1. The Historical Origin of Phenomenology as Transcendental**

The new phenomenology *did not originally grow up as pure psychology* — that is, *not* in the interest of the founding of a strict scientific empirical psychology; but rather as *transcendental phenomenology*, in the interest of a *reform of philosophy into a strict science*. Transcendental and psychological phenomenology, as *different in their basic sense*, must be *most sharply kept apart*. And this, although the one passes into the other through *mere alteration of attitude* — so that on both sides "the same" phenomena and essential insights appear, but, so to speak, with *different signs* that *fundamentally alter their sense*. (Heidegger annotates "founding": "*Rational psychology\!*")

*Locke's interest* too was *primarily* not the *founding of a pure psychology*; this was to be only the *means* for a *universal solution of the "understanding"-problems*. His primary theme was thus the *riddle of the understanding-accomplishments performed in subjectivity as knowledge and science*, with their *claims to objective validity*. In a word, Locke's *Essay* wants to be the sketch of a *theory of knowledge*, a *transcendental philosophy*. He and his school are subject to the charge of "*psychologism*." If, in the sense of the *transcendental problem*, the *sense and right* of an objectivity *becoming conscious in the immanence of pure subjectivity* and *supposedly demonstrating itself in the subjective grounding-process* are in question, then this question concerns *equally all and every objective*.

Already in Descartes' *Meditations* (and precisely thereby was he the epoch-making awakener of the transcendental problematic), the insight was prepared that *whatever* we address as real-being and so-being — and so finally the whole world-universe — *is* for the *knowing ego* only *being as believed in subjective belief*, and *so-being* as *meant, thought of, etc., with this or that sense*. So the *subjective consciousness-life in pure immanence* is the *site of all sense-giving and being-positing, being-corroborating*. It thus requires a *systematic and pure self-clarification of the knower*, an *unveiling of the thinking life purely from "inner experience"*, to lay clear *what subjectivity in the hidden immanence can accomplish, and does accomplish*.

Although Locke was guided by this great insight, he *missed the principled purity* and *fell into the error of psychologism*. It was *non-sensical*, when objective-real experience and knowledge in general was *transcendentally in question*, to *presuppose* any objective experiences and knowledges — *as if* the sense and right of their objective validity *did not itself belong to the problem*. A *psychology could not be the foundation of transcendental philosophy*. Even the phenomenologically pure psychology — thematically delimited by the *psychological-phenomenological reduction* — is still *positive science*; it has the world as *pre-given ground*. The pure souls and soul-communities are *souls of presupposed (only set-aside-from-consideration) bodies in nature*. This pure psychology is *itself transcendentally problematic* — like all positive science.

For purposes of a *transcendental philosophy*, an *extended and fully universal phenomenological reduction* is required — the *transcendental* one — which does justice to the *universality of the problem* and exercises *epoche* with regard to the *entire experiential world* and all positive knowledges and sciences based on it: transforming them all into *phenomena — transcendental phenomena*. Already Descartes had touched on this reduction, insofar as he set out of play (by his methodical principle of *epoche* with regard to all that could possibly be doubted) the *being of the whole experiential world*. He already recognized that then the *ego cogito as universum of pure subjectivity* remains in play; and that this — *which must not be taken as I, this human* — is the being that is in its *immanent validity presupposed in all positive knowledge*, so the in-itself-first over against it. (Heidegger annotates "this human": "*But indeed as 'humanity' — this understood as essence of the human*.")

If we connect to this Locke's great recognition of the necessity of *concretely describing the knowing life* (according to all its fundamental kinds and stages), and Brentano's *discovery of intentionality* in its new application, and finally the *recognition of the necessity of an a priori method*, then there results the *theme and method of the present transcendental phenomenology*. Instead of the mere *reduction to the purely psychic subjectivity* (to the *purely psychic in the human in the world*), a *reduction to the transcendental subjectivity* through methodical *epoche* with regard to the *real world simpliciter* — and equally also all *ideal* objectivities ("world" of numbers, etc.). What remains in *validity* is exclusively the *universum of "transcendentally pure" subjectivity*, including all actual and possible *phenomena* of objectivities, all *appearance-modes*, *consciousness-modes*, etc., referring to them.

Only through this radical method does transcendental phenomenology avoid the *non-sense of the epistemological circle*: that of *presupposing in particular* (as if unquestioned) what is itself co-contained in the general sense of the transcendental question. Moreover, only now is the *temptation of psychologism* fully understood. *Purely psychological phenomenology coincides in a certain way, point for point, with transcendental phenomenology* — only that in every statement, under the *phenomenologically pure*, what is now understood is at one time the *psychic*, a being-layer within the naturally valid world, and at the other time the *transcendentally-subjective*, in which the *sense and being-validity of this world springs*.

The transcendental reduction discloses a *wholly new-kind*, consistently-to-be-pursued *experience* — the *transcendental*. Through it there *unveils* itself the *everywhere-in-hiddenness functioning absolute subjectivity*, with all its *transcendental life*, in whose intentional syntheses *all real and ideal objects with their positive being-validity constitute themselves*. This delivers the *thematic field of an absolute phenomenological science* — called *transcendental* because it contains in itself all *transcendental* or *reason-theoretical* questionings. On the other hand, the *transcendental theory of reason* is only in its *starting-questionings* distinguished from it, since the carrying-out of such a theory presupposes the *universal study of the whole transcendental subjectivity*. It is *one and the same a priori science*.

---

#### **§2. Positive Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology as Ontology**

All positive sciences are *sciences in transcendental naïveté*. They research — without bringing it to *understanding* — in a *one-sided attitude*, in which the *whole life transcendentally constituting the real unities of experience and knowledge remains hidden* — although, as can be purely seen only after our reductions, *all such unities according to their own knowledge-sense are only what they are as unities of transcendentally-constituting manifolds*.

Only transcendental phenomenology (and in nothing else consists its *transcendental idealism*) makes possible sciences of *full concretions* — all-sided sciences, and therein at the same time *thoroughly self-understanding and self-justifying* sciences. Its theme concerns *every possible subjectivity in general*, in whose consciousness-life — in whose constitutive experiences and knowledges — a possible objective world comes to consciousness.

The world experienced in factual experience is the theme of the *completely-conceived system of the positive factual sciences*. On the ground of *free ideal variation* of factual experience in relation to its experiential world, springs the Idea of a *possible experience in general* as experience of a *possible world*, and thereby the Idea of the possible *system of experiential sciences as a priori belonging to the unity of a possible world*. So on the one hand an *a priori ontology* which systematically researches the *essence-necessarily-to-a-possible-world-belonging structures* — all that without which a world as such is ontically unthinkable.

On the other hand, *the possible world and its ontic structures* are investigated in *phenomenological correlation-research* with regard to the *possible sense-giving and being-grounding* (as world of *possible experience*), without which it is likewise unthinkable. In this way a *carried-out transcendental phenomenology* encompasses a *universal ontology* in an extended sense: a *full, all-sided, concrete* one, in which all correlative ontological concepts are *drawn from a transcendental originality* — leaving no sense- and right-questions, in any respect, in the dark.

The historically-developed a priori sciences do not even realize the full Idea of a *positive ontology*. They concern only (and in this respect also incompletely) the *logical form* of every possible world (formal *mathesis universalis*) and the *essence-form* of a possible *physical nature*. They remain stuck in the *transcendental naïveté* and so are afflicted with those *founding-defects* that are its necessary consequence. In this *naïve shape* they function as *methodical instruments* of the corresponding *exact* factual science — more precisely, they serve to *rationalize the factual domains*, to provide the factual, through *back-relation* to the essence-structure of a possible world-factum in general, *participation* (*methexis*) in necessity, and so to under-lay the merely-inductive rules with *laws*.

The *fundamental concepts* of all positive sciences (those out of which all concepts of worldly real are built up) are at the same time the *fundamental concepts of the corresponding rational sciences*. If their *originary clarity* is lacking — and with it the recognition of their *genuine and necessary sense* — then this unclarity *transmits itself into the whole theoretical inventory of the positive sciences*. In recent time the *crisis of foundations* into which all positive sciences (empirical and a priori alike) have come; the struggle over "paradoxes," over the genuine or merely-apparent evidence of the *arithmetical, chronological, etc., fundamental concepts and principles of tradition* — has unveiled the *incompleteness of all positive sciences*. According to their whole *methodical type*, they can *no longer count as genuine sciences*: as sciences that *understand and justify themselves down to the last*, and in *all-sided insight* can sketch out their *fixed paths*. From this untenable situation, modern science can be freed only by a *phenomenological reform*.

According to the above, *transcendental phenomenology is called* to realize the Idea (concealed in it) of a *universal ontology brought through the elevation-into-the-transcendental to concrete all-sidedness*: the Idea of a *science of the system of essence-forms of every possible knowledge-world as such, and of the correlative forms of its intentional constitution*. *Phenomenology is the originary site* of the *originary-genuinely-to-be-formed and in phenomenological growth from-the-start-free-of-all-unclarity fundamental concepts* of all *a priori sciences* (as branches of the *one ontology*), and so of all the corresponding *factual sciences* of our factual world. In *systematic development* this phenomenological ontology *prepares all the so-far-not-yet-founded a priori sciences*, and therewith *the further-development of all factual sciences into "exact" (rationalized) ones*. A main step in this is the *founding of an a priori pure psychology* with a similar function for the empirical, as a priori geometry has for empirical physics. A great task contained therein lies in the *phenomenological interpretation of history* and of the *universal "sense" enclosed in its one-time-ness*.

---

#### **§3. The Encompassment of the Whole of Culture and the Overcoming of Philosophical Oppositions**

The phenomenology of the *life of mood and will* — with the intentionality proper to it and *founded* in the *phenomenology of natural experience and knowledge* — encompasses the *whole culture* according to its necessary and possible essential configurations, as well as the correlative a priori belonging to the *essence-forms of sociality*. Naturally, every *normative discipline* and every *philosophical* one (in the special sense) belong in the circle of phenomenology, as indeed historically the philosophical phenomenology arose at the clarification of the Idea of a *pure logic* and a *formal axiology and praktik*. Phenomenology is *anti-metaphysical*, insofar as it rejects every metaphysics moving in *empty-formal substructions*. (Heidegger annotates: "*And especially insofar as one understands by metaphysics the presentation of a world-picture that is carried out in the natural attitude and is cut according to it in determinate historical situations of life — of its just factual knowledge-possibilities*.") But, like all genuine philosophical problems, *all metaphysical* problems *return on phenomenological ground* and find here their genuine, *drawn-from-intuition*, transcendental shape and method.

Phenomenology is *anything but a system-philosophy in the traditional style*; rather it is a *science moving in systematic concrete investigations*. Already the *lowest stage* — the purely descriptive essential analysis of the structures of a *transcendentally-pure subjectivity* (of an *ego as monad*) — is a *vast field of concrete research work* whose results are foundational for all philosophy (and psychology). In the *systematic work of phenomenology* — progressing from intuitive givennesses to the abstract heights — there *dissolve of themselves*, without arts of argumentative dialectic and without weakly compromise-seeking, the *long-traditional ambiguous oppositions* of philosophical standpoints: such as between *rationalism (Platonism)* and *empiricism*; *subjectivism* and *objectivism*; *idealism* and *realism*; *ontologism* and *transcendentalism*; *psychologism* and *anti-psychologism*; *positivism* and *metaphysics*; *theological* and *causalistic* world-conception. *Everywhere justified motives, but everywhere half-measures or impermissible absolutizings of only relatively and abstractly justified one-sidedness*.

*Subjectivism* can only be overcome by the *most universal and most consistent subjectivism* (the *transcendental*) — which is at the same time *objectivism*, insofar as it asserts the *right of every objectivity demonstrable through unanimous experience*, but also brings to validity its *full and genuine sense* — against which the supposed realistic *objectivism* sins in its lack of understanding of *transcendental constitution*. *Empiricism* can only be overcome by the *most universal and most consistent empiricism*, which in place of the limited "experience" of the empiricists sets the *necessarily extended concept of experience* — the *originarily giving intuition* — which in all its configurations (intuition of *eidos*, *apodictic evidence*, *phenomenological essence-intuition*, etc.) demonstrates through *phenomenological clarification* the manner and form of its *right-giving*.

*Phenomenology as eidetic* is, on the other hand, *rationalistic*; but it *overcomes* the *limited dogmatic rationalism* through the *most universal* one — the *unitarily-on-transcendental-subjectivity, on I-consciousness and conscious objectivity oriented essence-research*. Likewise with the other inter-woven oppositions. In the *doctrine of genesis*, phenomenology treats the *essence-doctrine of association*, *purifies and justifies Hume's pre-discoveries*, but yet *demonstrates* that the *essence of transcendental subjectivity and its essential lawfulness is through and through teleological*. Its *transcendental idealism* contains the *natural realism* fully within itself, but demonstrates itself *not through aporetic argumentation* but through the *consequence of phenomenological work itself*. Phenomenology *fights with Kant the hollow ontologism* of concept-analyses, but is itself *ontology* — only *drawn from transcendental "experience."*

Phenomenology rejects *all philosophical Renaissance*; it is, as philosophy of *most originary and most universal self-reflection*, directed at *self-worked-out concepts, problems, and insights* — and yet receives *suggestions* from the *great of the past*, whose *pre-intuitions* it *confirms* but *transposes to the ground of concrete, hand-applying, settling research*. It demands of the phenomenologist to *renounce the ideal of an own philosophy* and yet, as a *modest worker in community with others, to live for a* philosophia perennis.

## **The Encyclopaedia Britannica Article — Second Drafting Attempt**

The Second Drafting Attempt is announced as such by Husserl. The opening Introduction and Part I (down to p.263) are explicitly noted by Husserl as *redacted by Heidegger*. The text takes a more historical-ontological tone in its opening — visibly bearing the imprint of Heidegger's preoccupation with the question of being.

---

### **Introduction: The Idea of Phenomenology and the Return to Consciousness**

---

*The All of beings* (das All des Seienden) is the field from which the *positive sciences of nature, history, \[and\] space* gain their respective *object-domains*. Directed straight-on at *the being*, they undertake in their totality the *investigation of everything that is*. So it seems that *no field of possible investigation remains for philosophy*, which since antiquity has counted as *fundamental science*. The opening installs the question that will animate the introduction: if the positive sciences research everything-that-is, what is left for philosophy?

But does not Greek philosophy already, from its decisive beginnings, make *being itself* into the object of questioning? Certainly — but *not* in order to determine this or that being, but *in order to understand the being as being* — that is, with respect to its *being*. The Heideggerian phrasing is unmistakable: the ontological difference is here in play. The *question* and the *answers* long remained entangled in unclarities. Yet already in the beginnings something *remarkable* shows itself.

Philosophy seeks the *illumination of being* on the path of a *reflection on the thinking of being* (*Parmenides*). Plato's *unveiling of the Ideas* orients itself on the *self-talk* (*logos*) of the *soul* with itself. The *Aristotelian categories* spring up in the regard of the *asserting cognition of reason*. *Descartes* *expressly* founds the *first philosophy* on the *res cogitans*. *Kant's transcendental problematic* moves in the *field of consciousness*. *Is this turning of the gaze from beings to consciousness contingent — or, in the end, demanded by the proper nature of what is constantly sought under the title* being *as problem-field of philosophy*? The historical-philosophical narrative is unmistakable: from Parmenides onward, the path of philosophy is the turning to *consciousness as the site of the question of being*.

The *fundamental clarification of the necessity of the return to consciousness*; the *radical and explicit determination of the path and the step-laws of this return*; the *principled circumscription and systematic investigation of the field of pure subjectivity opening up on this return* — *that is called phenomenology*. The *ultimate clarification of the philosophical problem of being* and the *methodical leading-back to scientifically settling philosophical work* overcome the *undefined generality and emptiness of traditional philosophizing*. Question-setting, methodical research, ordering of the problems, and systematic working-out of the truths gain through phenomenology their *first determinacy*. With this, Husserl gives — under Heidegger's editing — a Heideggerian gloss on the meaning of *phenomenology*: it is the historical fulfillment of philosophy's own founding question, the question of being.

The remainder of the Second Drafting Attempt follows broadly the architecture of the First Draft, but with two significant differences. First, the introduction frames phenomenology as the *fulfillment of the philosophical question of being* rather than as a method of *reform* of philosophy. Second, the boundary between psychological and transcendental phenomenology is articulated more carefully as a *boundary within phenomenology itself* rather than as a competition with empirical psychology. The phenomenological reduction is here presented as the *epistemic structure required by the very question of being*. The collaboration with Heidegger reaches its high-water mark in this draft; in the Fourth Final Version, Husserl will retreat to a more characteristically Husserlian framing.

---

## **The Encyclopaedia Britannica Article — Fourth, Final Version**

The Fourth Final Version is the form in which the article was eventually published (in shortened and modified form) in the Encyclopaedia Britannica. Husserl has by this point worked through several attempts at the article, and has come to a more settled formulation of the relation between psychological and transcendental phenomenology. He drops the Heideggerian framing of the Second Drafting Attempt and returns to the framing of the First Draft, but with significant refinements that reflect his deeper working-through.

---

### **§1. The Twofold Sense of Phenomenology as Psychological Phenomenology and as Transcendental Phenomenology**

---

Husserl restates the working definition: phenomenology is a philosophical movement aimed at the radical re-grounding of scientific philosophy and the sciences, and also a *new fundamental science* serving these purposes. *Psychological* and *transcendental* phenomenology are to be distinguished. But the Fourth Version now stresses the *Doppelsinn* — the *twofold sense* — of phenomenology itself: it is not merely two *different* sciences but *one* science with *two structurally distinguishable senses* unified by the *change of attitude* between them.

*Psychological phenomenology* is *pure psychology* — the eidetic science of pure psychic life, won through the *psychological-phenomenological reduction*. *Transcendental phenomenology* is the eidetic science of *transcendental subjectivity*, won through the *transcendental reduction* in which the *being of the world* (the natural positing of an objective world-existence) is *bracketed*. The two sciences have *parallel content* — they unfold the same essential structures of intentional life — but operate in *different attitudes*, with *fundamentally different senses* of validity. The articulation of *parallel content with different sense* will be the distinctive contribution of this version.

---

### **§2. Pure Natural Science and Pure Psychology**

---

*Pure psychology* is to be parallel to *pure natural science*: as pure geometry is to physics, so pure psychology is to empirical psychology. *Pure natural science* — geometry, kinematics, etc. — provides the *necessary essential framework* within which any empirical-factual investigation of nature must hold itself. So *pure psychology* is to provide the *necessary essential framework* within which any empirical-factual investigation of psychic life must hold itself. The parallel is methodologically illuminating: just as no merely-inductive empirical natural science could establish itself without the eidetic framework of geometry, no merely-inductive empirical psychology can establish itself without the eidetic framework of phenomenological psychology.

The Fourth Version emphasizes the *propaedeutic* function of pure psychology for the transcendental phenomenology. Pure psychology is *accessible* through *inner reflection* in the natural attitude — it does not require the radical attitudinal break of the transcendental reduction. It is therefore *pedagogically prior*: anyone can be brought to recognize the field of pure psychology by reflecting on his own intentional life. Once pure psychology is established as an autonomous discipline, the further step into transcendental phenomenology can be motivated.

---

### **§3. The Method of Pure Psychology (Intuition and Reflection). Intentionality as the Fundamental Character of the Psychic**

---

*A priori truths* are not to be obtained as cheaply as earlier ages supposed. They spring up as genuine essence-truths *in apodictic insight* only from the *originary sources of intuition*. These sources must be opened up in the right manner. They can become fruitful only through *methodical grasping* and through the unfolding of the *all-sidedness of their horizons*. So for the actual founding of our *leading Idea of an a priori and pure psychology*, the *return to experiencing intuition* is needed — to a *methodically treated, all-sidedly opened* intuition, in which the *psychic* comes to *originarily concrete givenness*, in which it shows itself in its *proper-essential self-hood*. Therein the *single-wise* placed before our eyes functions *exemplarily*. The gaze-direction is from the outset on the *generally maintaining-itself in the free change of examples*, and *not* on the *contingently changing*.

The peculiarity of the necessary method will unfold itself gradually. The first — *because grounding* — is here the *exemplary actual and possible experience*. And *pure psychic experience* greatly needs *method*. Every experiencing or otherwise-on-the-psychic-directed-being is performed in the manner of *reflection*. As I-subject living is *manifold-psychic through-living* — but our through-lived life is, as it were, *anonymous*: it runs on, but we are not directed at it; it is *unexperienced*, since experiencing means as much as *grasping something in its self-hood*.

In waking life we are constantly busied with *something*, now with this and now with that, and at the lowest level with the *non-psychic*: perceiving, we are busied with the perceived *windmill*, on it and only on it directed; in remembering, with the remembered; in thinking, with the thoughts; in feeling-valuing life, with the respective beautiful or otherwise valuable; in willful striving, with the goals or means. So *straight-on busied*, we *know* nothing of the *life thereby in play*, nothing of all its various peculiarities that essentially belong — *in order that we can have the respective busying themes*, in order that, e.g., things in bodily presence or recollection-fashion as past, or thoughts, values, purposes, etc., *stand in the thematic gaze* and we can so-and-so be busied with them.

It is first *reflection* — the *turning of gaze from the straight-thematic* — that brings the *psychic life itself*, the so-very-different *being-busied-with*, *thematic-having*, *conscious-having* (with all its peculiarities and possibly its backgrounds) into the *thematic gaze*. In a *reflective perceiving* and generally in *experiencing*, it is grasped and itself becomes *theme* of various busying. Naturally this *new experiencing and generally thematic-having of reflection* is itself *latent*, but again *unveilable through higher reflection*.

What in general becomes accessible to us through reflection has a *remarkable general character* — that of *consciousness-of-something*, of *having-something-conscious*, or, correlatively, of *being-conscious* — we speak of *intentionality*. This is the *essential character* of psychic life in the pregnant sense; so *inseparable from it* simpliciter. From perceiving, which reflection unveils to us, *inseparable* is this: that it is *perceiving of* such-and-such. Likewise the lived-experience of remembering is in itself *remembering of, remembering of* such-and-such; likewise *thinking of* such-and-such thoughts, *fearing before* something, *loving* something, etc.

We can also bring in the talk of *appearing* — or of having-something-appearing. Wherever we speak of *appearance*, we are referred back to *subjects to whom something appears*; at the same time also to *moments of their psychic life*, according to which an appearing as appearing-of-something *takes place*; and this latter is just *what therein appears*. In a certain way and with some exaggeration, one can say of every psychic lived-experience that in it *something appears to the respective I*, insofar as it is somehow conscious to it. *Phenomenality* — as the peculiarity of *appearing* and the *appearing-as-such* — would then, in this widened sense, be the *fundamental character of the psychic*. The pure psychology now under consideration as to its possibility would then be designated as *phenomenology*, and specifically as *a priori phenomenology*. Naturally it would also have to treat of the I-subjects (singly and in community), purely as subjects of such phenomenality — in the manner of an a priori discipline.

---

### **§4. The Significance of the Concept of Purity**

---

Here arises the first question: *how is this experience to be methodically operated, that it as pure may actually unveil what in the psychic is to be intuited as proper-essentially belonging to it*? (a) The *purity* of which we speak naturally means at first *purity from all psycho-physical*. The psychic lived-experiences count, in psychological attitude, as *real moments of the animal* — and in the first instance human — *realities*: they are, as such, always interwoven with *corporeal* in the concrete animal experience. What *is in this physical and psycho-physical experience* must thus consistently *remain out of play*; it shall not be operated. We shall practice *exclusively and purely phenomenological experience*, and only what *it gives, what in it explicates itself*, take into consideration. We shall leave *non-thematic* at the psychic what gives it *position and connection in real nature*. The same holds obviously for all considerations of *conceivable psychological possibilities* that, with all detachment from factual experienced actuality, are yet *real psychic possibilities*, givennesses of possible psychological experience.

Here lie certain difficulties: *how far* one can really consistently practice pure phenomenological experience — actual and especially possible — and so progress from self-given psychic to new psychic, coming to a unitary and *purely psychic experiential field* that *in infinitum* draws nothing essentially-foreign to the psychic into the unity of the purely-intuitive connection, of a *self-enclosed realm of possible purely phenomenological intuitions*.

(b) On the other hand, *pure experience* naturally means *freedom from all prejudices* that, *stemming from other (scientifically otherwise preferred) experiential spheres*, could make blind to what phenomenological reflection actually presents — what it actually offers, for a first purely-intuitively progressing taking-cognizance, for a first exemplary explication, and along all dimensions of implicit purely-psychic moments. Both difficulties in their combination have so strongly worked that one can dare the paradox that *in the whole psychology of modernity it has never come to an actual carrying-out of an intentional analysis* — and this, although it has wanted to be psychology *on the ground of inner experience for centuries* and occasionally also a *descriptive psychology of the pure consciousness-givennesses*. Here I cannot exempt *Brentano* himself and his school — although he has the epoch-making merit of having *introduced intentionality as the descriptive fundamental character of the psychic* and demanded a build-up of empirical psychology on the foundation of a *systematic and first purely descriptive consciousness-research*. But the *proper sense* and the *method* of a *pure consciousness-analysis* has remained *hidden* to him.

The *stable prejudices* that made unreceptive for what is here to be accomplished stem from the *natural-scientific exemplariness*. In fact, from there stems the *naturalization of the psychic*, ruling into our days, and the *supposed self-evidence of the essential identity of psychological and natural-scientific method*. Historically these prejudices appear already at the great beginners of modern psychology — at Descartes and Hobbes — in sharpest mintage in Locke's *tabula-rasa* interpretation of the soul. The phenomenological psychology, by contrast, demands as its first principle the *radical freedom from all naturalistic prejudice* and the *radical immersion in the proper essence of intentionality*.

---

## **The Amsterdam Lectures**

The Amsterdam Lectures (1928) are a culminating presentation of phenomenology to a wider audience. They follow the architecture of the Fourth Final Version of the Encyclopaedia Britannica article, but in a fuller and more polished form. They were Husserl's most carefully prepared public statement of the relation between psychological and transcendental phenomenology in the late 1920s.

---

### **Closing Section: Transcendental Phenomenology as Ontology, and Philosophy as Universal Science in Absolute Grounding**

---

In consideration of the *reach* of transcendental phenomenology, remarkable consequences result. In its systematic carrying-out it realizes the *Leibnizian Idea of a universal ontology* as systematic unity of all conceivable a priori sciences — but in a *new, dogmatism-overcoming-through-the-transcendental-phenomenological-method founding*. Phenomenology — as science of all conceivable transcendental phenomena, and specifically in the *synthetic total-configurations* in which they alone concretely possible: those of *transcendental singular subjects, connected to subject-communities* — is *eo ipso* an *a priori science of all conceivable beings*. But then not *merely* of the *All of objectively being* (in an attitude of natural positivity), but in *full concretion* of *being in general*, as it draws its being-sense and validity from the correlative intentional constitution.

This includes the *being of the transcendental subjectivity itself* — whose demonstrable essence is to be *transcendentally constituted in and for itself*. Accordingly, *a carried-through phenomenology*, over against the only-apparently-universal ontology in positivity, is the *truly universal* one — thereby *overcoming the dogmatic one-sidedness and consequent unintelligibility of the former*, while yet justifying its *legitimate kernel*.

With this, the closing horizon opens onto *philosophy as universal science in absolute grounding* — the *philosophia perennis* of the closing paragraph of the First Draft, now in fuller articulation. Phenomenology is not a *standpoint* but a *method* — and a method that, carried through, *realizes* what philosophy in its long history has been *striving* for: the *absolute, self-justifying, all-encompassing* science of being.

The Amsterdam Lectures thus close on a note that connects them back to Husserl's philosophical lineage (from Plato through Descartes, Leibniz, Locke, Hume, Kant, Brentano, and beyond), and forward to the projected complete *system* of phenomenology — which Husserl would continue working toward through the *Cartesian Meditations* (1929), the *Krisis* (1936), and the still-unpublished manuscripts of his later years. The supplementary texts of Hua IX thus stand as the *programmatic statement* of the phenomenological project at its mature articulation in the late 1920s — the project whose full execution Husserl, by §45 of the 1925 lectures, had himself acknowledged he could only *sketch*.

---

### **B. Appendices (Beilagen)**

The thirty-two Beilagen of Hua IX consist mostly of Husserl's working drafts and elaborations from the period 1925–1928, originally appended to the systematic part by Walter Biemel (the volume's editor) according to thematic relevance. Each is keyed to a section (or sections) of the lecture course or of the supplementary treatises. The titles are mostly editorial (in angle brackets). Some Beilagen are dense single-page sketches; others extend over a dozen pages of intensely worked-out analysis. The commentary below treats each Beilage thematically and shows its connection to the corresponding systematic section.

---

#### **Beilage I (to §1): Toward the Development of Modern Psychology**

The first Beilage expands the historical orientation of §1 of the systematic part. Husserl traces the *modern psychological tradition* through three decisive nodes — Locke, Descartes (with Hobbes), and Brentano — and uses this lineage to locate his own intervention. Locke is the *arch-father* of the new psychology; even where modern empirical psychology is methodologically modeled on physics, it differs from objective natural science on one decisive point: in natural science *description* is a *mere transit-piece* of objective method (the truly described is *not* the objective itself, which is only indirectly indicated), whereas in psychology the *sensation-data and other psychic life* are, according to the basic conception, *actually given in inner perception in their own being* (even if they then vanish and require psychological recollection for scientific grasping). In the long-ruling empirical psychology, the psychic is *not* sought as a *transcendent* and indirectly theoretically determined — and this is the source of the *need for a descriptive psychology*.

Husserl then traces the *fermenting element* that gradually dissolves this naturalistic style: the *epistemological developmental motives* that Descartes gave to modern philosophy at its outset, through his critique of sensibility and the reduction of world-knowledge to the *ego cogito*. In the cogito, what we call *consciousness* was introduced into philosophy, and at once for future psychology the determining concept of soul — the *pure mens*, the absolutely-for-itself being *ego enclosed in its cogitationes* — was pre-sketched. Descartes' perceptual analysis (distinguishing sensuous data from the moment of judging belief interwoven with them) further sharpens the epistemological question: *how can this immanent judgment-experience ever become a grounded judgment, demonstrating without doubt the being of an objective world for the ego*? In Leibniz' monadology, the Cartesian motif works itself out more deeply (but in the form of a metaphysical world-interpretation, not a systematic epistemology). Only in Kant is the consciousness-problem radically taken up as an epistemological theme, determining the future philosophical movements from the ground up.

In the naturalistic-sensualistic epistemology that issues from Locke and works itself out consistently in Hume, the problem of how consciousness can *mean out beyond* itself to an objectivity is given a *skeptical* answer: the meaning-out lacks rational grounding, and objectivity itself becomes a fiction emerging in the consciousness-life. Against Hume, Kant's transcendental idealism teaches that already the *experience-object of the intuitively appearing world* — that of everyday life — is a *formation constituting itself in the transcendental synthesis of imagination*; and at a higher stage the true object of Newtonian physics is a formation of *transcendental subjectivity* growing out of logical-categorial minting in the form of necessary objective validity. The decisive question Husserl puts at the close: *could the transcendental I with its deeply hidden accomplishments be kept separate from the I and the soul in the general sense*? Did all such accomplishments not have to play out in the very-difficult-to-access depths of every human psychic life? *Was that not itself a new-kind psychology*? This is the historical setting in which Brentano's reform takes its place: his *Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkt* (1874) inaugurates a different starting point from the physiology-interwoven psychology of his contemporaries, finding in *intentionality* the general descriptive essence of all givennesses of inner experience. Husserl marks Brentano's discovery as the *epoch-making* moment that makes the phenomenological movement possible.

---

#### **Beilage II (to §2): Toward the Confrontation with Dilthey**

Beilage II extends §2 by sharpening the *contrast between Brentano and Dilthey*. While Brentano's descriptive psychology focused on *psychic experiences* with regard to their psychic elements (apart from the psychophysical interweavings with corporeality), Dilthey's intervention had a *special note* that lifted it promisingly above its time. Dilthey, with the *universe of historical Geisteswissenschaften* in view, was guided by the problem of grounding a psychology that could be *fundamental science for the Geisteswissenschaften*. The traditional psychology was *individual-psychologically* attuned and, led by Cartesian dualism (and the soon-following psychophysical naturalism), focused on individual-psychic individual experiences and their inductive interweaving with the individual physical facts of the bodily corporeality. Even the descriptive-psychological reaction (the demand for a psychology on the ground of inner experience) maintained the direction onto individual experiences of the individual.

For Dilthey, by contrast — whose ruling interest spanned the universe of Geisteswissenschaften — the *individual psychic individual* was from the outset *seen as personal subject in community-life*, and *this in its unitary history* (which is naturally community-history). This yielded, even for the singular human-subject, a changed thematic direction: not onto merely individual experiences but from the outset onto the *total unity of the experience-stream* — and generally onto the *concrete whole of the purely psychic being of every psychic individuality as person*, whose being shows itself in her ways of behavior, her deeds, her creations. Dilthey emphasizes the *inner teleology*, the having-purposes and *ceaselessly-effective spiritually-accomplishing life*.

The thematic concerns not an inwardly-encapsulated person; it goes onto the *connected unity of all these individualities in the wholeness of their personal psychic life*, and onto the higher wholeness of *the unity of the all-spanning historical life*. Here Dilthey came upon the discovery that this whole connection of historical life and accomplishment is *throughout the unity of an inwardly-understandable connection*, and that to the *understanding* (*Verstehen*) here possible and to be produced in *Geisteswissenschaftlich* manner, the psychophysical mode of knowledge *contributes absolutely nothing*. He recognized that *universal spirituality is in the unity of one spirit-life, accessible from within in the unity of an inner spirit-experience*, and that in this experience all spiritual connections — especially all connections of *motivation* (the specifically spiritual causality) — themselves become *intuitable* as life-moments belonging to this life. This *life-unity* is, of course, at first *hidden*, but it is *as what it itself is, unveilable*, capable of being brought to actual intuition — much as in the singular individual her own life-past is at first hidden, but unveilable through recollection.

From this new orientation of Dilthey open up the *great problems* that he was unable to give shape to — though they are the proper problems of the principled clarification of the *Geisteswissenschaften* and of the actual installation of a *psychology of the spirit*. The Beilage marks Husserl's debt to Dilthey while also marking the methodological lacuna that *only* phenomenology — with its eidetic method and its phenomenological reduction — can close.

---

#### **Beilage III (to §2): Toward the Problem of Personal Psychology**

This Beilage extends the Dilthey discussion by working out more carefully what a *personal psychology* must include. The personal subject is constituted in social acts, in deeds and works, in the manifold positionings of will, value, and conviction that build up over a life-history. Personal psychology cannot be a psychology of episodic mental contents (in the Lockean-Humean style); it must take as its theme the *enduring personal individuality* and its *historical becoming*. The methodological move that Husserl insists on (and that Dilthey could not yet make) is the *eidetic*: a personal psychology must investigate not merely the contingent facts of this or that person's history, but the *essential structures* that any conceivable personality must exhibit. Only such an eidetic foundation can lift personal psychology out of mere biographical narration into rigorous science.

---

#### **Beilage IV (to §3): Radical Logic as Doctrine of Science**

Beilage IV (an extended treatise) elaborates §3's account of *radical logic* as *doctrine of science*. Husserl's argument is that the *idea of a doctrine of science* — what science *as such* would be in its proper essence — requires a deepening of formal logic to a *transcendental logic* that traces the *constitutive* origins of all scientific concepts in intentional life. The radical logic is not a doctrine of *formal rules of inference* (though it contains this) but a *theory of the meaning-foundations* of scientific knowing as such. It includes a theory of the *evidence-types* by which scientific knowledge can be corroborated; a theory of the *judgment-modalities* (certainty, possibility, probability, doubt) and their interweaving; a theory of the *unity of scientific theme* and its relations to other themes; and ultimately a theory of the *constitution of scientific objectivity* in the intentional life of the inquiring subject (and the community of inquirers). The radical logic is thus the *philosophical foundation* of any genuine science — including phenomenology itself.

The Beilage marks the continuity between this 1925 working out and the projected *Formale und transzendentale Logik* (1929), which would carry forward many of these themes. Husserl is here laying out, in compressed form, the program that *FTL* would develop.

---

#### **Beilage V (to §5): Phenomenology as Absolute Geisteswissenschaft**

The Beilage names phenomenology as the *absolute Geisteswissenschaft* — the *science of spirit* that, freed by the phenomenological reduction from all naturalistic prejudice, would be the *foundational* spirit-science. The Geisteswissenschaften of the natural attitude (history, sociology, philology, etc.) describe spirit *in the world*; phenomenology describes spirit *as constituting the world*. This change of attitude is the move from the *relative* to the *absolute*: the natural Geisteswissenschaften are bound to *factual* spirit; phenomenology investigates *spirit as such* in its essential structures.

The deepening shows itself in the connection to §5 (the delimitation of phenomenological psychology and the calling-into-question of the concepts of nature and spirit). Where in §5 Husserl had argued that *both* nature and spirit are, in the naturalistic attitude, unintelligible, here he argues positively that phenomenology, as the *absolute Geisteswissenschaft*, *makes spirit intelligible to itself* — and through that, makes nature intelligible too (since nature is constituted in spirit). The Beilage thus completes the polemical move of §5 with a constructive proposal.

---

#### **Beilage VI (to §11): The Peculiar Character of Natural Experience**

Beilage VI sharpens §11's distinction between Husserl's concept of experience and the Kantian one. Husserl insists that *natural experience* — the experience of ordinary world-things in the natural attitude — has its *own essential character* that must be respected before any abstraction is made. Natural experience is *not* a Kantian construction from sensuous manifold plus categorial synthesis; it is the *original presence* of the world, in which the experiencer is *already at* the things, already in their *causal style*, already *intersubjectively bound* to other experiencers. The "peculiar character" of natural experience is its *original world-relatedness* — its being *experience of one world* shared with others — and this peculiarity is what makes it the starting point for both phenomenological psychology and natural science.

---

#### **Beilage VII (to §§15 and 36): The Mistaken Conception of an Encapsulated Soul**

This Beilage corrects what Husserl calls a *mistaken conception* that has dominated psychology since Locke: the *image of the soul as encapsulated* — as an inner chamber of mental items, sealed off from the world, communicating with the world only by representational *images*. Husserl argues, with force, that this picture is *incompatible* with the phenomenology of intentional life. *Consciousness is not in itself a closed container into which the world's images are deposited*; consciousness is *intentional* — it *reaches out* to the things themselves, has the things themselves *as its proper themes*. The mistaken picture has had catastrophic consequences in psychology and epistemology alike: it grounds skepticism (since the soul could never check whether its images correspond to anything outside), and it grounds psychologism (since logical relations get reduced to associative relations among internal images).

The phenomenology of intentionality replaces the encapsulated soul with the *intentionally-open* I — the I that is *with* the things, *for* the things, *in* the world (though in a sense different from the way natural things are in the world). This corrects both §15 (where Husserl had established the inseparable interleaving of body and soul) and §36 (where he had analyzed the intentional object of perception as the noematic correlate of the noesis).

---

#### **Beilage VIII (to §15): The Somatological Structure of Objective Animals**

This Beilage works out the *somatological structure* of objective animals — that is, the structure of the *animate body* (Leib) as it is given in inner self-experience and as it appears as foreign body to others. The Leib is *zero-point of orientation*; it is the *organ of all organs*; it is the *site of kinesthesia* (the bodily sensations of position and movement that accompany every perception); it is the *seat of the I's freely-willed motility*. The Leib is thus *both* a *thing-in-the-world* (a physical body among bodies) *and* the *medium of all access* to the world. This double character — Leib as object and Leib as instrument — is the structural fact that natural science cannot capture (since it knows only the Leib as Körper, the merely physical body) and that phenomenological psychology can finally render intelligible.

---

#### **Beilage IX (to §16): The Structure of Language and the Spirituality Embodied in It**

Beilage IX takes up the *structure of language* — language as the *prime example* of cultural objectivity that *embodies spirituality* (§16). Language is in a precise sense *spirit incarnate in sensible signs*: the sound or written mark is sensuously perceived, and *in one with that* the meaning is *understood*. Language is not a sign for an inner thought (in the Lockean picture, where words are arbitrary tokens of mental images); it is the *medium* in which thought becomes *publicly accessible* and *historically transmissible*. The phenomenology of language must investigate the *layered structure* of the linguistic act: the sensuous sign, the meaning-intention that animates it, the corroborating fulfillment when meaning is fulfilled in intuition, the modes of understanding by which one subject grasps another's meaning, and the historical sedimentation by which meanings become *stable cultural acquisitions*.

This Beilage prefigures themes that would become central to Husserl's later concerns with the *origin of geometry* and the historical transmission of ideal meanings.

---

#### **Beilage X (to §§16 and 28): The Factual and the Various Subjective**

This Beilage works out a distinction central to §28's analysis: between the *factual* object of perception and the various *subjective* modes in which it appears. The same factual cube has *manifold perspectival appearances*, *changing orientations*, *modes of attention*, *modes of empty and filled intentionality*. Husserl's point is that the *factual* is *only ever* given *through* the *subjective how* — there is no access to the factual that bypasses the subjective modes of appearance. Yet the factual is not identical with any of its modes of appearance, nor with the sum of them; it is the *invariant pole* meant *through* the manifold of modes.

This double character — *factual* and *subjective how* — is the *structural fact* that all phenomenology must hold in view. To collapse the factual into the subjective is to fall into psychologism; to forget the subjective in favor of the factual is to fall into objectivism. The phenomenologist must hold both in unity.

---

#### **Beilage XI (to §24): Act, Intention, Interest, Having**

Beilage XI distinguishes four structural moments of conscious life that figure centrally in §24's analysis of the unity of the psychic: *act* (Akt — the spontaneously initiated conscious doing), *intention* (the directedness-toward), *interest* (the *practical* orientation that determines the direction of attention), and *having* (Habe — the possession of contents as acquired and disposable). These are not four kinds of consciousness but four structurally distinguishable moments of intentional life. Every act is intentional (directed at something); every act expresses an interest (a particular practical-cognitive concern); every act issues in a having (a content now possessed). The unity of the I, Husserl argues, is constituted in the interweaving of these moments: I am the unity of my acts, intentions, interests, and havings.

---

#### **Beilage XII (to §§24, 40, and 41): The Unity of the Soul**

Beilage XII addresses the most general question of §24 (and reconnected in §§40-41): what makes the *soul one*? The unity of the soul is not the unity of a thing (it has no factual properties to be summed up); it is not the unity of a process (it cannot be described in mere temporal succession); it is the unity of an *intentional life-stream* with its *passive and active accomplishments*, its *habitualities and convictions*, its *historical becoming*. The unity is *structurally* characterized by (1) the unity of *time-consciousness* (every now passes into retention, every protention emerges from the now); (2) the unity of *I-pole* (every act emanates from one I); (3) the unity of *habituality* (every act leaves a sedimentation that determines the I's further life); (4) the unity of *world-belief* (every act presupposes the constant background-validity of a world that the I inhabits).

The unity of the soul is therefore *constituted* in the interweaving of these structures, not given as an ontological fact.

---

#### **Beilage XIII (to §25): Natural Science — Abstract; Personal Geisteswissenschaft**

Beilage XIII contrasts the *abstract* character of natural science (which abstracts from the personal, the subjective, the historical) with the *concrete* character of personal Geisteswissenschaft (which takes the personal in its full thematic field). Natural science gains its objectivity by abstraction; Geisteswissenschaft gains its objectivity by remaining within the *concrete* personal-historical life. The contrast is methodological, not metaphysical: the *same* world admits both attitudes, but the *thematic contents* differ correspondingly.

---

#### **Beilage XIV (to §§28 ff.): The Significance of Synthesis for Intentionality**

Beilage XIV — one of the longest Beilagen — works out the *significance of synthesis* for understanding *intentionality*. The intentional object is *never* given in a single act of consciousness; it is *constituted in* the synthesis of *manifold acts of consciousness*. The cube is given through the synthesis of manifold perspectives; the person is given through the synthesis of manifold expressions; the mathematical theorem is given through the synthesis of manifold proof-steps. Synthesis is therefore the *structural condition* of intentionality, and the phenomenology of intentionality must be at once a *phenomenology of syntheses*.

Husserl distinguishes several kinds of synthesis: *passive* (the pre-given unification of sensuous data), *active* (the spontaneous unification by the I), *identifying* (the synthesis of the same as same across appearances), *modalizing* (the synthesis of certainty into doubt or probability), and *fulfilling* (the synthesis by which an empty intention is *fulfilled* by an intuitive presentation). Each kind has its own essential structure, and the comprehensive phenomenology of intentionality unfolds them all.

---

#### **Beilage XV (to §§28 ff.): On Intentionality**

Beilage XV further sharpens the concept of intentionality. Husserl insists that *intentionality is not a relation* between two terms (the conscious act and the object); it is a *structural feature* of conscious life itself. The act *is* directed; directedness is not added to it. The object is *in* the act *as meant*; the act *means* the object. This *immanent directedness* is what makes consciousness *consciousness of* something — and it cannot be analyzed away into anything more elementary. The grasp of intentionality as a *primitive structural feature* is the *fundamental phenomenological insight*, the insight from which all phenomenological analysis proceeds.

---

#### **Beilage XVI (to §34): True and Intentional Object**

Beilage XVI works out the relationship between *true* and *intentional* object that §34 had introduced. The *intentional* object is the *meant-as-meant*, the noematic correlate of a particular act; the *true* object is what is meant *correctly*, what stands *corroborated* in the progressive unanimous synthesis of experience. The true object is therefore *not a different object* from the intentional object — it is the intentional object *in the mode of validated being*, the object that stands corroborated in experience. Where the intentional object is *not* corroborated — where the experience fails to unfold in unanimity — the intentional object remains an intentional object but *fails to be* a true one: it is *meant but illusory*.

---

#### **Beilage XVII (to §36): On the Doctrine of Intentional Constitution**

Beilage XVII addresses the *doctrine of intentional constitution* — the systematic theory of *how* objects of various types *are constituted* in intentional life. Constitution is not an *act* (as if the I created the object); it is the *structure of intentional accomplishment* by which an object *comes to be given* as a unitary, identifiable, determinable something *for* consciousness. The doctrine of constitution must distinguish *what is constituted in what manner*: physical things constitute themselves through perspectives in the synthesis of unanimous perception; persons constitute themselves through expressive bodies and intersubjective understanding; cultural objects constitute themselves through purposeful production and subsequent interpretation; ideal objects (numbers, theorems) constitute themselves through repeated identifying acts and their sedimentation.

This doctrine is the *centerpiece* of mature phenomenology, and the Beilage gestures toward what would become its systematic working-out in *Cartesian Meditations* and the constitution-manuscripts that fill many later Husserliana volumes.

---

#### **Beilage XVIII (to §37): The Winning of the Field of Pure Subjectivity**

Beilage XVIII addresses the *winning of the field of pure subjectivity* through the phenomenological reduction (§37). The field is *not* discovered as a pre-existing region of being; it is *won* through a methodological operation — the *bracketing* of the natural positing — which redirects the gaze from the world to consciousness. The field, once won, proves itself an *immense* domain: it includes everything that is given to consciousness *as so given*, in all the variety of intentional acts and their noematic correlates, in their syntheses and modalizations, in their passive and active accomplishments. The Beilage stresses that this field is *infinitely rich*, not a thin domain of "subjective contents," and that it requires *systematic*, *patient*, *concrete* investigation.

---

#### **Beilage XIX (to §37): Toward the Systematic Description of the Realm of Pure Subjectivity**

Beilage XIX outlines a *systematic description* of the realm of pure subjectivity. The systematic order goes: (1) hyletic data and their passive synthesis; (2) outer perception and its layered intentional structure; (3) the body and its kinesthetic structure; (4) the time-consciousness with retention, primal impression, and protention; (5) recollection and expectation; (6) the I-pole and its habitualities; (7) the syntheses of identification, evidence, and modalization; (8) imagination and quasi-positional consciousness; (9) intersubjective experience and empathy; (10) the constitution of nature, of persons, of cultural objects, of communities. This systematic order is a *guideline* — not a rigid sequence, since each layer presupposes and is presupposed by others — but a guideline that makes possible *systematic* rather than scattered phenomenological work.

---

#### **Beilage XX (to §37): Toward the Problem of Originary Experience**

Beilage XX addresses what *originary experience* is. *Originary* (originär) is a technical term in Husserl's vocabulary: it designates the *most direct* mode of givenness, the mode in which something is given *as itself*, *bodily*, *in its primal presence*. Originary experience is to be distinguished from *re-presentations* (recollection, expectation, imagination), which give their objects *not* as themselves but *as previously, future-coming, or imagined*. The phenomenology of originary experience must identify what is originary in each region of being: perception is originary for spatial things, recollection is originary for one's own past, empathy is originary for the foreign psychic life, ideation is originary for essences. Each originary mode has its own essential structure, and the typology of originary modes is fundamental to phenomenology.

---

#### **Beilage XXI (to §37): Psychological and Transcendental Reduction**

Beilage XXI works out the *distinction* — and *relation* — between the *psychological* and the *transcendental* reduction that §37 had introduced. Both reductions are *bracketings*, but they bracket different things. The *psychological* reduction brackets the *world-positing* but *remains within the natural attitude* (it remains a science of *psychic* subjectivity within the world). The *transcendental* reduction brackets the *being of the world altogether* and turns to *transcendental subjectivity* as the *absolute ground* of all positing. The two reductions yield *parallel sciences* with overlapping content but fundamentally different *senses* of validity.

The relation between them is *propaedeutic*: the psychological reduction is *easier* to motivate (since it remains within the natural attitude); through it, the *field* of intentional life is first opened up. Once this field is clear, the *transcendental* reduction can be carried out as a *further radicalization* of the bracketing. This is the *indirect path* to transcendental phenomenology that Husserl recommended in the Amsterdam Lectures.

---

#### **Beilage XXII (to §37): Empirical and Rational Psychology**

Beilage XXII is among the longest Beilagen. It develops the architectonic distinction between *empirical* and *rational* (eidetic) psychology. *Empirical* psychology investigates the *factual* psychic life of factual humans and animals; it proceeds by inductive generalization from cases. *Rational* psychology investigates the *essential* structures of psychic life as such; it proceeds by ideation, by Wesensschau, by free imaginative variation from cases (now used only as exemplars) to grasp the invariant.

The relation is methodological-foundational: rational psychology provides the *eidetic framework* within which empirical psychology must work. Without this framework, empirical psychology can only collect facts without understanding their structural conditions. With this framework, empirical psychology becomes *rigorous* — not merely a collection of inductions but a *science* in the strict sense.

Husserl insists that *both* are needed, and that they stand to each other as pure and applied mathematics stand to each other (or pure geometry to factual physics). The Beilage emphasizes that the rational psychology Husserl is calling for is *new*: it has never been *systematically* worked out (Brentano's descriptive psychology was a beginning, but stopped short of full eidetic method), and *modern empirical psychology* is therefore in the *exact* situation of pre-Galilean natural science — collecting data without proper conceptual framework.

---

#### **Beilage XXIII (to §40): I and Time**

Beilage XXIII connects the I-pole (§§41-42) with the time-consciousness (§40). The I, as the bearer of self-founded convictions and habitualities, is *temporal*: it has a *history*, it *endures*, it *changes* while *remaining identical*. The structure of this enduring identity is given by the structure of time-consciousness: the *now* in which the I is *currently active*; the *retention* in which the I's past acts are still *passively present*; the *protention* in which the I's coming acts are *anticipated*. The I is the *unitary pole* that *runs through* this temporal articulation, gathering its past into itself (as habituality), engaging its present (as actuality), and orienting itself to its future (as projected purpose).

The Beilage closes a gap between the time-consciousness analyses of §40 (which focused on the time-stream of intentional life) and the I-analyses of §§41-42 (which focused on the I-pole). The two sets of analyses are *one* — the I is *temporal*, and time is *I-related*.

---

#### **Beilage XXIV (to §41): The Thematizing Functions of the I**

Beilage XXIV works out the *thematizing functions* of the I that §41 had pointed to. The I does not merely *experience* — it *thematizes*: it brings something *into the gaze*, *holds it in grip*, *develops* it, *abandons* it, *returns* to it. These thematizing functions are *I-acts* in the strict sense, distinguished from passive moments of consciousness. The thematizing functions include: *noticing* (bringing something out of the background into the focused attention); *grasping* (taking something as *theme* of inquiry); *holding-in-grip* (maintaining a theme across changing modes of consideration); *releasing* (letting a theme fall back into the background); *taking-up-again* (re-thematizing what was released).

The thematic life of the I is thus *structurally articulated*; it is not a continuous undifferentiated stream but a *patterned* succession of thematic operations. The phenomenology of thematizing is essential for understanding the *active* dimension of intentional life.

---

#### **Beilage XXV (to §§41 and 43): On the Essence of the Monad**

Beilage XXV addresses *the essence of the monad* — the concept introduced at §43. The monad is, Husserl insists, *not* a metaphysical term as in Leibniz; it is a *phenomenological-descriptive* term for the *concrete unity of pure subjectivity*. The essence of the monad includes: (1) the *unity of the time-stream*; (2) the unity of the *I-pole* with its habitualities; (3) the unity of the *intentional-noematic* correlates that the monad has-as-its-world; (4) the unity of the *passive and active* genesis through which the monad has come to be what it is.

The monad is *concrete* (a *complete* unity of all the abstract moments of consciousness); it is *individual* (each monad has its own history); it is *open* (it can enter into intersubjective community with other monads through empathy and shared meaning). The monad is, in short, the *most concrete unit* of phenomenological analysis — what *every* phenomenological description ultimately refers back to.

---

#### **Beilage XXVI (to §43): On the Monad**

Beilage XXVI extends Beilage XXV with further considerations on the monad's essential structures. The monad has *passive* and *active* genesis: in passive genesis, the monad's intentional life unfolds without explicit I-acts (sense-data flow in, are unified, retained, modified); in active genesis, the I performs deliberate acts (judging, deciding, valuing). Both kinds of genesis are *constitutive* — both contribute to the monad's *becoming what it is*. The phenomenology of the monad is therefore *both* static (analyzing the relatively-stable structural moments) *and* genetic (analyzing the processes by which these structural moments come to be). Static and genetic phenomenology are complementary, not in conflict.

---

#### **Beilage XXVII (to §44): Subjectivity and the Natural Spirit-World**

Beilage XXVII addresses §44's claim that phenomenological psychology is foundational *both* for the natural research of the psyche *and* for the personal-Geisteswissenschaftliche research. The Beilage develops what this means concretely. *Personal* Geisteswissenschaft takes its theme in the *natural spirit-world* — the world of persons, communities, cultures, histories — as it is *given in the natural attitude*. The personal Geisteswissenschaftler does *not* perform a phenomenological reduction; he remains in the natural attitude, but with the *interest* directed onto the personal rather than the natural. Yet his work is *grounded* in phenomenological-psychological insights: the very *concepts* with which he works (person, motivation, community, history, work, etc.) are *clarified* by phenomenological analysis. Without this clarification, his concepts remain vague and his conclusions unsteady. With this clarification, his work becomes *rigorous* — gains its proper essential framework.

The Beilage thus argues for the *concrete usefulness* of phenomenological psychology *for* the existing Geisteswissenschaften — not as replacement but as *foundation*.

---

#### **Beilage XXVIII (to §45): Summarizing Overview of the Lectures**

Beilage XXVIII is the *long retrospective summary* of the entire lecture course. Husserl, looking back, identifies the major *moves* the lectures have made: (1) the *historical* introduction situating phenomenology against the lineage Locke–Hume–Kant–Brentano–Dilthey; (2) the *delimitation* of phenomenological psychology against natural science and against the existing Geisteswissenschaften; (3) the working-out of the *experiential world* as pre-given soil for both natural science and the spirit-sciences; (4) the *method of essential intuition* (Wesensschau) as the means of grasping the eidetic structures of the world and of psychic life; (5) the *radical articulation* of the world into nature and spirit through the abstraction-method; (6) the *analysis of perception* as the entry into pure subjectivity; (7) the *intentional structure* of consciousness as the fundamental discovery; (8) the *I-pole* and *habituality* as the structural moments of the personal subject; (9) the *monad* as the concrete unity of subjective life; (10) the transition from *solus-ipse* phenomenology to *intersubjective* phenomenology.

The Beilage also marks the *foreshortenings* of the lectures: the time-consciousness was only briefly treated; the intersubjectivity-analyses were only sketched; the constitution-doctrine was not systematically developed. Husserl acknowledges these limits and points to the work that remains.

The Beilage also includes a crucial passage on the *apriori of We and our Umwelt* — *Das Apriori Wir und unsere Umwelt geht allem Apriori vorher und schließt alles in sich*: the a priori of We-and-our-Umwelt *precedes* all a priori and contains everything in itself. This formulation anticipates the *Krisis* and is one of the most powerful single statements of the late-Husserlian position: the *common world of an intersubjective We* is the *most originary* phenomenological datum, the *unsurpassable* horizon within which all phenomenological work takes place.

---

#### **Beilage XXIX (to p. 278): Introduction to the Third Version of the Encyclopaedia Britannica Article**

This Beilage preserves an attempted introduction (now editorially identified as the "third version") that Husserl ultimately did not retain in the final form of the article. It is significant for marking Husserl's continued *re-working* of the introduction: the proper *opening* of the article — how phenomenology is first introduced to the reader — was for Husserl a perpetually-renewed problem, since it determined the *entire pedagogical structure* of the presentation. The Beilage gives a sense of the alternatives Husserl considered.

---

#### **Beilage XXX (to the III. Part of the EB Article): Phenomenology and Anthropology**

This Beilage takes up the *relation between phenomenology and anthropology* — a topic that pressed itself on Husserl with increasing urgency in the late 1920s under the impact of Heidegger and Scheler. Husserl insists that phenomenology is *not* anthropology — phenomenology investigates the essential structures of pure subjectivity (whether singular or intersubjective), not the factual structures of the *human* as a particular kind of being. To collapse phenomenology into anthropology would be to *psychologize* it — and to lose the *transcendental* dimension. Nevertheless, anthropology *can* be a legitimate field, *if* it is properly understood as a *regional ontology* of *human being* (in its essential structures, not its factual contingencies), founded on the phenomenology of pure subjectivity.

---

#### **Beilage XXXI (to the Amsterdam Lectures): Phenomenology of Empathy**

Beilage XXXI addresses the *phenomenology of empathy* — the foundational analysis by which the foreign I is given to me as a *foreign* I. Husserl's analysis is intricate: empathy is *not* an inference (I do not *infer* the other's psychic life from his bodily behavior); it is a *direct* but *appresentative* intentional act in which the other's body is *given as expressing* an inner life that is *not directly accessible to me*. The other's psychic life is *appresented*: it is *meant-along-with* but not *originarily given*. This double character — directness yet inaccessibility — is the structural peculiarity of empathy.

Through empathy, the *solus-ipse* phenomenology is broken open into *intersubjective* phenomenology. The world that is constituted in *my* monad is *the same* world that is constituted in *foreign* monads; the *one objective world* is *the world for the community of monads*. Empathy is therefore the *foundation* of intersubjectivity, of community, of objectivity, and ultimately of the *one* world that we all share.

---

#### **Beilage XXXII (to the Amsterdam Lectures): Individuality and Generality in the Genesis**

The closing Beilage addresses the relation between *individuality* and *generality* in the *genesis* of conscious life. Each monad is *individual* — it has its own particular history, its own habitualities, its own world-experience. Yet each monad also exhibits *general* features that any monad of the relevant kind must exhibit — *eidetic* features that are the proper theme of eidetic phenomenology. The relation between individuality and generality in the genesis is therefore: *the individual genesis instantiates eidetic laws of genesis*. Just as every individual triangle instantiates the eidetic laws of triangles, every individual monad instantiates the eidetic laws of monadic genesis (associative laws, laws of motivation, laws of habituality-formation, etc.).

Genetic phenomenology has the task of *uncovering these eidetic laws of genesis* — laws that hold for *any* conceivable monad and so for *any* conceivable subjective life. This program would be continued through *Analysen zur passiven Synthesis* (Hua XI) and the later genetic manuscripts. The Hua IX Beilagen end, then, with a programmatic statement of what remains to be done: the eidetic theory of monadic genesis as the deepest layer of phenomenological work.

---

## **Closing Note on Hua IX as a Whole**

Hua IX, the *Phänomenologische Psychologie* of summer semester 1925, stands as Husserl's most extended public *introduction* to phenomenology in the period between *Ideas I* (1913) and the *Cartesian Meditations* (1929). Where *Ideas I* introduced phenomenology *directly* in its transcendental sense (with the eidetic-pure ego of the third reduction) — and where the *Cartesian Meditations* would later introduce phenomenology through the radical Cartesian doubt — *Phänomenologische Psychologie* introduces phenomenology through the *propaedeutic* path of *pure psychology*. This middle path between Cartesian rigor and Idea-I directness reflects Husserl's pedagogical experimentation in the mid-1920s — a search for the *right way to introduce* phenomenology to readers and listeners coming from the natural attitude.

The lectures are also remarkable for the *systematic articulation* of the field that they sketch. Husserl moves through: the historical lineage of psychology; the radical method of essential intuition; the structural analysis of the experiential world; the abstraction-procedure yielding pure physis; the analysis of perception with its hyle and intentional function, its immanence and transcendence, its empty horizons and its primal presence; the time-consciousness and its retention-protention structure; the I-pole and its habitualities; the monad as concrete unity; and the transition from singular to intersubjective phenomenology. The systematic part is a *condensed but complete* sketch of mature Husserlian phenomenology — a sketch that, as Husserl himself acknowledges at §45, would require the *full execution* of the constitutive analyses to be carried out beyond what these lectures could deliver.

The Ergänzende Texte (the three EB drafts, the Amsterdam Lectures, and the 32 Beilagen) extend the systematic part with: focused historical analyses (Beilagen I–II); methodological clarifications of the relation between psychological and transcendental phenomenology (the EB drafts, Amsterdam Lectures, Beilagen XXI); deepenings of particular structural analyses (Beilagen on intentionality, synthesis, monad, empathy, time); and forward-pointers to work yet to come (Beilage XXII on rational psychology, Beilage XXXII on genetic phenomenology). Taken together, the volume offers a *more complete* picture of the mature Husserlian phenomenology than any other single Husserliana volume — a picture in which the *psychological* gateway is foregrounded as the *propaedeutic* path to the *transcendental* foundation.

With this, the paragraph-by-paragraph commentary on Hua IX is complete.

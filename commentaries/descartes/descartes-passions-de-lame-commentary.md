# Descartes, *The Passions of the Soul* — Commentary

The text is the 1649 edition as reproduced in Adam–Tannery XI, 301–488: a Preface of four letters with a short notice (AT XI 301–326), then 212 numbered articles in three Parts. The paragraph is the unit in the Preface and the article in the treatise; both are cited by AT XI page. The Latin version of 1650, *Passiones animae* by "H. D. M.", was made without Descartes' knowledge, and AT prints only its translator's preface (AT XI 489–490). Latin readings are therefore discussed where the volume supplies them: Descartes' own Latin quoted in the Preface, the translator's choice of *passiones* (art. 1), his theological gloss (reply of 14 August 1649), and the editors' note at art. 160. Five misreadings are guarded against throughout. First, "passion" has two senses: any perception whatever, insofar as the soul receives it (art. 17), and the narrow class of perceptions the soul refers to itself (art. 25). Descartes moves between them on purpose, and collapsing them makes the definition of art. 27 look either trivial or arbitrary. Second, the physiology of spirits, heart-heat and gland gives the passion's proximate cause, not its nature. A passion is a thought; the mechanism explains why it arises and why it persists. The prefatory letter of 14 August 1649 says the passions are treated "as a physicist" (*en physicien*), and this means a causal account of something that remains mental, not a reduction of it. Third, the small gland is where the soul exercises its functions immediately, not where it is located; art. 30 makes the soul joined to the whole body and without relation to extension. Fourth, the soul has no parts. What tradition calls the conflict of reason and appetite is a conflict between the soul and the body pushing the same gland, and the treatise is built against the scholastic division into sensitive and rational, concupiscible and irascible powers. Fifth, mastery of the passions is not their extirpation. It works indirectly, through representation and habit, and the work ends (arts. 211–212) by calling the passions good in their use.

## Preface

### Notice from One of the Author’s Friends

Notice (AT XI 301). The friend who received the book from Descartes, with permission to print it and add whatever preface he liked, will write no preface of his own. He will print instead the letters he wrote to Descartes to obtain it, since they contain several things the public has an interest in knowing. Adam and Tannery argue in their editorial notice (AT XI 294–297) that the friend is probably the abbé Picot, not Clerselier as Baillet claimed. The first letter quotes the preface Descartes had addressed to Picot as translator of the *Principles*, and only the translator had so direct a stake in seeing the *Principles* continued. The editors also raise the possibility that the letters were "somewhat arranged for the public," since their chronology fits badly with a letter to Clerselier of April 1649 that already announces the printing and an enlargement by a third. The frame matters more than the attribution. The reader enters a treatise on the passions through a staged exchange in which the author's own passions are put on trial: his modesty, his supposed indignation at public ingratitude, his ambition for posthumous glory and his negligence. Before Descartes defines a single passion, the reader sees him accused of several and watches him decline the charges.

### First Letter, to Monsieur Descartes

Paragraph 1 (AT XI 301–305). The friend had been glad to see Descartes in Paris the previous summer (1648), thinking he had come to settle where experiments were easiest to make and so to finish the treatises promised to the public. Descartes' return to Holland ended that hope, and the friend is still angry that Descartes would not let him see the treatise on the passions he is said to have written. He recalls the preface to the French *Principles* of 1647 (AT IX-2, 17), where Descartes said that finishing philosophy would need experiments too costly for a private person, that he did not expect public aid, and that posterity would excuse him if he worked henceforth only for his own instruction. The friend fears Descartes now means to withhold his remaining discoveries, and he sets out to "torment" him in revenge for the refused treatise. He does not believe money is the obstacle: those who dispose of public funds would grant whatever Descartes asked if he explained the matter properly. Descartes has always lived as if he would refuse help even if offered, yet he claims posterity will excuse him for lacking it. The friend concludes that Descartes is negligent, perhaps lacks the courage to hope to finish, and is vain enough to want posterity to believe he failed through the age's ingratitude, not his own fault. His ambition gains either way, since resting earns as much reputation as working, perhaps more, "because the good one possesses is usually less esteemed than the good one desires or regrets." To remove this excuse, the friend will print the letter, and he explains that a passing mention in a preface is not a request: the public notices only those who make a noise and promise much. The letter is a moral diagnosis built from passions the treatise will later analyse. Indignation at ingratitude (arts. 194–195), ambition and glory (art. 204), cowardice (art. 174) and the overvaluing of absent goods (arts. 67, 209) are all imputed to the author in advance. The stake is institutional as well as personal: the friend presses the Baconian claim that natural philosophy is a public enterprise requiring public money, and the *Passions* arrives as a fragment of the unfinished physics. *Principles* IV, 188 had promised treatises on animals and on man, and the treatise on the passions is the part of that promise Descartes chose to deliver.

Paragraph 2 (AT XI 305–306). The friend anticipates the reply that Descartes' temperament keeps him from asking for anything or speaking well of himself, since the first seems a mark of baseness and the second of pride. This temperament should be corrected, because it comes from error and weakness, not from honest modesty. Requests are shameful only when made for one's own need to people one has no right to ask; requests that serve those asked bring glory, especially when one has already given them more than one asks. Speaking well of oneself is ridiculous pride when the claims are false and contemptible vanity when they are true but made for show and benefit no one. When others need to know them, keeping silent is "a vicious humility, which is a species of cowardice and weakness." The public needs to know what Descartes has found so that it will help him find more, and what he has already given is worth incomparably more than anything he could ask. The paragraph anticipates the moral vocabulary of Part Three almost word for word. Vicious humility as a form of weakness is the definition of art. 159, and the pairing of baseness and pride as the two vices flanking a just self-estimate is the structure of arts. 155–159. The friend had not seen the treatise, so either Descartes' moral vocabulary was already current among his intimates, or the editors' suggestion that the letters were arranged for print is correct. Either way, the Preface teaches the reader to see self-esteem as a question of just valuation, the question art. 152 will answer.

Paragraph 3 (AT XI 306–308). Descartes may say his works speak for themselves and that promises and boasts suit charlatans, not a man of honour seeking truth. But charlatans are blameworthy because their claims are false and unprovable, not because they are great; Descartes' claims are true and proved in his writings, so propriety permits them and charity requires them. His writings speak only to those who examine them carefully, and statesmen have no leisure to read them. Praise reaching them is discounted, because the most extravagant terms are routinely spent on mediocre people, whereas a man of good birth who speaks of himself, and is known not to be a charlatan, is listened to and his words taken in their true sense. The friend therefore states what Descartes should say: that no one but he, at least among those whose writings survive, has discovered the true principles and first causes of everything produced in nature; that he has already explained the most common phenomena by them; and that with more particular observations he could explain minerals, plants, animals and everything useful to medicine and the other arts. All peoples should contribute, and Descartes should proclaim this in the titles of his books. The argument rests on an economics of praise. Flattery has inflated the currency (art. 157 will make flattery the cause of groundless pride), so only self-testimony by a credible person still carries value. The appeal to good birth anticipates the debate of art. 161 on whether generosity depends on birth. The programme announced, minerals, plants, animals and man, is the plan of *Principles* IV, and the treatise on the passions is the part on man that Descartes completed.

Paragraph 4 (AT XI 308–310). The friend wants three points made known to everyone: that physics has endless useful things still to discover, that they may be expected from Descartes, and that he will find more the more facilities he has for experiments. The first point is needed because most people think nothing better can be found than what the ancients found, and many do not even know what physics is or what it is for. Excessive respect for antiquity harms the sciences. The peoples of America are of as ancient an origin as Europeans and could as well rest on their fathers' wisdom, yet they have fewer conveniences; the nations most attached to tradition are the most ignorant and rough. Recent inventions such as the compass, printing and the telescope show how much remains to know. Medicine shows it best. God has surely supplied the earth with everything needed to keep people healthy to extreme old age, and knowledge of this was once the chief study of kings and sages, yet physicians cannot recognize minor illnesses and often worsen them. For those who do not know what physics is, it suffices to say it is the science that should teach the nature of man and of all foods and remedies so well that one could avoid every illness. The argument is the moderns' case against the ancients, the polemic that opens art. 1 of the treatise. Medicine as the chief fruit of physics repeats *Discours* VI, where preserving health is called the first good of this life. The treatise belongs to that project: its physiology of the passions is also a hygiene, as arts. 97, 141 and 211 show in their attention to health.

Paragraph 5 (AT XI 310–312). Above all the world must know that Descartes has proved this of himself, and for that he must do some violence to his temperament and drop the excessive modesty that has kept him from saying what he must. This will not embroil him with the learned. Men of letters and jurists have no interest in the matter. Theologians and physicians have none except as philosophers, because theology does not depend on physics at all, and medicine as practised by its best members follows rules taught by long experience rather than staking lives on the uncertain reasonings of the School. Only philosophers remain, and all the intelligent ones are already on his side. Only pedants will take offence, and since they are the laughing-stock of honest people and already his enemies, he need not care; if he made himself valued as he should, they would be too far below him to dare attack. Nothing would serve better than what he wrote in the letter to Father Dinet, Provincial of the French Jesuits, printed seven years earlier. The separation of theology from physics is a strategic safe-conduct, and it matters for the treatise: a physics of the passions can proceed without encroaching on theology's account of sin. The Latin translator's preface makes this boundary explicit (see the reply of 14 August below). "Pedants" points to the Utrecht and Leiden controversies, and the letter to Dinet (1642), appended to the Seventh Objections, was Descartes' public defence against Bourdin and Voetius.

Paragraph 6 (AT XI 312). The friend quotes Descartes' own Latin from the letter to Dinet (AT VII 579–580). In his *Essais*, Descartes wrote, he explained not one or two but more than six hundred questions that no one before him had explained in this way. Many have looked at his writings askance and tried every means to refute them, yet no one, so far as he knows, has found anything untrue in them. If the questions solved by all other philosophies over the centuries were counted, they would perhaps be neither so many nor so notable. He professes that no solution to any question ever given by principles peculiar to Peripatetic philosophy cannot be shown by him to be illegitimate and false, and he invites a test on a few selected questions. This is the one passage of Latin in the work, and it is Descartes' own, so the French paraphrase in the next paragraph can be checked against it. The Latin claim *quae sic a nullo ante me fuerant explicatae* says the questions were explained "in this way" by no one before him, a claim about the kind of explanation, mechanical explanation from principles, not about its quality. The friend renders it as questions "that no one before you had known how to explain so well," which turns a claim of method into a claim of superiority. The French also softens the challenge. The Latin asserts that every Peripatetic solution can be shown *illegitimam & falsam*, invalid in form and false in content, while the French has Descartes defying anyone to name a solution in which he cannot show "some error." The shifts suit the friend's purpose, which is reputation, not method, but they blur what the treatise's own opening claims (art. 1): not that Descartes will do better what the ancients did, but that he will explain the passions by a different kind of principle.

Paragraph 7 (AT XI 312–318). Despite his modesty, the friend continues, the force of truth made Descartes write this, and since it was addressed to a Jesuit Provincial and printed seven years ago, the ablest of that body would have refuted it if it were not wholly true or at least arguable. Their silence shows that all the learned know School physics contains nothing that is not doubtful, and in a science, being doubtful is hardly better than being false, since a science must be certain and demonstrative. Descartes' writings contain only demonstrations on matters no one knew before. Statesmen judge the future by the past, and having seen School philosophy produce only pedants, they will not expect better from his unless shown that his is true and the other false. Useful inventions have come by chance and practice, never through School physics; if any science contributed, it was mathematics, the only human science that has yielded indubitable truths. Philosophers want mathematics to be part of physics, but the truth is the reverse: true physics is part of mathematics. Descartes' excellence in mathematics is conceded even by the envious, his *Géométrie* seems to have gathered the whole harvest, and his method is infallible. He has already shown in the *Principles* that physics is part of mathematics by explaining all sensible qualities through size, figure and motion alone, and the experience of every age confirms that no true knowledge of corporeal nature was ever reached by non-mathematical principles. A scruple remains, since famous mathematicians do not always understand Descartes' physics; but reputation in mathematics comes from reading books, which tires and even injures the imagination and breeds prejudice, while understanding another's truths needs only a mind free of prejudice and attention. Finding a few isolated truths is not hard, as Thales, Pythagoras, Archimedes, Gilbert, Kepler, Galileo and Harvey did; nor is it hard to imagine a body of philosophy less monstrous than Aristotle's, as some in this century have. But to form one containing only truths demonstrated as clearly as mathematics is so rare that in more than fifty centuries only Descartes has shown he can. As an architect who has laid the foundations and raised the main walls can surely finish, so the *Principles* show that his method suffices for the rest of physics. Three points stand out. "Doubtful is hardly better than false" is the standard of *Regulae* II and the First Meditation applied to a whole discipline. The inversion of the scholastic order of sciences, physics as part of mathematics rather than mathematics as a tool subordinate to physics, is the programme the treatise applies to the passions: in Part One, they are explained by the size, shape and motion of the spirits. The list of discoverers of isolated truths includes Harvey, which matches art. 7's partial endorsement: circulation accepted, the theory of the heartbeat rejected. The unnamed builders of a "less monstrous" philosophy on probable conjectures are best read as Gassendi and the atomists.

Paragraph 8 (AT XI 318–321). Yet however skilled an architect, he cannot finish without materials, and however perfect the method, it cannot pursue the explanation of natural causes without the experiments that determine their effects. This is the third point, which most people fail to grasp. Those who discourse on nature from their study can say how they would have made the world if God had given them the charge, writing chimeras as suited to the weakness of their minds as the beauty of the universe is to the infinite power of its author. Descartes' method does not teach divining, only deducing from given things all the truths deducible from them, and in physics the given things can only be experiments. These are of two kinds: easy ones, which depend only on reflection on what presents itself to the senses, and rare ones, which require study and expense. Descartes has already drawn from his writings everything deducible from the easy ones and from the rare ones he could learn from books, explaining sensible qualities, the common bodies, the heavens, the magnet and many chemical observations. Nothing more can be expected until he has more experiments. The friend is not surprised Descartes does not make them at his own cost: he has heard that the magnet alone cost Gilbert more than fifty thousand écus, and Bacon's *Instauratio magna* and *New Atlantis*, which have the best thoughts on method of anyone before Descartes, would require the revenues of two or three of the most powerful kings. Descartes needs fewer experiments, since his skill and his truths supply many, but particular bodies are almost infinite, each has many properties, his method requires examining related things together to make enumerations, and capable assistants work properly only for pay. He cannot complete the plan of the *Principles*, to explain minerals, plants, animals and man as he explained the elements and the heavens, without public funds. Deduction is distinguished from divination: principles fix the possible mechanisms, and experiment decides which one God actually used, the doctrine of *Principles* III, 46 and *Discours* VI. The treatise relies on the "easy" experiments, observations anyone can make on pulse, colour, digestion and tears (arts. 97–101, 112–135), together with what the anatomists reported, which is exactly what this paragraph says Descartes could achieve without funds. The *Passions* is thus the kind of physics a private man could still write.

Paragraph 9 (AT XI 321–322). Since all this is easy to understand and beyond doubt, the friend is sure that if Descartes presented it so that it reached those to whom God has given power over peoples and the charge of the public good, none of them would refuse to contribute to so useful a design. France alone, his native country, is powerful enough to supply everything, but since other nations have no less interest, several would be generous enough not to yield to France in this, and none would be so barbarous as to refuse a share. Science is presented as an international public good, and its patronage as a duty attached to sovereignty. The appeal is to the generosity of princes, a virtue the treatise will define as doing good to others above one's own interest (art. 156). The irony of the date is plain: Descartes left for Sweden in September 1649 under Christina's patronage, and died there.

Paragraph 10 (AT XI 322). If all this does not change Descartes' temperament, the friend asks at least that he send the treatise on the passions and allow him to print it with a preface. He will write it so that Descartes can disapprove nothing in it and so that everyone of intelligence and virtue who reads it will share his zeal for the advancement of the sciences. The treatise appears as a consolation, what the friend will settle for if the physics cannot be had. The promised preface became, by the Notice at the head of the volume, this very letter, so the letter of complaint turned into the treatise's introduction.

### Reply to the Preceding Letter

Reply of 4 December 1648 (AT XI 323–324). Among the insults and reproaches of the friend's long letter, Descartes finds so much to his advantage that if it were printed, people might think there was more collusion between them than there is, and that he had asked the friend to put in things that propriety would not let him say himself. He will therefore not reply point by point but give two reasons against publication. First, he does not believe the design he supposes the friend had in writing it can succeed. Second, he is not at all of the temperament the friend imagines: he has no indignation and no distaste that takes away his desire to do all he can to serve the public, to which he feels much obliged for the favourable reception of his writings. He refused the writing on the passions only so as not to be obliged to show it to others who would not have profited from it. He had composed it only to be read by a princess whose mind is so far above the common that she understands without effort what seems hardest to our doctors, so he had explained only what he thought was new. He promises to revise it, add what is needed to make it more intelligible, and then send it to do with as the friend pleases. The reply observes the propriety the friend's second paragraph attacked: Descartes will not praise himself, and he fears seeming to have praised himself through another. He denies by name two of the passions imputed to him, indignation and distaste (arts. 195, 208), and so answers the moral diagnosis with a self-report. The princess is Elisabeth, for whom the first draft was written in the winter of 1645–46 (AT XI 297–298). "Only what I thought was new" explains the treatise's economy and its claim in art. 1 to write as though no one had treated the subject, and the promise of additions for intelligibility is what the editors connect with the third Part.

### Second Letter, to Monsieur Descartes

Second letter, 23 July 1649 (AT XI 324–325). Descartes has kept him waiting so long that the friend is beginning to lose hope and to imagine the treatise was promised only to stop him from publishing his first letter. He has reason to think Descartes would be annoyed to lose the excuse he uses for not finishing his physics, and the letter was meant to remove it. Its reasons are such that no one with any regard for honour and virtue could read it without wishing, as he does, that Descartes obtain from the public what the experiments require, and he hoped it would reach people with access to those who dispose of public funds, or who dispose of them themselves. He would thus have given Descartes work in spite of himself, knowing he has so much heart that he would repay with interest what was given him, and that this would cure him of the negligence the friend cannot help accusing him of. The letter keeps up the moral pressure and adds a theory of motivation: a benefit would oblige Descartes to work, since gratitude seeks to repay (art. 193 will define gratitude by the desire to make a return). Adam and Tannery note that this letter is hard to square with the April 1649 letter to Clerselier announcing the printing, which supports their view that it was not written by Clerselier, or that the exchange was arranged for publication.

### Reply to the Second Letter

Reply of 14 August 1649 (AT XI 325–326). Descartes is innocent of the trick the friend suspects: he had no need of it, since he does not believe the first letter would have had the effect intended, and he is not so inclined to idleness that fear of the labour of examining many experiments, had the public given him the means, would outweigh his desire to instruct himself and to write something useful to others. He cannot so easily excuse the negligence he is blamed for. He admits he spent longer revising the little treatise than composing it, and yet added only a little and changed nothing in the discourse, which is so simple and brief that it will show that his design was not to explain the passions as an orator, nor even as a moral philosopher, but only as a physicist. He foresees that the treatise will fare no better than his other writings: its title may invite more readers, but it will satisfy only those who take the trouble to examine it carefully. This is the treatise's only statement of method, and it names three ways of treating the passions. The orator treats them for persuasion, the tradition of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II and Cicero. The moral philosopher treats them for their regulation, the tradition of the Stoics and of Senault's *De l'usage des passions*. The physicist treats them for their causes. "Not even as a moral philosopher" does not mean the treatise has no ethics, since Part Three plainly does. It means the moral conclusions are drawn from the physics, as the branch from the trunk in the image of the *Principles*' preface: generosity and the remedies follow from the mechanism of art. 50 and the institution of art. 44. "Added only a little" sits awkwardly with the editors' evidence that the third Part was added after the Elisabeth draft (AT XI 297), and the letter, written for public view, may understate the revision. The Latin translator, who printed these letters with his version, glosses the method in his own preface (AT XI 490): Descartes considers man "in the philosophical manner" and does not enter into man's native corruption, which he calls the most fertile mother of our disordered motions and assigns to theology. The gloss draws a boundary Descartes left implicit. In the treatise the disorders of the passions are entirely natural: excess, error of judgment and misdirected habit (arts. 138, 211), never the effects of the Fall. The translator thereby names the omission that Augustinian readers, for whom passionate disorder is the mark of sin, would find most striking.

## Part One. On the Passions in General, and Incidentally on the Whole Nature of Man

Article 1, "That what is passion with respect to one subject is always action in some other respect" (AT XI 327–328). Descartes declares the ancients' teaching on the passions the clearest proof that their sciences are defective. The subject looks easy, since everyone feels passions in himself and needs no borrowed observation; yet what the ancients taught is meagre and mostly incredible, so he will write as though no one had touched the matter. He then lays down a general principle: whatever newly happens is called a passion with respect to the subject it happens in and an action with respect to what makes it happen. Agent and patient may differ widely, but action and passion are one thing bearing two names, according to the subject it is referred to. The principle is Aristotle's (*Physics* III.3: one motion, actualized by the mover in the moved). The break with antiquity therefore spares its theory of change and targets its psychology, above all the Thomist account of the passions as motions of the sensitive appetite (*Summa theologiae* I–II, qq. 22–48) that still governed the French treatises of Coeffeteau and Senault. The Latin translator kept *passiones* rather than the more classical *affectus*, saying he wished to stay closer to the author's principles (AT XI 490), and the choice is right: only *passio* keeps the correlation with *actio* on which this definition rests. The claim of total novelty is polemical, but the method is new: passion is first defined relationally, as the patient side of an action, not as a kind of psychic content. That definition sets the strategy of the whole Part. To know what is passion in the soul, find what acts on it.

Article 2, "That to know the passions of the soul one must distinguish its functions from those of the body" (AT XI 328). No subject acts on the soul more immediately than the body it is joined to, so what is a passion in the soul is commonly an action in the body. The best route to the passions is therefore to examine the difference between soul and body and assign each of our functions to one or the other. Art. 1's relational definition turns an inquiry into the passions into an inquiry into their agent, and since the agent is the body, the road runs through physiology. "Commonly" is exact: some perceptions are caused by the soul itself (arts. 19–20), and those are excluded from the passions proper. The real distinction of the *Meditations* is presupposed, not re-proved; the treatise inventories functions and sorts them by substance, replacing the Aristotelian soul as principle of life with a ledger in two columns.

Article 3, "What rule must be followed for this purpose" (AT XI 329). Whatever we experience in ourselves and also see can belong to wholly inanimate bodies must be attributed to our body alone; whatever is in us and cannot be conceived in any way to belong to a body must be attributed to our soul. The two criteria are not symmetrical. The first is empirical comparison ("we see" that lifeless bodies can have it); the second is conceptual ("we cannot conceive" a body having it). The soul's share is fixed negatively, as what resists every corporeal conception. The rule partitions without remainder and leaves no third column for the union, which the letter to Elisabeth of 21 May 1643 had called a primitive notion of its own; the union returns not as a third bearer of functions but as the interaction of arts. 30–50.

Article 4, "That the heat and movement of the limbs proceed from the body, and thoughts from the soul" (AT XI 329). Since body cannot be conceived to think, every kind of thought belongs to the soul. Since inanimate bodies such as flame move in as many ways and are as hot as our limbs, or more so, all heat and motion in us, insofar as they do not depend on thought, belong to the body. The qualifier matters: voluntary movement has a mental cause, and arts. 18 and 41 will say how. The flame is not a casual example. In Descartes' physics fire is matter in violent motion (*Le Monde*, ch. 2, in this volume), and the heart's heat is a "fire without light" (*Discours* V). The heat of life and the heat of a candle are the same kind of thing.

Article 5, "That it is an error to believe that the soul gives movement and heat to the body" (AT XI 330). Avoiding this error removes what Descartes thinks the first obstacle to explaining the passions. Seeing that corpses lack heat and therefore movement, people inferred that the soul's absence stopped them, and so made natural heat and bodily motion depend on the soul. The causal order runs the other way: the soul departs at death because the heat stops and the organs of movement decay. The target is the Aristotelian–Galenic conception of the soul as principle of life, with innate heat as its instrument (*De anima* II). Why is this error the first cause of failure about the passions? If the soul animates the body, its affective commotions are assigned to a sensitive soul or appetite; once life is detached from soul, the passions can be treated as thoughts with bodily causes. The *Description of the Human Body* in this volume opens with the same reversal.

Article 6, "What difference there is between a living body and a dead one" (AT XI 330–331). Death never comes through the soul's fault but because some principal part of the body decays. A living body differs from a corpse as a wound watch or other automaton—a machine that moves of itself—differs from the same machine broken. The wound machine contains the corporeal principle of the movements it was built for; the broken one does not. Defining the automaton as self-moving hands mechanism the very mark by which Aristotle recognized the ensouled. Life becomes a state of a machine, not the presence of a soul. "Corporeal principle" points ahead to art. 8. The comparison repeats the opening of the *Treatise of Man* (AT XI 120, with its clocks and fountains) and *Discours* V. Since the soul leaves when the organs fail, the union itself depends on bodily arrangement, as art. 30 will state.

Article 7, "Brief explanation of the parts of the body and some of its functions" (AT XI 331–332). Descartes reviews the body in grades of public knowledge. Everyone knows we have heart, brain, stomach, muscles, nerves, arteries and veins, and that food's juice passes through liver and veins into the blood. Anyone with a little medicine knows the heart's structure and the path of the blood: from the vena cava into the right side, to the lungs through the "arterial vein" (pulmonary artery), back into the left side through the "venous artery" (pulmonary vein), and out through the great artery. Those not blinded by the ancients and willing to examine Harvey's doctrine of circulation know that veins and arteries are streams through which the blood runs constantly and fast, the heart's two cavities acting as sluices through which all of it passes on each circuit. Muscles work in opposed pairs: one shortens and pulls, its opponent lengthens. All movement and all sensation depend on the nerves, small threads or tubes from the brain that contain a very subtle air or wind called animal spirits. The grading signals that nothing beyond what is publicly checkable is being assumed. Harvey is endorsed on circulation only; art. 9 will reject his account of why the heart beats. The Galenic vessel names survive, and so does the traditional language of spirits as "wind," which art. 10 will deflate.

Article 8, "What is the principle of all these functions" (AT XI 333). What is not commonly known is how spirits and nerves serve movement and sense, and what corporeal principle drives them. Referring to earlier writings (the editors cite *Discours* V, AT VI 46–56, and the *Dioptrique*), Descartes restates briefly that while we live a continual heat in the heart, a kind of fire fed by venous blood, is the corporeal principle of every movement of our limbs. "Principle" is the Aristotelian *archē* reassigned to a heat-engine. "Every movement" includes voluntary ones: the soul supplies none of the motive force of the body and, on the account of art. 41, only moves the gland that switches the spirits. This anticipates the view later attributed to Descartes, that the mind alters the direction of motion without adding to its quantity.

Article 9, "How the movement of the heart takes place" (AT XI 333–334). The heat's first effect is to dilate the blood filling the heart's cavities, which, needing more room, rushes from the right cavity into the arterial vein and from the left into the great artery. When the dilation stops, new blood enters from the vena cava and the venous artery. Valves at the four openings make the flow one-way. The new blood is rarefied in its turn. In this alone consists the pulse of heart and arteries, which beats as often as new blood enters, and this alone moves the blood, which carries the heart's heat to every part and nourishes it. Against Harvey, the heart is not a muscle that squeezes but a vessel whose heat expands the blood and drives it out; the "alone" insists on a single mechanism. The point bears directly on the passions. Since the heat acts through rarefaction, any change in the blood's entry or composition changes the spirits produced (art. 15), and through the heart-nerves this is how a passion comes to be felt "as in the heart" (arts. 33, 36).

Article 10, "How the animal spirits are produced in the brain" (AT XI 334–335). The liveliest and finest parts of the blood rarefied in the heart pour continuously into the brain's cavities. The blood leaving through the great artery runs straight toward the brain; the passages there are narrow, so only the most agitated and subtle particles get through, while the rest spreads through the body. These particles are the animal spirits. They need no transformation, only separation from coarser blood. What Descartes calls spirits are nothing but bodies, distinguished only by being very small and very fast, like the particles of a torch's flame. They never rest; as some enter the cavities, others leave through the brain's pores into nerves and muscles and move the body. The Galenic *pneuma*, concocted through a hierarchy of natural, vital and animal spirits, is reduced to mechanical filtration. Art. 47 will invoke this article to show that the so-called lower part of the soul is simply body.

Article 11, "How the movements of the muscles take place" (AT XI 335–336). Limbs move only because some muscles shorten and their opponents lengthen, and a muscle shortens rather than its opponent only because slightly more spirits reach it from the brain. The brain's spirits do not do the work alone. They release the spirits already held in both muscles, which rush out of one and into the other through small openings so valved that the slightest excess on one side opens all passages that way and closes those running back. The emptied muscle slackens; the filled one swells and pulls the limb. A small input from the brain triggers a large redistribution. This amplifying switch, already drawn in the *Treatise of Man* (AT XI 133–137), explains in advance how the least motions of the gland (art. 31) can move the whole body, and why the brain acts as a relay rather than a source of force.

Article 12, "How external objects act upon the sense organs" (AT XI 336–337). Spirits do not always flow alike into the muscles. Besides the soul's action, which is truly one cause and will be treated later, two causes depend on the body alone. The first is the variety of motions objects excite in the sense organs. Repeating the *Dioptrique* (Disc. IV) so that the treatise can stand alone, Descartes distinguishes three components in a nerve: the marrow, fine threads running from the brain to the extremities; the membranes sheathing them, continuous with the brain's envelopes, forming small tubes; and the spirits flowing through these tubes, which keep the threads free and taut, so that the least movement at the extremity moves the corresponding place in the brain, as pulling one end of a cord moves the other. The nerve thus has two channels: the thread carries sensory motion inward, the spirits in the tube carry motor action outward. Sensation becomes instantaneous mechanical transmission; nothing like a sensible species travels. "Truly" one of the causes is the soul: the treatise affirms mind–body causation flatly before explaining any of it.

Article 13, "That this action of external objects can direct the spirits into the muscles in various ways" (AT XI 338–339). According to the *Dioptrique* (Disc. VI), visible objects reach us only by moving, through the transparent bodies between, the threads of the optic nerves at the back of the eye and so the parts of the brain those nerves come from, in as many ways as we see differences in things. What represents objects to the soul are the motions in the brain, not those in the eye. Sounds, smells, tastes, heat, pain, hunger, thirst—objects of the external senses and of the inner appetites alike—work the same way. These brain motions give the soul its sensations, but they can also, without the soul, send the spirits into some muscles rather than others. The proof: when a friend thrusts his hand at our eyes in jest, we know he will not hurt us, yet we can hardly keep from blinking. The blink happens against our will, which is the soul's only or at least principal action, so the soul does not cause it; the body is built so that the hand's approach excites a brain motion that drives the spirits into the eyelid muscles. Three things are established. The representing motion sits in the brain, which prepares the gland. One brain motion has two effects, a sensation in the soul and a movement without it, and this double pathway is the template for the passions as both felt and bodily preparatory (arts. 36–40). The test of non-mental causation is contrariety to the will, and the identification of the soul's activity with will anticipates art. 17. The example is a precise description of a reflex.

Article 14, "That the difference among the spirits can also vary their course" (AT XI 339). The second bodily cause is the unequal agitation of the spirits and the variety of their particles. Larger and more agitated particles travel farther in a straight line into the brain's cavities and pores, and so are sent into different muscles than weaker ones would be. The fluid itself becomes a variable: the same stimulus produces different movements depending on the condition of the spirits. This is the opening for temperament, and for the moods without an object that art. 51 lists among the causes of passions.

Article 15, "What the causes of this difference are" (AT XI 340–341). The inequality may come from the materials the spirits are made of, as when wine vapours pass quickly into the blood, rise to the brain and become spirits stronger and more abundant than usual, able to move the body in strange ways. It may come from the dispositions of heart, liver, stomach, spleen and the other organs that contribute to the spirits. Small nerves set in the base of the heart widen and narrow the entrances to its cavities, so that the blood dilates more or less and yields spirits differently disposed. And though blood reaches the heart from everywhere, it is often pushed more from some parts than others by the nerves and muscles serving them, and it dilates differently according to its source: blood from the lower liver where the bile is, from the spleen, from the veins of arms and legs, and fresh food-juice from the stomach, each in its own way. The four-humour physiology is rebuilt mechanically: bile and the spleen's black bile become sources of blood with different rarefying properties. The heart-nerves are the decisive piece. They are the channel by which a brain motion alters the heart's dilation, and so the loop through which a passion feeds itself (arts. 36, 46). Drunkenness gives a purely corporeal case of altered spirits producing altered behaviour.

Article 16, "How all the limbs can be moved by the objects of the senses and by the spirits without the help of the soul" (AT XI 341–342). The body is built so that every change in the spirits' movement can open some brain pores more than others, and conversely, when a sensory nerve opens a pore slightly more or less than usual, the spirits' movement changes and they are sent into the muscles that move the body in the way it is usually moved on such an occasion. All the movements we make without the will contributing—breathing, walking, eating, all the actions we share with beasts, as often happens—depend only on the arrangement of our limbs and the course that heart-heated spirits naturally take through brain, nerves and muscles, as a watch moves by its spring and the shape of its wheels. "The way it is usually moved on such an occasion" gives the machine a history: pathways become habitual, which prepares the memory traces of art. 42 and the training of art. 50. Walking appears here as involuntary and in art. 18 as voluntary; complex behaviour does not need the will, though it can be taken up by it. The beast-machine thesis of *Discours* V follows naturally from this article, but Descartes does not draw it here, and art. 50 will hedge it. Art. 34 refers back to this article as the summary of the bodily machine.

Article 17, "What the functions of the soul are" (AT XI 342). With the body's functions set aside, nothing remains for the soul but thoughts, of two principal kinds: its actions and its passions. Its actions are all our volitions, because we experience them as coming directly from the soul and seeming to depend on it alone. Its passions, in the general sense, are all our perceptions or cognitions, because the soul often does not make them what they are and always receives them from the things they represent. The editors refer to the letter to Elisabeth of 6 October 1645 (AT IV 310–311), where the division first appears. This is the broad sense of passion, and it covers intellection: understanding is passive, as Descartes had told Regius in May 1641. The two justifications differ in strength. Volitions only "seem" to depend on the soul alone; perceptions are "often" not shaped by it but "always" received from what they represent. The "always" reaches even self-caused perceptions: when I imagine a chimera, I still receive the perception from the content represented. Passivity is defined by the perception's relation to its object, not by a bodily cause. The pairing restates *Principles* I, 32: every mode of thinking is either perception of the understanding or operation of the will.

Article 18, "Of the will" (AT XI 342–343). Volitions are again of two kinds. Some terminate in the soul itself, as when we will to love God or, generally, to apply our thought to some immaterial object. Others terminate in the body, as when, from the mere fact that we will to walk, our legs move. Volitions are sorted by their terminus, not their origin, since all originate in the soul. Choosing the love of God as the example of an inward volition anticipates the later treatment of love as consent of the will (arts. 79–80) and the intellectual love of the letter to Chanut of 1 February 1647. "From the mere fact that we will" states the bodily volition's efficacy without yet explaining it; art. 41 supplies the mechanism and art. 44 the limits.

Article 19, "Of perception" (AT XI 343). Perceptions are of two kinds, some caused by the soul and some by the body. Those caused by the soul are the perceptions of our volitions and of all the imaginations or other thoughts that depend on them. We cannot will anything without perceiving by that very fact that we will it. Willing is an action of the soul, but perceiving that it wills may be called a passion in it. Since this perception and this volition are really one and the same thing, it is named after the nobler aspect and called an action, not a passion. Every volition carries an awareness of itself, as the definition of thought in the Second Replies requires: thought is whatever is in us such that we are immediately aware of it. That awareness is not a second act reflecting on the first; it is the volition itself under another description, and so no regress opens. Art. 1's formula of one thing with two names returns, but altered: the two names no longer answer to two subjects, as with agent and patient, but to two aspects of one act in one subject. The scholastic rule that denomination goes by the nobler (*a potiori fit denominatio*) keeps the self-awareness of willing from turning volition into a passion.

Article 20, "Of the imaginations and other thoughts formed by the soul" (AT XI 344). When the soul applies itself to imagine something that does not exist, an enchanted palace or a chimera, or to consider something intelligible but not imaginable, such as its own nature, its perceptions depend principally on the will that makes it perceive them, and they are counted as actions rather than passions. "Principally" marks a mixture: the content is still received in the sense of art. 17, but the will steers. The examples cover imagination and pure intellect, and the soul's own nature as the paradigm of the purely intelligible repeats the Second Meditation's lesson that the mind knows itself without imagery. Art. 17 placed all perceptions among the passions; here some perceptions count as actions. Action and passion name the predominant cause of a thought, so the distinction is a matter of denomination by what weighs most.

Article 21, "Of the imaginations caused only by the body" (AT XI 344–345). Most perceptions caused by the body depend on the nerves, but some do not. These are imaginations like those of art. 20, except that the will plays no part in forming them, so they cannot count as actions of the soul. They arise because the spirits, variously agitated and meeting the traces of earlier impressions in the brain, take their course by chance through some pores rather than others. Such are the illusions of dreams and the waking reveries in which thought wanders idly without applying itself to anything (the editors cite the same letter to Elisabeth, AT IV 311). Some of these imaginations are passions in the most proper and particular sense, and all may be called passions in the general sense; but because their cause is less notable and determinate than that of nerve-borne perceptions, and they seem only the shadow and painting of these, the latter must be distinguished first. The doctrine of brain traces is introduced here and given its mechanism in art. 42. The order of exposition follows the order of dependence: the copy is explained after the original. That some of these imaginations are passions in the strictest sense prepares art. 26, where a dreamed sadness is a real sadness.

Article 22, "Of the difference among the other perceptions" (AT XI 345). All the perceptions not yet explained reach the soul through the nerves, and they differ in that we refer some to external objects striking our senses, some to our body or its parts, and some to our soul. The tripartition is by reference, not by cause: all three classes arrive by the same route. Reference is the way a perception presents itself as of something, out there, in a limb, or in the soul. *Principles* IV, 190 had two internal senses, the natural appetites and the passions; here they become two distinct classes beside the external senses. The guard matters for what follows: a passion is referred to the soul even when its cause lies outside it (art. 25).

Article 23, "Of the perceptions we refer to objects outside us" (AT XI 346). Perceptions referred to external things are caused, at least when our opinion is not false, by those objects, which excite motions in the external organs and through the nerves in the brain, and these make the soul sense them. When we see a torch and hear a bell, light and sound are two different actions that excite two different motions in our nerves and brain and so give the soul two different sensations. We refer these so firmly to their supposed causes that we think we see the torch itself and hear the bell, not merely sense motions coming from them. The parenthesis concedes that reference can misfire, as in dreams (art. 26). The reference is built into the perception, not an inference drawn from it: what appears is the torch, though the causal chain ends in brain motions. Descartes does not call this an error; it is the natural working of sense. Calling light and sound "actions" is consistent with the *Dioptrique*, where light is an action transmitted through a medium.

Article 24, "Of the perceptions we refer to our body" (AT XI 346–347). Perceptions referred to the body or its parts are hunger, thirst and the other natural appetites, together with pain, heat and the other affections we feel as in our limbs and not as in outside objects. Through the same nerves we can feel at once the coldness of our hand and the heat of the flame it approaches, or the warmth of the hand and the cold of the air. The actions that make us feel heat or cold in the hand do not differ from those that make us feel it outside, except that one supervenes on the other, and we judge the first to be already in us and the newcomer to be not yet in us but in the object causing it. The physiology does not mark the line between bodily and external reference; temporal order does. The standing state counts as ours, the new one is referred outward. The verb is "judge": unlike the torch of art. 23, this reference involves a tacit judgment, and it is relative to a baseline. The same hand can feel its own cold and the fire's heat, so the boundary between body and world is drawn inside perception itself.

Article 25, "Of the perceptions we refer to our soul" (AT XI 347–348). Perceptions referred to the soul alone are those whose effects we feel as in the soul itself and for which we commonly know no proximate cause to refer them to. Such are joy, anger and the like, sometimes excited by objects that move our nerves and sometimes by other causes. All perceptions, those referred to outside objects and those referred to the body's affections, are truly passions of the soul in the general sense, but usage restricts the term to those referred to the soul itself, and only these are what the treatise explains under the name of passions of the soul. Here the narrow sense is fixed. Its mark is negative: a passion feels like the soul's own state because no proximate cause is presented with it. The frightening animal is what fear is about, but fear is not felt as located in the animal, as colour is. The passion's object and its reference come apart. "Commonly" points to the gap the treatise fills: ordinary experience does not know the proximate cause, and the physicist will supply it as the motion of the spirits (arts. 27, 29).

Article 26, "That the imaginations that depend only on the chance movement of the spirits can be passions as true as the perceptions that depend on the nerves" (AT XI 348–349). Everything the soul perceives through the nerves can also be represented to it by the chance course of the spirits, the only difference being that impressions arriving through the nerves are usually livelier and more distinct. That is why art. 21 called the latter their shadow or painting. The painting is sometimes so like the original that we are deceived about perceptions referred to outside objects or to parts of the body, but not in the same way about the passions. These are so close to the soul and so inward that it cannot feel them without their being truly such as it feels them. Asleep, or even awake, one may imagine things so vividly that one thinks one sees them or feels them in the body when they are not there; but even dreaming, one cannot feel sad or moved by any passion without its being very true that the soul has that passion. Error is asymmetric. Perceptions referred outward can miss their object; a passion is referred to the soul, where it actually is, so its reference cannot miss. What is guaranteed is only that the passion is had, not that it is apt or that its cause is known. The structure repeats the Second Meditation's "at least I seem to see," which survives every doubt. Vividness as the sole intrinsic difference between sensing and imagining anticipates Hume's impressions and ideas. The inner certainty of feeling is also the Cartesian thesis on which Michel Henry's reading of the *cogitatio* as self-affection can draw.

Article 27, "The definition of the passions of the soul" (AT XI 349). Having seen how the passions differ from all the soul's other thoughts, Descartes defines them generally as perceptions, or feelings, or emotions of the soul, which are referred particularly to it, and which are caused, maintained and strengthened by some movement of the spirits. The editors point to the letter to Elisabeth of 6 October 1645 (AT IV 309–313), where this definition was first worked out. It has three parts: a genus given under three names, a differentia (reference to the soul), and a cause (the spirits). The triple verb gives the passion a duration. A passion lasts as long as the motion sustaining it, and this is the root of its resistance to the will (art. 46). The definition by efficient cause makes passions passive in art. 1's strict sense: their agent is the body. Arts. 28–29 justify each part.

Article 28, "Explanation of the first part of this definition" (AT XI 349–350). Passions can be called perceptions if the word means all thoughts that are not actions of the soul or volitions, but not if it means evident cognitions. Experience shows that those most agitated by their passions are not those who know them best, and the passions belong among the perceptions that the close alliance of soul and body renders confused and obscure. They can be called feelings (*sentiments*) because the soul receives them as it receives the objects of the external senses and knows them no otherwise. They are better still called emotions of the soul, not only because the word fits every change in the soul, every thought that comes to it, but because no other kind of thought agitates and shakes it so strongly. Each name is fitted with its limit. Passions are certain as had (art. 26) yet obscure as to their nature, so certainty of possession and clarity of content come apart. As feelings, they are known only by being undergone, without any other access. "Emotion" in the seventeenth century still meant a commotion, of the blood or of a crowd; Descartes uses it for the soul's being shaken and so helps fix its psychological sense. The inverse relation between being agitated and knowing is the treatise's reason for existing, and the confusion is the one the Sixth Meditation traced to the union.

Article 29, "Explanation of its other part" (AT XI 350). Saying that passions are referred particularly to the soul distinguishes them from the other feelings, referred either to outside objects (smells, sounds, colours) or to our body (hunger, thirst, pain). Saying that they are caused, maintained and strengthened by some movement of the spirits distinguishes them from our volitions, which may also be called emotions of the soul referred to it, but which the soul causes itself. It also gives their last and most proximate cause, which distinguishes them once more from the other feelings. Each clause excludes a rival. Volitions, it turns out, also satisfy the first clause, so reference to the soul alone would include them; only the causal clause separates passion from will. The proximate cause separates passions a second time from sensations: sensations arise from nerve-borne impressions in the brain, passions from a particular agitation of the spirits acting on the gland (arts. 37, 51). "Last and most proximate" implies first and remote causes, and Part Two begins by seeking them (art. 51).

Article 30, "That the soul is united to all the parts of the body jointly" (AT XI 351). To understand all this better, one must know that the soul is truly joined to the whole body and cannot properly be said to be in any one part to the exclusion of the others. There are two reasons. The body is one and in a way indivisible, because its organs are so related to one another that removing any one leaves the whole defective. And the soul is of a nature that has no relation to extension, dimensions or the other properties of matter, but only to the whole assembly of the organs. This shows in the fact that we cannot conceive half or a third of a soul or what extension it occupies, that it does not shrink when part of the body is cut off, and that it leaves entirely when the assembly of organs is dissolved. The body's unity is organizational, not material, since matter is endlessly divisible; the soul is united to an organization. The letter to Mesland of 9 February 1645 says the same: a body stays numerically the same while its matter changes, as long as it stays joined to the same soul. The article blocks in advance the reading of art. 31's gland as the soul's location. Amputation diminishes the body, not the soul, because the soul has no parts to lose.

Article 31, "That there is a small gland in the brain in which the soul exercises its functions more particularly than in the other parts" (AT XI 351–352). Although the soul is joined to the whole body, there is one part where it exercises its functions more particularly than in the rest. People commonly take it to be the brain, because the sense organs report there, or the heart, because the passions are felt as in it. On careful examination Descartes claims to have found evidently that the part where the soul exercises its functions immediately is neither the heart nor the whole brain but only its innermost part, a very small gland in the middle of its substance. The gland hangs above the passage by which the spirits of the front cavities communicate with those of the back, so that its least movements can greatly change the course of the spirits, and the least changes in that course can greatly change its movements. "Immediately" is the key word: the soul is joined to the whole body but acts directly only here. The gland's suspension makes it sensitive in both directions and gives the amplifying switch of art. 11 a central seat. The treatise never names it; the letters call it the *conarion*, and the *Treatise of Man* labels it gland H (AT XI 176ff.). The old quarrel over heart or brain, Aristotle against Galen, is settled for the brain only by being displaced to a point within it. The anatomy was soon contested, notably by Steno, who showed the gland is not suspended free in the ventricles.

Article 32, "How we know that this gland is the principal seat of the soul" (AT XI 352–353). The other parts of the brain are all double, as are eyes, hands and ears, and indeed all the organs of the external senses. Since we have only one simple thought of one thing at one time, there must be some place where the two images coming from the two eyes, or the two impressions coming from one object through the double organs of the other senses, can unite before reaching the soul, so that they do not represent two objects instead of one. These images can readily be conceived to unite in the gland through the spirits filling the cavities, and there is no other place in the body where they can be united except as a consequence of their being united there. The argument runs from the unity of perception to an unpaired organ. Its hidden premise is that the soul cannot itself fuse two impressions into one, and this follows from art. 34's correlation of one gland motion with one perception: a single thought needs a single bodily correlate. The Aristotelian common sense (*De anima* III.2) is relocated in an organ, as in the *Treatise of Man*, where the gland is the seat of common sense and imagination.

Article 33, "That the seat of the passions is not in the heart" (AT XI 353–354). The opinion that the soul receives its passions in the heart deserves no consideration. It rests only on the fact that the passions make us feel some alteration there, and that alteration is felt as in the heart only through a small nerve running down to it from the brain, as pain is felt as in the foot through the foot's nerves and the stars are seen as in the sky through their light and the optic nerves. The soul no more needs to act immediately in the heart to feel its passions there than it needs to be in the sky to see the stars. Felt location does not reveal where the soul acts; art. 22's doctrine of reference applies to the heart. The passions now show a double reference: the passion itself is referred to the soul (art. 25), while the bodily alteration accompanying it is referred to the heart. The foot parallels the phantom pains of the Sixth Meditation and *Principles* IV, 196, where pain is felt in an amputated hand. The target is the cardiocentrism of Aristotle and of the medical tradition that made the heart's movement the bodily side of every passion.

Article 34, "How the soul and the body act on each other" (AT XI 354–355). Descartes asks us to conceive that the soul has its principal seat in the small gland at the centre of the brain, from which it radiates through the rest of the body by means of the spirits, the nerves and even the blood, which takes on the impressions of the spirits and carries them through the arteries to every limb. Recall from art. 16 that the nerve threads are so distributed that the motions sensible objects excite in them open the brain's pores in various ways, sending the spirits into the muscles and moving the limbs in every way they can be moved, and that any other cause that moves the spirits can also steer them. Add that the gland hangs between the cavities so that the spirits can move it in as many ways as there are sensible differences in objects, and that the soul can move it too. The soul is so made that it receives as many different impressions, that is, has as many different perceptions, as there are different movements in the gland. Conversely, whenever the gland is moved, by the soul or by any other cause, it drives the surrounding spirits toward the pores that lead them through the nerves into the muscles. "Principal seat" might seem to undo art. 30, but it names where the soul's action starts, and "radiates," a metaphor from light, makes the soul's efficacy spread through bodily media without the soul being spread out. The article states a law of correlation, one gland motion for one perception, not an explanation of how the two are linked. The bodily effect is indifferent to the source of the gland's motion, "the soul or any other cause," and for that reason volitions and passions compete on the same field (art. 47).

Article 35, "Example of how the impressions of objects unite in the gland at the centre of the brain" (AT XI 355–356). If an animal comes toward us, the light reflected from it paints two images, one in each eye. Through the optic nerves these form two more on the inner surface of the brain facing its cavities. From there, through the spirits filling the cavities, the images radiate toward the gland so that the motion composing each point of one image tends toward the same point of the gland as the motion forming the corresponding point of the other image, the one representing the same part of the animal. The two brain images thus make a single one on the gland, which, acting immediately on the soul, makes it see the animal's shape. The point-to-point convergence carries out the unification argued for in art. 32. The image on the gland is not inspected by an inner eye: the *Dioptrique* (Disc. IV) had warned that sensation does not require resemblance, and here the image "acts immediately on the soul," a causal relation, not a view of a picture. The approaching animal leads straight into art. 36.

Article 36, "Example of how the passions are excited in the soul" (AT XI 356–357). If the shape is very strange and frightening, that is, if it bears much relation to things that have harmed the body before, it excites in the soul the passion of apprehension (*crainte*), and then either boldness or else fear (*peur*) and terror, depending on the body's temperament or the soul's strength, and on whether one has protected oneself before, by defence or by flight, against the harmful things the present impression resembles. In some people this so disposes the brain that the spirits reflected from the image on the gland pass partly into the nerves that turn the back and move the legs for flight, and partly into those that widen or narrow the heart's orifices or so agitate the parts that send it blood that the blood, rarefied otherwise than usual, sends to the brain spirits fit to maintain and strengthen fear, that is, to keep open or reopen the pores that lead them into the same nerves. Merely by entering these pores the spirits excite a particular movement in the gland, instituted by nature to make the soul feel this passion. Because these pores connect mainly with the small nerves of the heart's orifices, the soul feels the passion chiefly as in the heart. The frightening is defined by relation to past harm. An object's affective meaning is an association laid down by experience, not a quality it carries, and the outcome varies with temperament, strength of soul and personal history. The article then traces the loop that art. 27's "maintained and strengthened" meant: gland, heart-nerves, altered blood, new spirits, the same pores held open. "Instituted by nature" ties the passion to a gland motion without resemblance, as the Sixth Meditation's teachings of nature tie sensations to brain states. The proximate cause of fear is the spirits' passage toward the heart, which is exactly why the heart is where fear is felt (art. 33).

Article 37, "How it appears that they are all caused by some movement of the spirits" (AT XI 357). The same holds for all the other passions: they are caused principally by the spirits in the brain's cavities as these flow toward the nerves that widen or narrow the heart's orifices, or that push the blood of other parts toward it in various ways, or that in any other way maintain the same passion. This makes clear why the definition said they are caused by some particular movement of the spirits. The generalization adds a word. Art. 27 said "some movement"; here it is "some particular movement." Each passion has its own pattern in the spirits, and this correspondence of kinds is what makes Part Two's physiology of each primitive passion possible (arts. 96–111).

Article 38, "Example of bodily movements that accompany the passions and do not depend on the soul" (AT XI 358). Just as the spirits' course toward the heart-nerves suffices to move the gland in the way that puts fear in the soul, so the spirits that go at the same moment to the nerves moving the legs for flight cause another movement in the gland, by which the soul feels and perceives this flight. The flight can thus be excited in the body by the arrangement of the organs alone, without the soul contributing. The soul learns of its body's flight as a fact; it does not will it. Fear and flight are parallel effects of one cause, and the perception of fleeing is a separate perception from fear. This differs from James and Lange, for whom the felt flight is the emotion, and also from the scholastic order in which appetite commands motion. Descartes puts the behaviour ahead of any volition and alongside the passion.

Article 39, "How one and the same cause can excite different passions in different people" (AT XI 358–359). The same impression a frightening object makes on the gland, which causes fear in some, can excite courage and boldness in others, because brains are not all disposed alike. In these others the same gland movement sends the spirits into pores leading partly to the nerves that move the hands for defence and partly to those that agitate and push the blood toward the heart in the way needed to produce spirits fit to continue the defence and keep up the will to it. One gland motion, different outcomes: the difference lies in the brain's dispositions downstream, innate or acquired, and individual character is accordingly treated as physiological. The final clause, spirits that "keep up the will," shows the passion supporting a volition by bodily means and leads into art. 40.

Article 40, "What the principal effect of the passions is" (AT XI 359). The principal effect of all the passions in human beings is that they incite and dispose the soul to will the things for which they prepare the body. The feeling of fear incites it to will to flee, that of boldness to will to fight, and so on. The passion's function is to bring the will into line with the body's preparation; it does not move the body through the soul. "In human beings" marks the difference from beasts, where the preparation occurs without will (art. 50). Inciting and disposing is not compelling, as art. 41 at once insists. Art. 52 will widen this into the general use of the passions, and art. 137 will tie that use to the body's good.

Article 41, "What the soul's power is with respect to the body" (AT XI 359–360). The will is by nature so free that it can never be constrained. Of the two kinds of thought distinguished in the soul—its actions, the volitions, and its passions in the general sense, all perceptions—the former are absolutely in its power and can be changed only indirectly by the body, while the latter depend absolutely on the actions that produce them and can be changed only indirectly by the soul, except when the soul itself causes them. The soul's whole action consists in this: merely by willing something, it makes the small gland to which it is closely joined move in the way needed to produce the effect corresponding to that volition. Indirectness runs both ways: the body reaches the will only indirectly, and the soul reaches perceptions only indirectly. Freedom here means immunity from constraint, the minimal sense compatible both with the Fourth Meditation and with the two-way power of the letter to Mesland of 1645. Descartes gives no mechanism for the soul's moving the gland, only the "merely by willing"; this is where the union's primitive notion from the 1643 letters to Elisabeth applies, known through its working, not explained. "The way needed" leaves the means to nature and habit (art. 44); the soul need not know which motion it produces.

Article 42, "How we find in memory the things we want to remember" (AT XI 360). When the soul wants to remember something, the volition makes the gland lean successively to different sides, driving the spirits toward different parts of the brain until they reach the place holding the traces the object left. These traces are nothing but pores through which the spirits passed earlier because of the object's presence, and which thereby became easier than others to open again in the same way. The spirits enter them more readily and excite a particular movement in the gland that represents the same object to the soul and makes it know that this is the one it wanted to recall. Memory is facilitation: a trace is a pathway made easier to open, a disposition, not a stored picture. Recall is search, the gland sweeping until the spirits meet less resistance. Recognition comes with the evoked motion itself, since the motion makes the soul know that this is what it sought. The will steers but does not find; finding is the body's work. This is corporeal memory only; the purely intellectual memory admitted in the letters to Mesland (2 May 1644) and Arnauld (1648) is not in play.

Article 43, "How the soul can imagine, be attentive, and move the body" (AT XI 361). To imagine something never seen, the volition makes the gland move as needed to drive the spirits toward the pores whose opening can represent it. To hold attention on one object for some time, the volition keeps the gland leaning toward one side. To walk or move the body otherwise, the volition makes the gland drive the spirits toward the muscles that serve the purpose. Three operations share one instrument, the orientation of the gland. Imagining the never-seen recombines existing traces. Attention is will, a holding still, and this physiological account of attention underwrites the remedies of art. 46, where attending elsewhere screens out weak passions, as it underwrote the attention required for clear and distinct perception in the *Regulae* and the *Meditations*.

Article 44, "That each volition is naturally joined to some movement of the gland, but that by effort or habit it can be joined to others" (AT XI 361–362). The will to produce some movement or effect does not always produce it; this varies with the way nature or habit has joined each movement of the gland to each thought. If we will to look at a distant object, the pupil widens; at a near one, it narrows. But if we think only of widening the pupil, willing it does nothing, because nature joined the gland motion that drives spirits to the optic nerve in the way needed for widening or narrowing the pupil not to the volition to widen or narrow it but to the volition to look far or near. Likewise, when speaking we think only of the meaning of what we want to say, and we move tongue and lips much faster and better than if we thought of all the movements needed to utter the words. The habit acquired in learning to speak has joined the soul's action, which can move tongue and lips through the gland, with the meaning of the words rather than with the movements themselves. The will's objects are ends, not mechanisms. The link between volition and gland motion is instituted, by nature or by habit, and is set at the level of what is intended: we will to see far, and the body handles the pupil. This plasticity is the hinge of art. 50's optimism. The speech example also recalls the opening of *Le Monde* (AT XI 4, in this volume), where words signify without resembling and serve as the model for sensations that do not resemble their causes.

Article 45, "What the soul's power is with respect to its passions" (AT XI 362–363). Our passions cannot be directly excited or removed by the will either, but they can be indirectly, by representing to ourselves things usually joined with the passions we want to have and contrary to those we want to reject. To excite boldness and remove fear, it is not enough to will it. One must apply oneself to consider the reasons, objects or examples that persuade one the danger is not great, that there is always more safety in defence than in flight, that one will have glory and joy for having won, whereas from flight one can expect only regret and shame. The will acts through attention to contents whose associated passions counter the present one. The list mixes reasons with passions, glory and joy against regret and shame, so passion is countered largely by passion, with reasoning as one representation among others. This is not the Stoic model. For Chrysippus a passion is an assent that can be withdrawn; for Descartes it is a bodily-caused perception that can only be counterbalanced. The remedy is the one Descartes had recommended in the 1645 letters to Elisabeth on Seneca.

Article 46, "What reason prevents the soul from fully controlling its passions" (AT XI 363–364). A particular reason prevents the soul from changing or stopping its passions quickly, and it is why the definition (art. 27) says they are not only caused but also maintained and strengthened by some particular movement of the spirits. Nearly all of them are accompanied by some commotion in the heart, and so in all the blood and the spirits, and until that commotion stops they remain present to thought just as sensible objects remain present while they act on the sense organs. The soul, by attending hard to something else, can keep from hearing a small noise or feeling a slight pain, but not from hearing thunder or feeling fire burn the hand. In the same way it can easily overcome the lesser passions but not the most violent, until the commotion of blood and spirits has died down. The most the will can do while the commotion is at its height is to withhold consent from its effects and restrain many of the movements to which it disposes the body: if anger raises the hand to strike, the will can usually hold it back; if fear moves the legs to flee, the will can stop them. A passion behaves like a sensation, its presence imposed by an ongoing bodily cause. Its persistence is physiological inertia, the feedback loop of art. 36, not a failure of will. Attention screens weak stimuli but not strong ones. Against the strong, the will controls action, not feeling. "Consent" is the Stoic and Augustinian term, kept in a narrowed sense: one cannot refuse the passion, only its effects. "Emotion" here keeps its literal meaning of a commotion in heart and blood.

Article 47, "What the conflicts consist in that people commonly imagine between the lower and higher parts of the soul" (AT XI 364–366). All the conflicts people imagine between the lower, sensitive part of the soul and the higher, rational part, or between the natural appetites and the will, consist only in the opposition between the movements that the body through its spirits and the soul through its will tend to excite at the same moment in the gland. There is only one soul, with no diversity of parts: the same soul is sensitive and rational, and all its appetites are volitions. The error of making it play several characters, usually opposed, comes from failing to separate its functions from the body's, to which alone must be attributed whatever in us opposes reason. The only conflict is that the gland, pushed on one side by the soul and on the other by the spirits, which are only bodies (art. 10), often receives contrary impulses, and the stronger blocks the other. Two kinds of movement are excited in the gland by the spirits. Some represent to the soul the objects that move the senses or the impressions in the brain, and exert no force on the will. Others do exert some force: those that cause passions or the bodily movements accompanying them. The first often hinder the soul's actions or are hindered by them, but since they are not directly contrary, no conflict is noticed. Conflict appears only between the second kind and the volitions opposing them, for instance between the force with which the spirits push the gland to cause desire for something and the force with which the soul pushes it back by willing to avoid that thing. What chiefly makes the conflict visible is that the will, unable to excite passions directly (art. 45), must use effort and consider various things one after another. If one consideration changes the spirits' course for a moment, the next may not, and they soon resume it, because the disposition in nerves, heart and blood has not changed; so the soul feels pushed almost at once to desire and not to desire the same thing. From this people imagined two warring powers in it. Some conflict can also be seen in the fact that the cause exciting a passion often excites bodily movements the soul does not contribute to and stops, or tries to stop, as soon as it notices them, as when what excites fear sends the spirits into the leg muscles and the will to be bold holds them back. This is the treatise's central polemic, aimed at Plato's divided soul, Aristotle's parts and the Thomist sensitive appetite with its concupiscible and irascible powers. "All its appetites are volitions" is the boldest clause: whatever in appetite belongs to the soul at all is will, and the rest is body. The conflict is real but between substances, located at the gland, and described as a contest of forces in which the stronger wins. That leaves unexplained how a soul's push and a body's push can be compared, and Descartes does not address it. The class of gland motions that "exert force on the will" makes the passions solicitations, not representations. The felt division of desiring and not desiring is explained as alternation over time, not simultaneous division in the soul. Desire as the example looks ahead to its central place in the moral doctrine (arts. 143–144).

Article 48, "How we recognize the strength or weakness of souls, and what the weakest suffer from" (AT XI 366–367). By the outcome of these conflicts each person can learn the strength or weakness of his soul. Those in whom the will can naturally most easily overcome the passions and stop the accompanying bodily movements have the strongest souls. Some cannot test their strength because they never make the will fight with its own weapons, only with weapons some passions supply against others. Its own weapons are firm and determinate judgments about the knowledge of good and evil, by which it has resolved to conduct the actions of its life. The weakest souls are those whose will does not determine itself to follow such judgments but lets itself be carried continually by present passions. These, often contrary to one another, pull it by turns to their side, set it fighting against itself, and put the soul in the most wretched state possible. If fear presents death as an extreme evil avoidable only by flight, while ambition presents the infamy of flight as worse than death, the will obeys first one, then the other, keeps opposing itself, and makes the soul a slave and unhappy. Strength of soul is measured not by the absence of passion but by the will's capacity to follow its own judgments. Defeating one passion with another is not strength, even when it wins. The "own weapons" are resolved judgments, the line that runs from the second maxim of *Discours* III, firmness in action once resolved, to generosity (arts. 152–153). A self-opposition now appears, but it is the will pulled apart by passions over time, not a split between parts of the soul.

Article 49, "That strength of soul is not enough without knowledge of the truth" (AT XI 367–368). Very few are so weak and irresolute that they will only what their passion dictates. Most have determinate judgments by which they regulate part of their actions. These judgments are often false, and even founded on passions by which the will was earlier overcome or seduced, but since the will continues to follow them when the passion that caused them is absent, they can be counted as its own weapons, and souls judged stronger or weaker by how far they can follow them and resist contrary present passions. Yet there is a great difference between resolutions arising from some false opinion and those resting on knowledge of the truth. Following the latter, one is sure never to regret or repent; following the former, one always does once the error is discovered. Strength is formal, a matter of consistently following one's judgments, and so compatible with being firmly wrong. A passion can settle into a maxim, and once the will adopts it the maxim becomes its own weapon. Strength is therefore necessary but not sufficient; knowledge of the truth protects against repentance. Freedom from regret is the criterion, as in the letter to Elisabeth of 4 August 1645, where contentment requires having done what one judged best. Repentance itself will be treated as a passion in art. 191.

Article 50, "That there is no soul so weak that it cannot, if well guided, acquire absolute power over its passions" (AT XI 368–370). As already said (art. 44), each movement of the gland seems joined by nature to one of our thoughts from the beginning of life, yet habit can join it to others. Words show this: by nature they excite gland motions that represent to the soul only their sound when spoken or the shape of their letters when written, but through the habit of thinking of their meaning on hearing or seeing them, they usually make us conceive the meaning rather than the letters or syllables. Likewise, though the movements of gland, spirits and brain that represent certain objects are naturally joined with those that excite certain passions, habit can separate them and join them to very different ones, and this habit can be acquired by a single action without long practice. If one unexpectedly finds something filthy in food one is eating with appetite, the surprise can so change the brain's disposition that one can afterwards see such food only with horror, though one used to eat it with pleasure. The same appears in beasts. They have no reason, and perhaps no thought at all, yet all the movements of spirits and gland that excite passions in us occur in them too, serving to maintain and strengthen not passions but the movements of nerves and muscles that usually accompany them. A dog naturally runs at a partridge and flees at a gunshot, yet setters are trained so that the sight of a partridge makes them stop and the shot that follows makes them run toward it. Knowing this should give everyone courage to study the regulation of his passions. Since a little effort can change the brain's movements in animals without reason, it can do so still better in human beings, and even the weakest souls could acquire a very absolute command over all their passions if enough effort were used in training and guiding them. Habit can loosen two links: that between a sign and what it naturally represents, and that between a perception and the passion naturally joined to it. Both are institutions, so both can be re-instituted. The one-trial aversion shows how quickly this can happen. The beasts are given all the mechanism of passion without the passion, and "perhaps" no thought; the hedge matches the letter to More of 5 February 1649, where Descartes concedes that no proof shows animals lack thought. The trained dog proves that natural responses can be reversed; the argument for humans is *a fortiori*, since they add reason and will to direct the training. The "absolute power" of the title is acquired over time by reshaping dispositions; it does not cancel the limits of arts. 45–46 on what the will can do in the moment. "Well guided" carries the democratic premise of the *Discours*: souls differ less in endowment than in conduct. Part Three's general remedy (art. 211) rests on this article.

## Part Two. On the Number and Order of the Passions, and the Explanation of the Six Primitive Ones

Article 51, "What the first causes of the passions are" (AT XI 371–372). Art. 34 showed that the last and most proximate cause of the passions is the agitation by which the spirits move the small gland. That cannot tell the passions apart, so their sources and first causes must be sought. They can sometimes be caused by the soul's action when it resolves to conceive this or that object, and also by the body's temperament alone or by impressions met by chance in the brain, as when one feels sad or joyful without being able to say why. Still, all of them can also be excited by the objects that move the senses, and these are their most ordinary and principal causes. To find them all, it is therefore enough to consider all the effects of these objects. The proximate cause is uniform across the passions; only the remote cause differentiates them. Descartes therefore classifies passions by their objects' bearing on us, not by their physiology, though physiology fixed their genus in Part One. The objectless moods are admitted and set aside: a sadness without a reason is still sadness (art. 26), but it is not where one learns what sadness is. The choice of sense objects as the guiding case is methodological, the most frequent cause taken as the clue to the full list.

Article 52, "What their use is, and how they can be enumerated" (AT XI 372). Objects that move the senses do not excite different passions in us according to every difference in them, but only according to the different ways they can harm or benefit us, or in general matter to us. The use of all the passions consists only in disposing the soul to will the things nature tells us are useful and to persist in that volition, just as the agitation of the spirits that usually causes them disposes the body to the movements that serve to carry those things out. To enumerate them, one need only examine in order in how many ways that matter to us our senses can be moved by their objects. The principle of division is importance to us, not the object's features. A taxonomy of passions is thus a taxonomy of the ways things can concern us, and the list will be generated, not collected. The use stated here is art. 40's principal effect generalized and doubled: the passion makes the will want and keep wanting, while its bodily side prepares execution. "Nature tells us" ties the use of passions to the Sixth Meditation's teaching of nature, oriented to the good of the composite, which is why art. 137 will judge their use relative to the body.

### The Order and Enumeration of the Passions

Article 53, "Wonder" (AT XI 373). When our first encounter with an object surprises us and we judge it new, or very different from what we knew before or from what we supposed it ought to be, we wonder at it and are astonished. Since this can happen before we know at all whether the object suits us, wonder (*admiration*) is the first of all the passions. It has no contrary, since if the object has nothing surprising in it, we are not moved at all and consider it without passion. Wonder comes first because it precedes the evaluation that all the other passions require. Its object is novelty relative to our expectations, including what we "supposed it ought to be," so wonder registers a violated expectation, not mere unfamiliarity. Having no contrary sets it apart: the alternative to wonder is not an opposite passion but the absence of passion. This is the treatise's most original move against the Thomist list, which has no place for an evaluatively neutral passion and begins with love. Wonder inherits the Platonic and Aristotelian *thaumazein*, the beginning of philosophy, as art. 75 will confirm. The editors cite the autumn 1645 letters to Elisabeth (AT IV 313, 332), where Descartes first undertook to enumerate the passions.

Article 54, "Esteem and contempt, generosity or pride, and humility or baseness" (AT XI 373–374). Joined to wonder are esteem or contempt, according as it is the greatness or the smallness of an object that we wonder at. We can esteem or despise ourselves in this way, and from this come the passions, and then the habits, of magnanimity or pride and of humility or baseness. Esteem and contempt are wonder specified by magnitude, still prior to good and evil. The self is admitted as an object of wonder from the start; the ethics of Part Three will rest on how one wonders at oneself. The text distinguishes the passions from the habits they generate: generosity is both a passion and a virtue (arts. 160–161). The title reads "generosity" where the body has "magnanimity," the Aristotelian *megalopsychia*. The substitution of the French chivalric term for the scholastic one is deliberate and will be justified in art. 161.

Article 55, "Veneration and disdain" (AT XI 374). When we esteem or despise other objects that we regard as free causes, able to do good or harm, esteem produces veneration and mere contempt produces disdain. A new determination enters: the object's freedom, its power to do good or harm. Veneration and disdain are esteem and contempt directed at agents. Good and evil appear only as the object's capacity, not yet as our evaluation of it, so these still precede love and hatred. The category of "free cause" includes God, human beings, and anything we imagine as agents, as the treatment of veneration in art. 162 will make explicit.

Article 56, "Love and hatred" (AT XI 374). All the passions so far can be excited without our perceiving at all whether their object is good or bad. When a thing is represented to us as good with respect to us, that is, as suitable to us, we feel love for it, and when it is represented as bad or harmful, we are moved to hatred. Here the enumeration crosses from wonder's family into evaluation. "Good" is glossed immediately as "suitable to us" (*convenable*), a relational good. The verb is "represented": love answers to how a thing is presented, not to what it is, so love of a false good is still love. Love and hatred depend only on the representation of good and evil, without regard to time, which is why the next article introduces time as the further principle of division.

Article 57, "Desire" (AT XI 374–375). All the remaining passions arise from the same consideration of good and evil. To set them in order, Descartes distinguishes times. Since the passions lead us to look at the future much more than at the present or past, he begins with desire. Not only when one desires to acquire a good not yet had or avoid an evil thought possible, but also when one wishes only to keep a good or for an evil to stay absent—which is all this passion can extend to—it plainly always looks to the future. Time becomes the second axis of the classification, after good and evil. The claim that the passions face mainly toward the future gives them a practical orientation, consistent with art. 52: they serve the will, and the will acts toward what is to come. Desire is defined by its temporal object. Even the wish that a present good continue is desire because it concerns the future of that good.

Article 58, "Hope, apprehension, jealousy, confidence and despair" (AT XI 375). It is enough to think that obtaining a good or avoiding an evil is possible to be incited to desire it. If one also considers whether one's chances are high or low, what represents them as high excites hope, and what represents them as low excites apprehension, of which jealousy is a species. Extreme hope changes its nature and is called confidence or assurance, as extreme apprehension becomes despair. The third axis is probability. Hope and apprehension are desire qualified by assessed likelihood. Two thresholds mark changes of kind: at the extremes the passion stops being a wavering and becomes a settled state, confidence or despair. Jealousy as a species of apprehension is the apprehension of losing a good one possesses (art. 167).

Article 59, "Irresolution, courage, boldness, emulation, cowardice and terror" (AT XI 375–376). One can hope and fear even when the outcome does not depend on us at all. When it is represented as depending on us, there can be difficulty in choosing the means or in carrying them out. Difficulty in choice gives rise to irresolution, which disposes us to deliberate and take advice. Difficulty in execution is met by courage or boldness, of which emulation is a species. Cowardice is the contrary of courage, as fear or terror is of boldness. The fourth axis is dependence on us. Once the outcome is ours to effect, the passions bear on agency in two phases, deliberation and execution. Irresolution is given a use, the disposition to deliberate, so even a passion of hesitation serves the will. The pairings place each passion that serves action opposite one that undermines it.

Article 60, "Remorse" (AT XI 376). If one has resolved on some action before irresolution was removed, this gives rise to remorse of conscience, which looks not to the future like the preceding passions but to the present or past. Remorse is the trace of a decision taken before deliberation was complete. It marks the return from the future axis to the present and past. Its distinction from repentance (art. 63) is implicit: remorse comes from acting while still in doubt, repentance from having done evil. The *Discours*' second maxim, to act firmly even on doubtful opinions, is meant to forestall exactly this remorse, as art. 177 will say.

Article 61, "Joy and sadness" (AT XI 376). Consideration of a present good excites joy in us, and of a present evil sadness, when the good or evil is represented as belonging to us. Joy and sadness are defined by present time and by belonging to us. The second condition opens the next articles, which consider goods and evils belonging to others. Time thus recedes, and the axis of whose good it is takes over.

Article 62, "Derision, envy, pity" (AT XI 376–377). When the good or evil is represented as belonging to other people, we can judge them worthy or unworthy of it. If we judge them worthy, this excites only joy, in that it is some good for us to see things happen as they should; but the joy that comes from a good is serious, while that from an evil is accompanied by laughter and derision. If we judge them unworthy, the good excites envy and the evil pity, which are species of sadness. The passions concerning present goods and evils can often also be referred to future ones, insofar as the belief that they will come represents them as present. Another's fortune moves us through a judgment of desert. Deserved fortune gives us joy because the world going as it should is a good for us, so the observer has a stake in justice. Derision is joy at a deserved evil, not malice. Envy and pity are both sadness at undeserved fortune, mirror images. The closing remark lets anticipated certainty convert future goods into quasi-present ones, so the temporal grid is not rigid.

Article 63, "Self-satisfaction and repentance" (AT XI 377). We can also consider the cause of a present or past good or evil. Good done by ourselves gives us an inner satisfaction, the sweetest of all the passions, while evil done by ourselves excites repentance, the bitterest. A fifth axis appears: agency. When we are the cause, the evaluation turns back on ourselves. Self-satisfaction is the sweetest passion because it depends on what is most our own; the theme culminates in the contentment of the letters to Elisabeth and in generosity's satisfaction with having used free will well (art. 153). Repentance is its exact counterpart.

Article 64, "Favour and gratitude" (AT XI 377–378). Good done by others makes us feel favour toward them even when it was not done to us; when it was done to us, we add gratitude to favour. Others' agency now yields two passions, depending on whether we are the beneficiary. Favour is a disinterested approval of beneficence, gratitude the interested form. The distinction parallels art. 62: there is a passion of the spectator and a passion of the party concerned.

Article 65, "Indignation and anger" (AT XI 378). Likewise, evil done by others, when not referred to us, only gives us indignation toward them; when referred to us, it also moves anger. The mirror of art. 64. Anger is indignation plus the injury's reference to ourselves. "Referred to us" picks up the vocabulary of art. 22 in a new register: here reference is a matter of whom the evil touches, not where a perception is felt.

Article 66, "Glory and shame" (AT XI 378). The good that is or has been in us, referred to the opinion others may have of it, excites glory, and the evil, shame. These are the passions of reputation: self-evaluation mediated by others' judgment. The good "in us," not "done by us," widens the field from acts to qualities. Glory is distinct from the self-satisfaction of art. 63, which needs no audience.

Article 67, "Distaste, regret and cheerfulness" (AT XI 378). Sometimes the duration of a good causes boredom or distaste, whereas the duration of an evil lessens sadness. A past good gives rise to regret, a species of sadness, and a past evil to cheerfulness, a species of joy. Duration completes the temporal analysis. A good that lasts can turn tiresome; an evil that lasts loses its sting. Both show the passions tracking change rather than states, consistent with art. 1's definition of passion as what newly happens. Regret and cheerfulness close the cycle of past time.

Article 68, "Why this enumeration of the passions differs from the commonly accepted one" (AT XI 379). Descartes knows his order departs from all earlier writers, but not without reason. They draw their list from distinguishing two appetites in the sensitive part of the soul, the concupiscible and the irascible. Since he recognizes no distinction of parts in the soul (art. 47), this can only mean that it has two faculties, one of desiring, one of being angry. But it has in the same way the faculties of wondering, loving, hoping, fearing, and so of receiving each of the other passions or performing the actions they impel it to, so he sees no reason to refer them all to concupiscence or anger. Besides, their list does not include all the principal passions, as he believes his does. He speaks only of the principal ones, since more particular ones could be distinguished and their number is indefinite. The polemic of art. 47 now becomes a critique of method. If there are no parts, the concupiscible–irascible division names two powers among many, and privileging them is arbitrary. The faculty-language is reduced to tautology: a "faculty of loving" says only that the soul can love. The scholastic list of eleven (Thomas, I–II, q. 23) also misses wonder, so it is incomplete as well as ill-founded. The admission that particular passions are indefinite in number makes the enumeration a generated system of principal kinds, not a catalogue.

Article 69, "That there are only six primitive passions" (AT XI 380). The simple and primitive passions are few. Reviewing the list, one easily sees that only six are such: wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy and sadness. All the others are composed of some of these or are species of them. So that their number does not confuse readers, Descartes will treat the six separately and then show how the others derive from them. This is a second, analytic ordering laid over the generative one. Arts. 53–67 generated the passions along axes of novelty, value, time, probability and agency; now they are reduced to six elements. The two orderings do not coincide: esteem, which came second, is not primitive but derived from wonder (art. 149), and desire is primitive though defined by time. The six correspond to the axes: wonder for novelty, love and hatred for value, desire for the future, joy and sadness for the present. The simplicity claim answers the Stoic four and the Thomist eleven with a more economical basis.

Article 70, "Of wonder: its definition and its cause" (AT XI 380–381). Wonder is a sudden surprise of the soul that makes it turn to consider attentively the objects that seem to it rare and extraordinary. It is caused first by the impression in the brain that represents the object as rare and so as worth close consideration, and then by the movement of the spirits, which this impression disposes to flow with great force toward the place in the brain where it is, to strengthen and preserve it there, and also to pass from there into the muscles that hold the sense organs in their current position, so that the impression is maintained by them if they formed it. Wonder is defined by its function: it directs attention. Its physiology works entirely within the brain and the muscles of the sense organs. The spirits reinforce the impression and fix the senses on the object; the passion is a natural mechanism of attention. Unlike other passions, its cause includes a judgment-like impression of rarity, "worth close consideration," so evaluation of a cognitive kind is built into the trigger. This bodily attention is the counterpart of the voluntary attention of art. 43.

Article 71, "That no change occurs in the heart or blood in this passion" (AT XI 381). Wonder has the peculiarity that it is not accompanied by any change in the heart and blood, as the other passions are. Since its object is not good or evil but only knowledge of the thing wondered at, it has no relation to the heart and blood, on which all the body's good depends, but only to the brain, where the sense organs serving this knowledge are. The physiology tracks the passion's object: passions concerned with good and evil pass through the heart because the heart governs the body's good. Wonder concerns knowledge, so its organ is the brain alone. This is an exception to art. 46, which said almost all passions involve a commotion of the heart, and to the definition of art. 27 as illustrated by fear. Wonder is a passion without bodily welfare at stake, a purely cognitive affect.

Article 72, "What the force of wonder consists in" (AT XI 381–382). Wonder is nonetheless very forceful because of surprise, the sudden and unexpected arrival of the impression that changes the movement of the spirits. Surprise is proper to this passion, so when it is found in others, as it usually is, augmenting them, it is because wonder is joined to them. Its force depends on two things: novelty, and the fact that the movement it causes has its full force from the start. Such a movement has more effect than those that begin weak and grow gradually and can easily be diverted. New sensory objects also touch the brain in places not usually touched, and these, being more tender than parts hardened by frequent agitation, increase the effect. This is no more surprising than that the soles of our feet, used to rough contact from the body's weight, barely feel it in walking, while a much lighter tickling is almost unbearable because unusual. Surprise is wonder's signature, and wonder thereby becomes a component of other passions, intensifying them. Force is analysed into two factors: suddenness, a motion at full strength from its onset, and novelty, a part of the brain not yet hardened by use. The tickled sole shows habituation as physiological desensitization. The brain has a history of wear, which is why familiarity dulls wonder (art. 78).

Article 73, "What astonishment is" (AT XI 382–383). Surprise can so drive the spirits in the brain's cavities toward the place of the impression that it sometimes sends all of them there, and keeps them so occupied in preserving it that none pass into the muscles or turn aside from the first traces they followed. The whole body then stays motionless as a statue, and one can perceive only the first face the object presented and so acquire no more particular knowledge of it. This is what is commonly called being astonished; astonishment is an excess of wonder and can only be bad. Excess turns wonder's function against itself. Wonder fixes attention in order to learn; astonishment fixes it so completely that learning stops, since the senses cannot explore the object further. The statue image is literal: all the spirits are used up in preserving one impression. "Can only be bad" makes astonishment the one item so far that has no use at all.

Article 74, "What all the passions serve for, and what harm they do" (AT XI 383). From what has been said, the usefulness of all the passions consists only in strengthening and prolonging thoughts in the soul that it is good for it to keep and that could easily be effaced without them. All the harm they can do consists in their strengthening and preserving these thoughts more than necessary, or strengthening and preserving others on which it is not good to dwell. The usefulness of the passions is reformulated in terms of thought: passions are fixatives of thought. This is the cognitive counterpart of art. 52's practical use. Both benefit and harm come from one function, so there are no bad passions in kind, only misapplied or excessive ones, which anticipates art. 211. The "maintained and strengthened" of art. 27 now turns out to be the passion's very point, not just its physiology.

Article 75, "What wonder in particular is useful for" (AT XI 384). Wonder is useful in making us learn and retain in memory things we did not know before. We wonder only at what seems rare and extraordinary, and nothing can seem so except because we did not know it, or because it differs from what we knew, which is what makes us call it extraordinary. When something unknown comes newly before our understanding or our senses, we do not retain it in memory unless the idea is strengthened in the brain by some passion, or by the application of the understanding, which our will determines to particular attention and reflection. The other passions can serve to make us notice things that seem good or bad, but for things that seem only rare we have wonder alone. That is why those with no natural inclination to it are usually very ignorant. Wonder is the passion of learning. Memory needs reinforcement, and the two sources named, passion and willed attention, are the involuntary and voluntary routes to the same physiological result. Wonder covers what the other passions cannot: the merely new, with no bearing on our welfare. The link between the passions and knowledge is thus internal to the system, not an add-on. Reading this alongside art. 42, memory traces are deepened by the force of the impression.

Article 76, "How it can harm, and how its deficiency can be made up and its excess corrected" (AT XI 385). It happens far more often that we wonder too much, and are astonished at things deserving little or no consideration, than that we wonder too little, and this can entirely remove or pervert the use of reason. Although it is good to be born with some inclination to this passion, because it disposes us to acquire the sciences, we should afterward try to free ourselves of it as much as possible. Its deficiency is easily made up by particular reflection and attention, to which our will can always oblige our understanding when we judge the matter worth it. But the only remedy against excessive wonder is to acquire knowledge of many things and to practise considering those that can seem rarest and strangest. The prescription is asymmetric. Wonder starts inquiry and should then be outgrown, since will-directed attention can replace it while nothing replaces knowledge as the cure for excess. The ideal knower has replaced the passion with the voluntary attention that performs its function. The cure is the encyclopaedic programme of the *Météores*, whose stated aim was to remove wonder at the phenomena of the sky by explaining them.

Article 77, "That it is neither the most stupid nor the cleverest who are most inclined to wonder" (AT XI 385–386). Only the dull and stupid have no natural inclination to wonder, but that does not mean the most intelligent are always most inclined to it. Those most inclined are mainly those who have fairly good common sense but no great opinion of their own sufficiency. The inclination depends on self-assessment as well as on ability. Someone who thinks himself sufficient is not surprised; someone with modest self-estimate finds more things exceed his expectations. Wonder thus connects with esteem and humility (art. 54): the right disposition to learn includes a modest opinion of oneself. The "fairly good common sense" echoes the *bon sens* of the *Discours*.

Article 78, "That its excess can turn into habit when one fails to correct it" (AT XI 386). Although wonder seems to diminish with use, since the more rare things one meets, the more one gets used to ceasing to wonder and thinking that anything further is ordinary, when it is excessive and makes one fix attention only on the first image of objects without acquiring further knowledge, it leaves a habit that disposes the soul to stop in the same way at every other object, provided it seems even slightly new. This is what prolongs the sickness of the blindly curious, who seek rarities only to wonder at them, not to know them. They gradually become so prone to wonder that trivial things stop them as much as things worth investigating. Two opposite effects of repetition: ordinary wonder habituates itself away; excessive wonder, which halts at first images, entrenches itself. The difference lies in whether inquiry follows. The pathology of curiosity takes up the Augustinian vice of *curiositas*, here given a mechanical genesis: a habit of halting, not a disordered desire.

Article 79, "The definitions of love and hatred" (AT XI 387). Love is an emotion of the soul, caused by the movement of the spirits, that incites it to join itself by its will to objects that appear suitable to it. Hatred is an emotion caused by the spirits that incites the soul to will to be separated from objects that present themselves as harmful. Saying that these emotions are caused by the spirits distinguishes love and hatred, which are passions and depend on the body, both from the judgments that also bring the soul to join itself by will to things it esteems good and separate itself from those it esteems bad, and from the emotions that these judgments alone excite in the soul. The definitions follow the pattern of art. 27. Love is an incitation to will a union, not the union itself. The distinction from judgment-based love is the point: there is a rational love, the will's union with what it judges good, and an intellectual emotion that judgment alone produces. The passion is the spirit-caused counterpart. Three things carry one name: the passion of love, the volition of union, and intellectual love, as in the letter to Chanut of 1 February 1647.

Article 80, "What it is to join or separate oneself by will" (AT XI 387). By "will" Descartes does not mean desire, which is a separate passion concerned with the future, but the consent by which one considers oneself as already joined with what one loves, imagining a whole of which one is only a part and the thing loved another. In hatred, on the contrary, one considers oneself alone as a whole, entirely separate from the thing one is averse to. Love is a present consent, not a desire, and so needs no future and no absence. Its content is mereological: the lover imagines a whole of which he is a part. Hatred is the converse: the self as a complete whole, sealed off. This is the article on which arts. 81–83 build. It removes from love the lack that Plato's *Symposium* and the tradition of *amor concupiscentiae* placed at its core. The image of the whole will return in sexual attraction (art. 90), where the lover feels like half of a whole.

Article 81, "Of the distinction commonly made between love of concupiscence and love of benevolence" (AT XI 388). Two sorts of love are commonly distinguished: love of benevolence, which incites us to wish well to what we love, and love of concupiscence, which makes us desire the thing we love. This distinction concerns only love's effects, not its essence. As soon as one is joined by will to an object of any kind, one has benevolence toward it, joining to it by will also the things one thinks suitable to it, which is one of love's principal effects. And if one judges it good to possess it, or to be associated with it otherwise than by will, one desires it, which is also one of love's most common effects. The editors cite the letter to Chanut of 1 February 1647 (AT IV 606), where the same correction is made. The scholastic distinction (Thomas, I–II, q. 26, a. 4) is demoted from a division of kinds to a division of effects. Benevolence follows from union because joining oneself to something makes its good one's own concern. Concupiscence is love plus desire, not a species of love. The essence of love is the union of art. 80, so every love is in principle benevolent.

Article 82, "How very different passions agree in sharing in love" (AT XI 388–389). There is no need to distinguish as many species of love as there are objects. The passions of an ambitious man for glory, a miser for money, a drunkard for wine, a brute for a woman he wants to violate, a man of honour for his friend or his mistress, and a good father for his children are very different, yet they are alike in sharing in love. But the first four love only the possession of their objects, not the objects themselves, for which they have only desire mixed with other particular passions. The good father's love for his children is so pure that he wants nothing from them, wants to possess them no otherwise than he does, nor be joined to them more closely; regarding them as other selves, he seeks their good as his own or more, because, representing himself and them as a whole of which he is not the better part, he often prefers their interests to his and does not fear losing himself to save them. The affection of men of honour for their friends is of this nature, though rarely so perfect, and their love for a mistress shares much in it but also a little in the other kind. The editors cite the letter to Chanut (AT IV 611–612). Love varies with what one is joined to. In the first four cases the object of union is possession; the person or thing is only an object of desire. The father's love is pure because union is already complete, and the self is ranked as the lesser part of the whole. The mereology of art. 80 now yields an ethics of sacrifice: love can rationally prefer the whole over oneself. The mistress, mixed between the two forms, keeps Descartes' honesty about erotic love.

Article 83, "Of the difference between simple affection, friendship and devotion" (AT XI 389–391). Love is better distinguished by the esteem one has for its object compared with oneself. When one esteems the object less than oneself, one has only simple affection; equal to oneself, friendship; more than oneself, devotion. One can have affection for a flower, a bird or a horse, but unless one's mind is very disordered, friendship only for human beings. They are so much its object that there is no man so imperfect that one cannot have a very perfect friendship for him, when one thinks he loves one and one has a truly noble and generous soul (arts. 154, 156). The principal object of devotion is the sovereign Divinity, to whom one cannot fail to be devoted when one knows it as one should, but one can also have devotion for one's prince, country, city, even a particular man, when one esteems him much more than oneself. The difference shows mainly in effects. Since in all three one considers oneself joined to the thing loved, one is always ready to give up the lesser part of the whole one composes with it to preserve the other. In simple affection one always prefers oneself to what one loves; in devotion one prefers the thing loved so much that one does not fear dying for it, as those have shown who exposed themselves to certain death for their prince, their city, or individuals they were devoted to. Love's kinds are fixed by combining love with esteem, the derived passion from wonder's family (art. 54). The whole-and-parts model of art. 80 plus comparative esteem produces a calculus: one sacrifices the part esteemed less. Friendship requires an equal, so only persons qualify, and any person qualifies for the generous, since generosity esteems all humans as equally possessing free will (art. 154). Devotion's paradigm is God; the political cases make dying for one's country a rational consequence of love joined with esteem.

Article 84, "That there are not as many species of hatred as of love" (AT XI 391). Although hatred is directly opposed to love, it is not divided into as many species, because we do not notice the differences among the evils we are separated from by will as much as those among the goods we are joined to. The asymmetry is psychological, not logical. Union attends to its object; separation turns away from it, so evils are less discriminated.

Article 85, "Of attraction and repulsion" (AT XI 391–392). The only considerable distinction common to love and hatred is that their objects can be represented to the soul by the external senses, or by the internal senses and its own reason. We commonly call good or bad what our internal senses or our reason make us judge suitable or contrary to our nature, and beautiful or ugly what is so represented by the external senses, especially sight, which alone is more esteemed than all the others. Hence two species of love: for good things and for beautiful things, the latter of which may be called attraction (*agrément*), to distinguish it from the other and from desire, which is often called love. Likewise two species of hatred, for bad things and for ugly things, the latter called horror or aversion. Most notably, attraction and horror are usually more violent than the other kinds of love and hatred, because what reaches the soul through the senses touches it more strongly than what reason represents, yet they usually contain less truth, so that of all the passions they deceive most and must be guarded against most carefully. The beautiful is defined as the good as presented to the external senses, chiefly vision. Aesthetic value is not autonomous; it is value presented through a less reliable channel. The inverse relation of force and truth reappears from art. 28: the senses move most and inform least. Attraction is named separately to keep it apart from desire, which ordinary language calls love, and art. 90 will show why ordinary usage confuses them.

Article 86, "The definition of desire" (AT XI 392). The passion of desire is an agitation of the soul caused by the spirits that disposes it to will for the future the things it represents as suitable. So one desires not only the presence of an absent good but also the preservation of a present one, and moreover the absence of evil, both of evil one already has and of evil one thinks may come. The definition says "agitation," not "emotion." Desire is the most physically agitating passion (art. 101) and the most directly practical, "disposes to will." Its scope, already fixed in art. 57, includes the preservation of goods and the removal of evils, and this prepares art. 87.

Article 87, "That it is a passion that has no contrary" (AT XI 393). In the Schools, the passion that tends toward pursuing good, alone called desire, is opposed to the one that tends toward fleeing evil, called aversion. But since there is no good whose privation is not an evil, and no evil, considered as something positive, whose privation is not a good, and since in pursuing riches one necessarily flees poverty and in fleeing illness one pursues health, it seems to Descartes that one and the same movement leads to pursuing good and fleeing the evil contrary to it. The only difference is that desire tending toward a good is accompanied by love, then hope and joy, while the same desire tending away from the contrary evil is accompanied by hatred, apprehension and sadness, which is why it is judged contrary to itself. But considered as relating at once to a good to be pursued and the opposite evil to be avoided, it is plainly a single passion doing both. The Thomist pair desire and aversion (I–II, q. 23, a. 4) is collapsed. The argument rests on the convertibility of good and evil by privation, with an important caveat: evil "considered as something positive." Descartes elsewhere treats evil as privation; here he concedes that the passions treat evils as positive presences, as pain is. The apparent opposition comes from the accompanying passions, not from desire. Desire therefore joins wonder as a passion without contrary, though for a different reason: wonder precedes value, while desire covers both poles of value.

Article 88, "What its various species are" (AT XI 394). There would be more reason to divide desire into as many species as there are objects pursued. Curiosity, which is nothing but a desire to know, differs greatly from the desire for glory, and this from the desire for revenge, and so on. But it is enough to know that there are as many species of desire as of love and hatred, and that the strongest and most considerable arise from attraction and horror. Desire inherits its varieties from the love or hatred behind it, so its species are derivative. Curiosity, a species of desire, must be kept distinct from excessive wonder, the blind curiosity of art. 78. Pointing to attraction and horror sets up arts. 89–90.

Article 89, "What the desire is that arises from horror" (AT XI 394–395). Although there is only one desire pursuing a good and fleeing the contrary evil, desire from attraction is very different from desire from horror. These two, which are truly contrary, are not the good and evil that serve as the objects of these desires, but only two emotions of the soul that dispose it to pursue two very different things. Horror is instituted by nature to represent to the soul a sudden and unexpected death. So even if it is sometimes only the touch of a worm, the sound of a trembling leaf, or its shadow that causes horror, one first feels as much emotion as if a very evident danger of death presented itself to the senses. This suddenly produces the agitation that leads the soul to use all its strength to avoid so present an evil, and this species of desire is commonly called flight or aversion. Attraction and horror, not good and evil, are the contraries here. Horror has a fixed natural meaning, sudden death, whatever the trigger, so its intensity is set by the institution, not by the actual stimulus. This explains the disproportion between a rustling leaf and the panic it causes. It is a clear case of art. 85's thesis that sensory passions have force without truth. The scholastic "aversion" returns as the name of this desire, now as a species, not as desire's contrary.

Article 90, "What the desire is that arises from attraction" (AT XI 395–396). Attraction, on the contrary, is particularly instituted by nature to represent the enjoyment of what attracts as the greatest of all goods belonging to man, which makes one desire it very ardently. There are various sorts of attraction, and the desires arising from them are not all equally powerful: the beauty of flowers only moves us to look at them, that of fruit to eat it. But the principal one comes from the perfections one imagines in a person one thinks could become another self. With the difference of sex that nature has put in humans as in animals without reason, it has also put certain impressions in the brain that make one, at a certain age and time, regard oneself as deficient, as if one were only half of a whole of which a person of the other sex should be the other half. The acquisition of this half is confusedly represented by nature as the greatest of all imaginable goods. Though one sees several persons of the other sex, one does not wish for several at once, because nature does not make one imagine needing more than one half. When one notices something in one that attracts more than what one notices in others at the same time, this determines the soul to feel for that one alone all the inclination nature gives it to pursue the good it represents as the greatest one can possess. This inclination or desire arising from attraction is more commonly called love than the passion of love described above. It has stranger effects, and it is the main material of novelists and poets. The Aristophanic myth of the *Symposium* is rebuilt as a natural institution in the brain, timed by age. The structure of art. 80, a whole with two parts, recurs, but here the half is lacking, so erotic "love" is really desire, as ordinary usage hides. Exclusivity is explained by nature's arithmetic: one half completes a whole. Choice of partner is a comparative determination at a given moment, which leaves room for contingency and the "strange effects." "Confusedly represented" places erotic love among the most forceful and least truthful passions (art. 85).

Article 91, "The definition of joy" (AT XI 396–397). Joy is a pleasant emotion of the soul in which consists its enjoyment of the good that the brain's impressions represent to it as its own. The enjoyment of good consists in this emotion, because the soul receives no other fruit from all the goods it possesses, and while it has no joy in them, it enjoys them no more than if it did not possess them. The qualification "that the brain's impressions represent as its own" keeps this joy, a passion, distinct from purely intellectual joy, which comes into the soul by its own action alone and may be called a pleasant emotion excited in itself, in which consists the enjoyment of the good its understanding represents as its own. While the soul is joined to the body, intellectual joy can hardly fail to be accompanied by passionate joy. As soon as our understanding notices that we possess some good, even one so different from anything bodily that it is unimaginable, the imagination at once makes some impression in the brain, from which follows the movement of the spirits that excites the passion of joy. Joy is not a reaction to possession; it is the enjoyment itself. Possession without joy has no value for the soul, so the passion completes the good, not just signals it. The doublet of passionate and intellectual joy, and the claim that the second almost always produces the first, make the union an amplifier of even purely intellectual goods. Imagination is the bridge: it translates an unimaginable good into a brain impression. This is the general rule of the treatise's intellectual emotions (arts. 147–148).

Article 92, "The definition of sadness" (AT XI 397). Sadness is an unpleasant languor in which consists the discomfort the soul receives from the evil or deficiency that the brain's impressions represent to it as belonging to it. There is also an intellectual sadness, which is not the passion but is hardly ever without it. "Languor," not "emotion": sadness is a lowering, in keeping with its physiology of narrowed heart orifices (art. 105). "Evil or deficiency" widens its object to lack. The parallel with art. 91 is exact, including the intellectual counterpart.

Article 93, "What the causes of these two passions are" (AT XI 398). When intellectual joy or sadness excites the passion, the cause is evident, and it follows from the definitions that joy comes from the belief that one possesses some good, sadness from the belief that one has some evil or deficiency. But one often feels sad or joyful without being able distinctly to identify the good or evil that causes it, namely when the good or evil makes its impressions in the brain without the soul's mediation. This happens sometimes because it belongs only to the body, and sometimes, though it belongs to the soul, because the soul does not consider it under the form of good or evil but under some other form whose impression is joined in the brain with that of good or evil. The objectless moods of art. 51 are now explained. There are two routes to unexplained joy or sadness: bodily goods and evils that never pass through judgment, and goods and evils of the soul presented under another description whose brain impression is associated with value. The second route is an account of affect by unconscious association, explained by brain linkage, and arts. 94–95 illustrate both.

Article 94, "How these passions are excited by goods and evils that concern only the body, and what titillation and pain consist in" (AT XI 398–400). In full health and fine weather one feels a gaiety that comes from no function of the understanding, only from the impressions the spirits make in the brain; and one feels sad in the same way when the body is unwell, though one does not know it. Titillation of the senses is followed so closely by joy, and pain by sadness, that most people do not distinguish them. They differ greatly, though, since one can sometimes suffer pain with joy and receive titillations that displease. Joy usually follows titillation because what is called titillation or pleasant feeling consists in sense objects exciting some movement in the nerves that would harm them if they lacked strength to resist or if the body were not well disposed. This makes an impression in the brain which, instituted by nature to testify to this good disposition and strength, represents it to the soul as a good belonging to it insofar as it is united to the body, and so excites joy. Almost the same reason makes us take natural pleasure in being moved by all kinds of passions, even sadness and hatred, when they are caused only by strange adventures represented on a stage or similar subjects that cannot harm us and seem to tickle our soul by touching it. Pain usually produces sadness because the sensation called pain always comes from an action so violent that it injures the nerves; instituted by nature to signify to the soul the damage the body receives and its weakness in failing to resist, it represents both as evils that are always unpleasant to it, except when they cause goods it values more. Pleasure and pain are sensations referred to the body (art. 24), joy and sadness passions referred to the soul, and the article separates them. Pleasure is defined strikingly: it is a near-harm successfully resisted, a stimulus that would injure a weaker body and so testifies to strength. Pleasure signifies capacity, pain signifies damage and weakness. The theatre is the case of the soul's own titillation: passions that would be painful are enjoyed because nothing is at risk, an account of tragic pleasure rooted in the mechanism of pleasure, not in Aristotelian catharsis. It prepares the letters' and Part Three's thesis that the soul can enjoy even sadness when it is aware of its own strength (art. 147).

Article 95, "How they can also be excited by goods and evils the soul does not notice, though they belong to it, such as the pleasure of taking risks or of remembering past evils" (AT XI 400). Young people often take pleasure in undertaking hard things and exposing themselves to great dangers, even when they expect neither profit nor glory. This comes from the thought that what they undertake is difficult, which makes an impression in their brain that, joined with the one they could form if they thought it good to feel brave, fortunate, skilful or strong enough to risk so much, makes them take pleasure in it. The contentment of old men in remembering the evils they suffered comes from representing to themselves that it is a good to have been able to survive in spite of them. These are the second route of art. 93: goods of the soul, here the sense of its own strength, presented under another form, difficulty or past suffering, and felt without being identified. The pleasure in risk is a pleasure in one's own capacity, the psychic analogue of art. 94's titillation. In both cases the soul enjoys a good it does not explicitly represent, a model of affective life partly opaque to itself, and Descartes explains it without any appeal to a hidden agency in the soul, by association of brain impressions.

Article 96, "What movements of the blood and spirits cause the five preceding passions" (AT XI 401). The five passions now under discussion, love, hatred, desire, joy and sadness, are so joined to or opposed to one another that it is easier to consider them together than one by one, as was done for wonder. Their cause is not, like wonder's, in the brain alone, but also in the heart, spleen, liver and all the other parts that serve to produce the blood and so the spirits. Although all the veins carry their blood toward the heart, the blood of some is sometimes pushed there more forcefully than that of others, and the openings by which blood enters or leaves the heart are sometimes wider and sometimes narrower. The editors refer to Elisabeth's letter of 25 April 1646 and Descartes' reply of May 1646 (AT IV 404, 407–408), where she questioned exactly this physiology. The five are treated as a system because they share an organ, the heart, and two variables, the force with which blood is driven from each source and the aperture of the heart's orifices. Wonder's exclusion follows from art. 71. The approach is experimental, as the next article's title says.

Article 97, "The principal observations that serve to show these movements in love" (AT XI 401–402). Considering the alterations experience shows in the body while the soul is agitated by various passions, Descartes observes that in love, when alone, that is, unaccompanied by any strong joy, desire or sadness, the pulse is even and much fuller and stronger than usual, a gentle warmth is felt in the chest, and food is digested very quickly in the stomach, so that this passion is good for health. The editors cite the same exchange with Elisabeth (AT IV 404, 408–409). The decisive qualifier is "when alone." Passions are usually mixed, and the physician must isolate pure cases to read their signs, as art. 112 will acknowledge. That love is healthy is a medical claim drawn from the data, and it will support the thesis that the passions serve the body (art. 137).

Article 98, "In hatred" (AT XI 402). In hatred, by contrast, the pulse is uneven, smaller and often faster; one feels chills mixed with a certain harsh, prickling heat in the chest; the stomach stops doing its work and tends to vomit and reject the food eaten, or at least to spoil it and turn it into bad humours. Each sign is the inverse of love's: unevenness against evenness, chill and prickle against gentle warmth, rejection against ready digestion. The "bad humours" keep a Galenic notion as a description of corrupted digestion. The symmetry prepares arts. 102–103, where love and hatred have opposed mechanisms.

Article 99, "In joy" (AT XI 402–403). In joy, the pulse is even and faster than usual but not as strong or full as in love; a pleasant heat spreads not only in the chest but through all the outer parts of the body with the blood seen flowing there abundantly; and yet one sometimes loses appetite, because digestion proceeds less well than usual. Joy differs from love by distribution: love concentrates warmth in the chest, joy spreads it outward. Loss of appetite is an observation, not an inference, and art. 104 explains it: in joy the nerves of the digestive organs are not active.

Article 100, "In sadness" (AT XI 403). In sadness, the pulse is weak and slow, one feels as it were bonds around the heart squeezing it and ice freezing it and communicating its cold to the rest of the body; yet one sometimes still has a good appetite and feels the stomach doing its work, provided no hatred is mixed with the sadness. The images of bonds and ice record how sadness is felt, and art. 105 turns them into mechanics: narrowed orifices, unagitated venous blood. The preserved appetite separates sadness from hatred, so mixtures can be read by their digestive signs.

Article 101, "In desire" (AT XI 403). Desire has this peculiarity: it agitates the heart more violently than any other passion and supplies the brain with more spirits, which, passing into the muscles, make all the senses sharper and all the parts of the body more mobile. Desire is the most energizing passion and the only one whose sign is a general readiness to act. This fits its role as the passion most directly tied to the will (art. 86) and prepares art. 120, where the mobility is qualified.

Article 102, "The movement of the blood and spirits in love" (AT XI 403–404). These observations and many others led Descartes to judge that when the understanding represents some object of love, the impression this thought makes in the brain sends the animal spirits through the nerves of the sixth pair to the muscles around the intestines and stomach, in such a way that food juice, which turns into new blood, passes quickly to the heart without stopping in the liver. Pushed there more forcefully than the blood of other parts, it enters in greater abundance and produces a stronger heat, because it is coarser than blood already rarefied several times in passing through the heart. The heart therefore also sends to the brain spirits whose parts are coarser and more agitated than usual, and these, strengthening the impression the first thought of the loved object made there, oblige the soul to dwell on that thought. In this consists the passion of love. Love begins in "the understanding" representing its object, so the cognitive trigger comes first and physiology follows. The mechanism explains each sign of art. 97: food juice sent straight to the heart explains good digestion, coarse blood the strong pulse and warmth. It ends by closing the loop of art. 36: the new spirits reinforce the initiating impression and hold the soul to its object. "In this consists the passion" names the whole loop, not one link. The "sixth pair" are the vagus nerves in the old numbering.

Article 103, "In hatred" (AT XI 404–405). In hatred, the first thought of the object of aversion so directs the spirits toward the muscles of the stomach and intestines that they prevent food juice from mixing with the blood, closing all the openings through which it usually flows. It also directs them toward the small nerves of the spleen and the lower liver, where the bile is stored, so that the parts of the blood usually thrown back there leave and flow with the blood of the vena cava toward the heart. This makes its heat very uneven, since blood from the spleen barely warms and rarefies, while blood from the lower liver, where the gall always is, ignites and expands very quickly. The spirits going to the brain therefore have very unequal parts and very irregular movements, and they strengthen the ideas of hatred already imprinted there and dispose the soul to thoughts full of sourness and bitterness. Hatred is a mixture of black bile and yellow bile, so the humoral temperaments survive as sources of blood with different rarefaction (art. 15). Unevenness at the source explains the uneven pulse and the mixed chills and heat of art. 98, and the closed digestive passages explain the stomach's revolt. The "bitterness" of hateful thoughts is no longer a metaphor alone: the bile's spirits give thought its tone.

Article 104, "In joy" (AT XI 405–406). In joy, it is less the nerves of spleen, liver, stomach or intestines that act than those in all the rest of the body, and especially the one around the heart's orifices, which, opening and widening them, lets the blood that the other nerves drive from the veins to the heart enter and leave in greater quantity than usual. Because this blood has already passed several times through the heart, coming from the arteries into the veins, it expands very easily and produces spirits whose parts are very even and fine, fit to form and strengthen the brain impressions that give the soul cheerful and tranquil thoughts. Joy works by aperture and by already-refined blood, not by new matter. That explains the even, quick pulse and the warmth spread outward (art. 99). The inactivity of the digestive nerves explains the lost appetite. Fine, even spirits match even, tranquil thoughts, a correspondence between the texture of the spirits and the quality of the thinking.

Article 105, "In sadness" (AT XI 406). In sadness, the heart's openings are much narrowed by the small nerve around them, and the venous blood is not agitated at all, so very little goes to the heart. Meanwhile the passages through which food juice flows from stomach and intestines to the liver stay open, so appetite does not diminish, except when hatred, often joined to sadness, closes them. The editors cite the letters to Elisabeth (AT IV 405, 409). The "bonds" of art. 100 are the narrowing nerve. The preserved appetite confirms the mechanism, since digestion is left untouched. Sadness and hatred are distinguished by whether the digestive passages close.

Article 106, "In desire" (AT XI 406–407). Desire has this property: the will to obtain some good or avoid some evil promptly sends the spirits from the brain to all the parts of the body that can serve the actions required, and particularly to the heart and the parts that supply it with most blood, so that, receiving more than usual, it sends a greater quantity of spirits to the brain, both to maintain and strengthen the idea of this volition and to pass from there into all the sense organs and all the muscles that can be used to obtain what is desired. Here the starting point is "the will to obtain," which seems to blur the line between desire as passion and volition. The best reading is that the passion's loop includes the volition it disposes the soul toward (art. 86): the passion inclines the will, the will's resolve sends spirits, and the spirits reinforce both the idea and the bodily readiness. This explains the general mobility of art. 101.

Article 107, "What the cause of these movements is in love" (AT XI 407–408). Descartes derives the reasons for all this from what was said above: soul and body are so linked that once we have joined some bodily action with some thought, one never presents itself afterward without the other. Those who drank some medicine with great aversion while ill cannot afterwards drink or eat anything tasting like it without the same aversion, nor think of the aversion one has for medicines without the same taste coming to mind. The first passions our soul had when it began to be joined to the body must have arisen when the blood or other juice entering the heart was a more suitable food than usual for maintaining the heat that is the principle of life. This led the soul to join that food to itself by will, that is, to love it. At the same time the spirits flowed from the brain to the muscles that could press or agitate the parts from which the food had come to the heart, so that they would send more, namely the stomach and intestines, whose agitation increases appetite, or the liver and lungs, which the diaphragm's muscles can press. That is why this same movement of the spirits has accompanied the passion of love ever since. The editors refer to the letter to Chanut of 1 February 1647 (AT IV 604–605), where the infantile origin of the four passions is first set out. The explanation is genetic. The current physiology of love is a fossil of the first nourishment. The law of association, once joined, always joined, explains why an adult's love of a person still moves the digestive organs: the movement was wired to love when love's first object was food. The medicine example shows that association works in both directions, from taste to aversion and from aversion to taste. This is a theory of affect laid down before reflection, in the prenatal or infant union, and it explains the peculiar non-rationality of passionate bodily signs.

Article 108, "In hatred" (AT XI 408–409). Sometimes, by contrast, some foreign juice came to the heart that was unfit to maintain its heat or could even extinguish it. Then the spirits rising from the heart to the brain excited hatred in the soul, and the spirits went from the brain to the nerves that could push blood from the spleen and the small veins of the liver toward the heart to keep the harmful juice out, and also to those that could drive the juice back toward the intestines and stomach, or sometimes make the stomach vomit it. That is why these movements usually accompany hatred. One can see that the liver has many fairly wide veins through which food juice passes from the portal vein to the vena cava and the heart without stopping, but also countless smaller ones where it can stop and which always hold reserve blood, as the spleen does; this blood, being coarser, can better feed the heart's fire when stomach and intestines fail to supply it. Hatred began as rejection of bad food. The reserve blood of spleen and liver was mobilized to displace it, which is why the spleen and bile are active in hatred (art. 103). The anatomical aside about liver channels, the wide ones that bypass and the narrow ones that store, supplies the plumbing that lets love's food juice reach the heart directly (art. 102) and hatred draw on reserves.

Article 109, "In joy" (AT XI 409). Sometimes too, at the beginning of life, the blood in the veins was a food suitable enough to maintain the heart's heat, and in such quantity that the heart needed no other nourishment. This excited joy in the soul and at the same time made the heart's orifices open more than usual, and the spirits, flowing abundantly from the brain not only into the nerves that open the orifices but generally into all those that push venous blood toward the heart, prevented new blood from coming from liver, spleen, intestines and stomach. That is why the same movements accompany joy. Joy is the original state of sufficiency: the heart content with the blood already circulating. It explains why joy works with recirculated blood and idle digestion (art. 104).

Article 110, "In sadness" (AT XI 410). Sometimes, by contrast, the body lacked nourishment, and this must have made the soul feel its first sadness, at least the sadness not joined with hatred. This also narrowed the heart's orifices, since they received little blood, and a fair part of that blood came from the spleen, the last reservoir supplying the heart when it gets too little from elsewhere. That is why the movements of spirits and nerves that narrow the heart's orifices and bring blood from the spleen always accompany sadness. Sadness is the original state of want, and the spleen's role in it is the mechanical basis of melancholy's old association with black bile. The qualifier "at least the sadness not joined with hatred" keeps the genealogy precise: pure sadness is lack, while sadness mixed with hatred also involves bad matter.

Article 111, "In desire" (AT XI 410–411). All the first desires the soul could have had when newly joined to the body were to receive things suitable to it and repel those harmful to it. For these effects the spirits began from then on to move all the muscles and sense organs in every way they could. That is why now, when the soul desires something, the whole body becomes more agile and ready to move than it usually is, and when the body happens to be so disposed for other reasons, this makes the soul's desires stronger and more ardent. Desire's genealogy is the general mobilization of a newborn organism. The final sentence reverses the arrow: a body already agitated strengthens desire, so the loop runs from body to soul as well as from soul to body. Taken with arts. 107–110, the five passions form a nutritional system: love, joy, hatred and sadness index the quality and quantity of the heart's food, desire the effort to get or avoid it. All later passions, however refined their objects, carry this archaic physiology.

Article 112, "What the external signs of these passions are" (AT XI 411). What has been said makes clear the cause of the differences in the pulse and the other properties attributed to these passions, so no more needs to be said about them. But since Descartes noted in each only what can be observed when it occurs alone, and what serves to show the movements of blood and spirits producing it, there remain several external signs that usually accompany them and are noticed much better when they are mixed, as they usually are, than when they are separate. The principal signs are the actions of the eyes and face, changes of colour, trembling, languor, fainting, laughter, tears, groans and sighs. Two kinds of evidence are separated. The inner signs of arts. 97–101, pulse, warmth and digestion, were read from pure cases to infer mechanism. The outward signs are the ones others see, and they are clearest in mixtures, so their analysis will explain each sign as the product of several passions combined. The list reads like a physician's or an actor's inventory, and the section that follows is the treatise's semiotics of the body, a counterpart to Le Brun's later drawings of the expressions of the passions, which drew on it.

Article 113, "Of the actions of the eyes and face" (AT XI 412–413). No passion is without some particular action of the eyes that reveals it, and in some this is so obvious that even the most stupid servants can tell from their master's eye whether he is angry with them. But although these actions are easily perceived and their meaning known, they are hard to describe, because each is composed of many changes in the movement and shape of the eye so small and particular that none can be perceived separately, though their combined result is easily noticed. Almost the same is true of facial actions: though larger, they are hard to distinguish, and so little different that some people make almost the same face weeping as others laughing. Some are quite notable, such as the wrinkled forehead in anger and certain movements of nose and lips in indignation and derision, but they seem less natural than voluntary. Generally all actions of face and eyes can be changed by the soul when, wishing to hide its passion, it strongly imagines a contrary one, so they can serve to dissimulate the passions as well as to reveal them. Expression is perceived holistically: we read a configuration whose components lie below the threshold of perception. Other minds are read through a whole whose parts are each imperceptible. The most recognizable expressions are partly conventional, closer to gestures than to effects. Because the face is under the will through imagination (art. 45's indirect method), it is an unreliable witness, which prepares the next article's contrast.

Article 114, "Of changes of colour" (AT XI 413). We cannot so easily keep from blushing or turning pale when some passion disposes us to it, because these changes do not depend on the nerves and muscles, as the preceding ones do, but come more immediately from the heart, which may be called the source of the passions insofar as it prepares the blood and spirits to produce them. The colour of the face comes only from the blood, which, flowing continually from the heart through the arteries into all the veins and from the veins back to the heart, colours the face more or less according as it fills the small veins near its surface. Colour is the honest sign. It escapes the will because it bypasses the nerve–muscle system the will steers. The heart is called "the source of the passions" only in a qualified sense, as preparer of blood and spirits; their seat remains the gland (art. 33). The circulation of art. 7 supplies the mechanism.

Article 115, "How joy makes one flush" (AT XI 413). Joy makes the colour livelier and redder because, opening the heart's sluices, it makes the blood flow faster in all the veins, and the blood, becoming warmer and finer, moderately swells all the parts of the face, giving it a more smiling and cheerful look. The open orifices of art. 104 produce visible effects. The "smiling air" of joy is attributed to vascular swelling, not muscular expression, keeping it in the involuntary class of art. 114.

Article 116, "How sadness makes one pale" (AT XI 414). Sadness, narrowing the heart's orifices, makes the blood flow more slowly in the veins and, becoming colder and thicker, take up less room. It retreats into the larger veins nearest the heart and leaves the farthest ones, of which the most visible are those of the face, which then looks pale and drawn, especially when the sadness is great or comes suddenly, as in terror, whose surprise increases the action that tightens the heart. Pallor is blood withdrawing toward the centre. Surprise, the mark of wonder, again amplifies another passion (art. 72): terror is sadness or fear sharpened by suddenness.

Article 117, "How one often flushes when sad" (AT XI 414–415). Often, though sad, one does not grow pale but red. This must be attributed to other passions joined with sadness, namely love or desire, and sometimes hatred. These, heating or agitating the blood from the liver, intestines and other inner parts, push it to the heart and from there through the great artery to the veins of the face, and sadness, which tightens the heart's orifices, cannot prevent this unless it is very excessive. But even moderate sadness easily prevents the blood that has reached the face from going back down to the heart while love, desire or hatred push more up from the inner parts. Held there, the blood makes the face red, even redder than in joy, both because the colour of blood shows better the slower it flows and because more can gather in the facial veins than when the heart's orifices are wider open. This appears chiefly in shame, which is composed of love of oneself and a pressing desire to avoid present infamy, which drives blood from the inner parts to the heart and through the arteries to the face, together with a moderate sadness that keeps it from returning. It also appears in weeping, since love joined with sadness causes most tears, and in anger, where a quick desire for revenge is often mixed with love, hatred and sadness. This is the analytic method at work on a mixture. Opposite signs can come from one passion combined with others, so sadness can yield pallor or flushing depending on its partners. The blush of shame is a cooperative effect: inflow driven by self-love and desire, outflow blocked by sadness. Shame is analysed here into components, anticipating its definition in art. 205, and anger is already a compound of four passions (art. 199).

Article 118, "Of trembling" (AT XI 415–416). Trembling has two causes. Sometimes too few spirits come from the brain into the nerves, and sometimes too many to close properly the small passages of the muscles which, as art. 11 said, must be closed to determine the movements of the limbs. The first cause appears in sadness and fear, as in trembling from cold, since these passions, like cold air, can thicken the blood so that it does not supply enough spirits to the brain. The other appears in those who desire something ardently, those much moved by anger, and the drunk, since these two passions, like wine, sometimes send so many spirits to the brain that they cannot be conducted from there in an orderly way into the muscles. One sign, two opposite causes, deficit and excess. Both are failures of the valve mechanism of art. 11, which needs a regulated flow. The parallels with cold and wine put passion on a continuum with purely bodily disturbances, as art. 15 did.

Article 119, "Of languor" (AT XI 416). Languor is a disposition to slacken and be motionless felt in all the limbs. Like trembling, it comes from too few spirits going into the nerves, but differently. Trembling occurs because there are not enough spirits in the brain to obey the gland's determinations when it pushes them toward some muscle; languor occurs because the gland does not determine them toward any muscle rather than others. The editors note that this is the passage Elisabeth questioned (AT IV 411, 414–415), and that Descartes appears to have reworked it in response. Languor is not a shortage of spirits but an absence of direction: the gland issues no command. The distinction between supply and steering, already implicit in art. 41, separates the two kinds of weakness.

Article 120, "How it is caused by love and by desire" (AT XI 417). The passion that most often causes languor is love joined to desire for something whose acquisition is not imagined as possible for the present. Love so occupies the soul in considering the loved object that it uses all the spirits in the brain to represent its image and stops all movements of the gland that do not serve this. And as for desire, the property attributed to it of making the body more mobile belongs to it only when one imagines that something can be done right away to obtain the desired object. If one imagines that nothing useful can be done for now, all the agitation of desire stays in the brain without passing into the nerves, and, entirely used to strengthen the idea of the desired object, it leaves the rest of the body languid. Lovesickness, the old *amor hereos*, is explained as a misallocation of spirits: love captures all of them for the image, and desire without an outlet turns inward. The correction to art. 101 is precise: desire mobilizes the body only when action is possible, so the mobility belongs to desire plus the representation of feasibility. Languor is attention without action, the passionate counterpart of the gland held still in voluntary attention (art. 43).

Article 121, "That it can also be caused by other passions" (AT XI 417–418). Hatred, sadness and even joy can also cause some languor when very violent, because they wholly occupy the soul with their object, especially when joined with desire for something one can do nothing to obtain at present. But since one dwells much more on objects one joins to oneself by will than on those one separates from oneself, or on any others, and since languor does not depend on surprise but needs some time to form, it occurs much more in love than in the other passions. The causal condition is total occupation of the soul by an object plus blocked action. Love wins because union keeps attention on its object, while hatred turns away from its object (art. 84). That languor needs time sets it against the sudden passions of wonder's family: it is a passion of duration.

Article 122, "Of fainting" (AT XI 418). Fainting is not far from death. One dies when the fire in the heart goes out altogether, and one faints when it is smothered so that some remains of heat are left that can rekindle it. Various bodily indispositions can cause fainting, but among the passions only extreme joy is observed to have this power. Descartes thinks it does so by opening the heart's orifices so extraordinarily that venous blood enters so suddenly and in such quantity that the heat cannot rarefy it fast enough to lift the small valves closing the veins' entrances, and so it smothers the fire it usually feeds when it enters in measure. The heart-fire of art. 8 now explains a passion's most extreme effect. Death and fainting differ only in degree, extinguished or smothered fire, consistently with art. 6. The paradox that only joy causes fainting is resolved mechanically: too much of the fire's fuel at once chokes it, as too many logs smother a flame.

Article 123, "Why one does not faint from sadness" (AT XI 419). It seems that great sadness arriving unexpectedly should so tighten the heart's orifices as to put out its fire too, but this is not observed, or only very rarely. The reason, Descartes thinks, is that there can hardly be so little blood in the heart that it cannot maintain the heat when the orifices are almost closed. The asymmetry is explained by the fire's needs: it tolerates scarcity better than flooding. The article is typical of the treatise's method in this section, an expected effect that does not occur, explained by the same mechanism that explained the one that does.

Article 124, "Of laughter" (AT XI 419). Laughter consists in this: the blood from the heart's right cavity, passing through the arterial vein, suddenly and repeatedly swells the lungs, forcing the air they contain out impetuously through the windpipe, where it forms an inarticulate, explosive sound. Both the lungs swelling and the air rushing out push all the muscles of the diaphragm, chest and throat, and these move the facial muscles connected with them. Only this action of the face with this inarticulate, explosive voice is called laughter. Laughter is defined purely as a bodily event, a respiratory spasm with its sound and facial correlate. It is a sign, not a passion, which is why the next articles must ask which passions produce it. Since Aristotle, laughter was the property of man; Descartes gives it a pulmonary mechanism and leaves its humanity to the passions that cause it.

Article 125, "Why it does not accompany the greatest joys" (AT XI 420). Although laughter seems one of the principal signs of joy, joy can cause it only when moderate and when some wonder or hatred is mixed with it. Experience shows that when one is extraordinarily joyful, the subject of this joy never makes one burst out laughing, and one cannot even be made to laugh by other causes as easily as when sad. The reason is that in great joys the lung is always so full of blood that it cannot be swelled further by jerks. A common belief is corrected by observation and explained mechanically. Laughter needs room in the lungs, which great joy has filled. The paradox that sad people laugh more easily than very happy ones follows directly. Laughter thus belongs to mixed and moderate states, which prepares the composite analysis of arts. 126–127.

Article 126, "What its principal causes are" (AT XI 420–421). Descartes finds only two causes that can suddenly swell the lungs. The first is the surprise of wonder, which, joined with joy, can open the heart's orifices so promptly that a great quantity of blood, entering its right side suddenly by the vena cava, is rarefied there and, passing through the arterial vein, swells the lungs. The other is the admixture of some liquid that increases the blood's rarefaction, and the only one fit for this is the most fluid part of the blood from the spleen. Driven toward the heart by some slight emotion of hatred, helped by the surprise of wonder, and mixing there with the blood joy brings in abundance, it can make this blood expand much more than usual, as many liquids on a fire suddenly swell when a little vinegar is thrown in, since the most fluid part of the spleen's blood is like vinegar. Experience also shows that in every occasion of explosive laughter there is always some small cause for hatred, or at least for wonder. People with unhealthy spleens are not only sadder but also, at intervals, gayer and more inclined to laugh, because the spleen sends two kinds of blood to the heart, one very thick and coarse that causes sadness, the other very fluid and fine that causes joy. Often after much laughing one feels naturally inclined to sadness, because once the spleen's most fluid blood is used up, the coarser part follows it to the heart. The editors refer to the exchange with Elisabeth (AT IV 405, 409–410). Laughter's recipe is joy plus surprise, or joy plus a slight hatred, the theory of laughter as mild malice and incongruity, here grounded in the spleen. The vinegar analogy imports kitchen chemistry into physiology. The splenic account explains the melancholic's alternation of gloom and hilarity and the sadness after laughter, so the old doctrine of black bile is kept for its observations and refounded on mechanism.

Article 127, "What its cause is in indignation" (AT XI 421–422). The laughter that sometimes accompanies indignation is usually artificial and feigned. When natural, it seems to come from joy at seeing that one cannot be harmed by the evil one is indignant at, and from surprise at its novelty or unexpected occurrence, so that joy, hatred and wonder contribute. But Descartes is willing to believe that it can also be produced without any joy, by the mere movement of aversion, which sends blood from the spleen to the heart, where it is rarefied and driven into the lungs, which it easily swells when it finds them nearly empty. Generally, whatever can suddenly swell the lungs in this way causes the outward action of laughter, except when sadness changes it into the groans and cries that accompany tears. In this connection Vives writes of himself that after fasting a long time, the first morsels he put in his mouth made him laugh, which could come from his lungs, emptied of blood for want of food, being quickly swelled by the first juice passing from his stomach to the heart, which the mere imagination of eating could send there even before the food had arrived. The first edition's margin cites Juan Luis Vives, *De anima*, Book III, chapter on laughter, one of Descartes' rare acknowledged sources and a reminder that the claim of total novelty in art. 1 was polemical. Laughter is ultimately defined by its bodily cause, any sudden swelling of the lungs, so it can occur without joy, in pure aversion. Vives' anecdote serves as an experiment: the imagination of eating triggers the mechanism before the food does, a case of the anticipatory associations of art. 107.

Article 128, "Of the origin of tears" (AT XI 422–423). As laughter is never caused by the greatest joys, tears do not come from extreme sadness but only from moderate sadness accompanied or followed by some feeling of love, or of joy. To understand their origin, note that although many vapours constantly leave all parts of the body, none leave so abundantly as the eyes, because of the size of the optic nerves and the many small arteries that bring the vapours there; and as sweat is only vapours from other parts turning to water on their surface, tears are vapours from the eyes. The symmetry with laughter is deliberate: both signs belong to moderate, mixed passions. Tears are reduced to a special case of sweat, concentrated at the eyes for anatomical reasons. The puzzle of why we weep from love as well as grief gets its solution in art. 131.

Article 129, "Of how vapours change into water" (AT XI 423–424). As the *Météores* explained (AT VI 239–247), the vapours of the air turn into rain when they are less agitated or more abundant than usual. Likewise, when vapours leaving the body are much less agitated than usual, even if not more abundant, they turn into water, which causes the cold sweats that sometimes come from weakness in illness. And when they are much more abundant, provided they are not also more agitated, they also turn into water, which causes the sweat of exercise. But then the eyes do not sweat, because during bodily exercise most of the spirits go into the muscles that move it, and fewer go through the optic nerve to the eyes. It is one and the same matter that makes the blood while in the veins and arteries, the spirits in the brain, nerves and muscles, the vapours when it leaves them as air, and finally sweat or tears when it thickens into water on the surface of the body or the eyes. The meteorology of the 1637 *Essais* is applied to the body, so the human body obeys the same physics as clouds. Two variables, agitation and quantity, account for two kinds of sweat. The closing sentence states the unity of bodily matter: blood, spirits, vapour and tears are one substance in different states of motion, the clearest expression in the treatise of Descartes' anti-Galenic monism of bodily fluids.

Article 130, "How what hurts the eye makes it weep" (AT XI 424–425). Descartes finds only two causes that turn the eyes' vapours into tears. The first is a change, by any accident, in the shape of the pores through which they pass. This slows the vapours' movement and disturbs their order, and can make them turn to water. So it takes only a speck of dust falling in the eye to draw some tears, because by causing pain it changes the disposition of its pores, narrowing some so that the small parts of the vapours pass more slowly, and instead of leaving at equal distances and so staying separate, they meet because the order of the pores is disturbed, join, and turn into tears. The first cause is purely mechanical, without any passion. Condensation is explained by the particles losing their spacing, a kinetic model of phase change. This prepares art. 131 by showing that the passionate route must produce the same physical result, slowed or crowded vapours.

Article 131, "How one weeps from sadness" (AT XI 425). The other cause is sadness followed by love or joy, or generally by some cause that makes the heart push much blood through the arteries. Sadness is required because, cooling all the blood, it narrows the eyes' pores. But since, as it narrows them, it also diminishes the quantity of vapours they must let through, this does not suffice to produce tears unless the quantity of vapours is at the same time increased by some other cause. Nothing increases it more than the blood sent to the heart in love. So we see that sad people do not weep continuously but only at intervals, when they reflect anew on the objects of their affection. Tears need two conditions that pull against each other: narrowed pores, from sadness, and abundant vapour, from love. That is why grief alone dries the eyes and why weeping comes in waves, each renewal of loving thought supplying the missing vapour. Grief's tears are an expression of love. The explanation turns an emotional observation, that we weep over what we love, into a hydraulic necessity.

Article 132, "Of the groans that accompany tears" (AT XI 425–426). Then the lungs too are sometimes suddenly swelled by the abundance of blood entering them, which drives out the air they contained, and this, leaving by the windpipe, produces the groans and cries that usually accompany tears. These cries are usually higher-pitched than those that accompany laughter, though produced in almost the same way, because the nerves that widen or narrow the vocal organs to make the voice deeper or higher are joined with those that open the heart's orifices in joy and narrow them in sadness, so that these organs widen or narrow at the same time. Sobbing is laughter's twin, the same pulmonary mechanism. Pitch is explained by a coupling of nerves: the larynx narrows with the heart in sadness, so grief's voice is high. The nervous linkage is a fixed institution, comparable to the joining of thought and gland in art. 44.

Article 133, "Why children and old people weep easily" (AT XI 426–427). Children and old people are more inclined to weep than the middle-aged, but for different reasons. Old people often weep from affection and joy, because these two passions together send much blood to their heart and from there much vapour to their eyes, and the coldness of their constitution so slows these vapours that they easily turn into tears, even when no sadness has preceded. If some old people also weep easily from vexation, it is not so much their bodily temperament as that of their mind that disposes them to it, and this happens only to those so weak that they let themselves be entirely overcome by small causes of pain, fear or pity. Children rarely weep from joy but much more from sadness, even without love, because they always have enough blood to produce many vapours, whose movement, slowed by sadness, turns into tears. Age changes the variables of art. 131. In the old, natural coldness does sadness's work of slowing; in children, abundant blood does love's work of supplying vapour. The distinction between bodily temperament and the "temperament of the mind" keeps a moral residue: some weeping reflects weakness of soul in the sense of art. 48, not physiology.

Article 134, "Why some children turn pale instead of weeping" (AT XI 427). Some children turn pale instead of weeping when upset. This can show unusual judgment and courage, when it comes from their considering the size of the evil and preparing a strong resistance, as older people do. But more often it is a sign of a bad nature, when it comes from their being inclined to hatred or fear, since these passions reduce the matter of tears. Conversely, those who weep very easily are inclined to love and pity. One sign, pallor, has two opposite moral meanings depending on the passion behind it, so the external sign underdetermines character. The general rule that easy tears indicate love and pity follows from art. 131's requirement of love for weeping.

Article 135, "Of sighs" (AT XI 427–428). The cause of sighs is very different from that of tears, though they too presuppose sadness. Whereas one is incited to weep when the lungs are full of blood, one is incited to sigh when they are almost empty and some imagination of hope or joy opens the orifice of the venous artery that sadness had narrowed. The little blood left in the lungs then falls suddenly into the left side of the heart through the venous artery, driven there by the desire to attain this joy, which at the same time agitates all the muscles of the diaphragm and chest, and air is quickly drawn through the mouth into the lungs to fill the space the blood leaves. This is what is called sighing. The editors refer to the exchange with Elisabeth (AT IV 405, 410–411). A sigh is an inhalation, not an exhalation: air rushes into lungs emptied of blood. Its passionate formula is sadness interrupted by a glimpse of hope or joy and the desire for it, which captures what a sigh feels like, a longing within grief.

Article 136, "Where the effects of the passions peculiar to certain people come from" (AT XI 428–429). To supply briefly whatever could be added about the various effects or causes of the passions, Descartes repeats the principle on which all he has written rests: soul and body are so linked that once we have joined some bodily action with some thought, neither presents itself to us afterward without the other, and it is not always the same actions that are joined to the same thoughts. This suffices to explain whatever peculiarities anyone may observe in himself or others that have not been explained here. For example, the strange aversions of some who cannot stand the smell of roses or the presence of a cat come only from their having been greatly offended by such objects at the beginning of their life, or from having shared the feeling of their mother who was offended by them while pregnant. There is certainly a relation between all the mother's movements and those of the child in her womb, so that what is contrary to one harms the other. The smell of roses may have given an infant a bad headache in the cradle, or a cat may have frightened it badly without anyone noticing, and without the child having any memory of it afterwards; yet the idea of the aversion it had for these roses or this cat stays imprinted in its brain to the end of its life. The editors refer back to art. 50, though the principle is stated in the same words in art. 107. The association principle is made the treatise's foundation. Its second clause, that the joinings vary between people, explains individual differences without new principles. The idiosyncratic aversion is explained by a forgotten early association: the trace outlasts the memory, so a passion can have a cause its subject cannot recall. Prenatal sympathy, the mother's impressions passing to the fetus, is a traditional belief that Descartes accepts and mechanizes. The structure of an affect fixed by a forgotten infantile experience is the one psychoanalysis would later make central, here explained wholly by brain traces.

Article 137, "Of the use of the five passions explained here, insofar as they relate to the body" (AT XI 429–430). Having defined love, hatred, desire, joy and sadness and treated all the bodily movements that cause or accompany them, only their use remains to be considered. By nature's institution, they all relate to the body and are given to the soul only insofar as it is joined to the body, so that their natural use is to incite the soul to consent and contribute to actions that can serve to preserve the body or make it in some way more perfect. In this sense sadness and joy are the first two employed. The soul is immediately warned of what harms the body only by the feeling of pain, which produces in it first sadness, then hatred of what causes the pain, and third the desire to be rid of it. Likewise the soul is immediately warned of what is useful to the body only by some kind of titillation, which excites joy, then love of what one believes to cause it, and finally the desire to acquire what can make the joy continue or bring a similar one later. This shows that all five are very useful with respect to the body, and that sadness is in a way prior to and more necessary than joy, and hatred than love, because it matters more to repel what harms and can destroy than to acquire what adds some perfection without which one can subsist. The ordering of use is the reverse of the order of enumeration. In the natural order, sadness comes first, through pain, then hatred, then desire; joy, love and desire follow the same sequence on the side of benefit. Priority of the negative passions is grounded in survival: harm threatens existence, benefit only adds perfection. Arts. 107–110 gave the genealogy, and this article gives the teleology, the institution of nature of the Sixth Meditation, which is oriented to the preservation of the composite.

Article 138, "Of their defects, and the means of correcting them" (AT XI 431). Although this use of the passions is the most natural they can have, and all animals without reason conduct their life only by bodily movements like those that usually follow them in us and to which they incite our soul to consent, it is not always good. Many things harmful to the body cause no sadness at first, or even give joy, and others useful to it are at first unpleasant. Moreover the passions almost always make the goods and evils they represent appear much greater and more important than they are, so that they incite us to pursue the one and flee the other with more ardour and care than is fitting, as we also see that beasts are often deceived by baits and, to avoid small evils, throw themselves into greater ones. So we must use experience and reason to distinguish good from evil and know their just value, so as not to take one for the other or go to excess in anything. Nature's signals are reliable in general but fallible in particular, the argument of the dropsy example in the Sixth Meditation. The second defect is systematic: passions magnify. That magnification is the cost of their function as fixatives of thought (art. 74). The remedy is evaluation by "experience and reason," which re-scales what the passions present. The beast's bait shows the passions working perfectly as mechanism and failing as guidance.

Article 139, "Of the use of the same passions insofar as they belong to the soul, and first of love" (AT XI 432). This would suffice if we had only a body, or if it were our better part, but since it is only the lesser, we must chiefly consider the passions insofar as they belong to the soul. With respect to the soul, love and hatred come from knowledge and precede joy and sadness, except when these last two take the place of knowledge, of which they are species. When this knowledge is true, that is, when the things it leads us to love are truly good and those it leads us to hate truly bad, love is incomparably better than hatred. It cannot be too great, and it never fails to produce joy. Such love is extremely good because, joining true goods to us, it perfects us accordingly. It cannot be too great because the most excessive can only join us so perfectly to these goods that the love we have particularly for ourselves makes no distinction between them and us, which Descartes believes can never be bad. And it is necessarily followed by joy, because it represents what we love as a good belonging to us. The perspective shifts from the body's good to the soul's, and the order of the passions reverses again: for the soul, knowledge comes first, then love or hatred, then joy or sadness. The parenthesis that joy and sadness can "take the place of knowledge" recognizes the bodily route of art. 137, where feeling precedes and informs judgment. Love grounded in truth admits no excess, since its maximum is a total union in which self-love no longer separates the self from its good, the whole of art. 80 completed. Joy follows necessarily because the loved good is now represented as one's own (art. 91). This is the treatise's closest approach to an ethics of love, akin to the intellectual love of God in the letter to Chanut.

Article 140, "Of hatred" (AT XI 433). Hatred, by contrast, cannot be so slight as not to harm, and it is never without sadness. It cannot be too slight because we are not incited to any action by hatred of evil that we could not be incited to still better by love of the good contrary to it, at least when the good and evil are well enough known. Descartes grants that hatred of evil manifested only by pain is necessary with respect to the body, but he speaks here only of hatred from clearer knowledge and refers it only to the soul. It is never without sadness because evil, being only a privation, cannot be conceived without some real subject in which it exists, and there is nothing real that has no goodness in it. So hatred that removes us from some evil removes us at the same time from the good joined to it, and the privation of this good, represented to the soul as a lack belonging to it, excites sadness. For example, hatred that removes us from someone's bad morals also removes us from his company, in which we might otherwise find some good whose loss we regret. So in every hatred some cause for sadness can be found. Two arguments. The first is practical substitution: love of the good does everything hatred of evil does, and better. The second is metaphysical: evil as privation inheres in a subject that has some goodness, so hatred always cuts us off from some good too. The privation doctrine, which art. 87 bracketed for the passions, is reinstated for the soul's reflective use. The example of the vicious man's company makes the point humane: hatred of a vice always costs us a person.

Article 141, "Of desire, joy and sadness" (AT XI 434). Desire, when it proceeds from true knowledge, cannot be bad, provided it is not excessive and that knowledge regulates it. Joy cannot fail to be good, nor sadness bad, with respect to the soul, because all the discomfort the soul receives from evil consists in sadness and all its enjoyment of its good consists in joy. So if we had no body, Descartes would venture to say that we could not give ourselves too much to love and joy nor avoid hatred and sadness too much. But the bodily movements accompanying them can all be harmful to health when very violent and useful when moderate. For the soul, joy and sadness are good and bad by definition (arts. 91–92). The counterfactual "if we had no body" isolates the soul's verdict, and the body re-enters only as a constraint of moderation: the passions' physiological intensity, not their mental content, sets the limit. Desire alone needs regulation by knowledge even for the soul, which prepares arts. 143–144.

Article 142, "Of joy and love compared with sadness and hatred" (AT XI 434–435). Since hatred and sadness must be rejected by the soul even when they come from true knowledge, they must be rejected all the more when they come from false opinion. But one may doubt whether love and joy are good when so ill-founded. Considered precisely in themselves, with respect to the soul, joy is less solid and love less advantageous than when better founded, but they are still preferable to equally ill-founded sadness and hatred. In the occurrences of life where we cannot avoid the risk of being deceived, we always do much better to lean toward the passions that tend to good than toward those concerned with evil, even if only to avoid it. Often even a false joy is better than a sadness whose cause is true. But Descartes dares not say the same of love compared with hatred. When hatred is just, it removes us only from the subject that contains the evil it is good to be separated from, whereas unjust love joins us to things that can harm, or at least do not deserve so much consideration, which debases and lowers us. The asymmetry between joy and love is the subtle point. False joy beats true sadness, since joy's value for the soul lies in the feeling itself. Love, however, joins the self to its object, so a false love changes what we are united to and degrades us. Hatred concerns separation, so a just hatred costs little. The ranking anticipates the Stoic objection to false comfort and answers it: joy can be preferred even when ill-founded, but love cannot.

Article 143, "Of the same passions insofar as they relate to desire" (AT XI 435–436). What has been said of these four passions holds only when they are considered precisely in themselves and do not lead us to any action. Insofar as they excite desire in us, through which they regulate our conduct, it is certain that all those with a false cause can harm and all those with a just cause can serve. Even when equally ill-founded, joy is usually more harmful than sadness, because sadness gives restraint and apprehension and so disposes somewhat to prudence, whereas joy makes those who abandon themselves to it inconsiderate and rash. A second evaluation, practical rather than intrinsic. Through desire the passions govern action, and there the verdict of art. 142 reverses: false joy is worse than false sadness because it makes us reckless. The distinction between a passion's value as a state of the soul and its value as a motive is the hinge of the treatise's ethics.

Article 144, "Of desires whose outcome depends only on us" (AT XI 436–437). Since these passions can lead us to action only through the desire they excite, it is this desire especially that we must take care to regulate, and in this consists the principal usefulness of morality. As said, desire is always good when it follows true knowledge and bad when founded on some error. The error most commonly committed about desires is not distinguishing enough between things that depend entirely on us and things that do not. For things that depend only on us, that is, on our free will, it is enough to know they are good to be unable to desire them too ardently, because doing the good things that depend on us is following virtue, and one cannot desire virtue too ardently. Moreover, what we desire in this way cannot fail to succeed, since it depends on us alone, so we always receive all the satisfaction we expected from it. The fault usually committed here is never desiring too much, only desiring too little. The sovereign remedy is to free the mind as far as possible from all other less useful desires, and then to try to know very clearly and consider attentively the goodness of what is to be desired. Morality is reduced to the regulation of desire, the point where passion meets action. The Stoic distinction from Epictetus' *Enchiridion*, what is up to us and what is not, becomes the criterion. For things up to us, desire has no upper bound, since its object is virtue and its success is guaranteed. Virtue is defined here as doing the good things that depend on us, a formal definition centred on the will that Part Three will name generosity. The third maxim of *Discours* III, to change one's desires rather than the order of the world, returns with a positive programme: concentrate desire on what is ours and see its goodness clearly.

Article 145, "Of those that depend only on other causes, and what fortune is" (AT XI 437–438). Things that do not depend on us at all, however good, should never be desired with passion, not only because they may not happen and so afflict us the more the more we wished for them, but mainly because, occupying our thought, they divert us from giving our affection to other things whose acquisition depends on us. There are two general remedies against these vain desires. The first is generosity, to be discussed later (arts. 153–154). The second is that we must often reflect on divine Providence and represent to ourselves that it is impossible for anything to happen otherwise than as Providence has determined from all eternity, so that it is like a fate or immutable necessity, which must be set against fortune to destroy it, as a chimera that comes only from an error of our understanding. We can desire only what we think in some way possible, and we can think possible things that do not depend on us only insofar as we think they depend on fortune, that is, judge that they can happen and that similar things have happened before. This opinion rests only on our not knowing all the causes that contribute to each effect. When a thing we thought dependent on fortune does not happen, this shows that some cause necessary to produce it was lacking, and so that it was absolutely impossible and that nothing similar ever happened, that is, nothing whose production lacked a similar cause; so if we had known this beforehand, we would never have thought it possible and so never desired it. The editors refer to the letters to Elisabeth (AT IV 405, 411). The cost of passionate desire for what is not ours is distraction from what is. Fortune is diagnosed as an epistemic illusion: "possible" means only "not known to be impossible." Since what does not happen lacked a necessary cause, it was impossible all along. Providence works here as a therapeutic representation, a determinism to be meditated so as to dissolve false hopes, close to the Stoic *fatum*, but with freedom explicitly excepted in the next article.

Article 146, "Of those that depend on us and on others" (AT XI 439–440). We must therefore entirely reject the vulgar opinion that there is outside us a fortune that makes things happen or not at its pleasure, and know that everything is guided by divine Providence, whose eternal decree is so infallible and immutable that, except for the things this same decree has willed to depend on our free will, we must think that nothing happens to us that is not necessary and as it were fated, so that we cannot without error desire it to happen otherwise. But since most of our desires extend to things not wholly dependent on us nor wholly on others, we must carefully distinguish in them what depends on us alone, so as to extend our desire to that alone. For the rest, though we must regard the outcome as entirely fated and immutable so that our desire does not dwell on it, we must still consider the reasons that make it more or less hopeful, so they may serve to regulate our actions. If we have business somewhere we can reach by two roads, one usually much safer than the other, it may be that Providence has decreed that if we take the road thought safer we will be robbed, while we could pass the other without danger. We must not therefore be indifferent between them or rest on the immutable fate of the decree. Reason requires that we choose the road usually safer, and our desire must be fulfilled in this when we have followed it, whatever evil happens to us, because that evil was unavoidable for us, and we had no reason to wish to be exempt from it, only to do the best our understanding could know, as Descartes supposes we did. By practising this distinction between fate and fortune, one easily gets used to regulating one's desires so that, since their fulfilment depends only on us, they can always give us entire satisfaction. Providence's decree is necessary except where it has itself made things depend on free will, so freedom is carved out within determinism by the decree itself. The road example solves the practical problem of mixed cases. Desire is redirected from outcomes to choices: we desire to choose well, which is wholly ours, while probability still guides which choice is best. The outcome is then accepted as fated. The satisfaction promised in art. 144 is secured by relocating desire onto the will's own act. This is the doctrine of the letter to Elisabeth of 4 August 1645 put in its final form.

Article 147, "Of the inner emotions of the soul" (AT XI 440–441). One more consideration greatly helps keep us from suffering any harm from the passions. Our good and evil depend chiefly on the inner emotions that are excited in the soul only by the soul itself, in which they differ from the passions, which always depend on some movement of the spirits. Although these emotions of the soul are often joined with the passions similar to them, they can also occur with others, and even arise from contrary ones. When a husband mourns his dead wife, whom, as sometimes happens, he would be sorry to see resurrected, his heart may be tightened by the sadness that the funeral display and the absence of a person whose company he was used to excite in him, and some remnants of love or pity presenting themselves to his imagination may draw real tears from his eyes, though he feels at the same time a secret joy in the innermost part of his soul, whose emotion has so much power that the sadness and tears accompanying it cannot diminish its force. And when we read of strange adventures in a book or see them represented on a stage, this excites in us sometimes sadness, sometimes joy, love or hatred, and generally all the passions according to the variety of objects presented to our imagination, but along with this we have pleasure in feeling them excited in us, and this pleasure is an intellectual joy that can arise from sadness as well as from all the other passions. The intellectual emotions of arts. 79, 91 and 92 now become the decisive layer. They are independent of the passions and can contradict them, so that the soul can be sad passionately and joyful intellectually at once. The widower's real tears and secret joy are Descartes' sharpest example of affective division, explained without parts of the soul: the division runs between passion and inner emotion, between the body-caused and the self-caused. The theatre returns from art. 94, now explained at the level of intellectual joy, the soul's pleasure in its own being moved, a second-order affect.

Article 148, "That the exercise of virtue is a sovereign remedy against the passions" (AT XI 441–442). Since these inner emotions touch us more closely and so have much more power over us than the passions they differ from that occur with them, it is certain that, provided our soul always has something to content itself with inwardly, all the disturbances coming from elsewhere have no power to harm it. Rather they serve to increase its joy, because, seeing that it cannot be harmed by them, it recognizes its perfection. For our soul to have such contentment, it need only follow virtue exactly. Whoever has lived so that his conscience cannot reproach him with ever having failed to do all the things he judged best, which is what Descartes here calls following virtue, receives from this a satisfaction so powerful in making him happy that the most violent assaults of the passions never have enough power to disturb the tranquillity of his soul. Part Two ends where the morality of the 1645 letters began. Virtue is defined formally as always doing what one has judged best, and its reward is an inner emotion, satisfaction, that outweighs any passion because it is self-caused and so closer to the soul. The passions are not suppressed: they are outweighed and even turned into occasions of joy, as the soul sees its own invulnerability, which extends the theatre's pleasure of art. 147 to life. The definition of virtue sets up generosity, the first major topic of Part Three after esteem (arts. 152–161).

## Part Three. On the Particular Passions

Article 149, "Of esteem and contempt" (AT XI 443–444). Having explained the six primitive passions, which are like the genera of which all the others are species, Descartes will note briefly what is particular in each of the others, keeping the order of the earlier enumeration. The first two are esteem and contempt. These names ordinarily signify only the opinions one has, without passion, of the value of each thing, but since passions often arise from these opinions and have no names of their own, the names may be given to them. Esteem as a passion is an inclination of the soul to represent to itself the value of the thing esteemed, caused by a particular movement of the spirits so directed in the brain that they strengthen the impressions serving this purpose. Contempt, by contrast, is an inclination of the soul to consider the baseness or smallness of what it despises, caused by the movement of the spirits that strengthens the idea of this smallness. Part Three follows the order of arts. 53–67, not the analytic order of art. 69, so the derived passions are taken one by one in the sequence of their generation. Esteem and contempt are named twice, as judgments and as passions; the passion is the spirit-reinforced inclination to dwell on a judged value. This double sense, opinion and passion under one name, governs the whole Part, where many entries turn out to be virtues, vices or opinions shadowed by a passion.

Article 150, "That these two passions are only species of wonder" (AT XI 444). These passions are only species of wonder. When we do not wonder at an object's greatness or smallness, we make neither more nor less of it than reason tells us to, and so we esteem or despise it without passion. Though esteem is often excited by love and contempt by hatred, this is not universal and comes only from our being more or less inclined to consider an object's greatness or smallness according to our affection for it. Esteem is wonder at magnitude, which is why reasoned valuation is passionless: the passion is the surprise added to the valuation. The link to love and hatred is contingent, a bias of attention. The structure explains how one can esteem what one does not love and love what one does not esteem, as with the friend of art. 83.

Article 151, "That one can esteem or despise oneself" (AT XI 444–445). These two passions can be referred to every kind of object, but they are most notable when referred to ourselves, that is, when it is our own merit that we esteem or despise. The movement of the spirits causing them is then so evident that it changes even the look, gestures, gait and generally all the actions of those who conceive a better or worse opinion of themselves than usual. Self-esteem is the passion with the most visible bodily expression, which is surprising given art. 71's claim that wonder leaves the heart untouched. The explanation is that self-esteem mixes wonder with joy and love (art. 160). Turning esteem on the self sets up the treatise's ethical centre.

Article 152, "For what cause one may esteem oneself" (AT XI 445). Since one of the principal parts of wisdom is to know in what way and for what cause each person should esteem or despise himself, Descartes gives his opinion. He observes only one thing in us that can give us just reason to esteem ourselves, namely the use of our free will and the command we have over our volitions. Only actions that depend on this free will can rightly earn us praise or blame, and it makes us in a way like God, making us masters of ourselves, provided we do not lose through cowardice the rights it gives us. Merit is located exclusively in the use of free will. The argument runs from praise and blame: only what depends on the will is imputable, so only the will's use can found self-esteem. The likeness to God repeats the Fourth Meditation, where the will is the faculty in which I bear God's image because it is formally infinite. "Provided we do not lose our rights through cowardice" makes mastery something that can be forfeited by disuse, which is why generosity must be a resolve, not just a possession.

Article 153, "What generosity consists in" (AT XI 445–446). True generosity, which makes a man esteem himself as highly as he legitimately can, consists only partly in knowing that nothing truly belongs to him but this free disposition of his volitions, and that he should be praised or blamed only for using it well or badly, and partly in feeling in himself a firm and constant resolution to use it well, that is, never to lack the will to undertake and carry out all the things he judges best. This is following virtue perfectly. Generosity has two components: a knowledge, that only free will is truly mine, and a feeling, the resolution to use it well. It is both cognitive and affective, hence both virtue and passion (art. 160). Its content is formal: no particular goods, only the will to do what one judges best, the definition of virtue from art. 148. Generosity is the reflexive form of that virtue, virtue known and felt as one's own. The *Discours*' second maxim, the morality of resolution, and the letter to Christina of 20 November 1647 converge here.

Article 154, "That it prevents one from despising others" (AT XI 446–447). Those who have this knowledge and feeling of themselves easily persuade themselves that every other person can also have them of himself, because nothing in this depends on others. So they never despise anyone, and though they often see others commit faults that show their weakness, they are more inclined to excuse than to blame them and to believe they commit them through lack of knowledge rather than lack of good will. As they do not think themselves much inferior to those who have more goods or honours, or even more intelligence, learning or beauty, or who surpass them in other perfections, so they do not think themselves much above those they surpass, because all these things seem very slight compared with the good will for which alone they esteem themselves and which they suppose to be, or at least to be able to be, in every other person. Generosity universalizes itself. Since its ground, free will, is in everyone, the generous esteem everyone potentially as they esteem themselves. Fault is attributed to ignorance rather than malice, the Socratic charity of a will that always tends to apparent good (art. 177). All natural and social advantages are levelled as insignificant. An egalitarian moral psychology is drawn from the metaphysics of free will.

Article 155, "What virtuous humility consists in" (AT XI 447). So the most generous are usually the most humble, and virtuous humility consists only in this: reflecting on the infirmity of our nature and on the faults we may have committed or are capable of committing, which are no less than those others can commit, we prefer ourselves to no one and think that others, having free will as we do, can use it as well. Humility is generosity's other face, not its opposite. The same ground, free will equally shared, plus awareness of one's own fallibility, yields both the highest self-esteem and refusal of any superiority. Christian humility is translated into a judgment about equal capacity, not abasement before others.

Article 156, "What the properties of generosity are, and how it serves as a remedy against all the disorders of the passions" (AT XI 447–448). The generous are naturally inclined to do great things, yet to undertake nothing they do not feel capable of. Since they esteem nothing greater than doing good to others and disregarding their own interest for this purpose, they are always perfectly courteous, affable and obliging toward everyone. They are also entirely masters of their passions: of desires, jealousy and envy, because they think nothing whose acquisition does not depend on them worth much wishing for; of hatred toward men, because they esteem them all; of fear, because their confidence in their virtue reassures them; and finally of anger, because, esteeming very little everything that depends on others, they never give their enemies the advantage of acknowledging that they are offended. Generosity's properties follow from its ground. Greatness of action is bounded by a realistic estimate of capacity. Benevolence is the highest thing it esteems. Mastery of the passions is derived item by item: each disordered passion depends on overvaluing what is not up to us, so generosity, which values only what is, removes their cause. This is why art. 145 named generosity the first remedy against vain desires, and why art. 161 calls it the key of the other virtues.

Article 157, "Of pride" (AT XI 448–449). All who think well of themselves for any other cause whatever do not have true generosity but only pride, which is always very vicious, the more so the more unjust the cause of their self-esteem. The most unjust of all is to be proud without any reason, that is, without thinking there is any merit in oneself for which one should be prized, but only because one takes no account of merit and, imagining that glory is nothing but usurpation, believes that those who claim most of it have most. This vice is so unreasonable and absurd that Descartes would hardly believe men fell into it if no one were ever praised unjustly; but flattery is so common everywhere that there is no man so defective that he does not often see himself esteemed for things that deserve no praise or even deserve blame, which gives the most ignorant and stupid occasion to fall into this kind of pride. Pride is self-esteem on any ground other than the use of free will. Its worst form is groundless: a theory of glory as seizure, where value is simply claimed. Its social cause is flattery, so pride is learned from others' misjudgments. The analysis is moral and sociological, not physiological.

Article 158, "That its effects are contrary to those of generosity" (AT XI 449). Whatever the cause of self-esteem, if it is other than the will one feels in oneself always to use one's free will well, from which generosity comes, it always produces a very blameworthy pride, so different from true generosity that its effects are entirely contrary. All other goods, such as intelligence, beauty, riches and honours, are usually esteemed the more the fewer people have them, and most are such that they cannot be shared among many. So the proud try to lower all other men, and being slaves of their desires, have souls constantly agitated by hatred, envy, jealousy or anger. The contrast is structural. Free will is non-rival: everyone can have it fully. Other goods are positional and scarce, so esteem based on them requires others' inferiority. Generosity therefore produces benevolence, pride produces hostility. The passions pride generates are exactly those generosity masters (art. 156), so the two are complete inverses.

Article 159, "Of vicious humility" (AT XI 450). Baseness, or vicious humility, consists mainly in feeling weak or irresolute, and in being unable, as if one lacked the full use of one's free will, to keep from doing things one knows one will regret; and also in believing one cannot subsist by oneself or do without many things whose acquisition depends on others. It is thus directly opposed to generosity, and those with the basest minds are often the most arrogant and haughty, as the most generous are the most modest and humble. The strong and generous do not change mood with prosperity or adversity, while the weak and abject are guided only by fortune, and prosperity puffs them up no less than adversity humbles them. They are often seen lowering themselves shamefully before those from whom they expect profit or fear harm, and at the same time raising themselves insolently above those from whom they hope or fear nothing. Baseness is the failure of both components of generosity: no felt resolve, no knowledge of self-sufficiency. It is dependency, since one's good lies with others, and so it coexists with arrogance, both being functions of fortune. The portrait of the courtier who cringes upward and bullies downward makes vicious humility and pride a single disposition with two faces.

Article 160, "What the movement of the spirits is in these passions" (AT XI 451–453). Pride and baseness are not only vices but passions, since their emotion shows strongly in those suddenly puffed up or cast down by some new occasion. One may doubt whether generosity and humility, which are virtues, can also be passions, since their movements show less, and virtue seems less in accord with passion than vice is. But Descartes sees no reason why the same movement of the spirits that strengthens a thought with a bad foundation cannot strengthen one with a just foundation. Since pride and generosity consist only in the good opinion one has of oneself, and differ only in that this opinion is unjust in one and just in the other, they can be referred to one passion, excited by a movement composed of those of wonder, joy and love, both the love one has for oneself and that for the thing that makes one esteem oneself. The movement that excites humility, virtuous or vicious, is composed of those of wonder, sadness and self-love mixed with hatred for the defects that make one despise oneself. The only difference in these movements is that wonder's movement has two properties: surprise makes it strong from the start, and it is even in its continuation, the spirits moving at a constant rate. The first is found much more in pride and baseness, the second in generosity and virtuous humility. Vice usually comes from ignorance, and those who know themselves least are most apt to become proud or abased too much, because everything new surprises them, and attributing it to themselves, they wonder at themselves and esteem or despise themselves according to whether what happens is to their advantage. Since one thing that makes them proud is often followed by another that humbles them, the movement of their passion is variable. In generosity, nothing is incompatible with virtuous humility and nothing else can change them, so their movements are firm, constant and always very like themselves. They do not come so much from surprise, since those who esteem themselves in this way know well enough the causes of their self-esteem. Still, these causes are so marvellous, the power to use one's free will, which makes one prize oneself, and the infirmities of the subject who has this power, which keep one from esteeming oneself too much, that every time one represents them anew they always give a new wonder. The editors note that the Latin renders Descartes' archaic "symbolize" as *convenire*: the doubt is whether virtue "agrees with" the passions as well as vice does, a question of compatibility, not of resemblance. The physiology is the same for virtue and vice; only the foundation of the opinion differs, so the passion is morally neutral and the virtue lies in the judgment it reinforces. The two properties of wonder separate them: vice has surprise and fluctuates; virtue has evenness and constancy. Ignorance of oneself makes one surprised by one's fortunes, so self-knowledge produces stability. The final sentence is remarkable: the generous never stop wondering, because free will and finitude together are inexhaustibly wonderful. This is wonder rehabilitated at the summit of ethics after art. 76's advice to outgrow it.

Article 161, "How generosity can be acquired" (AT XI 453–454). What are commonly called virtues are habits in the soul that dispose it to certain thoughts, so that they differ from these thoughts but can produce them and in turn be produced by them. These thoughts can be produced by the soul alone, but often some movement of the spirits strengthens them, and then they are both actions of virtue and passions of the soul. Though no virtue seems to owe so much to good birth as the one that makes one esteem oneself only at one's just value, and though it is easy to believe that not all the souls God puts in our bodies are equally noble and strong (which is why Descartes called this virtue generosity, following the usage of French, rather than magnanimity, following the usage of the Schools, where it is little known), it is certain that good upbringing does much to correct the defects of birth. If one often considers what free will is and how great are the advantages of a firm resolution to use it well, and on the other hand how vain and useless are all the cares that trouble the ambitious, one can excite the passion in oneself and then acquire the virtue of generosity, which, being like the key to all the other virtues and a general remedy against all the disorders of the passions, deserves to be noted. Virtue is a habit, thought is its act, and passion can strengthen that act. The passion can therefore be used to build the virtue: by repeated meditation, one excites the passion of generosity, and repetition forms the habit. This is art. 50's training of the passions applied to the highest virtue. The name is explained: *généreux* carries the sense of nobility of birth, which Descartes acknowledges only to override it by education. "Key to all the other virtues" places generosity where the Stoics placed wisdom and the scholastics prudence.

Article 162, "Of veneration" (AT XI 454–455). Veneration or respect is an inclination of the soul not only to esteem the object it reveres, but also to submit to it with some apprehension, to try to make it favourable. So we have veneration only for free causes that we judge capable of doing us good or harm without knowing which they will do. For those from whom we expect only good we have love and devotion rather than simple veneration, and for those from whom we expect only harm we have hatred; and if we do not judge the cause of this good or harm free, we do not submit to it to make it favourable. When the pagans venerated woods, springs or mountains, it was not properly these dead things they revered but the divinities they thought presided over them. The movement of the spirits exciting this passion is composed of that which excites wonder and that which excites apprehension. Veneration requires uncertainty about a free agent's disposition: it is esteem plus apprehension, aimed at appeasement. The analysis explains religion as a passion: the pagan venerated not the spring but the imagined free cause behind it, so animism is a projection of agency. Veneration differs from devotion (art. 83), which presumes a good disposition and so involves love, not apprehension.

Article 163, "Of disdain" (AT XI 455). Disdain is the inclination of the soul to despise a free cause, judging that although by nature it can do good or harm, it is so far beneath us that it can do us neither. The movement exciting it is composed of those exciting wonder and confidence or boldness. Disdain is the mirror of veneration: the free cause is judged powerless over us. Confidence replaces apprehension in its composition, so disdain is self-assurance before an agent.

Article 164, "Of the use of these two passions" (AT XI 455–456). Generosity and weakness of mind or baseness determine the good and bad use of these passions. The nobler and more generous one's soul, the more one is inclined to give each his due. So one has not only a very profound humility toward God but also gives without reluctance all the honour and respect due to men, each according to his rank and authority in the world, and despises nothing but vices. Those with base and weak minds are prone to err by excess, sometimes revering and fearing things that deserve only contempt, sometimes insolently disdaining those that most deserve reverence. They often pass very quickly from extreme impiety to superstition, then from superstition to impiety, so that there is no vice or disorder of mind they are not capable of. Generosity regulates veneration and disdain, as it regulates the other passions, by fixing true values. The generous honour social rank, which keeps the egalitarianism of art. 154 compatible with civil order: inner esteem is equal, outward respect follows rank. The oscillation between impiety and superstition is pride and baseness again (art. 159) in religious form, and it implicitly diagnoses the religious disorders of the century.

Article 165, "Of hope and apprehension" (AT XI 456). Hope is a disposition of the soul to persuade itself that what it desires will happen, caused by a particular movement of the spirits, namely that of joy and desire mixed. Apprehension is another disposition of the soul, which persuades it that it will not happen. Though contrary, these two passions can be had together, when one represents at the same time various reasons, some making one judge the fulfilment of the desire easy, others making it seem difficult. Hope and apprehension are dispositions to believe, passions that act on judgment about the future. Their composition, joy plus desire, makes hope an anticipated joy. They can coexist because their grounds are reasons, which can be mixed, so the soul can hope and fear at once without division.

Article 166, "Of confidence and despair" (AT XI 457). Neither of these passions ever accompanies desire without leaving some room for the other. When hope is so strong that it drives out apprehension entirely, it changes its nature and is called confidence or assurance. When one is sure that what one desires will happen, though one continues to will it, one ceases to be agitated by the passion of desire, which made one seek the outcome anxiously. Likewise, when apprehension is so extreme that it removes all room for hope, it turns into despair, and despair, representing the thing as impossible, entirely extinguishes desire, which reaches only to possible things. The extremes of art. 58 are explained. Desire needs uncertainty: certainty of success turns it into calm volition, certainty of failure kills it. This is why art. 145 could cure vain desires by showing their objects impossible. Desire is thus inseparable from hope and apprehension, which always share the field.

Article 167, "Of jealousy" (AT XI 457). Jealousy is a species of apprehension related to the desire to keep possession of some good, and it comes not so much from the force of reasons that make one judge one may lose it as from the great esteem one has for it, which makes one examine even the slightest grounds for suspicion and take them for very considerable reasons. Jealousy is apprehension amplified by esteem, not by evidence. Overvaluation makes attention hypersensitive, so jealousy is a distortion of judgment by a passion of value, a precise case of art. 138's magnification.

Article 168, "In what way this passion can be honourable" (AT XI 458). Since one should take more care to preserve very great goods than lesser ones, this passion can be just and honourable on some occasions. A captain guarding a very important stronghold has the right to be jealous of it, that is, distrustful of every means by which it could be surprised, and an honest woman is not blamed for being jealous of her honour, that is, for guarding not only against doing wrong but also against the slightest grounds for slander. Jealousy is justified when the good's value warrants the vigilance. The two examples move from war to reputation, both goods that can be lost through small breaches. The passion is good when esteem is just.

Article 169, "In what way it is blameworthy" (AT XI 458–459). But we laugh at a miser jealous of his treasure, brooding over it with his eyes and never willing to leave it for fear it will be stolen, because money is not worth guarding with such care. And we despise a man jealous of his wife, because it shows he does not love her in the right way and has a bad opinion of himself or of her. He does not love her in the right way, for if he had true love for her he would have no inclination to distrust her. It is not really her he loves, but only the good he imagines consists in having sole possession of her, and he would not fear losing this good if he did not judge himself unworthy of it or his wife unfaithful. This passion relates only to suspicions and distrust, since it is not properly jealousy to try to avoid some evil when one has just reason to fear it. Jealousy of a spouse is diagnosed through the theory of love: the jealous husband loves possession, not the person, the first four lovers of art. 82, not the father. True love joins one's will to the beloved's good and so excludes distrust. Its root is low self-esteem or low esteem of the other, so jealousy reveals a failure of generosity. The final clause restricts jealousy to unwarranted suspicion: grounded vigilance is prudence, not jealousy.

Article 170, "Of irresolution" (AT XI 459–460). Irresolution is also a species of apprehension which, holding the soul as if balanced among several actions it can perform, makes it perform none, and so gives it time to choose before deciding. In this it truly has a good use. But when it lasts longer than it should and makes one spend in deliberating the time needed for acting, it is very bad. It is a species of apprehension even though one may remain uncertain when choosing among several things of apparently equal goodness without any apprehension. That kind of irresolution comes only from the subject presented and not from any emotion of the spirits, so it is not a passion, unless the apprehension of choosing wrongly increases the uncertainty. This apprehension is so common and strong in some that even when they have no choice and see only one thing to take or leave, it holds them back and makes them look uselessly for others. Then it is an excess of irresolution that comes from too great a desire to do well and from a weakness of understanding, which, having no clear and distinct notions, has many confused ones. The remedy is to get used to forming certain and determinate judgments about everything that presents itself, and to believe that one always does one's duty when one does what one judges best, even if perhaps one judges very badly. Irresolution is split into two: a cognitive indifference among equal goods, which is not a passion, and the apprehension of error, which is. The second, in excess, comes from perfectionism joined with confused understanding. The cure is the *Discours*' second maxim generalized: form determinate judgments and count one's duty done when one has acted on one's best judgment. Fallibility is accepted; paralysis is not. The scholastic indifference of equilibrium, the liberty of indifference of the Fourth Meditation, is here distinguished from the passion that mimics it.

Article 171, "Of courage and boldness" (AT XI 460). Courage, when it is a passion and not a habit or natural inclination, is a certain heat or agitation that disposes the soul to apply itself vigorously to carrying out the things it wants to do, of whatever nature they may be. Boldness is a species of courage that disposes the soul to carry out the most dangerous things. The distinction between courage as passion and as habit repeats art. 161's scheme. Courage is defined physiologically, heat, and by function, execution, which makes it the passion of the will's effective power. Boldness specifies it by object, danger.

Article 172, "Of emulation" (AT XI 460–461). Emulation is also a species of courage, but in another sense. Courage can be considered a genus divided into as many species as there are different objects, and as many others as there are causes. In the first way boldness is a species of it, in the other emulation. Emulation is nothing but a heat that disposes the soul to undertake things it hopes it can succeed in because it sees them succeed for others, and so it is a species of courage whose external cause is example. There must also always be an internal cause, consisting in the body's being so disposed that desire and hope have more force in sending blood to the heart than apprehension or despair have in preventing it. Classification is two-dimensional: by object (boldness) and by cause (emulation). Example works as a reason for hope. The internal bodily cause makes emulation dependent on temperament, so the same example inspires some and discourages others (art. 39).

Article 173, "How boldness depends on hope" (AT XI 461–462). Although the object of boldness is difficulty, which usually brings apprehension or even despair, so that the most dangerous and desperate affairs call for most boldness and courage, one still needs to hope, or even be sure, that the end one proposes will succeed, in order to oppose the difficulties vigorously. But this end differs from that object, since one cannot be sure and despairing of the same thing at the same time. When the Decii threw themselves into the enemy and ran to certain death, the object of their boldness was the difficulty of preserving their life during this action, for which they had only despair, since they were certain to die; but their end was to inspire their soldiers by their example and win the victory, for which they had hope, or else their end was to have glory after their death, of which they were sure. The distinction between object and end resolves an apparent contradiction: boldness faces despair about its object and hope about its end. The Decii's self-sacrifice, the Roman *devotio*, is analysed by this distinction. It also shows how one can rationally die for something, as devotion in art. 83 already implied.

Article 174, "Of cowardice and fear" (AT XI 462). Cowardice is directly opposed to courage; it is a languor or coldness that prevents the soul from applying itself to carrying out the things it would do if free of this passion. Fear or terror, contrary to boldness, is not only a coldness but also a disturbance and astonishment of the soul that removes its power to resist the evils it thinks near. Cowardice is courage's privation, a cold languor. Fear adds astonishment, the excess of wonder (art. 73), so fear is paralysis by surprise. The physiology of art. 36's fright is now given a classificatory place.

Article 175, "Of the use of cowardice" (AT XI 462–463). Although Descartes cannot persuade himself that nature has given men any passion that is always vicious and has no good and praiseworthy use, he finds it hard to guess what these two can serve. Cowardice seems to have some use when it exempts us from efforts we might be incited to make by probable reasons, if other more certain reasons, which judged them useless, had not excited this passion. Besides exempting the soul from these efforts, it also serves the body by slowing the movement of the spirits and so preventing the dissipation of its forces. But ordinarily it is very harmful, because it turns the will away from useful actions. Since it comes only from not having enough hope or desire, one need only increase these two passions in oneself to correct it. The principle that nature gives no passion without a use (art. 211) is tested on the hardest case. Cowardice receives a narrow use, economy of effort when effort is futile. Its remedy is the substitution of art. 45: increase hope and desire. The candour of "I find it hard to guess" shows the teleology is a working hypothesis, not a dogma.

Article 176, "Of the use of fear" (AT XI 463). As for fear or terror, Descartes does not see that it can ever be praiseworthy or useful. Nor is it a particular passion; it is only an excess of cowardice, astonishment and apprehension, which is always vicious, as boldness is an excess of courage that is always good, provided the end one proposes is good. Since the principal cause of fear is surprise, there is nothing better for exempting oneself from it than to use premeditation and prepare for all events the apprehension of which can cause it. Fear escapes the teleology by being reclassified as an excess, not a passion in its own right, which preserves art. 175's principle at the cost of a definitional move. The asymmetry with boldness is ethical: excess of courage is good if the end is good. Premeditation is the Stoic *praemeditatio malorum*, given a physiological rationale: it disarms surprise, the cause of fear's violence (art. 72), and it reappears in the general remedy of art. 211.

Article 177, "Of remorse" (AT XI 464). Remorse of conscience is a species of sadness that comes from doubting whether something one is doing or has done is good. It necessarily presupposes doubt. If one were entirely sure that what one is doing is bad, one would abstain, since the will moves only toward things that have some appearance of goodness; and if one were sure that what one has done is bad, one would feel repentance, not merely remorse. The use of this passion is to make one examine whether the doubtful thing is good, and to keep one from doing it again while not sure it is good. But since it presupposes evil, it would be best never to have occasion to feel it, and it can be prevented by the same means as irresolution. Remorse is the sadness of doubt about one's own act. Its definition rests on a Socratic principle: the will moves only toward apparent good, so certain evil cannot be willed. Its use is investigative, and its cure is the decisiveness of art. 170. Remorse completes the scheme of art. 60, which linked it to action taken before irresolution was removed.

Article 178, "Of derision" (AT XI 464–465). Derision or mockery is a species of joy mixed with hatred, which comes from perceiving some small evil in a person one thinks deserves it. One hates the evil and is glad to see it in someone who deserves it. When this comes unexpectedly, the surprise of wonder makes one burst out laughing, as said above of laughter (arts. 124–127). The evil must be small, for if it is great, one cannot believe its bearer deserves it unless one has a very bad nature or bears him much hatred. Art. 62's derision is now composed: joy, hatred and, for laughter, wonder. The smallness condition is the Aristotelian definition of the comic, a fault that causes no great pain (*Poetics* 5). Laughter at great misfortune reveals malice, so the limit is moral as well as psychological.

Article 179, "Why the most imperfect are usually the most given to mockery" (AT XI 465). Those with very visible defects, such as the lame, one-eyed or hunchbacked, or who have received some public affront, are particularly inclined to mockery. Desiring to see everyone else as disgraced as they are, they are glad of the evils that befall others and judge them deserving. Mockery serves resentment: the defective want company in disgrace, so their judgment of desert is bent by desire. The observation repeats art. 158's mechanism in a small key: self-esteem resting on positional goods seeks others' lowering.

Article 180, "Of the use of raillery" (AT XI 465–466). Modest raillery, which usefully reproves vices by making them appear ridiculous, without laughing oneself or showing any hatred for the persons, is not a passion but a quality of the honest man, which shows the gaiety of his temper and the tranquillity of his soul, marks of virtue, and often the skill of his mind in giving an agreeable appearance to the things he mocks. Raillery is distinguished from derision as a social virtue from a passion, the *honnête homme*'s urbanity. It targets vices, not persons, as generosity hates vices but pities those who have them (art. 187). The absence of laughter in the railer marks self-command.

Article 181, "Of the use of laughter in raillery" (AT XI 466). It is not unseemly to laugh when one hears another's raillery; indeed it may be such that not to laugh would be sullen. But when one rallies oneself, it is more fitting to abstain, so as not to seem surprised by what one says or to wonder at one's skill in inventing it. And this makes it surprise the hearers all the more. Laughter signals surprise (art. 126), so laughing at one's own jokes betrays self-admiration, a small vanity. The etiquette of wit is derived from the physiology of laughter, and the rhetorical point that the straight face increases the hearer's surprise follows the same mechanism.

Article 182, "Of envy" (AT XI 466–467). What is commonly called envy is a vice consisting in a perversity of nature that makes some people vexed at the good they see coming to others. But Descartes uses the word here for a passion that is not always vicious. Envy as a passion is a species of sadness mixed with hatred, which comes from seeing good come to those one thinks unworthy of it. This can be thought with reason only of goods of fortune. For goods of the soul, or even of the body insofar as one has them from birth, it is enough to be worthy of them that one received them from God before one was capable of doing any wrong. The vice and the passion are separated, as with esteem (art. 149). Passion-envy is a judgment of injustice in distribution, so it can be rational. Natural endowments are exempted: receiving them from God before any act makes one worthy of them, so envy of talent or beauty is always unjust. Only fortune's goods can be undeserved.

Article 183, "How it can be just or unjust" (AT XI 467–468). When fortune sends someone goods of which he is truly unworthy, and envy is excited in us only because, naturally loving justice, we are vexed that it is not observed in their distribution, it is a zeal that can be excusable, especially when the good envied is such that it can turn to evil in their hands, like some office in whose exercise they may behave badly. Even when one desires the same good for oneself and is prevented from having it because others less worthy possess it, this makes the passion more violent, and it is still excusable provided the hatred it contains is directed only at the bad distribution of the good, not at the persons who possess or distribute it. But few are so just and generous as to have no hatred for those who get ahead of them in acquiring a good that cannot be shared among many and that they desired for themselves, even when those who acquired it are as worthy or more. What is usually most envied is glory, since though others' glory does not prevent us from aspiring to it, it makes access harder and raises its price. Just envy is a love of justice offended, aimed at the distribution, not the person. The test is where the hatred lands. Self-interest intensifies it without making it unjust, provided the target stays structural. The realistic admission that few meet this standard comes with the explanation: non-shareable goods, positional by nature (art. 158), make rivals hateful. Glory is the paradigm because it is inflationary: others' glory raises the price of one's own.

Article 184, "Why the envious tend to have a leaden complexion" (AT XI 468–469). No vice harms human happiness so much as envy. Besides afflicting themselves, the envious disturb others' pleasure with all their power. They usually have a leaden complexion, pale mixed with yellow and black, like bruised blood, which is why envy is called *livor* in Latin. This agrees well with what was said of the blood's movements in sadness and hatred. Hatred makes yellow bile from the lower liver and black bile from the spleen spread from the heart through the arteries into all the veins, and sadness makes venous blood less warm and slower than usual, which suffices to make the colour livid. But since yellow and black bile can also be sent into the veins by many other causes, and envy does not push them there in enough quantity to change the complexion unless it is very great and long-lasting, one should not think that everyone with this colour is inclined to envy. The etymology *livor* is confirmed by physiology: hatred's biles plus sadness's sluggish blood produce lividness. The caution at the end is a lesson in inference: the sign has many causes, so one cannot read character from colour. Physiognomy is allowed only as probable evidence.

Article 185, "Of pity" (AT XI 469). Pity is a species of sadness mixed with love or good will toward those we see suffering some evil of which we think them unworthy. It is thus contrary to envy by its object and to mockery by the way it considers it. Pity's place in the system is fixed by two oppositions. Against envy: undeserved evil versus undeserved good. Against mockery: unworthy versus worthy sufferer, and love versus hatred. The combinatorial structure of Part Two's enumeration pays off in such tight definitions.

Article 186, "Who are most given to pity" (AT XI 469). Those who feel very weak and subject to the adversities of fortune seem more inclined to this passion than others, because they represent others' evil to themselves as able to happen to them, and so they are moved to pity more by the love they bear themselves than by that they have for others. Common pity is self-love projected, Aristotle's pity for what could befall oneself (*Rhetoric* II.8). It reveals weakness rather than benevolence and prepares the contrast with the generous.

Article 187, "How the most generous are affected by this passion" (AT XI 469–470). Yet the most generous and strongest minds, who fear no evil for themselves and hold themselves beyond the power of fortune, are not free of compassion when they see the infirmity of other men and hear their complaints, because it is part of generosity to have good will for everyone. But the sadness of this pity is not bitter. Like that caused by tragic actions represented on a stage, it is more in the exterior and in the senses than in the interior of the soul, which meanwhile has the satisfaction of thinking it does its duty in sympathizing with the afflicted. There is this difference: while the common people pity those who complain because they think their evils very grievous, the principal object of the pity of the greatest men is the weakness of those they see complaining, because they think no accident can be so great an evil as the cowardice of those who cannot bear it with constancy. Though they hate vices, they do not therefore hate those subject to them; they only pity them. The generous feel pity, contrary to the Stoic sage, who has none, but its sadness stays at the surface while inner satisfaction prevails, the structure of art. 147's inner emotions. Its object is moral: they pity the complainer's weakness, not his misfortune, which is a striking and somewhat chilling revaluation of compassion. The final sentence gives generosity's maxim: hate the vice, pity the person.

Article 188, "Who are not affected by it" (AT XI 470–471). Only malicious and envious minds, who naturally hate all men, or those so brutal and so blinded by good fortune or made desperate by bad that they think no evil can happen to them any more, are insensible to pity. Insensibility to pity has three sources: general hatred, brutality, and an imagined invulnerability from extreme fortune either way. The last two are false versions of the generous man's security, since they rest on fortune rather than on the will. That the desperate feel no pity follows from art. 186: pity needs the thought that evil could still befall one.

Article 189, "Why this passion makes one weep" (AT XI 471). One weeps very easily in pity because love, sending much blood to the heart, makes many vapours leave through the eyes, and the coldness of sadness, slowing these vapours, makes them turn into tears, as said above (art. 131). The formula of tears, sadness plus love, coincides exactly with the definition of pity, so pity is the weeping passion by definition. Part Three confirms Part Two's physiology through compound passions.

Article 190, "Of self-satisfaction" (AT XI 471–472). The satisfaction always had by those who constantly follow virtue is a habit in their soul called tranquillity and repose of conscience. But the satisfaction newly acquired when one has just done some action one thinks good is a passion, a species of joy, which Descartes believes the sweetest of all, because its cause depends only on ourselves. When this cause is not just, that is, when the actions one takes great satisfaction in are of little importance or even vicious, it is ridiculous and serves only to produce pride and impertinent arrogance. This can be noticed particularly in those who, believing themselves devout, are only bigoted and superstitious: because they often go to church, recite many prayers, wear their hair short, fast and give alms, they think themselves entirely perfect and imagine they are such great friends of God that they could do nothing to displease him, and that whatever their passion dictates is a good zeal, though it sometimes dictates the greatest crimes men can commit, such as betraying cities, killing princes and exterminating whole peoples only because they do not follow their opinions. The habit, tranquillity of conscience, and the passion, fresh satisfaction, are again separated. The sweetness of the passion comes from its cause being wholly ours (art. 63). Its corruption is self-satisfaction in trivial or vicious acts, and the example is pointedly political: the zealot's satisfaction in outward observance licenses treason, regicide and massacre. In 1649, after the wars of religion, the assassination of Henri IV and the Thirty Years' War, the reference is unmistakable. Generosity, which values only the will to do what one judges best, is the antidote to this false devotion.

Article 191, "Of repentance" (AT XI 472–473). Repentance is directly contrary to self-satisfaction; it is a species of sadness that comes from believing one has done some bad action, and it is very bitter because its cause comes only from ourselves. This does not prevent it from being very useful when the action we repent of is truly bad and we know it certainly, because it incites us to do better another time. But weak minds often repent of things they have done without knowing for sure that they are bad; they only persuade themselves so because they fear it, and if they had done the contrary they would repent in the same way, which is an imperfection in them deserving pity. The remedies against this defect are the same that remove irresolution. Repentance is useful only under certainty; otherwise it is a free-floating apprehension that would attach to any choice. The remedy again is decisiveness and the conviction of having done one's best (art. 170), so the letter to Elisabeth's promise of a life without repentance (art. 49) is secured by the same means.

Article 192, "Of favour" (AT XI 473). Favour is properly a desire to see good come to someone for whom one has good will, but Descartes uses the word here for this good will insofar as it is excited in us by some good action of the person we have it for. We are naturally inclined to love those who do things we think good, even when no good comes to us from it. Favour in this sense is a species of love, not of desire, though the desire to see good come to the person one favours always accompanies it. It is usually joined to pity, because the misfortunes we see befalling the unfortunate make us reflect more on their merits. Favour is classified as love, not desire, by stipulation. The point is that we naturally love beneficence as such, even disinterestedly, a moral sense grounded in the passions. Its link with pity is psychological: misfortune draws attention to merit.

Article 193, "Of gratitude" (AT XI 473–474). Gratitude is also a species of love, excited in us by some action of the person we have it for, by which we believe he has done us some good, or at least intended to. So it contains everything in favour, and in addition is founded on an action that touches us and for which we desire to make a return. That is why it is much stronger, especially in souls that are at all noble and generous. Gratitude is favour plus self-reference plus the desire to reciprocate. Intention suffices for its object, so gratitude responds to will, not outcome, consistent with the doctrine that only the will is imputable (art. 152). Its strength in the generous anticipates art. 194's account of ingratitude.

Article 194, "Of ingratitude" (AT XI 474). Ingratitude is not a passion, since nature has put no movement of the spirits in us that excites it, but only a vice directly opposed to gratitude, inasmuch as gratitude is always virtuous and one of the principal bonds of human society. This vice belongs only to brutal and foolishly arrogant men who think everything is owed them, or to the stupid who never reflect on the benefits they receive, or to the weak and abject who, feeling their infirmity and need, basely seek others' help and, after receiving it, hate them, because, lacking the will to return the favour or despairing of being able to, and imagining that everyone is as mercenary as they are and that no one does good except in hope of reward, they think they have deceived them. Not every name in the moral vocabulary names a passion; ingratitude has no physiology, so it is a vice of omission or distortion. Gratitude is named a bond of society, so the passions have a civic function. The three types are pride, stupidity and baseness, and the last receives a sharp psychology: the abject benefactee hates his benefactor because he projects his own mercenary motives, a projection that makes him feel he has cheated, and that turns debt into hatred.

Article 195, "Of indignation" (AT XI 475). Indignation is a species of hatred or aversion we naturally have against those who do some evil, of whatever kind. It is often mixed with envy or pity, but its object is entirely different, since one is indignant only against those who do good or evil to persons who do not deserve it, whereas one envies those who receive this good and pities those who receive this evil. It is in a way doing evil to possess a good one does not deserve, which may be why Aristotle and his followers, supposing envy always a vice, called by the name of indignation the envy that is not vicious. Indignation targets the agent of misdistribution, envy and pity the recipients, so the three passions divide one scene by role. The reference is to *nemesis* in *Nicomachean Ethics* II.7 and *Rhetoric* II.9. Descartes reconciles his "just envy" (art. 183) with Aristotle's terminology: what Aristotle called righteous indignation at undeserved fortune Descartes calls excusable envy.

Article 196, "Why it is sometimes joined to pity and sometimes to mockery" (AT XI 475–476). Doing evil is also in a way receiving it, so some join pity to their indignation and others mockery, according to whether they bear good or bad will toward those they see committing faults. Thus the laughter of Democritus and the tears of Heraclitus could have come from the same cause. The wrongdoer is himself harmed by his wrongdoing, the Platonic thesis of the *Gorgias*, so he can be pitied or mocked. The old topos of the laughing and weeping philosophers is reduced to a difference of good or ill will toward the same object, a neat application of the combinatorics.

Article 197, "That it is often accompanied by wonder and is not incompatible with joy" (AT XI 476). Indignation is often accompanied by wonder, because we usually suppose that everything will be done in the way we judge it should be, that is, in the way we think good, so when it happens otherwise it surprises us and we wonder at it. Nor is it incompatible with joy, though more usually joined to sadness. When the evil we are indignant at cannot harm us, and we consider that we would not want to do the like, this gives us some pleasure, and this may be one cause of the laughter that sometimes accompanies this passion. Indignation includes surprise because it presupposes a normative expectation, the "ought" of art. 53's definition of wonder. The joy of feeling oneself superior to the wrongdoer explains the laughter of art. 127, and also hints at indignation's affinity with vanity, developed in art. 198.

Article 198, "Of its use" (AT XI 476–477). Indignation is found much more in those who want to appear virtuous than in those who truly are. Though those who love virtue cannot see others' vices without some aversion, they become passionate only against the greatest and most extraordinary. It is being difficult and peevish to feel much indignation for things of little importance; it is being unjust to feel it for things not blameworthy; and it is impertinent and absurd not to restrict this passion to human actions but to extend it to the works of God or Nature, as do those who, never content with their condition or fortune, dare to find fault with the government of the world and the secrets of Providence. Indignation is often a display of virtue rather than virtue. Its proper scope is narrowed three ways: to grave faults, to actual faults, and to human agents. Indignation at Providence is the vice of the discontented, the cosmic version of envy, and it is excluded by the doctrine of arts. 145–146.

Article 199, "Of anger" (AT XI 477–478). Anger is also a species of hatred or aversion we have against those who have done some evil or tried to harm, not indifferently anyone, but particularly us. So it contains everything in indignation, and in addition is founded on an action that touches us and for which we desire revenge, since this desire almost always accompanies it. It is directly opposed to gratitude, as indignation is to favour. But it is incomparably more violent than these three other passions, because the desire to repel harmful things and avenge oneself is the most pressing of all. Desire joined to self-love supplies anger with all the agitation of blood that courage and boldness can cause, and hatred makes it mainly bilious blood from the spleen and the small veins of the liver that receives this agitation and enters the heart, where, because of its abundance and the nature of the bile mixed with it, it excites a harsher and more burning heat than love or joy can. Anger completes a square of four passions: favour and indignation (spectator), gratitude and anger (party concerned), positive and negative. Its violence comes from the most urgent desire, repelling harm, which art. 137 ranked first in natural importance. The physiology combines courage's agitation with hatred's bile, which explains its distinctive burning heat.

Article 200, "Why those whom it makes flush are less to be feared than those whom it makes pale" (AT XI 478–479). The external signs of anger differ according to temperaments and the other passions composing it or joining it. Some turn pale or tremble when angry, others flush or even weep. The anger of those who turn pale is usually judged more to be feared than that of those who flush. When one will not or cannot take revenge otherwise than by looks and words, one spends all one's heat and force at the first moment of being moved, which makes one turn red; and sometimes the regret and self-pity at being unable to take revenge otherwise make one weep. Those who hold back and resolve on a greater revenge become sad at thinking themselves obliged to it by the action that angers them, and sometimes also apprehensive of the evils that may follow from their resolution, which makes them at first pale, cold and trembling. But when they come to carry out their revenge, they grow warmer the colder they were at first, as fevers that begin with a chill tend to be the strongest. The proverbial wisdom about pale anger is explained by composition: flushing marks discharge, pallor marks concentration with sadness and apprehension. Reading signs requires knowing the compound (art. 112). The fever analogy assimilates anger to a disease, in keeping with art. 203's warning.

Article 201, "That there are two kinds of anger, and that those with the most goodness are most subject to the first" (AT XI 479–480). Two species of anger can be distinguished: one very quick and outwardly manifest but with little effect and easily appeased, the other less apparent at first but gnawing the heart more and with more dangerous effects. Those with much goodness and love are most subject to the first. It comes not from deep hatred but from a quick aversion that surprises them: being inclined to imagine that everything should go in the way they judge best, as soon as something happens otherwise they wonder at it and are offended, often even when the matter does not touch them personally, because, having much affection, they take the part of those they love as of themselves. So what would be only a cause of indignation for another is for them a cause of anger. And since their inclination to love gives them much heat and blood in the heart, the aversion that surprises them cannot push so little bile there that it does not at first cause a great commotion in this blood. But this commotion does not last, because the force of surprise does not continue, and as soon as they see that the thing that angered them should not have moved them so much, they repent. Quick anger belongs to loving natures: their love extends the self to others (art. 80), turning indignation into anger, and their warm blood amplifies a small aversion. Its brevity comes from its source in surprise, which fades (art. 72). The portrait is sympathetic: the good-natured hothead, quick to flare and quick to repent.

Article 202, "That it is weak and base souls who let themselves be most carried away by the other" (AT XI 480–481). The other species of anger, in which hatred and sadness predominate, is not so apparent at first, except perhaps in making the face pale. But its force is increased little by little by the agitation that an ardent desire for revenge excites in the blood, which, mixed with the bile driven to the heart from the lower liver and the spleen, excites a very harsh and piercing heat there. And as it is the most generous souls that have the most gratitude, so it is those with most pride, and that are basest and weakest, that let themselves be most carried away by this species of anger. Injuries appear the greater the more pride makes one esteem oneself, and the more one esteems the goods they take away, which one esteems the more the weaker and baser one's soul, because they depend on others. Slow anger belongs to the proud and base. The explanation is the generosity theory: the size of an injury depends on self-esteem and on the value placed on goods that others can take. Those who value only what is up to them can scarcely be injured. Gratitude and slow anger are the virtuous and vicious poles of reciprocity.

Article 203, "That generosity serves as a remedy against its excesses" (AT XI 481). Though this passion is useful in giving us vigour to repel injuries, there is none whose excesses one should avoid more carefully, because by troubling judgment they often make one commit faults one later repents of, and sometimes even prevent one from repelling injuries as well as one could with less emotion. But as nothing makes anger more excessive than pride, so generosity is the best remedy for its excesses. Making one esteem very little all the goods that can be taken away, and on the contrary esteem greatly freedom and absolute command over oneself, which one ceases to have when one can be offended by anyone, it makes one have only contempt, or at most indignation, for the injuries others usually take offence at. Anger has a use, repelling injury, but excess defeats even that use. The remedy is generosity, which reclassifies injuries: what touches only goods of fortune deserves contempt or indignation, not anger. The striking claim is that to be offended is to lose one's freedom, since it gives others power over one's inner state. Generosity makes the self injury-proof by locating its good beyond others' reach.

Article 204, "Of glory" (AT XI 482). What Descartes here calls glory is a species of joy founded on self-love, which comes from the opinion or hope one has of being praised by some others. It thus differs from the inner satisfaction that comes from the opinion of having done some good action, since one is sometimes praised for things one does not think good and blamed for those one thinks best. But both are species of self-esteem as well as species of joy, since seeing oneself esteemed by others is a reason to esteem oneself. Glory is joy plus self-love plus the opinion of others; satisfaction is joy plus self-love plus one's own judgment. They can diverge, which makes reputation an unreliable guide. Yet glory is also a legitimate ground of self-esteem, since others' esteem is evidence. The double classification, species of joy and species of esteem, shows the system's two families meeting.

Article 205, "Of shame" (AT XI 482). Shame, on the contrary, is a species of sadness, also founded on self-love, that comes from the opinion or apprehension of being blamed. It is moreover a species of modesty or humility and of distrust of oneself. When one esteems oneself so highly that one cannot imagine being despised by anyone, one cannot easily be ashamed. Shame mirrors glory, with the added component of humility: it presupposes enough self-doubt to take blame seriously. The flush of art. 117 is now explained by the definition: self-love and the desire to avoid infamy push blood up, sadness holds it there.

Article 206, "Of the use of these two passions" (AT XI 482–483). Glory and shame have the same use: they incite us to virtue, one by hope, the other by apprehension. One need only instruct one's judgment about what truly deserves blame or praise, so as not to be ashamed of doing well or vain of one's vices, as happens to many. But it is not good to strip oneself entirely of these passions, as the Cynics once did. Though the people judge very badly, since we cannot live without them and it matters to us to be esteemed by them, we must often follow their opinions rather than our own regarding the outward aspect of our actions. Reputation motivates virtue, provided judgment corrects what counts as praiseworthy. The rejection of the Cynics' contempt for opinion keeps the sage within society: inner judgment governs the substance of action, public opinion its exterior. This is the first maxim of *Discours* III, obeying the customs of one's country, extended to manners.

Article 207, "Of impudence" (AT XI 483–484). Impudence or effrontery, a contempt for shame and often for glory too, is not a passion, since there is no particular movement of the spirits in us that excites it, but a vice opposed to shame and also to glory insofar as both are good, as ingratitude is opposed to gratitude and cruelty to pity. Its principal cause is having received great affronts several times. Everyone imagines when young that praise is a good and infamy an evil far more important to life than experience shows, when, having received some signal affronts, one sees oneself entirely deprived of honour and despised by all. So those become shameless who, measuring good and evil only by bodily comforts, see that they enjoy these after the affronts as well as before, or sometimes even better, because they are relieved of many constraints honour imposed on them, and if loss of goods is joined to their disgrace, charitable people are found to give them some. Impudence, like ingratitude, is a vice without a physiology. Its genesis is disillusion: repeated disgrace teaches that honour mattered less to bodily welfare than one thought. The cure for vanity becomes, in those who value only the body, a cure for shame as well. The cynical last clause about charity is a social observation in the style of the moralists.

Article 208, "Of distaste" (AT XI 484). Distaste is a species of sadness that comes from the same cause from which joy came before. We are so made that most things we enjoy are good for us only for a time and become unpleasant afterwards, as appears chiefly in eating and drinking, useful only while one has appetite and harmful when one no longer has it; and since they then cease to be pleasant to the taste, this passion has been called distaste. Distaste is satiety: the good's value is time-bound because our needs are. The name itself, from taste, records its paradigm, so the passion is an index of the body's changing need.

Article 209, "Of regret" (AT XI 484–485). Regret is also a species of sadness with a particular bitterness, because it is always joined to some despair and to the memory of the pleasure enjoyment gave us. We regret only goods we have enjoyed and that are so lost that we have no hope of recovering them at the time and in the way we regret them. Regret combines sadness, memory of past joy and despair of recovery. Its specification, "at the time and in the way we regret them," is precise: one can regret a youth one might still in some other form enjoy.

Article 210, "Of cheerfulness" (AT XI 485). What Descartes calls cheerfulness is a species of joy whose particular feature is that its sweetness is increased by the memory of evils one has suffered and from which one feels relieved, as if one felt discharged of some heavy burden long carried on one's shoulders. He sees nothing very remarkable in these three passions and has put them here only to follow the order of the enumeration. But the enumeration seems useful to show that no passion worth particular consideration was omitted. Cheerfulness is joy heightened by contrast with past evil, the old man's contentment of art. 95. The admission that the last three passions are unremarkable is a methodological statement: completeness of the enumeration, not interest of every item, justifies including them, as the *Discours*' fourth rule requires complete enumerations.

Article 211, "A general remedy against the passions" (AT XI 485–487). Now that we know them all, we have much less reason to fear them than before, since we see that they are all good by nature and that we need avoid only their bad uses or their excesses, against which the remedies explained might suffice if each person took enough care to practise them. But since among these remedies Descartes included premeditation and the effort by which one can correct the defects of one's nature by practising separating in oneself the movements of the blood and spirits from the thoughts they are usually joined to, he admits that few people have prepared themselves enough in this way against all kinds of occurrences. The movements excited in the blood by the objects of the passions follow so promptly from the mere impressions in the brain and the disposition of the organs, though the soul contributes nothing, that no human wisdom can resist them when one is not prepared enough. Many cannot keep from laughing when tickled, even when they take no pleasure in it, because the impression of joy and surprise that once made them laugh for the same reason, reawakened in their imagination, makes their lungs suddenly swell despite them with the blood the heart sends. And those strongly inclined by nature to the emotions of joy, pity, fear or anger cannot keep from fainting, weeping, trembling, or having their blood all stirred as if they had a fever, when their imagination is strongly touched by the object of one of these passions. But what one can always do on such occasions, which Descartes thinks he can put here as the most general and easiest remedy against all excesses of the passions, is this: when one feels one's blood so stirred, one must be warned and remember that everything presented to the imagination tends to deceive the soul and make the reasons for the object of its passion appear much stronger than they are, and those against it much weaker. When the passion urges only things whose execution allows some delay, one must abstain from judging them at once and divert oneself with other thoughts until time and rest have entirely calmed the commotion in the blood. And when it incites to actions on which one must resolve on the spot, the will must apply itself mainly to considering and following the reasons contrary to those the passion represents, even if they seem weaker. When one is unexpectedly attacked by an enemy, the occasion permits no time for deliberation; but those accustomed to reflect on their actions can always, when they feel seized by fear, try to turn their thought from the danger by representing the reasons why there is much more safety and honour in resistance than in flight. And conversely, when they feel desire for vengeance and anger inciting them to rush inconsiderately at their attackers, they will remember that it is imprudent to lose oneself when one can save oneself without dishonour, and that if the match is very unequal, it is better to make an honourable retreat or ask for quarter than to expose oneself brutally to certain death. The editors refer to the 1646 exchange with Elisabeth (AT IV 405, 411) on exactly this difficulty. All passions are good by nature: this is the treatise's conclusion, announced in the guards. The remedy of art. 50, rewiring by practice, is acknowledged to be rarely complete, since the reflex outruns wisdom, and the tickled laugher shows a trace firing against the will. So a second, always available remedy is given: a meta-cognitive warning. Know that passion distorts the weighing of reasons, then suspend judgment if time allows, or deliberately favour the counter-reasons if it does not. This is the method of doubt applied to affect: the felt force of a reason is not its weight, so one corrects for a known bias. The two military examples are symmetrical, correcting fear toward resistance and anger toward retreat, so the remedy aims at the mean, not at a fixed direction.

Article 212, "That on them alone depends all the good and evil of this life" (AT XI 488). The soul can have its pleasures apart, but those it shares with the body depend entirely on the passions, so that the men they can move most are capable of tasting the most sweetness in this life. They can also find the most bitterness in it when they do not know how to use them well and fortune is against them. But wisdom is chiefly useful in this, that it teaches one to make oneself so much their master and to manage them with such skill that the evils they cause are very bearable, and that one even draws joy from them all. The last word is an affirmation of the passions. The most passionate people have the widest range of life's goods, a reversal of the Stoic ideal of *apatheia*. The soul's separate pleasures, the intellectual emotions of art. 147, are acknowledged but confined to their own sphere; the life of the union is lived through the passions. Wisdom is mastery and skilful management, not suppression, and its final achievement is the intellectual joy of art. 147 drawn even from painful passions, as from tragedy in the theatre. The treatise thus ends where its definition began: passions are the soul's being moved through the body, and a soul that knows how it is moved can enjoy being moved.

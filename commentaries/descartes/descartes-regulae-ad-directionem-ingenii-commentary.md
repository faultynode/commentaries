# Descartes, *Regulae ad directionem ingenii*: A Commentary

The commentary follows the Latin text as printed in George Heffernan's bilingual edition (Amsterdam/Atlanta: Rodopi, 1998), whose book divisions, rule titles and paragraph numbers it adopts. Units are cited by rule and Heffernan's paragraph number; the Adam–Tannery span (AT X) is given with each rule statement. Heffernan renders *ingenium* as "natural intelligence"; the commentary uses that rendering. Latin is cited only where Heffernan's English shifts the sense in a way that bears on the argument. The date of composition is taken from Cottingham's translator's preface (*The Philosophical Writings of Descartes*, vol. I, p. 7): the work was written in Latin "probably in 1628 or a few years earlier" and was not published in Descartes' lifetime; a Dutch translation appeared in 1684, and the first Latin edition in Amsterdam in 1701.

Four misreadings are guarded against throughout. The *Regulae* are not the *Meditations* in embryo: there is no methodical doubt, no cogito as foundation, no general rule that what is clearly and distinctly perceived is true, and no divine guarantee of evidence; certainty rests on the self-warranting character of intuition and on method alone. Intuition (*intuitus*) is neither sensory perception nor a mystical vision; it is the mind's own act of grasping something so simply that no doubt about it is possible. The order the method follows is an order of knowing, not of being: "absolute" and "simple" name positions in a series of cognition, not ontological primacy. *Mathesis universalis* is not ordinary mathematics generalised, nor a formal calculus; it is the science of order and measure as such, of which arithmetic and geometry are only the most transparent instances.

## Book One: On Simple Propositions and Inferences

### Rule One: On Wisdom and Science

Rule One (AT X 359–361) states that the goal of study is to direct the natural intelligence toward solid and true judgments about whatever presents itself. The rule fixes the end before the means. Its object is judgment, not information: the work will be measured by whether it makes the knower able to judge, not by the stock of truths it delivers.

§ 1. People habitually transfer what holds of one thing to another thing that resembles it, even in the respect in which the two differ. This error has distorted the understanding of the sciences. Because one body cannot master every craft, and specialisation makes the craftsman, the arts have been treated as a model for the sciences, and the sciences have been divided by their objects and pursued one at a time. Descartes rejects the analogy at its root. The arts require bodily habituation, which is finite and specific; the sciences are cognition in the mind alone, and they are all one and the same human wisdom, no more differentiated by its objects than sunlight is by the things it illuminates. The Aristotelian–Scholastic principle that the diversity of objects grounds the diversity of sciences and faculties is the target, and Descartes inverts it: unity lies on the side of the knowing power, multiplicity merely on the side of what it is applied to. Knowing one truth therefore helps rather than hinders the discovery of another. Many study plants, stars and metals while almost no one studies good sense (*bona mens*) or this universal wisdom, though everything else is valuable only as it contributes to them. And the most dangerous way to lose the right path is not to pursue base ends such as fame or profit but respectable particular ones: the utility of the sciences, or the pleasure of contemplating truth. Even these, kept in view during study, lead one to skip steps that seem idle but are needed for knowing other things. The sciences are so interconnected that it is easier to learn them together than separately. One should therefore aim only at increasing the natural light of reason, so that the intellect can show the will what to choose in each situation of life. The practical end is not abandoned but relocated: it is served by the general cultivation of judgment, not by direct pursuit. Three later doctrines are seeded here: the unity of science (the tree of philosophy in the preface to the French *Principles*), the "natural light" as the sole criterion Descartes will recognise, and the interplay of intellect and will that the Fourth Meditation will make the locus of error.

### Rule Two: On Science and Knowledge

Rule Two (AT X 362–366) restricts inquiry to objects of which the natural intelligence seems capable of certain and indubitable cognition. The restriction is presented as a demand of the first rule's end: if the aim is solid judgment, one must not engage where judgment cannot be made solid.

§ 1. Rule Two opens with the treatise's definition of knowledge, where the Latin carries more than the English. *Omnis scientia est cognitio certa et evidens* says that every science, every systematic knowledge, is certain and evident cognition. Heffernan's "all knowledge is certain and evident cognition" keeps the formal structure but loses the asymmetry the argument needs: *scientia* is the achieved body of knowledge, *cognitio* the act or state of knowing a particular thing. Descartes is claiming that science is built from nothing but certain cognitions, so that a single merely probable element disqualifies the whole. From this follows a startling inversion of the scholastic ranking of the learned and the unlearned. Someone with many doubts is not more learned than someone who has never considered the questions; if his doubts have hardened into a false opinion, he is less learned. It is better not to study at all than to occupy oneself with objects where true and false cannot be told apart, since there the risk of losing knowledge outweighs the hope of gaining it. Descartes therefore rejects all merely probable cognition. He adds that certain truths are far more numerous than the learned believe. They have overlooked them as too obvious, and, ashamed to admit ignorance, have come to believe their own constructed arguments. The probable is thus not an inferior grade of knowledge but a corruption of it, self-induced.

§ 2. Descartes draws the severe consequence himself. On almost every question in the sciences gifted people have disagreed; where two people disagree, at least one is wrong, and in fact neither seems to know, because a certain and evident reasoning could be laid out so as to convince the other. Disagreement is thus evidence of the absence of knowledge, since evidence is communicable. Where only probable opinion is to be had, we cannot hope to do better than others, and perfect knowledge is out of reach. Of the existing sciences, only arithmetic and geometry survive the rule. The test is intersubjective in form but not in principle: it is not consensus that confers certainty, but certainty that would compel consensus.

§ 3. The rule does not condemn the established way of teaching philosophy, or the probable syllogisms of the scholastics, which suit disputation. They exercise and stimulate young minds, and shaping a young intelligence with uncertain opinions is better than leaving it unguided, since a path already tested by prudent teachers, though it sometimes strays, is safer than none. Descartes is glad to have been schooled this way; he is now released from the oath to the master's words and old enough to be beyond the rod. The concession is pedagogical, not epistemic: scholastic dialectic is a fit discipline for children and a dangerous one for adults seeking knowledge. Among the first rules for anyone who seriously wants to reach the heights of knowledge must therefore be one that forbids wasting leisure on difficult questions while neglecting easy ones. Those who do so build subtle conjectures and probable arguments, and discover too late that they have multiplied their doubts without gaining any knowledge.

§ 4. Why are arithmetic and geometry alone free of falsity and uncertainty? There are two ways of reaching knowledge of things, experience and deduction. Experience is often deceptive; deduction, the pure inference of one thing from another, may be omitted if it is not seen, but can never be performed wrongly by an intellect with even minimal reason. The claim is that inference as such cannot err; failure lies only in not drawing it. The scholastic logicians' chains, by which they think to govern human reason, are therefore of little use here, though Descartes allows they may serve other ends. Formal logic is idle because it regulates something that never goes wrong. All human error, he concludes, comes not from bad inference but from taking poorly understood observations for granted or from judging rashly and without ground. Error is thus displaced from reasoning onto the premises supplied by experience and onto judgment, the point at which the will will later be held responsible. The "two ways" here are experience and deduction; in Rule Three intuition will replace experience as deduction's partner, and Rule Twelve will rehabilitate experience in a restricted sense.

§ 5. Arithmetic and geometry are more certain than other disciplines because their object is so pure and simple that it presupposes nothing experience could make uncertain; they consist entirely of conclusions to be deduced by reason. They are the easiest and clearest sciences, and to err in them is hardly human except through inattention. Their privilege derives from the purity of their object, not from any special logical form. Descartes then explains why many nevertheless prefer other arts or philosophy: everyone allows himself more freedom to guess in obscure matters than in evident ones, and it is easier to conjecture about any question than to reach the truth about even an easy one. The appeal of the obscure is the licence it gives.

§ 6. The conclusion is carefully limited. It is not that only arithmetic and geometry should be studied, but that whoever seeks the right path to truth should deal with no object about which he cannot have a certainty equal to arithmetical and geometrical demonstration. Mathematics supplies the standard of certainty, not the boundary of the field. This distinction prepares the later claim of Rule Four that what makes mathematics certain can be found and applied elsewhere.

### Rule Three: On Intuition and Deduction

Rule Three (AT X 366–370) directs inquiry, concerning any proposed object, not to what others have thought or what we ourselves conjecture, but to what we can clearly and evidently intuit or certainly deduce, since knowledge is acquired in no other way. The rule both excludes (authority and conjecture) and names the only two admissible routes.

§ 1. Reading the ancients is useful: it teaches what has already been found and shows what remains to be worked out. But it carries a danger. However cautious the reader, attentive reading may leave the stains of the authors' errors. Writers who have lapsed into a controversial opinion defend it with every subtlety, while anything certain and evident they happen to find they wrap in ambiguity, either fearing that plain reasoning would make the discovery look slight or begrudging us the plain truth. The suspicion of concealment returns in Rule Four, where it is levelled at the ancient geometers.

§ 2. Even if authors were wholly candid, we could not know whom to believe, since nearly every claim is contradicted by someone. Counting votes would not help: on a hard question the truth is more likely to have been found by few than by many. And even unanimous authors would not suffice. Having memorised all of others' demonstrations, we would still not be mathematicians unless we could solve problems ourselves; having read all of Plato's and Aristotle's arguments, we would not be philosophers unless we could judge soundly about what is proposed. We would have acquired historical, not scientific, disciplines. The distinction between historical and scientific knowledge is decisive: knowledge in the Cartesian sense cannot be received, because it consists in the knower's own seeing and judging.

§ 3. One must also never mix conjecture into judgments about the truth. The vulgar philosophy contains nothing so evident that it cannot be disputed, because its practitioners first asserted obscure things reached by probable conjecture, then came to trust them, then mixed them indiscriminately with evident truths, and finally could draw no conclusion that did not depend on some obscure premise. The diagnosis explains the general uncertainty of philosophy mechanically: a single uncertain premise propagates uncertainty to everything derived from it.

§ 4. To avoid that error, Descartes reviews all the actions of the intellect by which we can reach knowledge without fear of error. He recognises only two, intuition and deduction. The inventory concerns acts of the intellect, not forms of proposition or argument; the theory of knowledge is from the start a theory of the operations of the knowing mind.

§ 5. Intuition is defined first negatively, then positively. It is neither the fluctuating testimony of the senses nor the misleading judgment of an imagination that composes badly. It is the conception (*conceptus*) of a pure and attentive mind, so easy and distinct that no doubt remains about what we understand; or, equivalently, the indubitable conception of a pure and attentive mind, which arises from the light of reason alone. *Conceptus* can mean both the conceiving and what is conceived; Heffernan's "conceptual act" decides for the former, which fits the argument, since what follows contrasts intuition with deduction as two acts distinguished by whether they involve movement. Intuition is more certain than deduction because it is simpler, though deduction too cannot be performed wrongly. Indubitability is built into the definition: intuition is not a seeing that is then certified as reliable, but a conceiving whose very ease excludes doubt. The examples are telling: that one exists, that one thinks, that a triangle is bounded by three lines, a sphere by one surface. "That I exist" and "that I think" stand on a level with geometrical trivia, as instances of what anyone can intuit; they are not yet the privileged first truth of the *Discourse* and *Meditations*, and nothing is built on them here. There are far more such truths than people suppose, because they disdain to attend to easy matters.

§ 6. Descartes defends his use of the term "intuition" and other words he will shift from their received sense. He disregards how such words have recently been used in the schools, since it would be difficult to use the same terms with very different meanings, and attends only to what they mean in Latin, borrowing the most apt words where proper ones are lacking. *Intuitus* in Latin means looking at something directly; Descartes takes it from the literal sense of sight and makes it the mind's direct grasp, setting aside its scholastic association with the intuitive knowledge of the blessed or of singular existents. The note licenses his whole technical vocabulary: words will mean what their ordinary Latin sense suggests, not what the schools have made of them.

§ 7. The evidence and certainty of intuition is required not only for single statements but for any reasoning whatever. In inferring that 2 + 2 equals 3 + 1, one must intuit that 2 + 2 makes 4, that 3 + 1 makes 4, and that the third proposition follows necessarily from these two. The connection itself is an object of intuition. This is the bridge to deduction: inference is not a separate faculty but a chain of intuited connections.

§ 8. Why then add deduction as a second mode of knowing? Deduction is everything concluded necessarily from other things known with certainty. It must be added because many things are known with certainty without being evident themselves, provided they are deduced from true and known principles by a continuous and uninterrupted movement of thought that clearly intuits each step. So we know that the last link of a long chain is joined to the first, though we cannot see all the intermediate links in one glance, provided we have run through them in turn and remember that each is attached to its neighbour. Intuition and deduction differ in two respects: deduction involves movement or succession, intuition does not; and deduction does not need present evidence, but borrows its certainty in a way from memory. Hence propositions concluded immediately from first principles can be said to be known, under different considerations, both by intuition and by deduction; first principles only by intuition; remote conclusions only by deduction. The distinction is functional rather than absolute: a short inference is both, and the line is drawn by whether the whole can be held in present view. The reliance on memory is the weak point of deduction, and Rules Seven and Eleven will try to repair it by the rapid, repeated survey that turns a chain back into something like a single intuition.

§ 9. These two are the most certain paths to knowledge. On the side of the natural intelligence no others should be admitted; all others are to be rejected as suspect and liable to error. This does not prevent belief that what has been divinely revealed is more certain than all knowledge. Faith in obscure matters is an act not of the intelligence but of the will; and if it has foundations in the intellect, these can and should be found by one of the two ways, as Descartes promises perhaps to show later. The qualification "on the side of the natural intelligence" leaves room for revelation while denying it any cognitive role in science. Belief in the obscure is again assigned to the will, anticipating the Fourth Meditation's account of judgment. The promise to examine the intellectual foundations of faith is never kept in the *Regulae*.

### Rule Four: On Method, Mathematics, and *Mathesis Universalis*

Rule Four (AT X 371–379) states that a method is necessary for investigating the truth of things. The rule is short because it introduces the concept that the whole treatise elaborates; its paragraphs are of two strata, a definition of method (§§ 1–4) and an autobiographical account of the discovery of *mathesis universalis* (§§ 5–6), most likely written earlier.

§ 1. Mortals are so blindly curious that they lead their intelligence down unknown paths with no ground for hope, only to see whether what they seek is there, like someone so eager for treasure that he roams the streets in case a passer-by has dropped some. So proceed nearly all chemists, many geometers and many philosophers. They sometimes stumble on a truth, but they are lucky, not careful. It is better never to seek truth than to seek it without method, because disordered study and obscure meditation confuse the natural light and blind the intelligence; those used to walking in the dark weaken their eyes until they can no longer bear daylight. Experience confirms it: people who have never studied often judge more soundly than those who have spent their lives in the schools. Descartes then defines method: certain and easy rules which, if exactly followed, ensure that one never takes anything false for true and, without wasting mental effort and by steadily increasing knowledge, arrives at true knowledge of everything one is capable of knowing. The definition has a negative and a positive clause, and both are defined relative to the knower's capacity, not to things. Method is an art of conduct for a finite intellect, not a logic of propositions.

§ 2. Descartes isolates the two clauses: never take the false for true, and reach knowledge of all things. If we fail to know something we could know, it is either because we have not noticed a way leading to it or because we have fallen into the contrary error. If the method shows how to use intuition so as not to fall into error and how to find deductions so as to reach knowledge of everything, nothing else is needed for it to be complete, since knowledge is had only through intuition and deduction. The method cannot teach how to perform these operations themselves. They are the simplest and first of all, so that unless the intellect could already use them it would not understand any precept of the method, however easy. The point is foundational: method presupposes intuition and deduction as native capacities and only regulates their use. The other operations which dialectic claims to direct with their help are useless or worse, because nothing can be added to the pure light of reason that does not somehow obscure it. Against scholastic logic, which supplies rules for the forms of inference, Descartes holds that inference needs no rules; what needs rules is the order in which the mind applies its attention.

§ 3. Since method is so useful that without it study does more harm than good, Descartes readily believes that the more gifted minds have already glimpsed it, guided by nature alone. The human mind has something divine in it, in which the first seeds of useful thoughts are sown so that, however neglected and choked by contrary studies, they often bear fruit of themselves. This is seen in the easiest sciences, arithmetic and geometry. The ancient geometers used a kind of analysis which they applied to the solution of all problems, though they may have begrudged it to posterity; and algebra now aims to do for numbers what the ancients did for figures. These two are spontaneous fruits of the innate principles of this method. The method is thus not invented but recovered, and its seeds are innate; mathematics is where they have grown most freely because its objects are simplest. The doctrine of innate seeds of truth is the *Regulae*'s first form of the later theory of innate ideas, and it already does the work of explaining why method is possible at all.

§ 4. Descartes's principal aim in the treatise is to cultivate these fruits in all the other arts. He would think little of his rules if they only solved the idle problems with which arithmeticians and geometers pass the time; that would be trifling with greater subtlety than others. Though he will say much about figures and numbers, because no other discipline gives such evident and certain examples, the attentive reader will see that he is thinking of anything but ordinary mathematics. He is expounding another discipline, of which these examples are the outer covering rather than the parts. This discipline should contain the first rudiments of human reason and extend to drawing out truths in any subject; it is more important than any knowledge handed down by human means, since it is the source of all the others. He speaks of a covering not to hide the doctrine from the public but to clothe and adorn it so that it is better suited to human minds. The image of shell and kernel fixes the relation between mathematics and method for the rest of the work: numbers and figures are not the subject matter but the medium of exposition, chosen for their transparency.

§ 5. The autobiographical stratum begins. When Descartes first studied mathematics he read most of the standard authors and cultivated arithmetic and geometry as the simplest disciplines and the gateways to the rest. None satisfied him. The arithmeticians taught many truths about numbers that he could verify by calculation; the geometers displayed many truths about figures and drew conclusions by inference; but neither showed the mind why things were so or how they had been found. Hence the able often abandon these arts as childish or are deterred as by something too difficult. Nothing is emptier than occupying oneself with bare numbers and imaginary figures and with superficial demonstrations found more by chance than by art, which address the eyes and imagination more than the intellect; one loses the habit of using reason. The complaint targets synthetic exposition: proof that shows *that* without showing *how it was found*. Descartes then asks why the founders of philosophy admitted no one ignorant of mathematics to the study of wisdom, and suspects they knew a mathematics quite different from that of his day. Not perfectly: their wild rejoicing and sacrifices over small discoveries show how primitive they were, and the machines that historians celebrate were probably simple devices exaggerated by the ignorant. But the first seeds of truth implanted in human nature, which our daily reading and hearing of errors stifle, had such force in that pure early age that the light by which those men saw that virtue is preferable to pleasure and the honourable to the useful also let them recognise the true ideas of philosophy and mathematics, though they could not yet master these sciences. Traces of this true mathematics survive in Pappus and Diophantus, who then suppressed it with a pernicious cunning, fearing that so easy and simple an art would be cheapened if divulged; they preferred to show sterile truths, cleverly demonstrated, for us to admire, rather than teach the art that would have dissolved the admiration. In Descartes's own time some very able people have tried to revive this art, which seems to be nothing other than what is barbarously called algebra, if only it could be freed from the multiplicity of numbers and inexplicable figures that encumber it. These reflections turned him from arithmetic and geometry to an inquiry into mathematics in general. What do people mean by "mathematics," and why are astronomy, music, optics, mechanics and others called parts of it? The etymology does not decide it, since *mathesis* means simply "discipline," and every discipline would have equal claim to the name. Yet even a beginner can distinguish what belongs to mathematics from what does not. Descartes concludes that only those things are referred to mathematics in which some order or measure is examined, whether in numbers, figures, stars, sounds or any other object. There must therefore be a general science that explains everything that can be asked about order and measure without application to any particular matter. It should be called by an established name, *mathesis universalis*, since it contains everything for which the other sciences are called parts of mathematics. It is superior to its subordinate sciences in utility, since it extends to everything they do and more, and in ease, since its difficulties are present in them too, along with others arising from their particular objects. Everyone knows its name and understands what it concerns, yet no one troubles to learn it, because people think it easy and the mind always hurries past what it thinks easy toward grander things. The definition is the core of the rule: mathematics is unified not by a kind of object but by a respect, order and measure, under which any object may be considered. Order is named first and is wider than measure; Rule Fourteen will reduce measure to comparison and Rule Six has already made order the principle of method. *Mathesis universalis* thus stands between mathematics and method, and the paragraph does not settle whether it is identical with the method or only its most developed instance. Marion's reading of the *Regulae* as a "grey ontology" turns on this point: things are treated only as objects ordered for knowledge, their own essence bracketed.

§ 6. Conscious of his own weakness, Descartes has resolved to keep an order that always begins with the simplest and easiest things and never moves on until nothing more seems to be desired in them. He has therefore cultivated this *mathesis universalis* as far as he could, so that he can now take up somewhat higher sciences without premature haste. Before going further he will collect into one and arrange in order whatever he has found noteworthy, both so that he can recover it from this book when age weakens his memory, and so that, his memory unburdened, he can apply a freer mind to what remains. The treatise is thus a record set down so that the mind may be freed from memory, the same function that enumeration and writing will serve within inquiry itself (Rules Eleven and Sixteen). It also presents the *Regulae* as a stage, not a terminus, in Descartes's progress toward "higher sciences."

### Rule Five: On the Order and Arrangement of Things

Rule Five (AT X 379–380) states that the whole method consists in the order and arrangement of the things on which the mind's gaze must be turned in order to find any truth. We follow it exactly if we reduce complicated and obscure propositions step by step to simpler ones, and then, starting from the intuition of the simplest, try to rise by the same steps to the knowledge of all the others. The rule identifies method with order and gives order a double movement, a descent by reduction and an ascent by composition. Since intuition and deduction cannot be taught (Rule Four, § 2), what method can contribute is only the sequence in which they are applied. "Proposition" here means the matter proposed for inquiry, a question, not a statement.

§ 1. This rule contains the whole of human industry, and whoever seeks knowledge must keep to it as Theseus kept to the thread in the labyrinth. Many ignore it, or think they do not need it, and attack difficult questions as if trying to leap from the ground floor to the roof without using the stairs. Three examples follow. Astrologers claim to predict the effects of the heavens without knowing their nature or even having fully observed their motions. Those who study mechanics apart from physics rashly build new devices for producing motion. Philosophers who neglect observation expect truth to spring from their heads as Minerva did from Jupiter's. Each case skips a prior step: astronomy before astrology, physics before mechanics, observation before theory. The third example matters most. It shows that order sometimes demands experience as a step, so that the method is not a rationalist programme of pure deduction; Rule Eight's anaclastic will make the same point.

§ 2. All these violate the rule openly. But the required order is often so obscure and complex that not everyone can see it, and so they can hardly avoid going wrong unless they observe what the next proposition sets out. Rule Five states the principle of order; Rule Six will supply the technique for finding it.

### Rule Six: On Simple Natures and Complex Relations

Rule Six (AT X 381–387) states that, to distinguish the simplest things from the complicated ones and pursue them in order, we must observe, in every series in which we have deduced some truths directly from others, which is simplest and how all the others stand more, less or equally removed from it. The rule makes the series of deduction itself the object of reflection: one notes not only what follows from what, but the distance of each item from the first.

§ 1. The proposition may seem to teach nothing new, but it contains the main secret of the art, and no proposition in the treatise is more useful. It teaches that all things can be arranged in series, not insofar as they belong to some genus of being, as the philosophers divided things into categories, but insofar as some can be known from others. Whenever a difficulty arises we can then see at once whether others must be examined first, and which, and in what order. This is the decisive break with Aristotle. The categories order things by what they are; Descartes orders them by how they are known. The series is an order of knowing, and the same thing may occupy different places in different series. Marion's thesis that the *Regulae* replace the Aristotelian *ousia* with the object as it is ordered for knowledge has its textual base here.

§ 2. To do this, one must note first that all things, insofar as they serve our purpose, where we do not consider their isolated natures but compare them with one another so that some may be known from others, can be called either absolute or relative. The qualifying clause is essential. "Absolute" and "relative" are not properties of things in themselves but positions they take when compared within a series.

§ 3. The absolute is whatever contains in itself the pure and simple nature in question: whatever is considered as independent, a cause, simple, universal, one, equal, similar, straight, and so on. Descartes also calls absolute the simplest and easiest thing, so that we can use it in resolving questions. The list is heterogeneous: it mixes metaphysical terms (independent, cause), logical terms (universal, one) and mathematical ones (equal, straight). The unity of the list lies in function: each is what the other terms of its series must be measured against.

§ 4. The relative is what shares the same nature, or something of it, by which it can be referred to the absolute and deduced from it through a series of steps; but its concept also involves further things, which Descartes calls relations. The relative is the dependent, effect, composite, particular, many, unequal, dissimilar, oblique and so on. The further relative things are from the absolute, the more such mutually subordinate relations they contain. The rule requires us to distinguish all these relations, to observe their connections and natural order, and to be able to reach the most absolute from the last term by passing through all the others. Distance from the absolute is measured by the number of relations a term involves; complexity becomes countable.

§ 5. The secret of the whole art is to attend in everything to what is most absolute. Some things are more absolute than others in one respect and more relative in another. The universal is more absolute than the particular because its nature is simpler, yet more relative because it depends on individuals in order to exist. Some things are more absolute than others without being most absolute: a species is absolute relative to individuals, relative to the genus; among measurable things extension is absolute, among extensions length. To make clear that he is considering the series of things to be known and not the nature of each, Descartes has deliberately counted cause and equal among the absolutes, though by nature they are relative. For philosophers cause and effect are correlatives; but if we ask what the effect is, we must first know the cause, not the reverse. Equals correspond to one another; but we know what is unequal only by comparison with equals, not the reverse. The example of cause is the clearest proof that the order is epistemic. In the order of being, cause and effect are strictly correlative; in the order of knowing, the cause comes first. Descartes knowingly overrides ontology for the sake of cognition.

§ 6. One must note second that there are only a few pure and simple natures that can be intuited first and by themselves, independently of any others, either in experience itself or by a light implanted in us. Heffernan's "situated in us" weakens the Latin. *Lumine quodam in nobis insito* means a light innate or implanted in us, and the word ties the simple natures to the innate seeds of truth in Rule Four, § 3. With "situated," the alternative between experience and an inborn light disappears, and that alternative is the point: some simple natures are given in experience, others by the mind's own light, and both are intuited. These natures must be carefully noted, since they are what we call the simplest in each series. All others are perceived only by deduction from them, either directly or through two, three or more inferences, whose number must be noted so that we know how many steps a conclusion stands from the first and simplest proposition. The chain of inferences from which the series arise, to which every question must be reduced, can everywhere be examined methodically. But since it is not easy to survey all the inferences at once, and they must be distinguished by sharpness of mind rather than retained by memory, we must train the intelligence to notice them at once when needed. The best way is to accustom ourselves to reflect with a certain sagacity on the smallest details of what we have already perceived. The simple natures enter here, to be classified in Rule Twelve; the training of perspicacity and sagacity is left to Rules Nine and Ten.

§ 7. Third, studies should not begin with difficult matters. Before tackling any particular question, we should collect, without selection, the truths that present themselves, then see whether others can be deduced from them, and others again from these. Then we should reflect on the truths found and consider why some were found earlier and more easily than others, so as to judge which to seek first when we take up a definite question. The example: if it occurs to me that 6 is double 3, I may ask what is double 6, namely 12, then double 12, 24, double 24, 48. I easily deduce that 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 are in continued proportion. These things are almost childishly clear, but by reflecting on them I understand the principle involved in every question about the proportions or relations of things, and the order in which such questions should be investigated. This alone covers the whole of pure mathematics. The method learns from its own successes: one first gathers easy truths, then studies the order in which they were reached. Mathematics is reduced to the theory of proportion, which anticipates Rule Fourteen's reduction of all comparison to equality and proportion.

§ 8. The reflection yields a typology of difficulty. First, finding double 6 is no harder than finding double 3; once a proportion between two magnitudes is known, countless others with the same proportion can be found; and the difficulty does not change in kind if three, four or more are sought, since each is found separately. Next, given 3 and 6, the third term 12 is found easily, but given the extremes 3 and 12, the mean 6 is not found so easily. The difficulty is of another kind: one must attend at once to both extremes and to their proportion, and obtain a new proportion by dividing it. Then, given 3 and 24, finding one of the two means 6 and 12 is harder still, since one must attend to three things at once to find a fourth. Given 3 and 48, finding the three means seems harder again; but it can be divided, by finding first the single mean 12 between 3 and 48, then 6 between 3 and 12, then 24 between 12 and 48, so that it reduces to the second kind. Difficulty is measured by the number of items that must be held in attention simultaneously, and the art consists in reducing a problem demanding many simultaneous attentions to a sequence of problems demanding fewer. The criterion is cognitive load, not the logical form of the problem.

§ 9. These examples also show that the same thing can be known by different routes, one much harder and more obscure than another. To find the four continued proportionals 3, 6, 12, 24, if two consecutive terms are given, the rest are found very easily: the proposition is examined directly. If two alternate terms are given, 3 and 12 or 6 and 24, it is examined indirectly in the first mode; if the two extremes are given, indirectly in the second mode. Many other consequences could be drawn, but this suffices to show what Descartes means by direct and indirect deduction, and to persuade the reader that attentive and discerning reflection on the easiest first things can discover much in other disciplines too. The direct–indirect distinction fixes terminology that Rules Thirteen and Eighteen take up. A single state of affairs can be accessed in several ways; which way is direct depends on what is given, so that order is again relative to the knower's starting point.

### Rule Seven: On Enumeration or Induction

Rule Seven (AT X 387–392) states that, to complete knowledge, everything bearing on our project must be surveyed, together and singly, by a continuous and wholly uninterrupted movement of thought, and gathered in a sufficient and ordered enumeration. "Induction" is Descartes's synonym for enumeration and should not be read as inference from particular observations to a general law; it means a complete or adequately classified survey.

§ 1. The rule is needed for admitting as certain those truths which are not deduced immediately from self-evident first principles. Such deduction sometimes runs through so long a chain that, on reaching the conclusion, we cannot easily recall the whole route. A continuous movement of thought must therefore aid the weakness of memory. If I have found by separate operations the relation of A to B, then of B to C, C to D and D to E, I do not thereby see the relation of A to E, nor can I understand it from the known relations unless I recall them all. So I run through them several times in a continuous movement that intuits each and passes on to the next, until I can pass from first to last so quickly that memory has almost no role and I seem to intuit the whole at once. This relieves memory, corrects the slowness of the intelligence and in a way extends its capacity. The rapid survey converts a deduction back into something close to an intuition, answering the problem left by Rule Three, § 8, where deduction borrowed its certainty from memory. The intellect cannot be enlarged, but its effective span can be widened by practice. Heffernan renders this second movement, like the first, as a movement "of thought"; the Latin says *continuo quodam imaginationis motu*, a movement of the imagination. The difference matters. The rule statement speaks of a movement of thought (*cogitationis*), but the remedy for memory is assigned to imagination, the faculty Rules Twelve and Fourteen will make the indispensable aid of the intellect. Already here the intellect cannot complete its own knowledge without a corporeal auxiliary.

§ 2. The movement must nowhere be interrupted. Those who deduce too quickly from remote principles often fail to run through the intermediate links carefully and pass over much without due attention; if the smallest thing is overlooked, the chain breaks and the whole certainty of the conclusion collapses. Certainty is all-or-nothing. A deduction is not more or less probable according to how many links are secure; one gap voids it.

§ 3. Enumeration is required to complete knowledge. The other precepts help solve most questions, but only enumeration ensures that we always judge truly and certainly whatever we apply our minds to, so that nothing escapes us and we know something about everything. The completeness promised in the definition of method (Rule Four, § 1) is secured by this operation.

§ 4. Enumeration or induction is an inquiry into everything bearing on a proposed question, so careful and exact that we may conclude certainly and evidently that nothing has been inadvertently omitted. Even when what we seek eludes us, we are more learned, at least in perceiving with certainty that it could not have been found by any route known to us. If, as often happens, we have surveyed every route open to human beings, we may boldly assert that knowledge of it lies wholly beyond the reach of human intelligence. Enumeration yields knowledge even in failure: certainty about the limits of inquiry is itself knowledge. This prepares Rule Eight.

§ 5. A sufficient enumeration or induction is one from which truth is concluded more certainly than by any other proof except simple intuition. Where knowledge cannot be reduced to simple intuition, this is the one path left to us, who have thrown off the chains of the syllogism, and we may follow it with confidence. Whatever we have deduced immediately from something else, if the inference was evident, has already been reduced to a true intuition. But if we infer one thing from many disconnected ones, our intellect is often unable to embrace them all in one intuition, and the certainty of the operation must suffice. We cannot distinguish all the links of a long chain in a single glance, but if we have seen the connection of each to its neighbours, we may say we have seen how the last is joined to the first. Enumeration thus ranks second in the scale of certainty, below intuition and above formal syllogism, which it replaces. It differs from deduction in being an inference from many separate items rather than a linear chain: deduction proper has collapsed into intuition, and what remains outside intuition is enumeration.

§ 6. The enumeration must be sufficient because it can often be defective and so open to error. Sometimes we survey many evident items but omit the slightest, and the chain breaks and certainty is lost. Sometimes we include everything but fail to distinguish the items, so that we know them all only confusedly. Two distinct failures are named, omission and confusion, corresponding to the two virtues of the next paragraph, completeness and distinctness.

§ 7. An enumeration must sometimes be complete, sometimes distinct, sometimes neither, which is why it is required only to be sufficient. To prove by enumeration how many kinds of corporeal being there are, or how many fall under the senses, I may not say there are so many and no more unless I have made sure that all are included and each distinguished from the others. To show that the rational soul is not corporeal, the enumeration need not be complete: it is enough to group all bodies into classes and show that the soul belongs to none of them. To show that the circle has the largest area of all figures with equal perimeter, I need not review all figures but only demonstrate it for some, and conclude the same of the rest by induction. Sufficiency is relative to the question: what must be counted depends on what is to be shown. The second example is the earliest form of the argument for the real distinction of mind and body, and it proceeds not from clear and distinct ideas but from exhaustive classification. The third is induction in a quasi-mathematical sense, where a demonstration on representative cases licenses a general conclusion.

§ 8. The enumeration must also be ordered. This is partly because there is no better remedy for the defects listed than to examine everything in order, and partly because surveying every relevant item separately would often take more than a lifetime, either because there are too many or because the same things would have to be repeated. If we arrange them in the best order, most will fall into definite classes, and it will be enough to examine one class, or one item from each class, or some classes rather than others; at the least we will never go over anything twice in vain. A well-planned order often lets us accomplish quickly and easily what at first looked immense. Classification is the instrument that makes completeness finite.

§ 9. The order of enumeration can usually vary and depends on each person's choice; to devise it well one should remember what was said in the fifth proposition. Many of the more trivial human devices are found solely by such ordering. To make a perfect anagram by transposing the letters of a name, there is no need to proceed from easier to harder or to distinguish absolute from relative, since these do not arise. It is enough to adopt an order of examining the transpositions in which none is repeated, and to divide them into classes so that one sees at once where there is most hope of finding what is sought. The work then becomes child's play rather than tedious. The anagram shows the bare minimum of method: where there is no series of dependence, order still serves by preventing repetition and directing search.

§ 10. The last three propositions should not be separated, because they usually have to be considered together and all contribute equally to the perfection of method. It did not much matter which was taught first. They have been explained briefly because the rest of the treatise will do almost nothing else but show in detail what has been treated here in general. Rules Five to Seven are the method in its entirety; everything after them is application and elaboration. This retrospective clause also answers the question left open in Rule Four: method is the art of order, and Rules Five to Seven are its whole theory.

### Rule Eight: On the Limits of Human Reason

Rule Eight (AT X 392–400) states that if, in the series of things to be investigated, something occurs that our intellect cannot intuit well enough, we must stop there and not examine what follows, refraining from useless labour. The rule converts a failure of intuition into an instruction: the place where intuition fails marks a boundary in the series.

§ 1. The three previous rules prescribe and explain order; this one shows when order is strictly necessary and when merely useful. Whatever forms a complete step in the series leading from relative to absolute, or the reverse, must be examined before everything that follows. But where many things belong to the same step, as often happens, it is useful to go through them in order, yet not obligatory; one may often proceed even when only a few, or one, of them are clearly known. Order binds between steps, not within a step. The stopping rule therefore applies only where a whole step fails.

§ 2. The rule follows from the reasons given for the second. It may seem only to keep us from discussing certain things, not to reveal any truth, and for beginners it teaches much what Rule Two taught, not to waste effort. But for those who have mastered the first seven rules it shows how they can satisfy themselves in any science so that they want nothing more. Whoever has followed the earlier rules exactly and is still told by this one to stop will know for certain that no effort could find the knowledge sought, because the obstacle lies not in his intelligence but in the nature of the difficulty or in the human condition. This recognition is no less knowledge than knowledge of the nature of the thing, and to push curiosity further would be unreasonable. The rule turns the ignorance that Rule Two counselled avoiding into a positive result. Knowledge of the boundary is knowledge in the full sense, because it is certain.

§ 3. Two examples. Someone who studies only mathematics seeks the line that in dioptrics is called the anaclastic, the line at which parallel rays are refracted so that all meet at one point. By Rules Five and Six he will see that it depends on the ratio of the angles of refraction to the angles of incidence. Since this ratio belongs to physics, not mathematics, he cannot find it and must stop at the threshold. He gains nothing by taking it from the philosophers or from experience, since that would violate Rule Three; besides, the question is composite and relative, and certain experience can be had only of things purely simple and absolute, as will be said later. Nor may he suppose some ratio he thinks most likely, for then he would be seeking not the anaclastic but whatever line follows from his supposition. The example is precise about the boundary. The mathematician's limit is the limit of his discipline, not of human reason, and both borrowed knowledge and hypothesis are excluded because they replace the question with another.

§ 4. Someone else, who studies not mathematics only but, following Rule One, seeks the truth about everything that occurs to him, will go further when he meets the same difficulty. He finds that the ratio depends on the change in the angles produced by the difference of media; that this change depends on how the ray passes through the transparent body; that knowledge of this passage presupposes knowing the nature of light; and that to understand light he must know what a natural power in general is, the most absolute term in the series. Having grasped this clearly by intuition, he will, by Rule Five, descend again by the same steps. If at the second step he cannot at once recognise the nature of light, he will, by Rule Seven, enumerate all the other natural powers, so as to understand light from one of them, at least by analogy. Then he will examine how the ray passes through the transparent body, and so pursue the rest in order until he reaches the anaclastic. Though many have sought it in vain, Descartes sees nothing to prevent anyone using his method perfectly from knowing it evidently. The second inquirer succeeds because Rule One's unity of the sciences lets him cross from mathematics to physics. The regress from anaclastic to natural power in general is the method's descending movement made concrete, and it reaches an absolute term of physics, not mathematics. The appeal to analogy foreshadows the reasoning by comparison in Rule Nine and the *Dioptrics*, where light is explained by the models of the blind man's stick and the ball. Descartes in fact derived the sine law of refraction and the anaclastic around 1626–27, so this paragraph reports a solved problem.

§ 5. The finest example is someone who sets himself the question of examining all the truths that human reason can know, which anyone seriously striving for good sense must do once in life. He will find by the rules that nothing can be known before the intellect, since the knowledge of everything else depends on it and not the reverse. Having surveyed what follows immediately from the knowledge of the pure intellect, he will enumerate the other instruments of knowledge besides the intellect, of which there are only two, imagination (*phantasia*) and the senses. He will devote himself to distinguishing and examining these three modes of knowing. Since truth and falsity in the proper sense exist only in the intellect, though they often take their origin from the other two, he will attend to everything that can deceive him so as to guard against it. He will also enumerate exactly all the routes to truth open to human beings so as to follow the certain one; there are not so many that a sufficient enumeration cannot find them all. The text then breaks off, and the sentence resumes with what will surprise the inexperienced: once he has distinguished, for each object, knowledge that merely fills or adorns the memory from knowledge that makes someone truly more learned, he will feel that it is not for lack of intelligence or art that he knows no more, and that no one else can know anything he could not know if he applied his mind properly. Many questions will be forbidden to him by this rule, but since he clearly sees they exceed human intelligence he will not think himself more ignorant than others; knowing that no one can know them will fully satisfy his curiosity. The question about the extent of human knowledge is the most absolute of all questions, because every other inquiry depends on the intellect that performs it. The priority of the intellect is an epistemic priority in the series, not yet the metaphysical priority of the mind over body in the *Meditations*. The claim that every properly directed mind can know whatever any other can is the egalitarian corollary of Rule One: if wisdom is one and its light natural, differences in learning are differences of method.

§ 6. So that we are not forever unsure what the mind can do and do not labour haphazardly, we must, once in life and before taking up particular things, inquire carefully what knowledge human reason can attain. To do this better, among equally easy things we should always investigate the more useful first. The "once in life" anticipates the opening of the *Meditations*, but the object differs: there it is the overthrow of opinion, here the survey of cognitive capacity.

§ 7. This method resembles the mechanical arts, which need no method beyond their own and themselves teach how to make their tools. Someone who wanted to be a smith and had no tools would at first use a hard stone or lump of iron as an anvil, a rock as a hammer, pieces of wood as tongs, and so on; with these he would not at once forge swords or helmets for others, but first make hammers, anvil, tongs and other tools for himself. Likewise, since we have so far found only rough precepts, which seem innate to our minds rather than produced by art, we should not use them at once to settle the disputes of philosophers or the puzzles of mathematicians, but first to find the other things more necessary for examining truth, which should be no harder to find than answers to the usual questions of geometry or physics. The smith analogy answers the regress objection to method: method needs no prior method, because its first crude instruments are innate and serve to fashion better ones. The method is self-constructing. The analogy with the crafts reverses Rule One's warning against modelling the sciences on the arts, but on a different point: not the division of labour, but the bootstrapping of tools.

§ 8. Nothing is more useful to inquire into than what human knowledge is and how far it extends. This is the first question to be examined by the rules, and everyone who loves truth even a little must do it once in life, since its investigation contains the true instruments of knowledge and the whole method. Nothing seems more foolish than to dispute boldly about the secrets of nature, the influence of the heavens on things below, the prediction of the future and the like, without having asked whether human reason can discover them. Defining the limits of the intelligence we feel in ourselves should not seem arduous, since we readily judge about things outside us and foreign to us. Nor is it an endless task to encompass in thought everything in the universe, to see how each may be subjected to our examination, since nothing is so manifold or scattered that it cannot be confined within limits and arranged under a number of headings by the enumeration discussed. To see this, the question is divided into two parts: it concerns either us, who are capable of knowledge, or the things themselves, which can be known. The examination of the knower precedes the knowledge of things and is easier than it, because it is closer. The division between knowing subject and known things organises Rule Twelve.

§ 9. In us, only the intellect is capable of knowledge, but it can be helped or hindered by three other faculties: imagination, senses and memory. We must see in order how each can hinder, so as to guard against it, and how each can help, so as to use all its resources. This part of the question will thus be treated by a sufficient enumeration, as the following proposition will show. The list now has three auxiliary faculties, adding memory to the two of § 5. The two lists probably belong to different drafts; the three-faculty version is the one Rule Twelve develops.

§ 10. We come then to the things themselves, considered only insofar as the intellect reaches them. In this sense they are divided into the simplest natures and complex or composite natures. The simple natures must be spiritual, corporeal, or common to both. Some composites the intellect experiences to be composite before deciding anything about them; others it composes itself. All this will be explained in the twelfth proposition, where it will be shown that falsity can exist only in the composites the intellect makes. For this reason the composites are further divided into those deduced from the simplest and self-evident natures, treated in the whole next book, and those that also presuppose others we experience as composite in fact, to which the whole third book is devoted. The restriction "insofar as the intellect reaches them" defines the object of the whole treatise: things as they fall within cognition, not as they are in themselves. Heffernan's note hears in the phrase "the things themselves" an anticipation of Husserl's motto; the anticipation is closer than a slogan, since Descartes too brackets the being of things to consider only their givenness to the knower. The plan of the work is fixed here: Book Two on perfectly understood questions deduced from simple natures, Book Three on questions involving experienced composites. Error is located in composition by the intellect, which makes the theory of falsity a theory of judgment.

§ 11. Throughout the treatise Descartes will try to follow all the routes to truth open to human beings so exactly, and show them so accessible, that anyone who has learned the method, however mediocre his intelligence, will see that no route is closed to him and open to others, and that it is not for lack of intelligence or art that he knows no more. Whenever he applies his mind to knowing something, he will either find it, or see that it depends on some observation not in his power and so stop without blaming his intelligence, or demonstrate that it exceeds human intelligence altogether, and so not think himself more ignorant, since knowing this is no less knowledge than any other. The three outcomes exhaust the possibilities of inquiry: success, a limit set by experience, a limit set by human nature. The second outcome concedes that some knowledge depends on observations beyond the knower's control, which Book Three was to address.

### Rule Nine: On Perspicacity of Intuition

Rule Nine (AT X 400–403) states that we must turn the whole gaze of the intelligence to the smallest and easiest things and dwell on them long enough to become used to intuiting the truth distinctly and clearly. Rules Nine, Ten and Eleven form a triad of exercises: perspicacity for intuition, sagacity for deduction, and the capacity of the intelligence for the combination of both.

§ 1. Having set out the two operations of the intellect, intuition and deduction, Descartes now explains how we can make ourselves more adept at them and cultivate the two chief faculties of the intelligence: perspicacity in the distinct intuition of single things and sagacity in deducing one thing from another skilfully. The shift from operations to faculties is significant. Rule Four, § 2, said that method cannot teach how to intuit or deduce; it can, however, train the dispositions that make these acts easier. The intelligence (*ingenium*) is the trainable power, the intellect its constant core.

§ 2. We learn how to use intuition by comparing it with sight. Whoever tries to see many objects in one glance sees none distinctly; whoever attends to many things in one act of thought has a confused mind. Craftsmen who work on minute objects and train their eyes on single points learn by practice to distinguish the finest details. So those who never let their thought be scattered over many things at once, but always give their whole attention to the simplest and easiest matters, become perspicacious. The visual analogy defines intuition by its limit: it is a focus, and focus excludes multiplicity. Distinctness is achieved by narrowing, not by widening, the field.

§ 3. People commonly find difficult things more attractive than easy ones. Many admire the lofty arguments of philosophers, though their foundations have never been properly examined, and think they know nothing when they see a very clear and simple cause, like the sick who think darkness clearer than light. But those who truly know discern truth equally easily whether they have drawn it from a simple or an obscure subject, since once they reach a truth they grasp each truth by a similar, single and distinct act. The difference lies wholly in the route, which must be longer the further the truth is from the first and most absolute principles. Every truth, however remote, is finally known by the same kind of act; complexity belongs only to the path. This is the epistemological basis for the unity of science asserted in Rule One: there are no higher and lower kinds of knowing, only longer and shorter routes.

§ 4. Everyone must therefore get used to embracing in thought things so few and so simple that they never think they know anything they do not intuit as distinctly as what they know most distinctly. Some are born far more apt at this, but art and exercise can greatly improve the intelligence. Above all, one must be firmly persuaded that the sciences, however abstruse, are to be deduced not from great and obscure things but only from easy and accessible ones. The standard of knowledge is internal and comparative: one's best case of distinct knowing serves as the measure for every other claim. The final principle reverses the scholastic order of dignity, in which the highest sciences treat the highest objects.

§ 5. An example. To examine whether a natural power can pass instantaneously to a distant place through the whole intervening medium, Descartes will not turn at once to magnetism, the influence of the stars or the speed of light, since those would be harder to examine than the question itself. He will consider the local motions of bodies, since nothing in this domain is more accessible to the senses. He will see that a stone cannot pass instantaneously from one place to another, since it is a body, but that a power like that which moves the stone is communicated instantaneously only if it passes bare from one object to another. If he moves one end of a staff, however long, the power moving that part necessarily moves all the others at the same instant, because the power is transmitted bare and does not reside in a body that carries it, as the stone does. The procedure substitutes a simpler, sensibly accessible case for a complex one, then distinguishes what is carried by a body from what is transmitted through it. The staff is the model for Descartes's theory of light as an instantaneous pressure transmitted through a medium, developed in *The World* and the *Dioptrics*, and it returns in the pen of Rule Twelve, § 7.

§ 6. Likewise, to learn how one simple cause can produce contrary effects at once, Descartes will not borrow from physicians remedies that expel some humours and retain others, nor talk of the moon warming by its light and cooling by an occult quality. He will look at a balance, in which the same weight at the same instant raises one pan and lowers the other. The occult quality is the scholastic placeholder that names an effect without explaining it; the balance replaces it with a mechanism whose working is fully visible. The simple model is not an illustration but an intuition that makes the complex case intelligible.

### Rule Ten: On Sagacity of Deduction

Rule Ten (AT X 403–406) states that to make the intelligence sagacious, we should exercise it in seeking things others have already found, and should go methodically through even the most trivial human devices, especially those that display or presuppose order.

§ 1. Descartes confesses that he was born with an intelligence that has always found its greatest pleasure not in hearing others' reasons but in finding them himself. This alone drew him, as a young man, to the sciences. Whenever a book promised a new discovery in its title, he tried before reading further whether he could reach something similar by an innate sagacity, and took care not to spoil this harmless pleasure by hasty reading. He succeeded so often that he eventually realised he was not reaching truth, as others do, by vague and blind inquiry and by luck rather than art, but had grasped through long experience certain rules that help greatly; with these he devised further rules, and so worked out the whole method. The method is presented as the reflective form of a practice already successful. Its origin is autobiographical and its first principle is the refusal of received knowledge already formulated in Rule Three: what is known must be found, not learned.

§ 2. Since not every intelligence is so inclined to discover on its own, the rule teaches that we should not begin with difficult things but with the most trivial and simple arts, above all those in which order prevails: those of the weavers of cloth and carpets, of women who embroider or make lace with infinitely varied patterns, all number games and whatever belongs to arithmetic. These exercise the intelligence remarkably, provided we find them out ourselves and do not take them from others. Since nothing in them is hidden and they are wholly suited to human capacity, they display very distinctly countless orders, all different and all regular. Human sagacity consists almost entirely in the careful observation of such orders. Sagacity is defined as the perception of order, which ties deduction to the method of Rules Five to Seven. The crafts chosen are those where order is fully exposed: the pattern of a weave is its whole content.

§ 3. That is why these things must be investigated methodically, and in trivial matters method is simply the constant observation of the order either present in the thing or ingeniously devised. To read a text written in an unknown cipher, no order is apparent, yet we will invent one, both to test every assumption we might make about letters, words or sentences, and to arrange them so that we may know by enumeration everything that can be deduced from them. Above all we must not waste time guessing at such things at random and without art. Even if they can often be found without art, sometimes faster by luck than by method, this dims the light of the intelligence and accustoms it to childish and empty things, so that it stays on the surface of things and cannot penetrate them. Nor should we fall into the error of those who think only about serious and lofty matters and, after much labour, acquire only a confused knowledge while desiring a profound one. We should first practise on easier matters, but with method, so that by accessible and familiar paths we grow used, as if in play, to penetrating to the inmost truth of things; then we will soon find that we can deduce from evident principles many propositions that look very difficult. Where the object displays no order, method imposes one. Order is not only found but devised (*excogitatus*), and devising an order is a legitimate cognitive act provided it is controlled by enumeration. The emphasis falls on how one finds, not on what one finds: a lucky find damages the intelligence, because it trains a habit of guessing.

§ 4. Some will be surprised that, in teaching how to deduce truths from one another, Descartes omits all the precepts of the dialecticians, who think they govern human reason by prescribing forms of argument whose conclusions follow so necessarily that reason relying on them, even if it takes a holiday from evident and attentive consideration of the inference, can still reach a certain conclusion by force of form. But truth often slips through these chains, while those who use them stay entangled in them, which happens less often to others. Experience shows that the cleverest sophisms seldom deceive anyone who uses pure reason; usually it is the sophists themselves who are deceived. The objection to formal logic is precise: it is designed to let reason stop attending. For Descartes certainty exists only in the attentive act, so a procedure that guarantees conclusions without attention offers a counterfeit certainty.

§ 5. Taking care above all that reason should not take a holiday while examining the truth of anything, we reject these forms as contrary to our purpose and seek instead every aid by which our thought may be kept attentive, as will be shown later. To make it still clearer that this art of reasoning contributes nothing to knowledge of truth, note that the dialecticians cannot form by their art a syllogism concluding to a truth unless they already have its matter, that is, unless they already know the truth the syllogism deduces. They learn nothing new from the form, so ordinary dialectic is useless to those who seek the truth of things; it can only help sometimes to explain to others reasons already known, and so should be transferred from philosophy to rhetoric. This is the classic objection that the syllogism begs the question, but it serves a constructive purpose: the logic of forms is replaced by aids to attention, the topic of Rules Twelve to Sixteen. Logic becomes a technique of exposition, not of discovery, which is consistent with Descartes's later contrast between synthetic presentation and analytic discovery.

### Rule Eleven: On Capacity of Inference

Rule Eleven (AT X 407–410) states that after we have intuited a number of simple propositions, if we infer something else from them, it is useful to run through them in a continuous and uninterrupted movement of thought, to reflect on their relations to one another, and to conceive as many of them together distinctly as possible. Our knowledge thus becomes more certain and the capacity of the intelligence is greatly increased.

§ 1. This rule offers the occasion to explain more clearly what was said about intuition in Rules Three and Seven: in one place it was paired with deduction, in the other only with enumeration, defined as an inference drawn from many disconnected things, and in the same place it was said that the simple deduction of one thing from another is performed by intuition. Descartes acknowledges an apparent inconsistency in his own terminology and undertakes to resolve it.

§ 2. Two things are required for intuition: that the proposition be understood clearly and distinctly, and that it be understood whole and at once, not successively. Deduction, considered as a process as in Rule Three, does not seem to happen all at once; it involves a movement of the intelligence inferring one thing from another, and so was rightly distinguished from intuition there. But considered as already completed, as in Rule Seven, it no longer denotes a movement but the end of a movement. So we suppose that deduction is seen by intuition when it is simple and clear, but not when it is manifold and involved. For the latter we have used the name enumeration or induction, since it cannot be grasped by the intellect all at once, and its certainty depends in a way on memory, which must retain the judgments about the individual parts enumerated so that one conclusion may be drawn from them all. The solution is to treat deduction under two aspects: as a movement it is distinct from intuition; as a completed result, if simple, it is intuited. Enumeration takes over the role of the non-intuitable deduction. The two requirements for intuition yield the formula "clearly and distinctly," here paired for the only time in the *Regulae*, together with simultaneity. The later "clear and distinct perception" lacks the requirement of simultaneity, because the *Meditations* no longer ground certainty in a temporal act but in the divine guarantee.

§ 3. These distinctions were needed to interpret the rule. Rule Nine dealt only with intuition, Rule Ten with enumeration; this one explains how the two operations help and complete each other, so that, through a movement of thought that attentively intuits each item and passes at once to the next, they seem to merge into a single operation. The ideal limit of method is a deduction so practised that it becomes an intuition. Descartes's claim that Rule Ten dealt with enumeration is inexact; it dealt with sagacity in deduction, which uses enumeration in the cipher example.

§ 4. The rule has two uses: the conclusion is known more certainly, and the intelligence becomes more apt to find other things. Since memory, on which the certainty of conclusions too large for one intuition depends, is weak and unstable, it must be renewed and strengthened by this continuous and repeated movement of thought. If I have recognised the relation between a first and second magnitude, then a second and third, a third and fourth, a fourth and fifth, I do not thereby see the relation of the first to the fifth, nor can I deduce it from the known relations unless I remember them all. So I must run through them repeatedly in thought until I can pass from the first to the last so quickly that I leave almost nothing to memory and seem to intuit the whole at once. Rule Seven, § 1, is restated almost verbatim, except that the movement is called a movement of thought (*cogitationis*), where Rule Seven assigned it to imagination; the two rules were probably written at different times.

§ 5. Everyone sees that this corrects the slowness of the intelligence and enlarges its capacity. But its greatest use is that, by reflecting on the mutual dependence of simple propositions, we acquire the habit of distinguishing at a glance what is more and what less relative, and by what steps the relative is reduced to the absolute. Running through continued proportionals, I see that I recognise with the same kind of conception, neither more nor less easily, the relation of the first to the second, the second to the third, and so on, but that I cannot so easily conceive how the second depends on the first and third together, and still less how it depends on the first and fourth. Hence I understand why, given the first and second, I easily find the third and fourth: it is done by particular and separate conceptions. Given the first and third, I cannot so easily find the middle, since that requires a conception embracing two of the earlier ones at once. Given the first and fourth, it is harder still to intuit the two means, since three conceptions are involved. Given the first and fifth, finding the three means might seem harder still, but it is not, for a different reason: although four conceptions are joined, they can be separated, since four is divisible, so that I can find the third from the first and fifth, then the second from the first and third, and so on. Whoever is used to such reflection sees at once, in a new question, what makes it difficult and what is the simplest way to resolve it, which is the greatest aid to knowing truth. The example from Rule Six, §§ 8–9, is now re-read as an analysis of the number of conceptions that must be held together. Difficulty is a function of the number of simultaneous conceptions, and its reduction a function of their divisibility. The power of method lies in making the structure of a difficulty itself an object of intuition.

### Rule Twelve: On the Power and Operations of the Natural Intelligence

Rule Twelve (AT X 410–430) states that we must use all the aids of intellect, imagination, senses and memory: to intuit simple propositions distinctly; to compare what is sought correctly with what is known, so as to recognise it; and to find the things that should be compared with one another, so that no resource of human industry is omitted. The rule closes Book One by mobilising every cognitive faculty for the three tasks of intuition, comparison and discovery. Its long exposition supplies the anthropology and the theory of objects that the method presupposed.

§ 1. This rule concludes everything said so far and teaches in general what will be explained in detail. It is a summary and a program at once: the doctrine of faculties and simple natures it presents is the ground on which Books Two and Three will build.

§ 2. In the knowledge of things only two points need consideration: we who know, and the things to be known. In us there are only four faculties we can use for this purpose: intellect, imagination, sense and memory. Only the intellect can perceive truth, but it must be helped by imagination, sense and memory so that we omit nothing within our power. On the side of things, it is enough to examine three matters: what presents itself spontaneously, how one thing is known from another, and what is deduced from what. This enumeration seems complete. The division follows Rule Eight, §§ 8–10. The three points on the side of things are not kinds of being but stages of cognition: the given, the relation of knowing one thing through another, and deduction. The theory of the object is again a theory of the order of knowing.

§ 3. On the first point, Descartes would like to explain what the human mind is, what the body is, how the body is informed by the mind, which faculties in the composite serve knowledge and what each does. But there is not enough space here to set out everything required before the truth about these matters can be evident to all. He always wants to write so as to assert nothing likely to be controversial without stating the reasons that led him to it and that he thinks can convince others. The scholastic vocabulary of the body "informed" by the mind is used without endorsement, and the metaphysics of mind and body is explicitly postponed. The *Regulae* will proceed without a settled metaphysics.

§ 4. Since this is not possible now, it is enough to explain as briefly as possible the way of conceiving our cognitive equipment that is most useful for the present purpose. The reader need not believe that things are so. But what prevents him from following these suppositions, if they take nothing from the truth of things and only make everything much clearer? So in geometry one makes assumptions about quantity that in no way weaken the demonstrations, though in physics one often thinks otherwise about the nature of quantity. The account of the faculties that follows is offered as a hypothesis, justified by its usefulness. This conventionalist turn is unusual in Descartes and is limited to the theory of the knower, not to its results. The comparison with geometry shows the model: a representation chosen for its tractability, whose truth is not at issue.

§ 5. First, all the external senses, insofar as they are parts of the body, sense properly only by passivity, though we apply them to objects by an action, local motion. They receive impressions as wax receives the impression of a seal. This is not an analogy: the outer shape of the sensing body is really changed by the object exactly as the surface of the wax is changed by the seal. This holds not only when we touch a body as shaped, hard or rough, but also when we perceive heat or cold by touch. The same applies to the other senses: the first opaque membrane of the eye takes the shape impressed by light of various colours, and the first membranes of ears, nose and tongue take a new shape from sound, smell and taste. The insistence that the seal and wax is no mere analogy is pointed. The Aristotelian image of perception as receiving form without matter is taken literally and mechanised: what is received is a real change of figure in a corporeal surface. Sensation becomes a physical event.

§ 6. Conceiving things this way helps greatly, since nothing falls more readily under the senses than figure, which is both touched and seen. That nothing false follows from this supposition rather than another is shown by the fact that the concept of figure is so common and simple that it is involved in everything sensible. Suppose colour to be whatever you like; you will not deny that it is extended and therefore has figure. What harm follows if, taking care not to admit uselessly and imagine rashly some new entity, and not denying what others have thought about colour, we abstract from everything in colour except its having the nature of figure, and conceive the difference between white, blue, red and so on as like the differences between these figures? Descartes illustrates with diagrams of different hatchings. The same can be said of everything, since the infinite multitude of figures suffices to express all the differences of sensible things. The argument for the supposition is minimal and strong: whatever colour is, it is extended, so representing it by figure loses nothing about its differences. This is the step that makes physics possible as a science of order and measure. The qualitative is not denied but coded: an abstraction under which every sensible difference becomes a difference of figure, and hence comparable. It anticipates the reduction of secondary qualities in *The World* and the Sixth Meditation, but in a methodological rather than ontological key.

§ 7. Second, when an external sense is moved by an object, the figure it receives is carried instantaneously to another part of the body, the common sense, without any real entity passing from one to the other. In the same way, as Descartes writes, at the instant each letter is traced on the paper, not only does the lower end of the pen move but the slightest motion there is transmitted to the whole pen, and all these differences of motion are traced in the air by the upper end, though nothing real passes from one end to the other. Who would think the parts of the human body less connected than those of a pen? The pen, like the staff of Rule Nine, § 5, models transmission without transport. It eliminates the "intentional species" of scholastic theory, the entities supposed to travel from object to sense and from sense to soul. Communication of figure is reduced to the propagation of motion through a continuous body.

§ 8. Third, the common sense also acts like a seal, forming in the imagination (*phantasia*) or fancy, as in wax, the same figures or ideas that come from the external senses pure and without body. This imagination is a real part of the body, large enough that its various parts can take many distinct figures and usually keep them for some time, in which case it is called memory. Memory is here not a distinct faculty but the imagination's retention of figures; this explains why Rule Eight, § 5, could list only two auxiliaries to the intellect. "Idea" is used of the corporeal figure in the brain, a use the *Meditations* will abandon. The phrase "pure and without body" says that the figure is transmitted without any corporeal entity travelling, not that it is incorporeal in itself.

§ 9. Fourth, the motive force, or the nerves themselves, originate in the brain, where the imagination is located, and it moves them in various ways, as the external senses move the common sense or the lower end of the pen moves the whole pen. The example also shows how the imagination can cause many motions in the nerves without containing their express images, but only other images from which they can follow: the whole pen does not move as its lower end does; its upper end seems to make a quite different and contrary movement. From this one can understand how all the movements of other animals can occur, even if they are granted no knowledge of things but only a purely corporeal imagination; and also how all the operations we perform without the help of reason take place in us. The doctrine of the animal-machine is stated here in its earliest form. Its key move is that cause and effect need not resemble each other: the figure in the brain need not picture the motion it produces. This principle of non-resemblance, later turned against the theory of sensation in the *Dioptrics* and *The World*, is first used here for the outward path from brain to muscle.

§ 10. Fifth, the power by which we properly know things is purely spiritual, no less distinct from the whole body than blood from bone or hand from eye. It is a single power, which either receives figures from the common sense together with the imagination, or applies itself to those kept in memory, or forms new ones that so occupy the imagination that it often cannot at the same time receive ideas from the common sense or pass them to the motive force as the purely corporeal disposition would. In all these functions this knowing power is sometimes passive, sometimes active, now resembling the seal, now the wax, though here only by analogy, since nothing quite like it is found in bodies. The analogy, rejected for the corporeal senses in § 5, is admitted for the mind precisely because the mind is not corporeal: its passivity and activity cannot be literal changes of figure. The examples of distinctness, blood from bone, hand from eye, are odd, since they distinguish parts of one body; they establish distinctness of function, not the real distinction of substances the *Meditations* will prove. The power is named by its functions. Applied with the imagination to the common sense, it is said to see, touch and so on; applied to the imagination alone as stocked with figures, to remember; applied to the imagination to form new figures, to imagine or conceive; acting alone, to understand, an operation Descartes promises to explain in its place. Accordingly the same power is called pure intellect, imagination, memory or sense. It is called *ingenium* in the proper sense when it forms new ideas in the imagination or attends to those already formed. Here Heffernan's "natural intelligence" misleads. The definition makes *ingenium* the knowing power working in and through the corporeal imagination, the one point where mind and brain cooperate; nothing in it concerns what is natural as opposed to acquired. The treatise's title thus names the direction of the mind precisely in its imaginative, embodied exercise, which is why Rules Fourteen to Sixteen will train the intellect through figures and notation. Once the reader has conceived all this, he will easily see what help can be sought from each faculty and how far human industry can make up for the intelligence's defects. The unity of the knowing power is what grounds the unity of the sciences asserted in Rule One.

§ 11. The intellect can be moved by the imagination or act on it; the imagination can act on the senses through the motive force, applying them to objects, or the senses can act on the imagination by depicting images of bodies on it. Memory, at least the corporeal memory like that of animals, is nothing distinct from imagination. Hence it follows with certainty that when the intellect deals with things in which there is nothing corporeal or like the corporeal, these faculties cannot help it; to keep them from hindering it, the senses must be held back and the imagination stripped as far as possible of every distinct impression. But if the intellect sets out to examine something that can be referred to body, its idea must be formed as distinctly as possible in the imagination, and to do this more easily the thing itself must be presented to the external senses. Several things do not help the intellect to intuit single things distinctly; but to deduce one thing from many gathered together, as must often be done, one must discard from the ideas of things whatever does not require present attention, so that the rest may be more easily retained in memory. In the same way, not the things themselves but certain abbreviated figures of them should be presented to the external senses, and the more compact these are, the more useful, provided they suffice to guard against lapses of memory. Whoever observes all this will have omitted nothing concerning this part. The rule of use is a rule of matching faculty to object. Purely intellectual objects demand the suspension of imagination, corporeal objects its full employment; the doctrine anticipates the opening of the Sixth Meditation, where pure understanding and imagination are contrasted on the chiliagon. The qualification "corporeal memory" leaves room for an intellectual memory. The last sentences announce the program of Rules Fourteen to Sixteen: the abstraction of what is irrelevant, the representation of objects by figures, and the replacement of figures by abbreviated notation that relieves memory.

§ 12. Turning to the second point, the things to be known, the aim is to distinguish accurately the notions of simple things from those composed of them, to see in each case where falsity can lie so as to avoid it, and which things can be known with certainty so as to deal with these alone. Again certain assumptions must be made that not everyone will accept. But even if they are thought no more real than the imaginary circles by which astronomers describe their phenomena, it hardly matters, provided they help us to tell which kind of knowledge of a thing can be true or false. The hypothetical status claimed for the theory of faculties in § 4 is extended to the theory of objects. The comparison with astronomical circles places the doctrine of simple natures in the tradition of "saving the phenomena": what counts is its power to sort true from false knowledge, not its claim about how things are.

§ 13. First, single things must be considered differently in the order of our knowledge and as they really exist. An extended, shaped body is, on the side of the thing, one and simple, since it cannot be called a compound of the natures of body, extension and figure: these parts never existed apart. But with respect to our intellect we call it composite, made of these three natures, because we understood each separately before we could judge that the three are found together in one subject. Dealing with things only as perceived by the intellect, we call simple only those whose knowledge is so clear and distinct that the mind cannot divide them into others known more distinctly: figure, extension, motion and so on; everything else we conceive as composed of these. This holds generally, even of what we abstract from the simple natures themselves. When we say that figure is the limit of an extended thing, "limit" is more general than "figure," since we also speak of the limit of a duration or a motion. But "limit" is not simpler for being more general: since it is applied to things wholly different in kind, its meaning is abstracted from all of them, so it is composed of many quite different natures and applies to them only equivocally. The treatise's "grey ontology" rests on this principle: simplicity is relative to the order of knowing, not to the order of being. A thing that is one in reality is many for the intellect. The argument about "limit" overturns the scholastic assumption that the more general is the more simple; generality is a sign of composition, because a general term gathers natures of different kinds under an equivocal name. Cartesian simples are therefore not genera but the most determinate intelligibles.

§ 14. Second, things called simple with respect to our intellect are purely intellectual, purely material, or common to both. Purely intellectual are those known by an innate light without any corporeal image: knowledge, doubt, ignorance, the act of will that can be called volition, and the like. No corporeal idea can be imagined that would represent them, yet we know them, and so easily that it is enough to share in reason. Purely material are those known only in bodies: figure, extension, motion. Common are those attributed indiscriminately to bodies and spirits: existence, unity, duration and the like. Here belong the common notions, which are like links connecting other simple natures and on whose evidence all reasoned conclusions rest, such as "things that are the same as a third are the same as each other," and "things that cannot be referred in the same way to the same third differ in some respect." These common notions can be known by the pure intellect or by the intellect intuiting images of material things. The tripartite classification anticipates the *Principles* (I, 48), with one difference: the intellectual simples are acts of mind, so that the mind's knowledge of its own operations is as immediate as its knowledge of extension. The common notions are classed as simple natures; the axioms that underwrite inference are themselves objects of intuition, not rules of form, which completes the critique of dialectic in Rule Ten.

§ 15. It is useful to count among simple natures their privations and negations, insofar as we understand them. The knowledge by which I intuit what nothing is, or an instant, or rest, is no less genuine than that by which I understand existence, duration or motion. This lets us say that everything else we know is composed of simple natures: if I judge that a figure is not moving, my thought is in some way composed of figure and rest. Negations are admitted not as beings but as objects of understanding, which confirms that the series is cognitive. Nothing, too, is an intelligible.

§ 16. Third, the simple natures are all known by themselves and contain no falsity. This is shown by distinguishing the faculty by which the intellect intuits and knows things from the faculty by which it judges, affirming or denying. We can think we do not know what we do know, if we suspect that something hidden lies in it beyond what we intuit or reach in thought, though we are wrong. So we err whenever we judge that we do not know one of the simple natures completely. If the mind attains anything of it at all, which it must if we judge about it, it follows that we know it completely; otherwise it would not be simple but composed of what we perceive in it and what we think we do not know. The argument is a dilemma built into the concept of the simple: what has no parts cannot be partly known. Error about simples is therefore not misperception but a false judgment of ignorance, imposed by the suspicion of a hidden essence. The distinction of intuiting and judging faculties is the germ of the Fourth Meditation's division of intellect and will, though both are still assigned to the intellect here.

§ 17. Fourth, the conjunction of simple things is either necessary or contingent. It is necessary when one is implied, however confusedly, in the concept of the other, so that we cannot conceive either distinctly if we judge them separate: figure is joined with extension, motion with duration or time, since no figure without extension or motion without duration can be conceived. If I say that 4 and 3 make 7, the composition is necessary, since we do not conceive 7 distinctly unless we include 3 and 4 in it confusedly. Whatever is demonstrated about figures or numbers is necessarily connected with its subject. This necessity holds in non-sensible things too: if Socrates says he doubts everything, it follows necessarily that he understands at least that he doubts, and therefore that he knows something can be true or false, since these are necessarily connected with the nature of doubt. Contingent is the union of things not joined by an inseparable relation, as when we say a body is animate or a man is clothed. Many things are necessarily joined though most people, not noticing their relation, count them contingent, as "I am, therefore God exists," or "I understand, therefore I have a mind distinct from the body." Finally, many necessary propositions become contingent when converted: from the fact that I exist I can conclude with certainty that God exists, but not from God's existence that I exist. Necessity is defined by inconceivability of separation, and the "confused" inclusion of one concept in the other means implicit containment, not obscurity. The examples of Socrates' doubt and of "I am, therefore God exists" are striking. The movement from doubt to knowledge of truth, and from my existence to God's, appear here as instances of necessary connection among simple natures, offered as examples, not as foundations. The *Regulae* already contain the materials of the *Meditations*' argument while giving them no foundational role. The remark on conversion fixes an asymmetry of dependence that the Third Meditation will develop: my existence implies God's, not the reverse.

§ 18. Fifth, we can never understand anything beyond these simple natures and a mixture or composition of them. It is often easier to notice several joined natures together than to separate one from the others. I can know a triangle without ever having thought that this knowledge includes knowledge of angle, line, the number three, figure, extension. This does not prevent us from saying that the nature of the triangle is composed of all these, and that they are better known than the triangle, since it is they that are understood in it. Perhaps many other natures are involved in the triangle that remain hidden, such as the sum of its angles equalling two right angles and the countless relations between sides and angles, or its area. All understanding is composition of simples, and the simples are better known than what is composed of them even when they are not noticed. The admission that a known nature may involve undiscovered properties distinguishes the composite from the simple, of which § 16 denied any hidden content. It also explains why mathematics can progress: demonstration unfolds what is involved in a composite.

§ 19. Sixth, the natures called composite are known either because we experience what they are or because we compose them ourselves. We experience whatever we perceive by the senses, whatever we hear from others, and in general whatever reaches our intellect either from outside or from its reflective contemplation of itself. The intellect can never be deceived by any experience if it intuits only the thing presented to it exactly as it has it, in itself or in an image, and does not judge that the imagination faithfully reports the objects of the senses, or that the senses take on the true figures of things, or that external things are always as they appear. In all these we are liable to error: when we believe a story told us is fact; when the jaundiced judge everything yellow because their eyes are tinged; when, through a disordered imagination, the melancholic take its confused images for real things. The wise man's intellect is not deceived: he judges that what he receives from the imagination is really depicted in it, but does not assert that it passed whole and unchanged from things to senses and from senses to imagination unless he knows this on other grounds. We compose what we understand whenever we believe there is something in it that our mind did not perceive immediately in any experience. The jaundiced man's thought that things are yellow is composite: made of what his imagination represents and of what he supplies himself, that the colour appears yellow not through a defect of his eyes but because the things are yellow. Hence we can be deceived only when we ourselves compose what we believe. Descartes's concept of experience is unusually wide: it includes testimony and the mind's reflection on itself, so that "experience" means whatever is given to the intellect as it is given. Experience as such is infallible; error arises only when we add to it a judgment about its source. The case of the jaundiced man separates the given (a yellow appearance) from the supplement (that the yellow belongs to the thing), anticipating the analysis of sensory error in the Sixth Meditation and, in Husserlian terms, the separation of the reduced appearance from the positing of its object.

§ 20. Seventh, this composition can come about in three ways: by impulse, by conjecture or by deduction. Those compose by impulse who are led by their intelligence to believe something without being persuaded by any reason, determined either by a higher power, or by their own freedom, or by a disposition of the imagination. The first never deceives, the second rarely, the third almost always; but the first has no place here, since it does not fall under art. Composition by conjecture is, for example, when from the fact that water, further from the centre of the earth than earth, is also finer in substance, and air, above water, is also rarer, we guess that above the air there is only a very pure ether, much finer than air. What we compose this way does not deceive us if we judge it only probable and never assert it as true, but it does not make us more learned. Impulse by a higher power is revelation, already set aside in Rule Three, § 9; impulse by freedom is the will's assent without reason, the source of error the Fourth Meditation will analyse. Conjecture is harmless only when it is held as conjecture; Rule Two's exclusion of the probable is relaxed to a prohibition on asserting it as true.

§ 21. Only deduction, then, remains as a way of composing things so that we can be certain of their truth. Even here there can be many defects, as when, perceiving nothing by sight, touch or any other sense in a space full of air, we conclude that it is empty, wrongly joining the nature of a vacuum with that of this space. This happens whenever we judge that something general and necessary can be deduced from something particular or contingent. But we can avoid this error by never joining things unless we intuit that their conjunction is wholly necessary, as when we deduce from the necessary connection of figure with extension that nothing unextended can have figure. Rule Two, § 4, held that inference cannot be performed wrongly; this paragraph shows that a "deduction" can still fail, but the failure lies in a composition presented as a deduction, a contingent link passed off as a necessary one. The test of a genuine deduction is the intuition of the necessity of each conjunction, which returns deduction to intuition.

§ 22. Several conclusions follow. First, what was shown at the beginning only confusedly and roughly has now been set out distinctly, and by a sufficient enumeration: there are no ways to certain knowledge open to human beings except evident intuition and necessary deduction; and the simple natures mentioned in the eighth proposition have been identified. Intuition extends to all the simple natures, to the necessary connections among them, and to everything else the intellect experiences precisely as it is, either in itself or in the imagination. More will be said about deduction later. The claim of Rule Three has been justified by the theory of composition: since every object of knowledge is a simple nature or a composite of them, and composition is certain only by necessary connection, there can be no third way. The scope of intuition is extended to whatever is experienced exactly as given, which joins the analysis of experience in § 19 to the doctrine of intuition.

§ 23. Second, no effort is needed to know the simple natures, since they are known well enough by themselves, but only to separate them and intuit each one separately with a steady gaze of the mind. No one is so dull as not to perceive that when he sits he is somehow different from when he stands, but not everyone distinguishes the nature of position as distinctly from everything else this thought contains, nor can everyone assert that only position has changed. This needs saying because the learned are often so clever that they find ways to blind themselves even to what is self-evident and every peasant knows. They do this when they try to explain what is known by itself through something more evident, and so explain something else or nothing. Who does not perceive whatever it is that changes when we change place? And who conceives the same thing when told that place is the surface of the surrounding body? That surface could change while I stay still, or move with me so that it still surrounds me while I am no longer in the same place. And do not those who say that motion, which everyone knows, is "the act of a being in potency insofar as it is in potency," seem to utter magic words beyond human understanding? Who understands them? Who does not know what motion is? Such things are never to be explained by definitions, lest we take composites for simples; only the simple natures, isolated from everything else, must be intuited attentively by each person by the light of his own intelligence. The labour of knowledge is analysis, not acquisition: the simples are always already known and only need to be isolated. The attack on Aristotle's definitions of place and motion is the clearest statement of the anti-definitional doctrine that will recur in the *Principles* (I, 10) and the *Search after Truth*: the simplest things are made obscure by logical definition, which can only compose them from less evident terms.

§ 24. Third, all human knowledge consists solely in seeing distinctly how these simple natures together make up other things. This is useful to note, since whenever a difficulty is proposed, almost everyone stops at the threshold, unsure which thoughts to apply, and sure they must seek some new kind of being unknown to them. Asked the nature of the magnet, they foresee something hard and obscure, turn away from everything evident, apply themselves to the most difficult things, and hope to stumble on something new by wandering through the empty space of many causes. But whoever thinks that nothing can be known in the magnet except what consists of certain self-evident simple natures is not unsure what to do. First he gathers all the observations he can about the stone; then he tries to deduce what mixture of simple natures is needed to produce all the effects he has found by experience; once he has found it, he can boldly say he has perceived the true nature of the magnet, so far as a human being can find it from the given observations. Cartesian physics is here in miniature. The new kind of being sought by the scholastics, an occult magnetic quality, is replaced by a combination of known simples; the method is to collect observations and find the mixture of simples that accounts for them. The qualification "so far as a human being can find it from the given observations" makes the result relative to the data, a hypothetico-deductive element that Book Three would have treated.

§ 25. Fourth, no kind of knowledge of things should be thought more obscure than another, since all knowledge is of the same nature and consists solely in the composition of self-evident things. Hardly anyone notices this. The bolder, assuming the opposite, allow themselves to assert their conjectures as true demonstrations, claim to see as through a fog truths often obscure in matters they do not know at all, and propose them wrapped in certain terms by which they discuss many things coherently, though neither they nor their hearers understand them. The more modest often refrain from examining many easy and necessary matters because they think themselves unequal to them, and, believing others more gifted can grasp them, embrace the opinions of those whose authority they trust. The homogeneity of knowledge, already argued in Rule Nine, § 3, is now grounded in the theory of composition: since everything known is composed of simples known equally well, there is no essentially obscure knowledge. The two vices named, presumption and deference, are the two forms of dependence on opinion that Rule Three excluded.

§ 26. Eighth, we can deduce only things from words, cause from effect, effect from cause, like from like, or parts or the whole from parts... The text breaks off. What survives lists the types of inference that deduction can perform, corresponding to the composites of Book Three and to the "imperfectly understood questions" whose matter includes words and causes. The topic is taken up again in Rule Thirteen, §§ 4–6.

§ 27. So that no one may miss the connection of the precepts, everything knowable is divided into simple propositions and questions. For simple propositions the only precepts are those that prepare the knowing power to intuit objects more distinctly and examine them more sagaciously, since simple propositions must present themselves spontaneously and cannot be sought; the first twelve precepts have done this, and contain everything that can facilitate the use of reason. Questions are either perfectly understood, though their solution is unknown, which the next twelve rules will treat exclusively, or not perfectly understood, for which the last twelve rules are reserved. The division is deliberate, so as not to say things that presuppose what follows, and to present first what must be cultivated first to train the intelligence. Among perfectly understood questions are only those in which three things are distinctly perceived: by what signs we will recognise what we seek when it appears; what exactly we should deduce it from; and how it is to be proved that these depend on each other so that one cannot change without the other. We would then have all the premises, and it would remain only to show how to find the conclusion, not by deducing one thing from one simple thing, which needs no precepts, but by unfolding one thing that depends on many things involved together, and with such art that no greater capacity of mind is needed anywhere than for the simplest inference. Since such questions are mostly abstract and arise almost only in arithmetic and geometry, they will seem of little use to the inexperienced. But whoever wants to master the later part of the method, dealing with all other questions, must study and practise this art for a long time. The plan of thirty-six rules is announced: twelve on simple propositions, twelve on perfectly understood questions, twelve on imperfectly understood ones. Only the first two parts survive, the second unfinished. The three conditions for a perfectly understood question amount to a complete determination of the unknown by the data, a functional dependence in which a change in one entails a change in the other. This is the form of an equation, and Book Two will in fact develop the method as a general theory of equations, the core of *mathesis universalis*. The aim stated in the last clause is the method's ideal: to decompose any problem so that no step requires more mental capacity than the simplest inference.

## Book Two: On Perfectly Understood Questions

### Rule Thirteen: On the Direct Resolution of Questions

Rule Thirteen (AT X 430–438) states that if we understand a question perfectly, it must be abstracted from every superfluous conception, reduced to its simplest form, and divided by enumeration into the smallest possible parts. The rule opens the treatment of questions announced in Rule Twelve, § 27, and its three operations, abstraction, simplification and division, apply the method of Rules Five to Seven to the question as such.

§ 1. In one respect only do we imitate the dialecticians: just as they presuppose that the terms or matter of syllogisms are known in order to expound their forms, we presuppose that the question is perfectly understood. But we do not distinguish two extremes and a middle term. Instead we consider the whole thus. First, every question must contain something unknown, or it would be pointless. Second, the unknown must be designated somehow, or we would not be determined to seek it rather than something else. Third, it can only be designated by something known. These hold for imperfect questions too: in asking the nature of the magnet, we know what the words "magnet" and "nature" mean, and this determines the inquiry. For a question to be perfect, however, it must be determined in every respect, so that nothing more is sought than what can be deduced from the data. For example: what is to be inferred about the magnet precisely from the experiments Gilbert claims to have made, true or false? Or what would I judge about the nature of sound precisely from the fact that three strings A, B and C sound the same note, given that B is twice as thick as A but no longer and stretched by a double weight, and C is no thicker than A but twice as long and stretched by a quadruple weight? These examples show how imperfect questions can be reduced to perfect ones, a matter to be explained later. They also show how a well-understood difficulty can be abstracted from every superfluous conception and reduced to the point where we no longer think we are dealing with this or that subject, but only with magnitudes in general to be compared with one another. Once we have decided to consider only certain experiments about the magnet, nothing prevents us from turning our thought away from everything else. The syllogism's triad of terms is replaced by the structure of a problem: an unknown, determined by conditions, through data. A perfect question is one in which the data fully determine the unknown, which is to say an equation. The reduction of imperfect questions to perfect ones works by stipulation: one fixes the data, however uncertain in themselves, and asks only what follows from them. The question about the magnet ceases to be about the magnet and becomes a question about magnitudes. The example of the strings is precise: by the law that pitch depends on length, thickness and tension, the three strings sound alike, and the "nature of sound" is reduced to proportional relations among these magnitudes.

§ 2. The difficulty must also be reduced to its simplest form, according to Rules Five and Six, and divided, according to Rule Seven. Examining the magnet from many observations, I will run through them separately and in turn; examining sound, I will compare strings A and B, then A and C, and so on, so as to embrace all of them in a sufficient enumeration. These three things alone the pure intellect must observe regarding the terms of a proposition before undertaking its final solution, though the solution may require the following eleven rules; how this is done will be clearer from the third part of the treatise. By "questions" is meant everything in which truth or falsity is found, and their kinds must be enumerated to determine what we can achieve with each. The preparation of a question is an act of the pure intellect; only its solution will enlist the imagination. "Question" is defined broadly, by the presence of truth or falsity, and so includes every judgment.

§ 3. It was said earlier that there is no falsity in the mere apprehension of things, simple or composite; in that sense they are not called questions, but become questions as soon as we decide to make a definite judgment about them. And questions are not only those asked by others. Socrates' question was about his own ignorance, or rather his own doubt: having noticed that he doubted, he began to ask whether it was true that he doubted everything, and affirmed that he did. The definition ties the question to judgment, consistent with Rule Twelve, § 16. The example of Socrates repeats Rule Twelve, § 17, and frames reflection on one's own doubt as a question in the technical sense: the most intimate self-examination falls under the same method as a problem in geometry.

§ 4. We seek either things from words, or causes from effects, or effects from causes, or the whole from the parts, or parts from other parts, or several of these from others. The list resumes the broken-off § 26 of Rule Twelve and supplies the typology of questions announced in § 2.

§ 5. We seek things from words when the difficulty lies in the obscurity of language. This includes all riddles, such as the Sphinx's about the animal that is four-footed first, then two-footed, finally three-footed, or that of the fishermen on the shore who say they no longer have what they caught but have what they have not yet been able to catch. It also includes most of what the learned dispute, where the question is almost always about words. We should not think so poorly of able people as to suppose they misconceive things whenever they explain them in inapt terms. When they call place "the surface of the surrounding body," they conceive nothing false; they only misuse the word "place," which in common use means the simple, self-evident nature by which a thing is said to be here or there. This nature consists wholly in a relation between the thing said to be in a place and the parts of extended space. Some, seeing the word "place" reserved for the surrounding surface, have improperly called it the "intrinsic where." Questions about words are so frequent that if philosophers always agreed about the meanings of words, almost all their disputes would end. The judgment on the Aristotelian definition of place is milder than in Rule Twelve, § 23: there it obscured the simple nature, here it merely misnames it. Place as a relation of a thing to the parts of extended space anticipates the *Principles*' distinction of internal and external place (II, 10–15). The reduction of philosophical controversy to verbal confusion assigns a whole class of questions to the clarification of language.

§ 6. We seek causes from effects when we investigate whether something is, or what it is... The text breaks off. Questions of existence and essence are classed as inferences from effects to causes, a placement that matters for the later proofs of God from his effects.

§ 7. When a question is proposed, we often do not see at once what kind it is, whether things are sought from words or causes from effects. So it seems useless to say more about these kinds in particular; it is more convenient to go through in order everything to be done in general to solve any difficulty. Whatever the question, we must first strive to understand distinctly what is sought. The typology is abandoned in favour of a general procedure, since the type of a question is often discovered only in solving it.

§ 8. Some are in such a hurry to investigate that they apply a vague mind to the solution before noticing by what signs they will recognise what they seek if it turns up. They are no less foolish than a servant sent on an errand who hurries off so eagerly that he leaves without instructions and without knowing where he is to go. The first of the three conditions of Rule Twelve, § 27, the signs by which the solution will be recognised, must be settled before inquiry starts.

§ 9. Every question must contain something unknown, but this must be marked out by definite conditions so that we are wholly determined to seek one thing rather than another. These conditions must be examined from the start, by fixing the mind's gaze on each in turn and asking how far each limits the unknown we seek. The mind tends to err here in two ways: by assuming more than is given to determine the question, or by leaving something out. Determinacy is the defining property of a perfect question, and its two failures are the subject of the next two paragraphs.

§ 10. We must not suppose more and stricter data than are given. This holds especially for riddles and other questions artfully devised to trap the mind, but sometimes for others too, as when we presuppose something as certain not on any certain ground but from an old opinion. In the Sphinx's riddle, "foot" must not be taken only for an animal's real feet; we must see whether it can apply to other things, as here to an infant's hands and an old man's stick, used like feet for getting around. In the fishermen's riddle, the thought of fish must not occupy the mind so as to distract it from the creatures the poor carry about on them unwillingly and throw away when caught. So too with the vessel Descartes once saw, with a column in the middle topped by a figure of Tantalus as if wanting to drink: water poured in stayed in as long as it was not high enough to reach Tantalus' mouth, but as soon as it touched the wretch's lips, all of it ran out. The artifice seems at first to lie in the figure of Tantalus, but the figure does not determine the question, it only accompanies it. The whole difficulty is how to construct a vessel from which all the water runs out once it reaches a certain height, and not before. Finally, if we ask what can be asserted with certainty about the motions of the stars from all our observations, we must not assume without reason, as the ancients did, that the earth is motionless at the centre of things because it has seemed so to us since childhood. This must itself be called into doubt so that we can examine what can be judged about it with certainty. The excess of data is always a prejudice: the habitual meaning of a word, a vivid but irrelevant feature, an opinion acquired in childhood. The last example is a cautious Copernicanism. Geocentrism is not denied but suspended, as a supposition the observations do not license. The phrase "called into doubt" marks an early methodical use of doubt, applied locally to a datum, not globally to all opinion as in the *Meditations*.

§ 11. We err by omission when we fail to consider some condition needed to determine the question, either expressed in it or to be understood. Someone seeking perpetual motion, not natural perpetual motion like that of the stars or springs but one produced by human industry, might believe, as some have, that since the earth turns perpetually on its axis and the magnet has all the earth's properties, he would obtain perpetual motion by setting up the stone to turn in a circle or at least to pass its motion and other powers to iron. Even if this happened, he would not be producing perpetual motion by art, but only using a natural motion, no differently than if he placed a wheel in a river's current so that it always turned. He would be omitting a condition required to determine the question. The omitted condition is implicit in the question's own terms ("by human industry"), which is why it is overlooked. The magnet, taken from Gilbert's identification of the earth with a great magnet, recurs as the treatise's standard example of a physical question.

§ 12. Once the question is sufficiently understood, we must see exactly where its difficulty lies, so that it can be more easily solved when abstracted from everything else. Understanding a question and locating its difficulty are distinct steps.

§ 13. To recognise where the difficulty lies, it is not always enough to understand the question; we must also consider the particular things it requires, so that we can leave aside any that are easy to find, and once these are removed from the proposition only what we do not know remains. In the vessel, once we discard as irrelevant the column in the middle, the painted bird and so on, there remains the bare difficulty: all the water in the vessel runs out once it reaches a certain height, and we must ask why. Locating the difficulty is subtraction: what is easily known is removed so that only the unknown remains. The vessel's mechanism, a concealed siphon, is not given; the point is the isolation of the problem, not its solution.

§ 14. The only thing worth doing, then, is to go through in order everything given in the proposition, rejecting what plainly does not bear on the matter, retaining what is necessary and setting aside what is doubtful for further examination. The threefold sorting of data, rejected, retained and deferred, is the enumeration of Rule Seven applied to a question's conditions.

### Rule Fourteen: On Representation and the Imagination

Rule Fourteen (AT X 438–452) states that the same question must be transferred to the real extension of bodies and represented wholly to the imagination by bare figures, since the intellect will thus perceive it far more distinctly. The rule realises the program of Rule Twelve, § 11: once a question has been reduced to magnitudes, the imagination is enlisted by representing them as extension.

§ 1. To use the imagination as an aid, note that whenever something unknown is deduced from something known, no new kind of being is discovered; the whole knowledge is extended only so far that we perceive that what is sought shares in one way or another in the nature of what is given in the proposition. A man born blind cannot be brought by argument to perceive the true ideas of colours as we have them from the senses. But someone who has seen the primary colours, though never the intermediate and mixed ones, can form images of these too by a kind of deduction, through their likeness to those he has seen. Likewise, if there is in the magnet some kind of being of which our intellect has never perceived the like, we cannot hope to know it by reasoning; we would need a new sense or a divine mind. But if we perceive very distinctly the mixture of known beings or natures that produces the effects appearing in the magnet, we will believe we have attained whatever human intelligence can attain in this matter. A marginal note in the Hanover copy adds that the example of colours is not absolutely true, but Descartes had found no better one to explain what is true. Deduction cannot generate new simple natures; it can only show how known natures combine. The intellect is therefore closed to what has no likeness to what it already knows, which is the strict corollary of Rule Twelve, § 18. The admission about the colour example points to the difficulty in treating sensory qualities as composites: one can arguably derive mixed colours from primary ones, but the claim that knowledge of qualities can be extended by deduction remains doubtful, as Descartes concedes.

§ 2. All these previously known beings, extension, figure, motion and the like, are recognised by the same idea in different subjects. We do not imagine the figure of a crown differently if it is silver than if it is gold. This common idea passes from one subject to another only by simple comparison, through which we affirm that what is sought is in this or that respect like, identical with or equal to something given. In all reasoning, only by comparison do we know truth precisely. Take "all A is B, all B is C, therefore all A is C": here the sought and the given, A and C, are compared in that each is B. But since the forms of syllogism are of no help in perceiving the truth of things, the reader should reject them altogether and understand that all knowledge, except that obtained by the simple and pure intuition of a single thing, is obtained by comparing two or more things. Almost the whole of human reason's industry consists in preparing this operation; when it is open and simple, we need no art but only the light of nature to intuit the truth obtained through it. Comparison is identified as the basic act of all non-intuitive knowledge, and the syllogism is re-read as a comparison of two terms through a third. The common idea, the same in silver and gold, is what makes comparison possible: identical natures in different subjects. This turns the whole of mediated knowledge into the measurement of one thing against another.

§ 3. Comparisons are called simple and open only when the sought and the given share equally in some nature. All others need preparation only because the common nature is not equally in both but is involved in other relations or proportions. The chief part of human industry consists in reducing these proportions until a clear equality appears between what is sought and something known. The task of method is defined as the reduction of proportion to equality. This is the precise sense in which *mathesis universalis* is a science of order and measure: every question becomes an equation.

§ 4. Nothing can be reduced to such an equality except what admits of more and less, all of which is covered by the word "magnitude." So once the terms of a difficulty have been abstracted from every subject according to the previous rule, we must understand that we are dealing only with magnitudes in general. Magnitude is defined by comparability in degree, not by a kind of object, so that whatever admits of more and less falls within *mathesis universalis*.

§ 5. To imagine something even then, and to use not the pure intellect but the intellect aided by images painted in the imagination, note finally that nothing can be said of magnitudes in general that cannot also be attributed to any particular species of magnitude. The inference from genus to species licenses the transfer that follows: whatever holds of magnitude in general can be studied in whichever species is easiest to represent.

§ 6. It follows that it will be very useful to transfer what we understand to be said of magnitudes in general to the species of magnitude that is most easily and distinctly pictured in our imagination. That this is the real extension of body, abstracted from everything but its having figure, follows from Rule Twelve, where the imagination together with its ideas was conceived as nothing but a real body, really extended and having figure. It is also evident in itself, since in no other subject are all the differences of proportions displayed more distinctly. One thing may be called more or less white than another, one sound more or less high, and so on, but we cannot determine exactly whether the greater exceeds the lesser in the ratio of two to one or three to one except by a certain analogy with the extension of a body having figure. It therefore stands fixed that perfectly determinate questions contain hardly any difficulty beyond that of developing proportions into equalities, and that everything in which this difficulty is found can and should be separated from every other subject and then expressed in terms of extension and figures. These alone will be treated until the twenty-fifth rule. The argument for the privilege of extension is double. Physiologically, the imagination is itself an extended body, so extension is its native medium. Epistemically, only in extension are ratios exactly determinable; intensities of colour or pitch can be ordered but not measured except by analogy with extension. This is the foundation of Descartes's analytic geometry: algebraic relations of any kind are represented by lines. The reference to Rule Twenty-five confirms the plan of twelve rules for Book Two.

§ 7. Descartes would like a reader well disposed toward arithmetic and geometry, though he would prefer one who has never studied them to one taught in the usual way. The rules expounded here are much easier to apply in these sciences, for which they wholly suffice, than in any other kind of question. Their use in pursuing a deeper wisdom is so great that he does not hesitate to say that this part of the method was not invented for mathematical problems; rather, mathematical problems should be studied almost solely for the sake of cultivating the method. He will presuppose nothing from these disciplines except some things self-evident and open to all. But the knowledge others usually have of these, even when free of open errors, is obscured by many crooked and badly conceived principles, which he will try to correct. The relation of mathematics to method is stated decisively: mathematics is the training ground of method, not its end. This repeats the shell-and-kernel image of Rule Four, § 4, and keeps method, mathematics and universal wisdom distinct.

§ 8. By extension is meant whatever has length, breadth and depth, without asking whether it is a real body or only a space; the notion needs no further explanation, since nothing is more easily perceived by the imagination. But since the learned use such subtle distinctions that they dissipate the natural light and find obscurities even in what peasants never fail to know, they must be told that extension here does not mean something distinct and separate from its subject, and that in general we recognise no philosophical entities that do not really fall under the imagination. Someone may persuade himself that it is not contradictory for extension to exist by itself if everything extended in nature were annihilated, but for this conception he would be using not a corporeal idea but the intellect alone, judging wrongly. He will admit this if he reflects attentively on the very image of extension he tries to form in his imagination: he will find that he does not perceive it apart from every subject, but imagines it quite differently from how he judges. Whatever the intellect may believe about the truth of the matter, these abstract entities are never formed in the imagination apart from subjects. The indifference between body and space anticipates the identification of body with extension in the *Principles* (II, 10–12). The criterion of reality offered here is the imagination: what cannot be imagined, such as extension without a subject, is a judgment of the intellect, not a representation. The phrase "whatever the intellect may believe" leaves the intellect free to entertain abstractions, but excludes them from the imaginative representation on which the solution of questions now depends.

§ 9. Since nothing will now be done without the imagination's help, the ideas by which the particular meanings of words are presented to the intellect must be distinguished carefully. Three expressions are proposed for consideration: "extension occupies place," "body has extension," and "extension is not body." The analysis of the three statements will separate two uses of abstract terms, one representable and one not.

§ 10. The first shows how "extension" is taken for "what is extended." Saying "extension occupies place" I conceive exactly what I conceive saying "what is extended occupies place." It is not better to say "what is extended" to avoid ambiguity, since that would not express so distinctly what we conceive, namely that a subject occupies place because it is extended. Someone might take it to mean only that what is extended is a subject occupying place, as if I said "what is animate occupies place." That is why Descartes speaks of extension rather than the extended, though he thinks extension is not conceived otherwise than the extended. The abstract noun is preferred because it names the respect under which the subject is considered, the reason for its occupying place, and not merely the subject that happens to have it.

§ 11. In the second, "body has extension," we understand "extension" to mean something other than "body," yet we form in the imagination not two ideas, one of body and one of extension, but a single idea of extended body. As far as the thing is concerned, it is no different from "body is extended," or rather "what is extended is extended." This is peculiar to beings that exist only in another and can never be conceived without a subject. It is otherwise with beings really distinguishable from their subjects: in "Peter has wealth" the idea of Peter is wholly different from that of wealth, and in "Paul is rich" I imagine something quite different from "the rich man is rich." Many, not noticing the difference, falsely think extension contains something distinct from what is extended, as Paul's wealth is distinct from Paul. The distinction between inseparable attributes and really distinct accidents anticipates the *Principles*' theory of modal and real distinction (I, 60–61). Its target is the scholastic real accident: extension is not a thing added to body.

§ 12. Finally, in "extension is not body," "extension" is taken quite differently from before, and in this sense no particular idea corresponds to it in the imagination. The whole statement is made by the pure intellect, which alone can separate such abstract entities. Many go wrong here: not seeing that extension so taken cannot be grasped by the imagination, they represent it by a real idea, and since that idea necessarily involves the concept of body, in saying that extension so conceived is not body they unwittingly say that the same thing is at once body and not body. It is very important to distinguish statements in which words like extension, figure, number, surface, line, point and unity have so strict a meaning that they exclude something from which they are not really distinct: "extension or figure is not body," "number is not the thing numbered," "a surface is the limit of a body," "a line is the limit of a surface," "a point is the limit of a line," "unity is not a quantity." All such propositions must be removed wholly from the imagination if they are to be true, and so will not concern us. The contradiction arises from a category mistake between faculties: a proposition true for the pure intellect becomes contradictory when the imagination supplies an image for its abstract term. The definitions of surface, line and point as limits are true only as intellectual abstractions, not as images, which is why the geometry of the following rules must avoid them.

§ 13. In all other propositions where these terms, though keeping the same meaning and used in abstraction from subjects, exclude or deny nothing from which they are not really distinct, we can and should use the imagination. Though the intellect attends only to what the word signifies, the imagination should form a real idea of the thing, so that the intellect can be turned to the other conditions not expressed by the word if needed, and never judge rashly that they have been excluded. If the question is about number, we should imagine some subject measurable by many units; the intellect may reflect for the moment only on the plurality, but we will see that it does not later conclude something implying that the thing numbered has been excluded from our conception. That is what those do who attribute marvellous and mysterious properties to numbers, sheer trifles they would not believe so firmly unless they conceived number as distinct from what is numbered. Likewise, with a figure we should think we are dealing with an extended subject conceived only as having figure; with a body, the same as having length, breadth and depth; with a surface, the same as having length and breadth, omitting depth but not denying it; with a line, the same as having length only; with a point, the same with everything omitted except that it is a being. The distinction between omitting and denying is the key. Abstraction in the imagination is selective attention, not separation: the surface is the body considered without its depth, not something without depth. The imagination keeps the abstraction honest by carrying the omitted features along. The attack on number mysticism targets the Pythagorean and Rosicrucian speculations Descartes encountered in his early years.

§ 14. Though he sets all this out in detail, the minds of mortals are so prejudiced that he fears few will be safe from going astray here, and most will find his discourse too long and its explanation too brief. Arithmetic and geometry themselves, though the most certain of arts, mislead us here. What arithmetician does not think his numbers are not only abstracted from every subject by the intellect but are really distinct from it in the imagination? What geometer does not confuse the evidence of his object with contradictory principles, judging that lines lack breadth and surfaces depth, and yet composing one from the other, without noticing that the line whose flow he conceives as generating a surface is a real body, while the line without breadth is only a mode of body? Rather than dwell on this, it is quicker to explain how the object must be conceived to demonstrate as easily as possible whatever truth is in arithmetic and geometry. The geometer's contradiction is precise: the line defined as breadthless and the line that sweeps out a surface are not the same line. One is an intellectual abstraction, the other an imagined body. The "crooked principles" of § 7 are here identified.

§ 15. We are dealing, then, with an extended object, considering nothing in it but extension, and deliberately avoiding the word "quantity," since some philosophers are so subtle as to distinguish quantity from extension. We suppose that all questions have been reduced to the point where only a certain extension is sought, to be known by comparison with another known extension. Since we expect no knowledge of a new being here, but only want to reduce proportions, however involved, to the point where the unknown is found equal to something known, it is certain that all the differences of proportion found in other subjects can also be found between two or more extensions. It suffices to consider in extension whatever can help express differences of proportion. There are only three: dimension, unity and figure. The avoidance of "quantity" refuses the scholastic category that would place extension among accidents of a substance. The three features listed are the components of a representational system: dimension supplies the respects of measurement, unity the common measure, figure the medium of display.

§ 16. By dimension is meant nothing but the mode and respect in which a subject is considered measurable. Length, breadth and depth are not the only dimensions of a body; weight is the dimension by which subjects are weighed, speed the dimension of motion, and there are infinitely many others. Division into several equal parts, whether real or only intellectual, is properly the dimension by which we count things, and the mode that makes number is properly a kind of dimension, though the words differ in meaning. If we consider the order of the parts to the whole, we are said to count; if we consider the whole as divided into parts, we measure. We measure centuries by years, days, hours and minutes; counting minutes, hours, days and years, we arrive at centuries. "Dimension" is generalised beyond space to any measurable respect, which makes weight and speed as geometrically representable as length. Counting and measuring are the same dimension traversed in opposite directions, from parts to whole and from whole to parts. This unifies arithmetic and geometry under one concept.

§ 17. It follows that there can be infinitely many dimensions in the same subject, that they add nothing to the things that have them, and that they are understood in the same way whether they have a real basis in the subjects or are devised by the mind's choice. The weight of a body is real, as is the speed of a motion or the division of a century into years and days; the division of the day into hours and minutes is not. Yet all function alike when considered only as dimensions, as must be done here and in mathematics; whether they have a real basis is for physicists to examine. Mathematics is neutral about the ontological status of its dimensions. This neutrality is the *Regulae*'s characteristic suspension of the question of being, now applied to measurement: a dimension is a respect of consideration whose foundation in the thing is a separate, physical question.

§ 18. This throws much light on geometry, since almost everyone wrongly conceives three species of quantity there: line, surface and solid. It has already been noted that line and surface are not conceived as really distinct from the solid or from each other. Considered simply as abstracted by the intellect, they are no more different species of quantity than "animal" and "living" in man are different species of substance. Incidentally, the three dimensions of body, length, breadth and depth, differ only in name, since nothing prevents taking any extension in a given solid as length, another as breadth, and so on. Though these three have a real basis in every extended thing simply as extended, we are no more concerned with them than with infinitely many others either devised by the intellect or with other foundations in things. To measure a triangle exactly, we must know three things about it: its three sides, or two sides and an angle, or two angles and the area, and so on; a trapezium requires five; a tetrahedron six. All these may be called dimensions. But to choose those that best help the imagination, we should never attend to more than one or two at once as pictured in the imagination, though we understand that the proposition contains indefinitely many others. It is a matter of art to distinguish as many dimensions as possible in such a way that we attend to very few at once and to all in succession. The geometric species of quantity collapse into one extension considered under different numbers of dimensions, and "dimension" now means any independent determination, so that a triangle has three. This is the modern notion of degrees of freedom. The rule of attending to at most two dimensions at once follows from the limits of the imagination and dictates the use of plane rectangles in § 24.

§ 19. Unity is the common nature in which, as said above, all things compared must share equally. If no unit is fixed in the question, we may take as unit one of the given magnitudes or any other, and it will be the common measure of all the rest. We will understand it to have as many dimensions as the extremes to be compared, and conceive it either simply as something extended, abstracted from everything else, in which case it will be the same as the geometers' point, whose flow generates a line, or as a line, or as a square. The unit is arbitrary: its role is to serve as the common measure that makes comparison possible, and its choice is free. This is the innovation that lets the *Geometry* of 1637 treat products of lines as lines rather than areas, by relating them to a chosen unit segment.

§ 20. As for figures, it has been shown how the ideas of all things can be formed by them alone. It remains to say that of the countless kinds of figure we will use only those that most readily express all differences of relations or proportions. There are only two kinds of things compared, multitudes and magnitudes, and two kinds of figures to display them to our conception. Points representing a triangular number, or a family tree setting out someone's genealogy, display multitudes; continuous and undivided figures like a triangle or a square illustrate magnitudes. The distinction between discrete and continuous quantity, arithmetic's and geometry's objects, is preserved at the level of figure and then subordinated to a single representational system.

§ 21. To explain which of all figures will be used here, note that all relations that can hold between beings of the same kind fall under two headings: order or measure. The pair of Rule Four, § 5, returns as the exhaustive division of relations.

§ 22. Much ingenuity is needed to devise an order, as this whole method shows, since it teaches almost nothing else. But there is no difficulty in recognising an order once found: by Rule Seven we can easily run through the ordered parts in mind, since in relations of this kind the parts are related to each other by themselves alone, not through a third term as in measures, whose development alone is treated here. I recognise the order of A and B without considering anything but the two extremes, but I do not know the ratio of magnitude between 2 and 3 without considering a third, the unit, which is their common measure. Order is a two-term relation, measure a three-term relation mediated by a unit. Order is primary and immediately intuitable; measure requires mediation and is therefore where difficulty lies. This is why the rules on perfect questions concentrate on measure.

§ 23. Sometimes, with the help of an assumed unit, continuous magnitudes can be wholly reduced to a multitude, and always at least in part. The multitude of units can then be arranged in such an order that the difficulty of knowing a measure finally depends only on inspecting an order, and the greatest help of the art lies in this progression. The goal is to reduce measure to order: by counting units, a ratio of magnitudes becomes a sequence that can be surveyed. This progression completes the subordination of measure to order announced in Rule Four.

§ 24. Finally, no dimensions of continuous magnitude are conceived more distinctly than length and breadth, and we should not attend to more than these two at once in the same figure in order to compare two different things. If more than two different things are to be compared, it is a matter of art to survey them in turn and attend to no more than two at once. The plane rectangle is the most complex figure the imagination can survey distinctly; any problem with more terms must be decomposed into successive comparisons of pairs.

§ 25. It follows that propositions here are to be abstracted from the figures the geometers treat, if the question concerns these, no less than from any other matter; and that no figures are to be retained for this purpose but rectilinear and rectangular surfaces, or straight lines, which are also called figures, since by them, no less than by surfaces, we imagine a really extended subject. By these same figures, continuous magnitudes, multitudes and number must be displayed. Human industry can find nothing simpler for expressing all differences of relations. Geometry's own figures are treated as one more subject matter to be abstracted from: circles, conics and polygons are replaced by straight lines and rectangles, the universal medium of representation. Geometry becomes an application of *mathesis universalis*, not its foundation.

### Rule Fifteen: On Representation and the Senses

Rule Fifteen (AT X 453–454) states that it is usually also useful to draw these figures and show them to the external senses, so that our thought may more easily be kept attentive. The rule extends the chain of auxiliaries from imagination to sense: what is imagined is made visible, so that the eye supports the imagination as the imagination supports the intellect.

§ 1. How these figures are to be drawn so that their images are formed more distinctly in the imagination when they are placed before the eyes is evident. The unit will be drawn in three ways: as a square, if we consider it as having length and breadth; as a line, if only length; as a point, if we consider only that it helps make up a multitude. However drawn and conceived, the unit is always understood as a subject extended in every way and capable of infinite dimensions. Likewise, if two different magnitudes among the terms of a proposition must be attended to at once, they will be displayed as a rectangle whose two sides are the two magnitudes: drawn as continuous sides if they are incommensurable with the unit, or divided into units if commensurable, and nothing more is needed unless the question concerns a multitude of units. If only one magnitude is considered, it is drawn either as a rectangle one side of which is that magnitude and the other the unit, when the line is to be compared with some surface, or as a length alone, continuous if incommensurable, or as a row of points if it is a multitude. The notation for the unit varies with the dimensions under consideration, but its referent is always the full extended subject; the drawing displays only the respect attended to, as § 13 of Rule Fourteen required. The rectangle with one side equal to the unit is the device that lets a line be compared with a surface, by treating every line as a rectangle of unit breadth, which Rule Eighteen, § 10, will use to convert products back into lines.

### Rule Sixteen: On Representation and the Memory

Rule Sixteen (AT X 454–459) states that what is necessary to the conclusion but does not need the mind's present attention is better designated by very brief notations than by complete figures. In this way memory cannot err, and thought is not distracted by having to retain these things while engaged in deducing others. After intellect, imagination and sense, the last of the four faculties of Rule Twelve, memory, is relieved by an external aid: writing.

§ 1. Since no more than two of the countless dimensions that can be pictured in the imagination are to be considered in one intuition, whether of the eyes or of the mind, all the others must be kept so that they come to mind readily when needed. Nature seems to have made memory for this; but since memory is often unreliable, and so that we need not spend part of our attention refreshing it while occupied with other thoughts, art has most aptly invented writing. Relying on it, we will entrust nothing to memory but, leaving the imagination wholly free for the ideas present to it, will set down on paper whatever must be kept. We will do so by very brief notations, so that after examining each item distinctly by Rule Nine, we can run through them all by Rule Eleven with the swiftest movement of thought and intuit as many as possible at once. The weakness of memory, a constant theme since Rule Three, § 8, receives its final remedy: an external, written store. Writing frees the imagination to hold only what is present, and the swift survey of Rule Eleven now runs over marks on paper. Cognition is distributed over mind, imagination and page.

§ 2. Whatever is to be considered as one thing for solving the difficulty will be designated by a single notation, which may be formed as one likes. For convenience, letters a, b, c and so on will express known magnitudes, and A, B, C unknown ones, often prefixed by numerals 2, 3, 4 to show how many there are, and followed by numerals indicating the number of relations they contain. Thus "2a³" means twice the magnitude designated by a, which contains three relations. This device saves many words and, more important, displays the terms of the difficulty so purely and barely that nothing useful is omitted and nothing superfluous is found that might needlessly occupy the capacity of the mind when it must embrace many things at once. This is an early form of Descartes's algebraic notation; in the *Geometry* the conventions change, with early letters for knowns and late letters (x, y, z) for unknowns. The exponent is defined not as a power but as a count of relations, which ties it to the series of continued proportionals. The value of notation is cognitive economy: a sign exhibits exactly the structure needed and nothing else.

§ 3. Arithmeticians usually designate magnitudes by several units or a number, but we abstract here from numbers themselves as much as we did from geometrical figures or anything else. We do this to avoid the tedium of long and superfluous calculation and, above all, so that the parts of the subject relevant to the nature of the difficulty always remain distinct and are not buried in useless numbers. If the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides 9 and 12 is sought, the arithmetician will say it is √225 or 15; we will write a and b for 9 and 12, and find the hypotenuse √(a² + b²), so that the two parts a² and b², which are merged in the number, remain distinct. A number is a result that conceals its derivation; a literal expression preserves the structure of the dependence. The argument anticipates the passage from arithmetic to general algebra later credited to Viète and Descartes.

§ 4. By the number of relations is meant the proportions following one another in continuous series, which others in common algebra try to express by various dimensions and figures, calling the first the root, the second the square, the third the cube, the fourth the square of the square, and so on. Descartes confesses he was long misled by these terms, since nothing seemed more clearly presented to the imagination, after the line and the square, than the cube and the other figures imagined after their likeness. He solved many difficulties with their help, but after much experience found that he had discovered nothing by this way of conceiving that he could not have known much more easily and distinctly without it, and that such terms must be wholly abandoned so as not to confuse the conception. A magnitude, even if called a cube or a square of a square, must never be presented to the imagination except as a line or a surface, according to the previous rule. Root, square, cube and so on are nothing but magnitudes in continued proportion, always supposed to be preceded by the assumed unit. The first proportional is related to the unit immediately and by one relation; the second by way of the first, and so by two relations; the third by way of the first and second, by three; and so on. Henceforth what algebra calls the root will be called the first proportional, the square the second proportional, and so on. This is the conceptual break that makes analytic geometry possible. The cossic terms tied powers to geometric dimensions, so that a fourth power had no figure and higher equations seemed unimaginable. Reinterpreting every power as a term in a series of proportionals beginning with the unit makes any power representable as a line. Dimensional homogeneity, the constraint of Greek and Vietan algebra, is dissolved by the unit. The autobiographical confession dates the discovery to Descartes's own practice.

§ 5. Although we abstract the terms of a difficulty from given numbers to examine its nature, it often happens that it can be solved more simply in the given numbers than in abstraction. This is because of the double use of numbers mentioned before: they express sometimes order, sometimes measure. So after examining the difficulty in general terms, we should restate it in the given numbers to see whether they offer a simpler solution. Having seen that the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides a and b is √(a² + b²), we substitute 81 for a² and 144 for b², which make 225, whose root, the mean proportional between the unit and 225, is 15. From this we will know that the hypotenuse 15 is commensurable with the sides 9 and 12, which we would not know in general merely from its being the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose sides are as 3 to 4. We who seek evident and distinct knowledge distinguish all these things, unlike the arithmeticians, who are content if the sum they seek turns up, though they do not see how it depends on the data; yet in this alone does knowledge properly consist. Numbers carry information that the general expression lacks, here commensurability, which depends on the particular values. The method uses both levels. The last sentence restates the definition of knowledge that runs through the treatise: to know is to see how the sought depends on the given, not merely to have the answer.

§ 6. In general, nothing that does not need constant attention should ever be entrusted to memory if it can be set down on paper, so that no needless recollection draws any part of the mind from the knowledge of the present object. We should make a table in which we write first the terms of the question as first proposed, then how they are abstracted, and finally by what notations they are designated, so that once the solution is found in these notations we can apply it to the particular subject of the question without the help of memory, since one always abstracts something more general from something less general. Descartes writes the figure of triangle ABC: the hypotenuse AC of the right triangle ABC is sought; the difficulty is abstracted so that in general the magnitude of the hypotenuse is sought from the magnitudes of the sides; then a is put for AB, which is 9, and b for BC, which is 12; and so on. The written table records the path of abstraction so that it can be retraced in reverse to apply the general solution, the double movement of Rule Five carried out on paper.

§ 7. In the third part of the treatise these four rules will be used further, in a somewhat broader sense than here, as will be explained in its place. Rules Thirteen to Sixteen, on abstraction, figure, drawing and notation, were meant to serve the imperfect questions of Book Three as well, a promise left unfulfilled.

### Rule Seventeen: On the Indirect Resolution of Questions

Rule Seventeen (AT X 459–462) states that the difficulty proposed is to be gone through directly, setting aside that some of its terms are known and others unknown, and intuiting the mutual dependence of each on the others by true reasoning. The rule's instruction is to treat unknowns as if they were known: the formulation of what will become the analytic method of setting up equations.

§ 1. The preceding four rules showed how determinate and perfectly understood difficulties are to be abstracted from particular subjects and reduced to the point where only certain magnitudes are sought, to be known from their bearing this or that relation to certain given ones. The following five rules will explain how these difficulties are to be treated so that, however many unknown magnitudes a proposition contains, they can all be subordinated to one another, so that the first is to the unit as the second to the first, the third to the second, the fourth to the third, and so on, and that however many there are they make a sum equal to some known magnitude. This will be done by a method so certain that we can safely say no effort could have reduced the magnitudes to simpler terms. The goal is stated: to reduce any perfect question to a single equation in which the unknowns form a series of continued proportionals, that is, the powers of one unknown, whose sum equals a known quantity. This is a polynomial equation in one unknown, the canonical form of the *Geometry*.

§ 2. In every question to be resolved by deduction there is one plain and direct way by which we pass most easily from one term to another, and all others are harder and indirect. To understand this, recall Rule Eleven, which explained a chain of propositions in which, comparing each with its neighbours, we easily see how the first and last are related, though we cannot so easily deduce the intermediate ones from the extremes. If we intuit the dependence of each on the next without any break in order, so as to infer how the last depends on the first, we are going through the difficulty directly. If, on the contrary, knowing the first and last to be connected in a certain way, we wish to deduce the intermediate ones that connect them, we follow a wholly indirect and reverse order. But since we are concerned here only with involved questions, in which certain intermediate terms must be known in a disordered sequence from known extremes, the whole artifice consists in supposing the unknown known so that we can prepare an easy and direct way of inquiry even in the most involved difficulties. Nothing prevents this, since we have presupposed from the beginning of this part that the unknown terms of the question are so dependent on the known that they are wholly determined by them. So if we reflect on the terms that first occur to us in recognising this determination, and count them, though unknown, among the known, so as to deduce from them step by step and by true reasoning all the others, even the known ones as if they were unknown, we will carry out everything this rule prescribes. Examples of this and of most of what follows are reserved for the twenty-fourth rule. The direct–indirect distinction of Rule Six, § 9, becomes the theory of analysis. A problem given in indirect order (extremes known, means sought) is converted into a direct one by naming the unknowns and computing forward from them until the known data are expressed twice, which produces the equation. The determinacy of perfect questions (Rule Thirteen, § 1) is what licenses the fiction: since the unknowns are fixed by the data, they can be treated as if given. This is the ancient method of analysis described by Pappus, which Rule Four, § 5, accused the ancients of concealing; Descartes here states it as an explicit procedure.

### Rule Eighteen: On the Operations of Geometrical Algebra

Rule Eighteen (AT X 462–468) states that for this only four operations are needed: addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, of which the last two are often to be postponed here, both to avoid involving ourselves in anything blindly and because they can be carried out more easily later. The reduction of algebraic procedure to four operations, all representable on lines and rectangles, completes the representational system of Rules Fourteen to Sixteen.

§ 1. A multitude of rules often comes from the teacher's inexperience, and what can be reduced to one general precept is less clear when divided among many particular ones. So all the operations needed to go through questions, that is, to deduce some magnitudes from others, are brought under four headings; how they suffice will be seen from their explanation. Economy of rules is a principle of clarity: the same conviction that led to the four precepts of the *Discourse*.

§ 2. If we know a magnitude because we have the parts of which it is composed, this is done by addition; if we know a part because we have the whole and the excess of the whole over the part, by subtraction. There are no other ways of deducing one magnitude from others taken absolutely and in which it is somehow contained. But if a magnitude is to be found from others quite different from it, in which it is in no way contained, it must be related to them in some way; if this relation is followed directly, we use multiplication, if indirectly, division. The four operations are derived from two distinctions: whether the sought magnitude is contained in the given ones (addition, subtraction) or related to them (multiplication, division), and whether the order is direct or reverse.

§ 3. To explain these two operations clearly, note that the unit is here the basis and foundation of all relations and holds the first place in a series of continued proportionals. The given magnitudes hold the second place, and the sought ones the third, fourth and further places if the proposition is direct; if it is indirect, the sought magnitude holds the second and the other intermediate places, and the given one the last. The unit grounds all relation because multiplication is defined as a proportion with the unit: every product is the fourth term of a proportion whose first term is the unit.

§ 4. If it is said that as the unit is to a, or a given 5, so b, or a given 7, is to the sought magnitude, which is ab or 35, then a and b hold the second place and their product ab the third. If it is added that as the unit is to c, or 9, so ab, or 35, is to the sought abc, or 315, then abc holds the fourth place and is produced by two multiplications of ab and c, which hold the second place. Likewise, as the unit is to a, or 5, so a, or 5, is to a², or 25; as the unit to a, so a² to a³, or 125; and as the unit to a, so a³ to a⁴, or 625. Multiplying a magnitude by itself is done no differently from multiplying it by a wholly different magnitude. Multiplication is construction of a fourth proportional. The last sentence makes powers a special case of products, so that squares and cubes lose any special geometrical status, as Rule Sixteen, § 4, demanded.

§ 5. If it is said that as the unit is to a given divisor, a or 5, so the sought B, or 7, is to the given dividend ab, or 35, the order is disordered and indirect, so B can be found only by dividing the given ab by the given a. The same holds if it is said that as the unit is to A, or 5, the sought magnitude, so A, the sought magnitude, is to a², or 25, the given magnitude; or that as the unit is to A, so A² is to a³, or 125; and so on. All these are included under division, though the latter cases are harder than the former, because the sought magnitude appears more often in them and so involves more relations. They mean the same as the arithmeticians' "extract the square root of a²" or "the cube root of a³," or in geometers' terms, "find the mean proportional between the unit and a²," or "two mean proportionals between the unit and a³." Root extraction is unified with division as an indirect operation, distinguished only by the number of relations in which the unknown recurs. The geometrical equivalent, finding mean proportionals, ties the algebra back to the problem of continued proportionals studied since Rule Six, § 8.

§ 6. These two operations suffice to find whatever magnitudes are to be deduced from others by some relation. Once understood, it remains to explain how they are to be presented to the imagination and even shown to the eyes, and then their use or practice. The order of exposition follows the auxiliaries: concept, imagination, sense.

§ 7. For addition or subtraction, the subject is conceived as a line, or an extended magnitude in which length alone is considered: to add line a to line b, they are joined end to end to make ab; to subtract b from a, one is laid on the other and the remainder is the difference. For multiplication, the two lines are joined at right angles to form a rectangle ab. To multiply ab by c, ab must be conceived as a line, so as to make the figure abc. In division, where the divisor is given, the magnitude to be divided is imagined as a rectangle one side of which is the divisor and the other the quotient: dividing the rectangle ab by a removes the side a and leaves b as quotient; dividing by b removes b and leaves a. Each operation has a figure: addition and subtraction on lines, multiplication and division on rectangles. The requirement that a rectangle be reconceived as a line before further multiplication follows from the two-dimension limit of Rule Fourteen, § 24, and is resolved in § 10.

§ 8. In divisions where the divisor is not given but only indicated by a relation, as in extracting square or cube roots, the term to be divided and all the others must always be conceived as lines in a series of continued proportionals, the first of which is the unit and the last the magnitude to be divided. How any number of mean proportionals between it and the unit is to be found will be said in its place. It is enough for now to note that such operations are not yet complete here, since they must be performed by indirect and reverse movements of the imagination, and we are now dealing only with questions gone through directly. Root extraction is deferred because it requires the reverse movement; the general construction of mean proportionals, which Descartes solved with his proportional compass and treated in the *Geometry*, is never reached in the *Regulae*.

§ 9. The other operations are easily carried out as conceived. It remains to explain how their terms are to be prepared. Though we are free, when first dealing with a difficulty, to conceive its terms as lines or rectangles and never assign them other figures, as Rule Fourteen stated, in the course of operating a rectangle produced by multiplying two lines must often be conceived as a line for another operation; or the same rectangle, or a line produced by addition or subtraction, must be conceived as another rectangle constructed on the line by which it is to be divided. The representational system needs a conversion between lines and rectangles so that the result of one operation can enter the next.

§ 10. It is worth explaining here how every rectangle can be transformed into a line and, conversely, how a line or rectangle can be transformed into another rectangle with a given side. This is very easy for geometers, provided they see that whenever we compare lines with a rectangle, as here, we always conceive the lines as rectangles one side of which is the length taken as unit. The whole matter thus reduces to this proposition: given a rectangle, to construct on a given side another rectangle equal to it. The device of Rule Fifteen, § 1, a line as a rectangle of unit breadth, now does its work: every product, however many factors, can be returned to a line, so that the algebra never leaves two dimensions. This is the basis of the *Geometry*'s treatment of all algebraic quantities as segments.

§ 11. Though even beginners in geometry know this, Descartes wants to explain it so as not to seem to have omitted anything. The text breaks off here; the manuscripts note that the rest is wanting. The exposition stops at the threshold of a construction every geometer knew (Euclid, *Elements* I.44–45), which may suggest that the treatise was abandoned rather than lost. The program of Rules Seventeen to Twenty-one passed, transformed, into the first book of the *Geometry*.

### Rule Nineteen

Rule Nineteen (AT X 468–469) survives only as a statement: by this method of reasoning, we must seek as many magnitudes expressed in two different ways as we suppose unknown terms known in order to go through the difficulty directly, for we will thus have as many comparisons between two equal terms. Each magnitude computed in two ways yields an equation, and the number of independent equations must match the number of unknowns. This is the rule for setting up a determinate system of equations, the direct continuation of Rule Seventeen's fiction of the known unknown.

### Rule Twenty

Rule Twenty (AT X 469) survives only as a statement: once the equations are found, we must carry out the operations we have left aside, never using multiplication when division is called for. The postponed operations of Rule Eighteen, multiplication and division, are now performed on the equations. The prohibition on multiplying where division is appropriate avoids raising the degree of an equation needlessly and so introducing spurious roots.

### Rule Twenty-One

Rule Twenty-One (AT X 469) survives only as a statement: if there are several such equations, they must all be reduced to a single one, namely the one whose terms occupy the fewest places in the series of magnitudes in continued proportion, according to which the terms are to be arranged in order. Elimination reduces a system to one equation in one unknown, of the lowest possible degree, with its terms ordered by powers. The criterion of "fewest places in the series" is the criterion of degree, defined by relations to the unit as in Rule Sixteen, § 4. The goal stated in Rule Seventeen, § 1, is thus reached in form: the whole of a perfect question reduced to a single equation in canonical order.

### Rules Twenty-Two through Twenty-Four

Nothing of these rules survives. Rule Seventeen, § 2, reserved the worked examples of the method for Rule Twenty-four.

## Book Three: On Imperfectly Understood Questions

### Rules Twenty-Five through Thirty-Six

Nothing of Book Three survives. Its subject, announced in Rule Eight, § 10, Rule Twelve, § 27, and Rule Thirteen, § 1, was to be the questions whose terms include composites known by experience, such as the nature of the magnet or of sound, and their reduction to perfect questions by fixing the data. The physics of *The World* and the *Dioptrics*, and the method of hypothesis and experiment in Part VI of the *Discourse*, occupy the place it would have filled.
